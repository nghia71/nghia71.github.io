Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 25: Algebraic Word Problem Models · Heads, Legs, and Two-Type Counting

Helena's vinyl record collection contains $33$ LP records and $45$ EP records. An LP record plays for three times as long as an EP record. How many more EP records must Helena buy so that the total playing time of her EP records equals the total playing time of her LP records?

(A)$45$(B)$54$(C)$63$(D)$78$(E)$87$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the playing time of a single LP with the playing time of a single EP.
Hint 2: key idea
Measure the total playing times in units of one EP, so that both collections use the same unit. (LPS VI, Chapter 25: Algebraic Word Problem Models)
Hint 3: approach
Multiply the LP count by the number of EP units in one LP, then subtract the playing time already supplied by the EP collection.
Common trap
The question asks for additional EPs, so account for the records Helena already owns.
Show the answer

(B) $54$

Show solution 1 – Measure in EP-lengths

Measure playing time in units of one EP record. Each LP lasts $3$ units, so the LP records last $33\cdot3=99$ units in total. The EP records last $45$ units, so Helena needs $99-45=54$ more EP records. \[\boxed{54}\]

Show solution 2 – Match LPs with EPs

Think of each LP as equivalent to a group of $3$ EPs. The $45$ EPs she owns fill exactly $45/3=15$ such groups, so they balance $15$ of the LPs. The other $33-15=18$ LPs still need $3$ EPs each: $18\cdot3=54$. \[\boxed{54}\]

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Percent Increase and Decrease · also AMC 12A Problem 1

A sign in a parking lot states that the total price includes a $27\%$ tax. To the nearest whole number, what percentage of the amount a customer pays is this tax?

(A)$21\%$(B)$22\%$(C)$27\%$(D)$37\%$(E)$73\%$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Distinguish the price before tax, the tax itself, and the amount paid.
Hint 2: key idea
Use a before-tax price of $100$ to turn the posted rate into a tax amount. (LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change)
Hint 3: approach
Add the tax to the base price, divide the tax by the resulting amount paid, then convert to a percentage and round.
Common trap
Use the full price paid as the denominator.
Show the answer

(A) $21\%$

Show solution 1 – Pick a base price

Suppose the price before tax is $100$. The tax is $27$ and the customer pays $127$. The tax is \[\frac{27}{127}\approx0.2126\] of the amount paid, that is, about $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Show solution 2 – Bound the fraction without dividing

The tax is $\frac{27}{127}$ of the amount paid. To round to the nearest percent we only need to know whether it is above or below $20.5\%$ and $21.5\%$: \[0.205\cdot127=26.035<27\qquad\text{and}\qquad0.215\cdot127=27.305>27.\] So $20.5\%<\frac{27}{127}<21.5\%$, which rounds to $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Problem 3

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry · AA Similarity · also AMC 12A Problem 2

Square $ABCD$ has side length $24$. Let $E$ be the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$, and let $F$ be the intersection of $\overline{AE}$ and $\overline{BD}$. What is the area of $\triangle BEF$?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$45$(D)$48$(E)$52$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Use $BE$ as the base and identify the distance from $F$ to line $BC$ as the height you need.
Hint 2: key idea
Since $BE\parallel AD$, compare triangles $FBE$ and $FDA$ by their angles. (LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the ratio of the parallel sides to find what fraction of the way $F$ lies from $B$ to $D$. Apply that fraction to the height of $D$ above $BC$ before using the triangle area formula.
Common trap
The diagonal segment $BF$ is slanted and is not the height above $BE$.
Show the answer

(D) $48$

Show solution 1 – Similar triangles

Since $BE\parallel AD$, triangles $FBE$ and $FDA$ are similar (alternate angles), with ratio $BE:DA=12:24=1:2$. So $F$ divides the diagonal $BD$ in the ratio $BF:FD=1:2$, and its distance from line $BC$ is $\frac13$ of the distance from $D$ to $BC$, namely $\frac13\cdot24=8$. Hence \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot BE\cdot8=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(24,0)$, $A=(0,24)$, $D=(24,24)$, so $E=(12,0)$. Line $BD$ is $y=x$ and line $AE$ is $y=24-2x$. They meet where $x=24-2x$, so $F=(8,8)$. Triangle $BEF$ has base $BE=12$ on the $x$-axis and height $8$: \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Show solution 3 – Area ratios

$[ABE]=\frac12\cdot AB\cdot BE=\frac12\cdot24\cdot12=144$. As in Solution 1, $AF:FE=DF:FB=2:1$, so $FE=\frac13 AE$. Triangles $BEF$ and $BEA$ share the vertex $B$ and have bases $FE$ and $AE$ on the same line, so \[[BEF]=\tfrac13[ABE]=\tfrac13\cdot144=\boxed{48}.\]

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · Polygon Angle and Diagonal Structure · also AMC 12A Problem 3

Half of the internal angles of a polygon measure $150^\circ$ each, and the other half of its internal angles measure $190^\circ$ each. How many sides does the polygon have?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$60$(D)$72$(E)$180$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Imagine walking around the polygon and turning at each vertex. Compare the direction of the turn at the two types of corner.
Hint 2: key idea
The signed turn at a vertex is $180^\circ$ minus the internal angle. Use the fact that the turns around a polygon add to a full turn. (LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Let $n$ be the side count and include $n/2$ turns of each type in your equation. Set the total equal to $360^\circ$ and solve for $n$.
Common trap
A reflex angle contributes a negative turn when walking counterclockwise.
Show the answer

(A) $36$

Show solution 1 – Exterior angles

Walking around the polygon, the turning (exterior) angle at a vertex is $180^\circ$ minus the interior angle: $30^\circ$ at a $150^\circ$ vertex and $-10^\circ$ at a $190^\circ$ (reflex) vertex. The turns add up to one full turn, $360^\circ$. With $n/2$ vertices of each kind, \[\tfrac n2\cdot30-\tfrac n2\cdot10=10n=360,\qquad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Show solution 2 – Interior angle sum

The interior angles of an $n$-gon add up to $180(n-2)$ degrees. Here they add up to $\frac n2\cdot150+\frac n2\cdot190=170n$. So \[170n=180n-360\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Recursive Definitions · also AMC 12A Problem 4

Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ be a sequence with $a_1 = 94$, $a_2 = 190$, and $a_n$ equal to the arithmetic mean of the previous $n-1$ terms for $n \ge 3$. What is the value of $a_{10}$?

(A)$47$(B)$95$(C)$142$(D)$284$(E)$564$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Write down the rule for obtaining the third term from the first two. What does the fourth term average?
Hint 2: key idea
If a list has mean $m$, write its sum as its length times $m$. Use that form to determine what happens to the mean when you append one more entry of value $m$. (LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns)
Hint 3: approach
Compute $a_3$ from the rule, then apply that observation step by step to describe $a_4,a_5,\ldots$ before reading off the requested term.
Show the answer

(C) $142$

Show solution 1 – Adding the mean keeps the mean

$a_3=\frac{94+190}2=142$. Now the first three terms have mean $142$: adding a number equal to the mean of a list does not change the mean. So $a_4=142$, and the mean is still $142$; the same happens at every step. Hence every term from $a_3$ on is $142$, and $a_{10}=\boxed{142}$.

Show solution 2 – Track the partial sums

Let $S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n$. For $n\ge2$ we have $a_{n+1}=S_n/n$, so \[S_{n+1}=S_n+\frac{S_n}{n}=\frac{n+1}{n}S_n,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{S_{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{S_n}{n}.\] So $S_n/n$ is constant for $n\ge2$: it equals $S_2/2=\frac{284}2=142$. Therefore $a_{10}=S_9/9=\boxed{142}$.

Problem 6

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition · Additive and Subtractive Area

A triangle with sides $16$, $30$, and $34$ is inscribed in a circle, dividing the interior of the circle into four regions. Let $A$, $B$, and $C$ be the areas of the non-triangular regions, with $C$ being the greatest. What is the value of $A+B$?

(A)$\dfrac{289\pi}{2}-240$(B)$\dfrac{289\pi}{2}-210$(C)$289\pi-480$(D)$289\pi-360$(E)$289\pi-240$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the triangle inside the circle. Which part of the disc lies beyond the longest side?
Hint 2: key idea
Test the side lengths with the Pythagorean theorem and use the resulting angle to identify a diameter. (LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the semicircle bounded by that diameter with the regions on the other side. In the other semicircle, subtract the right triangle's area to isolate the two requested regions.
Common trap
There are three non-triangular regions, and the largest is excluded from the sum.
Show the answer

(A) $\tfrac{289\pi}{2}-240$

Show solution 1 – The hypotenuse is a diameter

$16^2+30^2=256+900=1156=34^2$, so the triangle is right-angled and its hypotenuse $34$ is a diameter (Thales). The radius is $17$ and the circle has area $289\pi$.

The diameter cuts the disc into two half-discs of area $\frac{289\pi}2$ each. The region beyond the hypotenuse is a whole half-disc, so it is the largest region: $C=\frac{289\pi}2$. The other half-disc consists of the triangle (area $\frac12\cdot16\cdot30=240$) and the two small regions. So \[A+B=\frac{289\pi}2-240.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{289\pi}2-240}\]

Show solution 2 – Subtract everything else

As above, the circle has radius $17$ and $C=\frac{289\pi}2$. The four regions fill the disc: $A+B+C+240=289\pi$. Hence $A+B=289\pi-240-\frac{289\pi}2=\frac{289\pi}2-240$. (The answer choices with $289\pi$ forget that $C$ is half of the disc.) \[\boxed{\tfrac{289\pi}2-240}\]

Problem 7

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · Bounded Positive Solutions · also AMC 12A Problem 5

How many ordered triples of positive integers $(x,y,z)$ satisfy both of the following equations? \[x+y+z=31,\qquad x+3y+3z=41\]

(A)$4$(B)$5$(C)$6$(D)$7$(E)$8$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the coefficients of each variable in the two equations.
Hint 2: key idea
Subtract the first equation from the second to eliminate $x$ and isolate the sum of the other two variables. (LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations)
Hint 3: approach
For that sum, list the possible positive values of $y$ and determine $z$ from each. Check that the same positive value of $x$ works in every case.
Common trap
The triples are ordered, so exchanging unequal values of $y,z$ gives a different triple.
Show the answer

(A) $4$

Show solution 1 – Eliminate $x$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $2y+2z=10$, so $y+z=5$ and then $x=31-5=26$. The positive solutions of $y+z=5$ are $(y,z)=(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$, and each gives a valid triple with $x=26$. There are $\boxed{4}$ triples.

Show solution 2 – Treat $y+z$ as one unknown

Let $s=y+z$. Then $x+s=31$ and $x+3s=41$, a $2\times2$ system with $s=5$, $x=26$. The number of ways to write $5$ as an ordered sum of two positive integers is $5-1=4$ (choose $y\in\{1,2,3,4\}$). Answer: $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 8

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work · Distance, Rate, and Time

A driver sees an electronic highway sign stating that the next exit is $7$ miles away and it will take $5$ minutes to reach it. Assume both numbers have been rounded to the nearest integer. Among the following options, what is the lowest speed limit (in miles per hour) that would possibly allow the driver to legally reach the exit within the given time frame?

(A)$65$(B)$70$(C)$75$(D)$80$(E)$85$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the intervals of true distances and true times that can round to the displayed numbers.
Hint 2: key idea
To make the required average speed as small as possible, use the low end of the distance interval and approach the high end of the time interval. (LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work)
Hint 3: approach
Convert the limiting time from minutes to hours and compare the limiting speed with the choices. For the lowest candidate above the limit, check a permitted time slightly below the upper endpoint.
Common trap
The upper endpoint for the time is excluded, so the limiting speed need not be attainable.
Show the answer

(C) $75$

Show solution 1 – Slowest possible required speed

The true distance $d$ satisfies $6.5\le d<7.5$ miles and the true time $t$ satisfies $4.5\le t<5.5$ minutes. The required average speed $d/t$ can be made close to, but never as small as, the value with $d=6.5$ and $t=5.5$ (a time of $5.5$ minutes would round to $6$). So it is always more than \[\frac{6.5\text{ mi}}{5.5/60\text{ h}}=\frac{390}{5.5}\approx70.9\text{ mph}.\] So a $70$ mph limit never works. A $75$ mph limit can work: for example $d=6.5$ miles and $t=5.4$ minutes need only $6.5\cdot60/5.4\approx72.2$ mph. The answer is $\boxed{75}$.

Show solution 2 – Test the options as distances

At $70$ mph, in less than $5.5$ minutes the driver covers less than $70\cdot\frac{5.5}{60}\approx6.42$ miles, which is short of the smallest possible distance $6.5$ miles. So $70$ (and $65$) are too slow. At $75$ mph, in $5.4$ minutes (which rounds to $5$) the driver covers $75\cdot\frac{5.4}{60}=6.75$ miles, enough for a distance of, say, $6.6$ miles (which rounds to $7$). So the lowest option is $\boxed{75}$.

Problem 9

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry · Scale Effects

Two similar but not congruent scalene triangles each have a side with length $9$ and a side with length $12$. What is the length of the longest side of the larger triangle?

(A)$12$(B)$14$(C)$15$(D)$16$(E)$17$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the smaller triangle's sides as $9,12,x$ and introduce a scale factor greater than $1$ for the larger triangle.
Hint 2: key idea
The image of the side of length $12$ exceeds both shared lengths. Thus the two shared lengths in the larger triangle must come from the other two sides of the smaller one. (LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Decide which shared length can come from scaling the side of length $9$. Use that match to find the scale factor and check the largest side and the triangle inequalities.
Common trap
Equal numerical lengths in the two triangles need not be corresponding sides.
Show the answer

(D) $16$

Show solution 1 – Match the sides

Let the smaller triangle have sides $9,12,x$ and let the larger be $k$ times it, $k>1$. Its sides $9k,12k,xk$ must include $9$ and $12$.

$12k>12$, so $12k$ is neither $9$ nor $12$; it is the third side of the larger triangle. Then $9$ and $12$ are $9k$ and $xk$ in some order. $9k=9$ is impossible ($k\ne1$), so $9k=12$, $k=\frac43$, and $xk=9$, $x=\frac{27}4$. The triangles are $\frac{27}4,9,12$ and $9,12,16$, both genuine scalene triangles ($\frac{27}4+9>12$). The longest side of the larger triangle is $12k=\boxed{16}$.

Show solution 2 – A geometric chain

Write the sides of the smaller triangle in increasing order $p<q<r$; the larger is $kp<kq<kr$ with $k>1$. The two shared lengths must be two sides of the smaller triangle that equal two sides of the larger one. The only way two of $p,q,r$ can equal two of $kp,kq,kr$ is $q=kp$ and $r=kq$ (each larger side is the image of the next smaller one). So the four different lengths form a geometric progression $p,\,q,\,r,\,kr$ with ratio $k$, and the shared pair $q<r$ is $9,12$. Then $k=\frac{12}{9}=\frac43$ and the longest side of the larger triangle is $kr=\frac43\cdot12=\boxed{16}$.

Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Series and Summation Patterns · also AMC 12A Problem 6

For what odd positive integer $n$ is the mean of $1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots, n^2$ exactly $32\%$ greater than its median?

(A)$19$(B)$39$(C)$49$(D)$89$(E)$169$

On the AMC 12A paper (Problem 6) the word "odd" is left out; the answer is the same.

Hint 1: understand the problem
The squares are already listed in increasing order. For an odd number of entries, locate the middle index.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the sum-of-squares formula for the mean and square the middle index for the median. (LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns)
Hint 3: approach
Divide the mean by the median, set the ratio equal to the factor for a $32\%$ increase, and solve the resulting equation.
Common trap
The mean is $32\%$ greater than the median, rather than $32\%$ of it.
Show the answer

(C) $49$

Show solution 1 – Formulas for mean and median

For odd $n$ the median is the middle square $\left(\frac{n+1}2\right)^2$. The mean is \[\frac{1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2}{n}=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6.\] Their ratio is \[\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)/6}{(n+1)^2/4}=\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac{132}{100}=\frac{33}{25}.\] So $50(2n+1)=99(n+1)$, that is $100n+50=99n+99$, and $n=\boxed{49}$.

Show solution 2 – Check against the choices

Using the ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}$ from Solution 1, the answer must make it exactly $1.32$. For $n=49$: $\frac{2\cdot99}{3\cdot50}=\frac{198}{150}=1.32$. Directly: the median is $25^2=625$ and the mean is $\frac{50\cdot99}{6}=825=1.32\cdot625$. The ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac43-\frac{2}{3(n+1)}$ increases with $n$, so no other $n$ works. Answer $\boxed{49}$.

Problem 11

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

Gary writes down six distinct positive integers: $2, 5, 24, 60, 240$, and a three-digit number $n$. One of those six numbers is the least common multiple of all six of them. What is the sum of all possible values of $n$?

(A)$960$(B)$1680$(C)$2160$(D)$2280$(E)$2400$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Find the least common multiple of the five given numbers before considering the three-digit entry.
Hint 2: key idea
The least common multiple of all six must be at least as large as each given number. Identify the two entries that could play this role. (LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction)
Hint 3: approach
For each candidate, translate the least common multiple condition into a divisibility condition on $n$. List the eligible three-digit values from the two cases and sum them.
Common trap
The entries are distinct, so exclude $n=240$ from both cases.
Show the answer

(D) $2280$

Show solution 1 – Which number is the LCM?

The least common multiple $L$ of all six numbers is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(2,5,24,60,240)=240$, and it is the largest of the six. So $L$ is $240$ or $n$.

If $L=240$: $n$ divides $240$, has three digits, and differs from $240$; the only such divisor is $n=120$.

If $L=n$: $n$ is a three-digit multiple of $240$ other than $240$: $480,720,960$.

The sum is $120+480+720+960=\boxed{2280}$.

Show solution 2 – Prime exponents

$240=2^4\cdot3\cdot5$. Every number in the list must divide the largest one, which is $240$ or $n$.

If $n$ is not the largest, $n=2^a3^b5^c$ with $a\le4$, $b,c\le1$, three digits, $n\ne240$: the divisors of $240$ between $100$ and $999$ are $120$ and $240$, so $n=120$.

If $n$ is the largest, $240\mid n$, so $n=240m$ with $2\le m\le4$: $480,720,960$.

Sum: $\boxed{2280}$.

Problem 12

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · Dependent and Independent Events

A drawer has $100$ red socks and $100$ blue socks. Pippi randomly removes one sock at a time, without replacement, until all the socks of one color have been removed. What is the probability that there are exactly $2$ socks left in the drawer once that happens?

(A)$\dfrac{27}{196}$(B)$\dfrac{49}{198}$(C)$\dfrac{247}{990}$(D)$\dfrac{50}{199}$(E)$\dfrac{25}{99}$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Imagine continuing the draws after Pippi stops. Look at what the colors of the last few socks would have to be.
Hint 2: key idea
Exactly two socks remain when the final run of one color has length two. Identify the color pattern of the last three draws that gives such a run. (LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value)
Hint 3: approach
Read the random order backward and multiply the successive conditional probabilities for one choice of the final color. Then account for the other choice of color.
Common trap
The third-to-last sock must have the other color, since otherwise Pippi would stop earlier.
Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac{50}{199}$

Show solution 1 – Look at the end of the full order

Imagine Pippi keeps going and removes all $200$ socks; the order is a uniformly random arrangement. She stops when one colour runs out. Exactly $2$ socks are left then if and only if the last two socks have the same colour and the third-to-last sock has the other colour.

The probability that the last sock is red, the one before it red, and the one before that blue is $\frac{100}{200}\cdot\frac{99}{199}\cdot\frac{100}{198}$ (reading the order backwards). Doubling for the colours swapped, \[2\cdot\frac{100\cdot99\cdot100}{200\cdot199\cdot198}=\frac{50}{199}.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{50}{199}}\]

Show solution 2 – Count colour patterns

Every colour pattern of the $200$ socks (a word with $100$ R's and $100$ B's) is equally likely; there are $\binom{200}{100}$ of them. The good patterns end in $\dots XYY$ with $X\ne Y$: choose $Y$ ($2$ ways); the first $197$ positions hold $99$ socks of colour $X$ and $98$ of colour $Y$, in $\binom{197}{98}$ ways. So the probability is \[\frac{2\binom{197}{98}}{\binom{200}{100}}=\frac{2\cdot100\cdot100\cdot99}{200\cdot199\cdot198}=\frac{50}{199}.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{50}{199}}\]

Problem 13

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Structured Two-Variable Models

Two bicycle clubs, with disjoint memberships, go on a tour together. On the morning of the tour, members greet each other with a handshake. Everyone shakes hands with everyone else exactly once. There were $119$ handshakes between members of the same club and $112$ handshakes between members of different clubs. How many more people came from the larger club than from the smaller club?

(A)$0$(B)$4$(C)$6$(D)$7$(E)$8$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Add the two types of handshakes to find the total. Each handshake corresponds to a pair of people.
Hint 2: key idea
If the club sizes are $m,n$, use the total to find $m+n$ and the between-club count to find $mn$. (LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints)
Hint 3: approach
Find the two positive integers with the required sum and product, then subtract the smaller from the larger.
Common trap
Count each unordered pair of people just once.
Show the answer

(C) $6$

Show solution 1 – Total first

There are $119+112=231$ handshakes in all, so the number of people $N$ satisfies $\binom N2=231$, $N(N-1)=462$, $N=22$. The cross handshakes give $mn=112$ with $m+n=22$, so $m,n$ are the roots of $t^2-22t+112=0$: $8$ and $14$. The difference is $\boxed{6}$.

Show solution 2 – Square of the difference

As above, $m+n=22$ and $mn=112$. Then \[(n-m)^2=(m+n)^2-4mn=484-448=36,\] so $n-m=\boxed{6}$. (Check: $\binom82+\binom{14}2=28+91=119$.)

Problem 14

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Bounding Integer Solutions

Consider a triangle with positive area whose side lengths are functions of $t$ given by $a=t^2+1$, $b=5t+7$, and $c=4t+4$. How many different integer values of $t$ are possible?

(A)$0$(B)$7$(C)$9$(D)$10$(E)$\text{infinitely many}$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Write all three inequalities comparing the sum of two sides with the third side.
Hint 2: key idea
Simplify the three inequalities to quadratics in $t$. Factor those with real roots and use the sign of each quadratic to restrict $t$. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Intersect the allowed ranges, check positivity of the resulting side lengths, and count the integers in the intersection.
Common trap
The area must be positive, so endpoints giving equality in a triangle inequality are excluded.
Show the answer

(B) $7$

Show solution 1 – Three triangle inequalities

We need $a,b,c>0$ and the three strict triangle inequalities.

  • $a+b>c$: $t^2+t+4>0$, always true since $t^2+t+4=\left(t+\frac12\right)^2+\frac{15}4$.
  • $a+c>b$: $t^2-t-2>0$, i.e. $(t-2)(t+1)>0$: $t>2$ or $t<-1$.
  • $b+c>a$: $t^2-9t-10<0$, i.e. $(t-10)(t+1)<0$: $-1<t<10$.

Together: $2<t<10$, and then all sides are positive. The integers are $t=3,4,\dots,9$: $\boxed{7}$ values.

Show solution 2 – Find the degenerate cases first

The triangle flattens exactly when one side equals the sum of the other two. Solving $a=b+c$ gives $t^2-9t-10=0$, $t=10$ or $t=-1$; solving $b=a+c$ gives $t^2-t-2=0$, $t=2$ or $t=-1$; $c=a+b$ has no real solution. First, $c=4t+4>0$ forces $t>-1$. The starting fraction is in row zero, so count steps rather than fractions visited.
Show the answer

Every entry is less than $1$, and every entry is in lowest terms: the root is, and $\gcd(a,a+b)=\gcd(b,a+b)=\gcd(a,b)=1$. So a child $\frac pq$ of $\frac ab$, written in lowest terms, has $q=a+b$ and $p=a$ or $p=b$, so its parent is $\frac{p}{q-p}$ or $\frac{q-p}{p}$; only the one that is less than $1$ can be in the tree. Show solution 2 – Continued fractions

(One checks that the representations stay valid.) The root $\frac12$ has $\frac21=[2]$, sum $2$, so an entry in row $r$ has sum $r+2$.

Hint 3: approach

The caps apply to points, so a color can have more balls drawn than its point cap.
Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac{2}{429}$

Show solution 1 – Find the lowest score, then count

Let the draw have $r$ red, $k$ black and $w$ white balls, $r+k+w=7$.

If $r\ge3$, red gives $3$ points and $k+w=7-r\ge1$ gives at least $1$ more, so the score is at least $4$; it is exactly $4$ only when $k+w=1$, i.e. $r=6$: $(6,1,0)$ or $(6,0,1)$.

If $r=0$: $k+w=7$ with $k,w\le4$, so $\{k,w\}=\{4,3\}$ and the score is $2+2=4$: $(0,4,3)$ or $(0,3,4)$.

If $r=1$: $k+w=6$ with $k,w\le4$, so both are at least $2$ and the score is $1+2+2=5$.

If $r=2$: $k+w=5$, so both are at least $1$ and one is at least $3$; the score is at least $2+2+1=5$.

So the lowest score is $4$. The number of ways: $2\binom66\binom41+2\binom44\binom43=8+8=16$, out of $\binom{14}7=3432$: \[\frac{16}{3432}=\boxed{\tfrac{2}{429}}.\]

  • $r=6$: $3$ red extras, and the seventh ball is not an extra: $3$ extras.
  • $3\le r\le5$: $r-3\le2$ red extras, and the $7-r\le4$ others give at most $\max(0,7-r-2)$, in total at most $2$.

So at most $3$ extras (the lowest score is $4$), reached exactly by all $6$ reds plus one other ball, or no reds with $4$ of one of black/white and $3$ of the other. Counting as in Solution 1 gives $16$ draws out of $3432$: $\boxed{\tfrac2{429}}$.

Show solution 1 – Balance the far corners

Use coordinates on the table: the two face-sharing cubes stand on $[0,2]\times[0,2]$ and $[2,4]\times[0,2]$, the third on $[1,3]\times[-2,0]$; all have height $2$. Show solution 2 – A smallest circle in the top view

When $p=0$, discard any negative $q$ that the difference inequality would otherwise permit.
Show the answer

(D) $235$

Show solution 1 – Characterise the pairs: $|2p-3q|\le3$

Necessity. In a list of $L$ consecutive integers there are $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$ or $\lceil L/2\rceil$ even numbers, so $L-1\le2p\le L+1$, and $\lfloor L/3\rfloor$ or $\lceil L/3\rceil$ multiples of $3$, so $L-2\le3q\le L+2$. Hence $|2p-3q|\le3$.

Sufficiency. For each $d=2p-3q\in\{-3,\dots,3\}$ the pairs with that value of $d$ and $p,q\ge0$ are $(p_0+3k,\,q_0+2k)$, $k\ge0$, where the smallest pair $(p_0,q_0)$ and a list realising it are: \[\begin{array}{c|ccccccc} d&0&1&-1&2&-2&3&-3\\\hline (p_0,q_0)&(0,0)&(2,1)&(1,1)&(1,0)&(2,2)&(3,1)&(0,1)\\ \text{list}&\{1\}&\{2,3,4\}&\{3,4\}&\{2\}&\{3,4,5,6\}&\{4,\dots,8\}&\{3\} \end{array}\] Appending the next $6$ consecutive integers to a list adds exactly $3$ multiples of $2$ and $2$ multiples of $3$, i.e. adds $(3,2)$. So every pair with $|2p-3q|\le3$ occurs.

Counting. For a fixed $p$, the allowed $q$ satisfy $\frac{2p-3}3\le q\le\frac{2p+3}3$, all at most $67$. If $3\mid p$ there are $3$ values ($\frac{2p}3$ and its two neighbours), except that $p=0$ loses $q=-1$; otherwise there are $2$ values. With $34$ multiples of $3$ in $0,\dots,100$: \[34\cdot3-1+67\cdot2=101+134=\boxed{235}.\]

Show solution 2 – Count by $q$ instead

Use the characterisation $|2p-3q|\le3$ from Solution 1, but count column by column in $q$. For fixed $q$ we need $\frac{3q-3}2\le p\le\frac{3q+3}2$.

  • $q$ even: $p\in\{\frac{3q}2-1,\frac{3q}2,\frac{3q}2+1\}$, three values.
  • $q$ odd: $p\in\{\frac{3q-3}2,\frac{3q-1}2,\frac{3q+1}2,\frac{3q+3}2\}$, four values.

The edges: $q=0$ loses $p=-1$ (two values); $q=67$ gives $p\in\{99,100,101,102\}$, of which only $99,100$ are allowed; $q\ge68$ needs $p\ge101$. For $1\le q\le66$ there are $33$ odd and $33$ even values of $q$. Total \[2+33\cdot4+33\cdot3+2=\boxed{235}.\]

Problem 24

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Remainder, Factor, and Evaluation Logic · also AMC 12A Problem 20

How many polynomials $f(x)$ of degree $3$ satisfying \[f\left(3+\sqrt5\right)=f\left(3-\sqrt5\right)\] are there that have leading coefficient equal to $1$ and all of whose coefficients are integers between $-10$ and $10$, inclusive?

(A)$21$(B)$42$(C)$63$(D)$84$(E)$105$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Write $f(x)=x^3+bx^2+cx+d$ and label the two inputs $r,s$. Find their sum and product.
Hint 2: key idea
In $f(r)-f(s)$ the constant term cancels. Factor out $r-s$ and express the remaining terms using $r+s$ and $rs$. (LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
Use the resulting linear relation to find which integers $b$ give an allowed $c$. Multiply the number of compatible pairs by the number of allowed constant terms.
Common trap
The leading coefficient is fixed, while the constant term never enters the equality.
Show the answer

(D) $84$

Show solution 1 – Factor $f(r)-f(s)$

Let $f(x)=x^3+bx^2+cx+d$, $r=3+\sqrt5$, $s=3-\sqrt5$, so $r+s=6$, $rs=4$. Then \[f(r)-f(s)=(r-s)\left(r^2+rs+s^2+b(r+s)+c\right)=(r-s)(32+6b+c),\] using $r^2+rs+s^2=(r+s)^2-rs=32$. Since $r\ne s$ the condition is $c=-32-6b$. With $-10\le c\le10$: $-42\le6b\le-22$, so $b\in\{-7,-6,-5,-4\}$, giving $c=10,4,-2,-8$, all allowed. The constant $d$ is free: $21$ values. Total $4\cdot21=\boxed{84}$.

Show solution 2 – Conjugates

Compute $f(3+\sqrt5)$ in the form $P+Q\sqrt5$ with integers $P,Q$: $(3+\sqrt5)^2=14+6\sqrt5$ and $(3+\sqrt5)^3=72+32\sqrt5$, so \[f(3+\sqrt5)=(72+14b+3c+d)+(32+6b+c)\sqrt5.\] Replacing $\sqrt5$ by $-\sqrt5$ gives $f(3-\sqrt5)$. The two values are equal exactly when the $\sqrt5$ part vanishes: $32+6b+c=0$. Counting as in Solution 1: $4$ pairs $(b,c)$ and $21$ values of $d$, $\boxed{84}$ polynomials.

Problem 25

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Chris the squirrel has $20$ rooms in his cavern for storing food. The first room contains $1$ nut, the second room contains $2$ nuts, the third room contains $3$ nuts, and so on, with the $20$th room containing $20$ nuts. Starting from today, Chris will collect $18$ additional nuts each day and store them one per room in $18$ distinct rooms in his cavern. His goal is to have all the rooms contain the same number of nuts once he is finished. What is the minimum number of days required to accomplish this goal?

(A)$75$(B)$95$(C)$105$(D)$190$(E)$195$
Hint 1: understand the problem
Count the rooms that do not get a nut in one day. For each room, record its number of skipped days.
Hint 2: key idea
Express the final holdings in terms of the room index, the number of days and its skip count. Equal final holdings fix the differences between the skip counts. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Sum the skip counts to bound the number of days from below. Then show the bound is attained: you need a daily schedule in which each day skips two different rooms and each room is skipped exactly its required number of times.
Common trap
A daily schedule cannot skip the same room twice on one day.
Show the answer

(B) $95$

Show solution 1 – Count the skips

Each day Chris skips exactly $2$ rooms. After $D$ days let room $i$ have been skipped $s_i$ times; it then holds $i+D-s_i$ nuts. All rooms equal means $s_i=s_1+(i-1)$. Adding over the rooms, $\sum s_i=2D$ gives $20s_1+190=2D$, so $D=10s_1+95\ge95$.

$D=95$ is possible: room $i$ must be skipped $i-1$ times, $190$ skips in all, $2$ per day. Write the skips in a list grouped by room ($room\ 2$ once, $room\ 3$ twice, …, room $20$ nineteen times) and on day $j$ skip entries $j$ and $j+95$ of the list. These are in different rooms because no room has more than $19<95$ entries. Every room ends with $96$ nuts. Answer $\boxed{95}$.

Show solution 2 – Totals and the first room

After $D$ days the $20$ rooms hold $210+18D$ nuts, all with the same count $T$: $20T=210+18D$. Room $1$ needs $T-1$ nuts and gets at most one per day, so $T-1\le D$. Also $210+18D\equiv0\pmod{20}$ forces $D\equiv5\pmod{10}$. From $T\le D+1$: $210+18D\le20D+20$, so $D\ge95$, and $D=95$ satisfies the congruence ($T=96$). A schedule exists (see Solution 1). Answer $\boxed{95}$.
