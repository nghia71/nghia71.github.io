Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 25: Algebraic Word Problem Models

Helena's vinyl record collection contains $33$ LP records and $45$ EP records. An LP record plays for three times as long as an EP record. How many more EP records must Helena buy so that the total playing time of her EP records equals the total playing time of her LP records?

(A)$45$(B)$54$(C)$63$(D)$78$(E)$87$
Show the answer

(B) $54$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Measure in EP-lengths)

Measure playing time in units of one EP record. Each LP lasts $3$ units, so the LP records last $33\cdot3=99$ units in total. The EP records last $45$ units, so Helena needs $99-45=54$ more EP records. \[\boxed{54}\]

Solution 2 (Match LPs with EPs)

Think of each LP as equivalent to a group of $3$ EPs. The $45$ EPs she owns fill exactly $45/3=15$ such groups, so they balance $15$ of the LPs. The other $33-15=18$ LPs still need $3$ EPs each: $18\cdot3=54$. \[\boxed{54}\]

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · also AMC 12A Problem 1

A sign in a parking lot states that the total price includes a $27\%$ tax. To the nearest whole number, what percentage of the amount a customer pays is this tax?

(A)$21\%$(B)$22\%$(C)$27\%$(D)$37\%$(E)$73\%$
Show the answer

(A) $21\%$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pick a base price)

Suppose the price before tax is $100$. The tax is $27$ and the customer pays $127$. The tax is \[\frac{27}{127}\approx0.2126\] of the amount paid, that is, about $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Solution 2 (Bound the fraction without dividing)

The tax is $\frac{27}{127}$ of the amount paid. To round to the nearest percent we only need to know whether it is above or below $20.5\%$ and $21.5\%$: \[0.205\cdot127=26.035<27\qquad\text{and}\qquad0.215\cdot127=27.305>27.\] So $20.5\%<\frac{27}{127}<21.5\%$, which rounds to $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry · also AMC 12A Problem 2

Square $ABCD$ has side length $24$. Let $E$ be the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$, and let $F$ be the intersection of $\overline{AE}$ and $\overline{BD}$. What is the area of $\triangle BEF$?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$45$(D)$48$(E)$52$
Show the answer

(D) $48$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Similar triangles)

Since $BE\parallel AD$, triangles $FBE$ and $FDA$ are similar (alternate angles), with ratio $BE:DA=12:24=1:2$. So $F$ divides the diagonal $BD$ in the ratio $BF:FD=1:2$, and its distance from line $BC$ is $\frac13$ of the distance from $D$ to $BC$, namely $\frac13\cdot24=8$. Hence \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot BE\cdot8=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(24,0)$, $A=(0,24)$, $D=(24,24)$, so $E=(12,0)$. Line $BD$ is $y=x$ and line $AE$ is $y=24-2x$. They meet where $x=24-2x$, so $F=(8,8)$. Triangle $BEF$ has base $BE=12$ on the $x$-axis and height $8$: \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Solution 3 (Area ratios)

$[ABE]=\frac12\cdot AB\cdot BE=\frac12\cdot24\cdot12=144$. As in Solution 1, $AF:FE=DF:FB=2:1$, so $FE=\frac13 AE$. Triangles $BEF$ and $BEA$ share the vertex $B$ and have bases $FE$ and $AE$ on the same line, so \[[BEF]=\tfrac13[ABE]=\tfrac13\cdot144=\boxed{48}.\]

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · also AMC 12A Problem 3

Half of the internal angles of a polygon measure $150^\circ$ each, and the other half of its internal angles measure $190^\circ$ each. How many sides does the polygon have?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$60$(D)$72$(E)$180$
Show the answer

(A) $36$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Exterior angles)

Walking around the polygon, the turning (exterior) angle at a vertex is $180^\circ$ minus the interior angle: $30^\circ$ at a $150^\circ$ vertex and $-10^\circ$ at a $190^\circ$ (reflex) vertex. The turns add up to one full turn, $360^\circ$. With $n/2$ vertices of each kind, \[\tfrac n2\cdot30-\tfrac n2\cdot10=10n=360,\qquad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Solution 2 (Interior angle sum)

The interior angles of an $n$-gon add up to $180(n-2)$ degrees. Here they add up to $\frac n2\cdot150+\frac n2\cdot190=170n$. So \[170n=180n-360\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · also AMC 12A Problem 4

Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ be a sequence with $a_1 = 94$, $a_2 = 190$, and $a_n$ equal to the arithmetic mean of the previous $n-1$ terms for $n \ge 3$. What is the value of $a_{10}$?

(A)$47$(B)$95$(C)$142$(D)$284$(E)$564$
Show the answer

(C) $142$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Adding the mean keeps the mean)

$a_3=\frac{94+190}2=142$. Now the first three terms have mean $142$: adding a number equal to the mean of a list does not change the mean. So $a_4=142$, and the mean is still $142$; the same happens at every step. Hence every term from $a_3$ on is $142$, and $a_{10}=\boxed{142}$.

Solution 2 (Track the partial sums)

Let $S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n$. For $n\ge2$ we have $a_{n+1}=S_n/n$, so \[S_{n+1}=S_n+\frac{S_n}{n}=\frac{n+1}{n}S_n,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{S_{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{S_n}{n}.\] So $S_n/n$ is constant for $n\ge2$: it equals $S_2/2=\frac{284}2=142$. Therefore $a_{10}=S_9/9=\boxed{142}$.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition

A triangle with sides $16$, $30$, and $34$ is inscribed in a circle, dividing the interior of the circle into four regions. Let $A$, $B$, and $C$ be the areas of the non-triangular regions, with $C$ being the greatest. What is the value of $A+B$?

(A)$\dfrac{289\pi}{2}-240$(B)$\dfrac{289\pi}{2}-210$(C)$289\pi-480$(D)$289\pi-360$(E)$289\pi-240$
Show the answer

(A) $\tfrac{289\pi}{2}-240$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The hypotenuse is a diameter)

$16^2+30^2=256+900=1156=34^2$, so the triangle is right-angled and its hypotenuse $34$ is a diameter (Thales). The radius is $17$ and the circle has area $289\pi$.

The diameter cuts the disc into two half-discs of area $\frac{289\pi}2$ each. The region beyond the hypotenuse is a whole half-disc, so it is the largest region: $C=\frac{289\pi}2$. The other half-disc consists of the triangle (area $\frac12\cdot16\cdot30=240$) and the two small regions. So \[A+B=\frac{289\pi}2-240.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{289\pi}2-240}\]

Solution 2 (Subtract everything else)

As above, the circle has radius $17$ and $C=\frac{289\pi}2$. The four regions fill the disc: $A+B+C+240=289\pi$. Hence $A+B=289\pi-240-\frac{289\pi}2=\frac{289\pi}2-240$. (The answer choices with $289\pi$ forget that $C$ is half of the disc.) \[\boxed{\tfrac{289\pi}2-240}\]

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · also AMC 12A Problem 5

How many ordered triples of positive integers $(x,y,z)$ satisfy both of the following equations? \[x+y+z=31,\qquad x+3y+3z=41\]

(A)$4$(B)$5$(C)$6$(D)$7$(E)$8$
Show the answer

(A) $4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Eliminate $x$)

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $2y+2z=10$, so $y+z=5$ and then $x=31-5=26$. The positive solutions of $y+z=5$ are $(y,z)=(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$, and each gives a valid triple with $x=26$. There are $\boxed{4}$ triples.

Solution 2 (Treat $y+z$ as one unknown)

Let $s=y+z$. Then $x+s=31$ and $x+3s=41$, a $2\times2$ system with $s=5$, $x=26$. The number of ways to write $5$ as an ordered sum of two positive integers is $5-1=4$ (choose $y\in\{1,2,3,4\}$). Answer: $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 8

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work

A driver sees an electronic highway sign stating that the next exit is $7$ miles away and it will take $5$ minutes to reach it. Assume both numbers have been rounded to the nearest integer. Among the following options, what is the lowest speed limit (in miles per hour) that would possibly allow the driver to legally reach the exit within the given time frame?

(A)$65$(B)$70$(C)$75$(D)$80$(E)$85$
Show the answer

(C) $75$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Slowest possible required speed)

The true distance $d$ satisfies $6.5\le d<7.5$ miles and the true time $t$ satisfies $4.5\le t<5.5$ minutes. The required average speed $d/t$ is smallest when $d$ is smallest and $t$ largest, and it is always more than \[\frac{6.5\text{ mi}}{5.5/60\text{ h}}=\frac{390}{5.5}\approx70.9\text{ mph}.\] So a $70$ mph limit never works. A $75$ mph limit can work: for example $d=6.5$ miles and $t=5.4$ minutes need only $6.5\cdot60/5.4\approx72.2$ mph. The answer is $\boxed{75}$.

Solution 2 (Test the options as distances)

At $70$ mph, in less than $5.5$ minutes the driver covers less than $70\cdot\frac{5.5}{60}\approx6.42$ miles, which is short of the smallest possible distance $6.5$ miles. So $70$ (and $65$) are too slow. At $75$ mph, in $5.4$ minutes (which rounds to $5$) the driver covers $75\cdot\frac{5.4}{60}=6.75$ miles, enough for a distance of, say, $6.6$ miles (which rounds to $7$). So the lowest option is $\boxed{75}$.

Problem 9

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry

Two similar but not congruent scalene triangles each have a side with length $9$ and a side with length $12$. What is the length of the longest side of the larger triangle?

(A)$12$(B)$14$(C)$15$(D)$16$(E)$17$
Show the answer

(D) $16$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Match the sides)

Let the smaller triangle have sides $9,12,x$ and let the larger be $k$ times it, $k>1$. Its sides $9k,12k,xk$ must include $9$ and $12$.

$12k>12$, so $12k$ is neither $9$ nor $12$; it is the third side of the larger triangle. Then $9$ and $12$ are $9k$ and $xk$ in some order. $9k=9$ is impossible ($k\ne1$), so $9k=12$, $k=\frac43$, and $xk=9$, $x=\frac{27}4$. The triangles are $\frac{27}4,9,12$ and $9,12,16$, both genuine scalene triangles ($\frac{27}4+9>12$). The longest side of the larger triangle is $12k=\boxed{16}$.

Solution 2 (A geometric chain)

Write the sides of the smaller triangle in increasing order $p<q<r$; the larger is $kp<kq<kr$ with $k>1$. The two shared lengths must be two sides of the smaller triangle that equal two sides of the larger one. The only way two of $p,q,r$ can equal two of $kp,kq,kr$ is $q=kp$ and $r=kq$ (each larger side is the image of the next smaller one). So the four different lengths form a geometric progression $p,\,q,\,r,\,kr$ with ratio $k$, and the shared pair $q<r$ is $9,12$. Then $k=\frac{12}{9}=\frac43$ and the longest side of the larger triangle is $kr=\frac43\cdot12=\boxed{16}$.

Problem 10

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · also AMC 12A Problem 6

For what odd positive integer $n$ is the mean of $1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots, n^2$ exactly $32\%$ greater than its median?

(A)$19$(B)$39$(C)$49$(D)$89$(E)$169$

On the AMC 12A paper (Problem 6) the word "odd" is left out; the answer is the same.

Show the answer

(C) $49$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Formulas for mean and median)

For odd $n$ the median is the middle square $\left(\frac{n+1}2\right)^2$. The mean is \[\frac{1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2}{n}=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6.\] Their ratio is \[\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)/6}{(n+1)^2/4}=\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac{132}{100}=\frac{33}{25}.\] So $50(2n+1)=99(n+1)$, that is $100n+50=99n+99$, and $n=\boxed{49}$.

Solution 2 (Check against the choices)

Using the ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}$ from Solution 1, the answer must make it exactly $1.32$. For $n=49$: $\frac{2\cdot99}{3\cdot50}=\frac{198}{150}=1.32$. Directly: the median is $25^2=625$ and the mean is $\frac{50\cdot99}{6}=825=1.32\cdot625$. The ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac43-\frac{2}{3(n+1)}$ increases with $n$, so no other $n$ works. Answer $\boxed{49}$.

Problem 11

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction

Gary writes down six distinct positive integers: $2, 5, 24, 60, 240$, and a three-digit number $n$. One of those six numbers is the least common multiple of all six of them. What is the sum of all possible values of $n$?

(A)$960$(B)$1680$(C)$2160$(D)$2280$(E)$2400$
Show the answer

(D) $2280$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Which number is the LCM?)

The least common multiple $L$ of all six numbers is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(2,5,24,60,240)=240$, and it is the largest of the six. So $L$ is $240$ or $n$.

If $L=240$: $n$ divides $240$, has three digits, and differs from $240$; the only such divisor is $n=120$.

If $L=n$: $n$ is a three-digit multiple of $240$ other than $240$: $480,720,960$.

The sum is $120+480+720+960=\boxed{2280}$.

Solution 2 (Prime exponents)

$240=2^4\cdot3\cdot5$. Every number in the list must divide the largest one, which is $240$ or $n$.

If $n$ is not the largest, $n=2^a3^b5^c$ with $a\le4$, $b,c\le1$, three digits, $n\ne240$: the divisors of $240$ between $100$ and $999$ are $120$ and $240$, so $n=120$.

If $n$ is the largest, $240\mid n$, so $n=240m$ with $2\le m\le4$: $480,720,960$.

Sum: $\boxed{2280}$.

Problem 12

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value

A drawer has $100$ red socks and $100$ blue socks. Pippi randomly removes one sock at a time, without replacement, until all the socks of one color have been removed. What is the probability that there are exactly $2$ socks left in the drawer once that happens?

(A)$\dfrac{27}{196}$(B)$\dfrac{49}{198}$(C)$\dfrac{247}{990}$(D)$\dfrac{50}{199}$(E)$\dfrac{25}{99}$
Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac{50}{199}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Look at the end of the full order)

Imagine Pippi keeps going and removes all $200$ socks; the order is a uniformly random arrangement. She stops when one colour runs out. Exactly $2$ socks are left then if and only if the last two socks have the same colour and the third-to-last sock has the other colour.

The probability that the last sock is red, the one before it red, and the one before that blue is $\frac{100}{200}\cdot\frac{99}{199}\cdot\frac{100}{198}$ (reading the order backwards). Doubling for the colours swapped, \[2\cdot\frac{100\cdot99\cdot100}{200\cdot199\cdot198}=\frac{50}{199}.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{50}{199}}\]

Solution 2 (Count colour patterns)

Every colour pattern of the $200$ socks (a word with $100$ R's and $100$ B's) is equally likely; there are $\binom{200}{100}$ of them. The good patterns end in $\dots XYY$ with $X\ne Y$: choose $Y$ ($2$ ways); the first $197$ positions hold $99$ socks of colour $X$ and $98$ of colour $Y$, in $\binom{197}{98}$ ways. So the probability is \[\frac{2\binom{197}{98}}{\binom{200}{100}}=\frac{2\cdot100\cdot100\cdot99}{200\cdot199\cdot198}=\frac{50}{199}.\] \[\boxed{\tfrac{50}{199}}\]

Problem 13

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints

Two bicycle clubs, with disjoint memberships, go on a tour together. On the morning of the tour, members greet each other with a handshake. Everyone shakes hands with everyone else exactly once. There were $119$ handshakes between members of the same club and $112$ handshakes between members of different clubs. How many more people came from the larger club than from the smaller club?

(A)$0$(B)$4$(C)$6$(D)$7$(E)$8$
Show the answer

(C) $6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Total first)

There are $119+112=231$ handshakes in all, so the number of people $N$ satisfies $\binom N2=231$, $N(N-1)=462$, $N=22$. The cross handshakes give $mn=112$ with $m+n=22$, so $m,n$ are the roots of $t^2-22t+112=0$: $8$ and $14$. The difference is $\boxed{6}$.

Solution 2 (Square of the difference)

As above, $m+n=22$ and $mn=112$. Then \[(n-m)^2=(m+n)^2-4mn=484-448=36,\] so $n-m=\boxed{6}$. (Check: $\binom82+\binom{14}2=28+91=119$.)

Problem 14

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value

Consider a triangle with positive area whose side lengths are functions of $t$ given by $a=t^2+1$, $b=5t+7$, and $c=4t+4$. How many different integer values of $t$ are possible?

(A)$0$(B)$7$(C)$9$(D)$10$(E)$\text{infinitely many}$
Show the answer

(B) $7$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Three triangle inequalities)

We need $a,b,c>0$ and the three strict triangle inequalities.

  • $a+b>c$: $t^2+t+3>0$, always true.
  • $a+c>b$: $t^2-t-2>0$, i.e. $(t-2)(t+1)>0$: $t>2$ or $t<-1$.
  • $b+c>a$: $t^2-9t-10<0$, i.e. $(t-10)(t+1)<0$: $-1<t<10$.

Together: $2<t<10$, and then all sides are positive. The integers are $t=3,4,\dots,9$: $\boxed{7}$ values.

Solution 2 (Find the degenerate cases first)

The triangle flattens exactly when one side equals the sum of the other two. Solving $a=b+c$ gives $t^2-9t-10=0$, $t=10$ or $t=-1$; solving $b=a+c$ gives $t^2-t-2=0$, $t=2$ or $t=-1$; $c=a+b$ has no real solution. At $t=-1$ the side $c$ is $0$, so the useful boundary values are $t=2$ (sides $5,17,12$: $5+12=17$) and $t=10$ (sides $101,57,44$: $57+44=101$). Testing one value in each range, e.g. $t=3$ (sides $10,22,16$) works and $t=0$ (sides $1,7,4$) and $t=11$ (sides $122,62,48$) fail. So exactly $t=3,\dots,9$ work: $\boxed{7}$.

Problem 15

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · also AMC 12A Problem 10

A pyramid of fractions starts with $\frac12$ in row 0. Each successive row has $\frac{a}{a+b}$ and $\frac{b}{a+b}$ below each $\frac ab$; for instance, in row 1, $\frac13$ and $\frac23$ appear. In which row will $\frac{20}{27}$ appear?

(A)$5$(B)$8$(C)$12$(D)$26$(E)$\text{The node }\frac{20}{27}\text{ does not appear in the diagram.}$

AMC 12A Problem 10 states the same problem with "level" for "row".

Show the answer

(B) $8$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Climb back up the tree)

Every entry is less than $1$. A child $\frac pq$ of $\frac ab$ has $q=a+b$ and $p=a$ or $p=b$, so its parent is $\frac{p}{q-p}$ or $\frac{q-p}{p}$; only the one that is less than $1$ can be in the tree. Going up from $\frac{20}{27}$: \[\tfrac{20}{27}\to\tfrac{7}{20}\to\tfrac{7}{13}\to\tfrac{6}{7}\to\tfrac16\to\tfrac15\to\tfrac14\to\tfrac13\to\tfrac12.\] That is $8$ steps up to row $0$, so $\frac{20}{27}$ is in row $\boxed{8}$.

Solution 2 (Continued fractions)

For an entry $\frac ab$ write $\frac ba$ as a continued fraction $[c_0;c_1,\dots,c_m]$. Moving to a child adds exactly $1$ to the sum $c_0+c_1+\cdots+c_m$: the left child $\frac{a}{a+b}$ turns $\frac ba$ into $\frac{a+b}a=1+\frac ba$, which raises $c_0$ by $1$, and the right child $\frac b{a+b}$ turns $\frac ba$ into $\frac{a+b}b=1+\frac ab$, which puts a new $1$ in front of the expansion. (One checks that the representations stay valid.) The root $\frac12$ has $\frac21=[2]$, sum $2$, so an entry in row $r$ has sum $r+2$.

$\frac{27}{20}=1+\frac{7}{20}$, $\frac{20}{7}=2+\frac67$, $\frac76=1+\frac16$, so $\frac{27}{20}=[1;2,1,6]$ with sum $10$. The row is $10-2=\boxed{8}$.

Problem 16

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · also AMC 12A Problem 11

An urn contains $6$ red balls, $4$ black balls, and $4$ white balls. Suppose $7$ balls are drawn at random without replacement and a score is computed as follows:

  • $1$ point is given for each red ball up to a maximum of $3$ points.
  • $1$ point is given for each black ball up to a maximum of $2$ points.
  • $1$ point is given for each white ball up to a maximum of $2$ points.

For example, drawing $1$ red ball, $4$ black balls, and $2$ white balls would earn a score of $1+2+2=5$. What is the probability of getting the lowest possible score?

(A)$\dfrac{1}{858}$(B)$\dfrac{5}{3432}$(C)$\dfrac{1}{312}$(D)$\dfrac{2}{429}$(E)$\dfrac{5}{264}$
Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac{2}{429}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Find the lowest score, then count)

Let the draw have $r$ red, $k$ black and $w$ white balls, $r+k+w=7$.

If $r\ge3$, red gives $3$ points and $k+w=7-r\ge1$ gives at least $1$ more, so the score is at least $4$; it is exactly $4$ only when $k+w=1$, i.e. $r=6$: $(6,1,0)$ or $(6,0,1)$.

If $r\le2$, then $k+w\ge5$ with $k,w\le4$, so both $k,w\ge1$ and one of them is at least $3$: black and white give at least $2+1=3$ points. The score is at least $r+3$, which is $4$ only for $r=0$, $\{k,w\}=\{4,3\}$: $(0,4,3)$ or $(0,3,4)$.

So the lowest score is $4$. The number of ways: $2\binom66\binom41+2\binom44\binom43=8+8=16$, out of $\binom{14}7=3432$: \[\frac{16}{3432}=\boxed{\tfrac{2}{429}}.\]

Solution 2 (Think about what stays in the urn)

The $7$ balls left in the urn determine the draw. Points are lost only through "extra" balls: each red beyond $3$, each black or white beyond $2$. A score of $4$ means $3$ extra balls among the $7$ drawn. Extra balls are possible only in a colour of which we drew many, so the draw must be very lopsided: all $6$ reds plus one more ball (extras: $3$ reds), or all $4$ of one colour of black/white plus $3$ of the other (extras: $2+1$). A score of $3$ would need $4$ extras, e.g. $7$ reds, which is impossible. Counting as in Solution 1 gives $16$ draws out of $3432$: $\boxed{\tfrac2{429}}$.

Problem 17

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement

For each edge of a regular hexagon with side length $1$, a square is constructed inside the hexagon with that edge as one of its sides. The region interior to all six squares is the interior of a smaller hexagon, shown shaded in the figure below. The area of the smaller hexagon can be expressed in the form $\frac{a\sqrt b}{c}-d$, where $a,b,c$ and $d$ are all positive integers, $a$ and $c$ are relatively prime, and $b$ is not divisible by the square of any prime. What is $a+b+c+d$?

(A)$16$(B)$17$(C)$18$(D)$19$(E)$20$
Show the answer

(C) $18$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Width of the overlap)

Two opposite sides of the hexagon are $\sqrt3$ apart (twice the apothem $\frac{\sqrt3}2$). The squares on these two sides reach $1$ unit inward each, so they overlap in a strip of width $1+1-\sqrt3=2-\sqrt3$ centred at the centre of the hexagon. The strips for the three pairs of opposite sides (with the side-to-side limits of the squares, which are far away and do not matter) cut out a regular hexagon whose opposite sides are $2-\sqrt3$ apart. A regular hexagon with side $s$ has opposite sides $s\sqrt3$ apart, so $s=\frac{2-\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}$ and its area is \[\frac{3\sqrt3}2s^2=\frac{3\sqrt3}2\cdot\frac{7-4\sqrt3}3=\frac{7\sqrt3}2-6.\] So $(a,b,c,d)=(7,3,2,6)$ and $a+b+c+d=\boxed{18}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates with distances to the sides)

Put the centre at the origin, so each side lies on a line at distance $h=\frac{\sqrt3}2$ from the origin with outward unit normal $\mathbf n_k$. A point $\mathbf x$ lies in the square on side $k$ (as far as the inward direction is concerned) when its distance to that side is at most $1$: $h-\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_k\le1$, i.e. $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_k\ge h-1=-(1-h)$. Opposite sides have opposite normals, so all six conditions say $|\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_k|\le\delta$ with $\delta=1-\frac{\sqrt3}2\approx0.134$: a regular hexagon with apothem $\delta$. (The sideways limits of each square are at distance $\frac12$ from the centre and do not cut this tiny hexagon.) A regular hexagon with apothem $\delta$ has area $2\sqrt3\,\delta^2$: \[2\sqrt3\left(1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\right)^2=2\sqrt3\left(\tfrac74-\sqrt3\right)=\tfrac{7\sqrt3}2-6.\] Answer $7+3+2+6=\boxed{18}$.

Problem 18

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

The strictly decreasing sequence of $11$ positive integers $(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{10})$ is defined by the recursive rule $a_k=\frac12 a_{k-1}+2$, for $k\ge1$. Let $N$ be the minimum possible value of $a_0$. What is the sum of the digits of $N$?

(A)$8$(B)$9$(C)$10$(D)$11$(E)$12$
Show the answer

(D) $11$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Shift to the fixed point)

The fixed point of $x\mapsto\frac12x+2$ is $4$, and $a_k-4=\frac12(a_{k-1}-4)$. So $a_k=4+\frac{a_0-4}{2^k}$. The sequence decreases strictly exactly when $a_0>4$, and all terms are integers exactly when $2^{10}\mid a_0-4$. The smallest choice is $a_0-4=1024$, so $N=1028$ and its digit sum is $1+0+2+8=\boxed{11}$.

Solution 2 (Work backwards)

Reverse the rule: $a_{k-1}=2a_k-4=2(a_k-2)$. Since the sequence decreases, $a_{10}<a_9=2a_{10}-4$, so $a_{10}>4$; the smallest choice $a_{10}=5$ gives the smallest $a_0$ (each backward step is increasing in $a_k$). Going back: \[5,\ 6,\ 8,\ 12,\ 20,\ 36,\ 68,\ 132,\ 260,\ 516,\ 1028.\] So $N=1028$, digit sum $\boxed{11}$.

Problem 19

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

Three $2\times2\times2$ cubes sit on a table so that two of the cubes share a face, and a face of the third cube is centered along a shared edge of the other two cubes, as shown. A hemispherical cover is placed over the cubes so that its circumference rests on the table and the cubes are under the cover. What is the least possible value of the radius of the cover?

(A)$3$(B)$\dfrac{\sqrt{357}}{6}$(C)$\dfrac{\sqrt{681}}{8}$(D)$\dfrac{\sqrt{178}}{4}$(E)$2\sqrt3$
Show the answer

(C) $\tfrac{\sqrt{681}}{8}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Balance the far corners)

Use coordinates on the table: the two face-sharing cubes stand on $[0,2]\times[0,2]$ and $[2,4]\times[0,2]$, the third on $[1,3]\times[-2,0]$; all have height $2$. A hemisphere with centre $(x,y,0)$ and radius $R$ covers everything iff it covers all top corners: $(X-x)^2+(Y-y)^2+4\le R^2$ for every top corner $(X,Y,2)$.

The picture is symmetric about $x=2$, and the conditions are convex, so we may take $x=2$. The critical corners are the back corners $(0,2),(4,2)$ and the front corners of the third cube $(1,-2),(3,-2)$: \[4+(2-y)^2\le R^2-4,\qquad 1+(2+y)^2\le R^2-4.\] Raising $y$ helps the first and hurts the second, so the best $y$ makes them equal: $8-4y+y^2=5+4y+y^2$, $y=\frac38$. Then $R^2=4+4+\left(\frac{13}8\right)^2=\frac{681}{64}$; the remaining corners, such as $(0,0)$ with $4+\frac9{64}$, are closer. So $R=\boxed{\tfrac{\sqrt{681}}8}$.

Solution 2 (A smallest circle in the top view)

At height $2$ the hemisphere of radius $R$ is a disc of radius $\rho=\sqrt{R^2-4}$ centred above its centre. So we need the smallest disc containing the top-view outline of the three cubes. Any such disc contains the four far corners $(0,2),(4,2),(3,-2),(1,-2)$, which form an isosceles trapezoid. The smallest disc containing these four points is their circumcircle, because its centre lies inside the trapezoid. The centre $(2,y)$ satisfies $2^2+(2-y)^2=1^2+(y+2)^2$, so $y=\frac38$ and $\rho^2=4+\frac{169}{64}=\frac{425}{64}$. This disc also contains the rest of the outline: the farthest remaining corners $(0,0)$ and $(4,0)$ are at squared distance $4+\frac9{64}<\rho^2$. Then \[R^2=\rho^2+4=\frac{681}{64},\qquad R=\boxed{\tfrac{\sqrt{681}}8}.\]

Problem 20

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 34: Distributions and Stars-and-Bars

Boxes with dimensions $77\times78\times85$ or $2\times3\times85085$ have volume $510{,}510$. How many different boxes with integer side lengths at least $2$, including these two, have volume $510{,}510$? Boxes are considered different if they have different dimensions.

(A)$63$(B)$90$(C)$301$(D)$377$(E)$966$
Show the answer

(C) $301$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Surjections, then divide by $3!$)

$510510=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17$, a product of $7$ distinct primes. A box is a way to share these primes among three side lengths, each getting at least one prime (each side is at least $2$). Ordered assignments with no empty side: by inclusion–exclusion \[3^7-3\cdot2^7+3\cdot1^7=2187-384+3=1806.\] The three sides are always different (different sets of primes), so each box is counted $3!=6$ times. Answer $\frac{1806}6=\boxed{301}$.

Solution 2 (Set partitions)

A box is the same thing as splitting the set of the $7$ prime factors into $3$ non-empty unlabelled blocks. That number is the Stirling number $S(7,3)$. With $S(n,k)=k\,S(n-1,k)+S(n-1,k-1)$ and $S(n,1)=1$, $S(n,n)=1$: $S(3,2)=3$, $S(4,2)=7$, $S(5,2)=15$, $S(6,2)=31$; $S(4,3)=6$, $S(5,3)=25$, $S(6,3)=90$, $S(7,3)=3\cdot90+31=\boxed{301}$.

Problem 21

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · also AMC 12A Problem 16

Gerry is playing the Triples Game. Her task is to partition the $3\times3$ grid of squares shown on the left into three sections, each containing three adjoining squares. (Three squares are adjoining if each shares an edge with at least one of the other two.) One point will be awarded for each section containing more X's than O's. Shown on the right is a sample partition that will earn one point in total. A partition that earns two or more points will win the game. What fraction of the possible partitions of this particular grid will result in a win for Gerry?

(A)$\dfrac{3}{10}$(B)$\dfrac13$(C)$\dfrac25$(D)$\dfrac49$(E)$\dfrac12$
Show the answer

(C) $\tfrac25$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count all partitions, then the wins)

All partitions. The sections are straight or L-shaped trominoes. Every partition has a straight tromino along one edge of the grid (this can be checked by looking at the section that holds a corner square; Solution 2 lists all partitions). There is the all-rows partition, the all-columns partition, and for each of the $4$ edges, the remaining $2\times3$ rectangle can be split into two L's in $2$ ways. Total $2+4\cdot2=10$.

Wins. There are $4$ X's. A section scores with $2$ or $3$ X's, so a win needs two sections with exactly $2$ X's each, and the third section has no X: it is a tromino made of O's. The O's are at the middle of the top row, the right of the middle row, and the whole bottom row; the top O touches no other O. So the O-section is the bottom row or the L made of the middle-right, bottom-middle and bottom-right squares.

  • Bottom row: the top $2\times3$ splits as two rows (XOX, XXO: win) or two L's: one split gives XOX and XXO (win), the other gives XXX and OXO (only one point).
  • The L: the leftover squares split in $2$ ways: top row (XOX) $+$ middle-left, centre, bottom-left (XXO), or left column (XXO) $+$ top-middle, top-right, centre (OXX). Both win.

So $4$ wins out of $10$: $\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Solution 2 (List all ten by the bottom-left square)

Name the squares TL, TM, TR / ML, C, MR / BL, BM, BR (top, middle, bottom rows). The section containing BL is the bottom row, the left column, or one of the three L's inside the bottom-left $2\times2$ block. Listing the partitions in each case (X/O contents in brackets, $\checkmark$ for a scoring section):

  • Bottom row (OOO): the top two rows split as TL TM TR (XOX$\checkmark$) + ML C MR (XXO$\checkmark$): win; TL TM ML (XOX$\checkmark$) + TR C MR (XXO$\checkmark$): win; TL ML C (XXX$\checkmark$) + TM TR MR (OXO): $1$ point.
  • Left column (XXO$\checkmark$): the rest splits as TM TR C (OXX$\checkmark$) + MR BM BR: win; TM C BM (OXO) + TR MR BR (XOO): $1$ point; TM TR MR (OXO) + C BM BR (XOO): $1$ point.
  • L $\{$ML, C, BL$\}$ (XXO$\checkmark$): forces TL TM TR (XOX$\checkmark$) + MR BM BR: win.
  • L $\{$ML, BL, BM$\}$ (XOO): the rest is TL TM TR + C MR BR, or TL TM C + TR MR BR; $1$ point each.
  • L $\{$C, BL, BM$\}$ (XOO): forces TL TM ML + TR MR BR; $1$ point.

That is $3+3+1+2+1=10$ partitions, of which $2+1+1=4$ win: $\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Problem 22

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · also AMC 12A Problem 17

An engineer is designing a closely packed field of $7$ wind turbines for electricity production. Each turbine, whose center is marked by a labeled dot in the figure, occupies one of $7$ congruent elliptical regions. Turbines in the same row have centers that are $4$ units apart, and turbines in the same column have centers that are $9$ units apart, as shown in the figure below. The major axis of each ellipse is $m\sqrt p$, where $m$ and $p$ are positive integers and $p$ is not divisible by the square of any prime. What is $m+p$?

(A)$6$(B)$8$(C)$9$(D)$10$(E)$12$
Show the answer

(A) $6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangency at the midpoint)

Let each ellipse have horizontal semi-axis $a$ and vertical semi-axis $b$. Two congruent ellipses that are translates of each other and touch are symmetric about the midpoint of their centres, so they touch at that midpoint.

Neighbours in a row ($C$, $O$, $F$) have centres $4$ apart horizontally and touch at the midpoint, $2$ units from each centre along the horizontal axis: $a=2$.

$O$ and $A$: $A$ is $2$ units right and $\frac92$ units up from $O$ (the rows are $\frac92$ apart, since $B$ and $D$ in one column are $9$ apart). They touch at the midpoint $(1,\frac94)$ relative to $O$, which lies on $O$'s ellipse: \[\frac{1}{4}+\frac{(9/4)^2}{b^2}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^2=\frac{81}{16}\cdot\frac43=\frac{27}4,\quad b=\frac{3\sqrt3}2.\] The major axis is $2b=3\sqrt3$, so $m+p=3+3=\boxed{6}$.

Solution 2 (Squash the ellipses into circles)

Shrink the picture vertically by the factor $\frac ab$. The ellipses become congruent circles of radius $a$, still touching in the same pattern; since a row has three touching circles, $a=2$ and neighbouring centres are $4$ apart. The centres $O$ and $A$, originally $(0,0)$ and $(2,\frac92)$, become $(0,0)$ and $(2,\frac92\cdot\frac2b)$, and these circles touch, so \[2^2+\left(\frac9b\right)^2=4^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{81}{b^2}=12,\quad b=\frac{3\sqrt3}2.\] Major axis $2b=3\sqrt3$: $m+p=\boxed{6}$.

Problem 23

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 47: Combinatorial Number Theory

In a list of one or more consecutive integers, there are $p$ multiples of $2$ and $q$ multiples of $3$. How many ordered pairs $(p,q)$ are possible for this list if $p$ and $q$ are between $0$ and $100$, inclusive?

(A)$232$(B)$233$(C)$234$(D)$235$(E)$236$
Show the answer

(D) $235$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Characterise the pairs: $|2p-3q|\le3$)

Necessity. In a list of $L$ consecutive integers there are $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$ or $\lceil L/2\rceil$ even numbers, so $L-1\le2p\le L+1$, and $\lfloor L/3\rfloor$ or $\lceil L/3\rceil$ multiples of $3$, so $L-2\le3q\le L+2$. Hence $|2p-3q|\le3$.

Sufficiency. For each $d=2p-3q\in\{-3,\dots,3\}$ the pairs with that value of $d$ and $p,q\ge0$ are $(p_0+3k,\,q_0+2k)$, $k\ge0$, where the smallest pair $(p_0,q_0)$ and a list realising it are: \[\begin{array}{c|ccccccc} d&0&1&-1&2&-2&3&-3\\\hline (p_0,q_0)&(0,0)&(2,1)&(1,1)&(1,0)&(2,2)&(3,1)&(0,1)\\ \text{list}&\{1\}&\{2,3,4\}&\{3,4\}&\{2\}&\{3,4,5,6\}&\{4,\dots,8\}&\{3\} \end{array}\] Appending the next $6$ consecutive integers to a list adds exactly $3$ multiples of $2$ and $2$ multiples of $3$, i.e. adds $(3,2)$. So every pair with $|2p-3q|\le3$ occurs.

Counting. For a fixed $p$, the allowed $q$ satisfy $\frac{2p-3}3\le q\le\frac{2p+3}3$, all at most $67$. If $3\mid p$ there are $3$ values ($\frac{2p}3$ and its two neighbours), except that $p=0$ loses $q=-1$; otherwise there are $2$ values. With $34$ multiples of $3$ in $0,\dots,100$: \[34\cdot3-1+67\cdot2=101+134=\boxed{235}.\]

Solution 2 (Count by $q$ instead)

Use the characterisation $|2p-3q|\le3$ from Solution 1, but count column by column in $q$. For fixed $q$ we need $\frac{3q-3}2\le p\le\frac{3q+3}2$.

  • $q$ even: $p\in\{\frac{3q}2-1,\frac{3q}2,\frac{3q}2+1\}$, three values.
  • $q$ odd: $p\in\{\frac{3q-3}2,\frac{3q-1}2,\frac{3q+1}2,\frac{3q+3}2\}$, four values.

The edges: $q=0$ loses $p=-1$ (two values); $q=67$ gives $p\in\{99,100,101,102\}$, of which only $99,100$ are allowed; $q\ge68$ needs $p\ge101$. For $1\le q\le66$ there are $33$ odd and $33$ even values of $q$. Total \[2+33\cdot4+33\cdot3+2=\boxed{235}.\]

Show the answer

(D) $84$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor $f(r)-f(s)$)

Let $f(x)=x^3+bx^2+cx+d$, $r=3+\sqrt5$, $s=3-\sqrt5$, so $r+s=6$, $rs=4$. Then \[f(r)-f(s)=(r-s)\left(r^2+rs+s^2+b(r+s)+c\right)=(r-s)(32+6b+c),\] using $r^2+rs+s^2=(r+s)^2-rs=32$. Solution 2 (Conjugates)

Problem 25

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Chris the squirrel has $20$ rooms in his cavern for storing food. The first room contains $1$ nut, the second room contains $2$ nuts, the third room contains $3$ nuts, and so on, with the $20$th room containing $20$ nuts. Starting from today, Chris will collect $18$ additional nuts each day and store them one per room in $18$ distinct rooms in his cavern. His goal is to have all the rooms contain the same number of nuts once he is finished. What is the minimum number of days required to accomplish this goal?

(A)$75$(B)$95$(C)$105$(D)$190$(E)$195$
Show the answer

(B) $95$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the skips)

Each day Chris skips exactly $2$ rooms. After $D$ days let room $i$ have been skipped $s_i$ times; it then holds $i+D-s_i$ nuts. All rooms equal means $s_i=s_1+(i-1)$. Adding over the rooms, $\sum s_i=2D$ gives $20s_1+190=2D$, so $D=10s_1+95\ge95$.

$D=95$ is possible: room $i$ must be skipped $i-1$ times, $190$ skips in all, $2$ per day. Write the skips in a list grouped by room ($room\ 2$ once, $room\ 3$ twice, …, room $20$ nineteen times) and on day $j$ skip entries $j$ and $j+95$ of the list. These are in different rooms because no room has more than $19<95$ entries. Every room ends with $96$ nuts. Answer $\boxed{95}$.

Solution 2 (Totals and the first room)

After $D$ days the $20$ rooms hold $210+18D$ nuts, all with the same count $T$: $20T=210+18D$. Room $1$ needs $T-1$ nuts and gets at most one per day, so $T-1\le D$. Also $210+18D\equiv0\pmod{20}$ forces $D\equiv5\pmod{10}$. From $T\le D+1$: $210+18D\le20D+20$, so $D\ge95$, and $D=95$ satisfies the congruence ($T=96$). A schedule exists (see Solution 1). Answer $\boxed{95}$.
