Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios · Ceva's Theorem and Concurrency

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral. The extensions of $AB$ and $DC$ meet at $E$ and the extensions of $AD$ and $BC$ meet at $F$. The bisectors of $\angle BEC$ and $\angle DFC$ meet at $P$ which lies on $AC$. Let $PF$ intersect $CD$ at $U$ and $PE$ intersect $BC$ at $V$. Prove that

  • (i) $DB$ is parallel to $UV$,

  • (ii) the lines $AC$ and $EF$ intersect at the midpoint of $EF$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark which rays from $E$ coincide and which rays from $F$ coincide. In each of the triangles $EBC$ and $FDC$, record where the given angle bisector meets the opposite side.
Hint 2: key idea
Apply the sine rule in $EBC$ and $FDC$ to compare $EB/EC$ and $FD/FC$, using the supplementary opposite angles of the cyclic quadrilateral. Feed that comparison into the angle-bisector ratios for $V$ and $U$. (LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios)
Hint 3: approach
Compare how $V$ and $U$ divide the sides of $CBD$. Use the bisectors again in $AEC$ and $AFC$ to express $AP/PC$ and find a homothety centred at $A$ taking $E,F$ to $B,D$. Find a second homothety centred at $C$ that reverses the endpoint correspondence, and compare the division ratios at the intersections with $AC$.
Common trap
When the point order changes on an extended side, a triangle angle may become supplementary, so use the equality of its sine.
Show solution 1 – Sine rule, then two homotheties

Since $E$ lies on line $AB$ outside segment $AB$, and on line $DC$ outside segment $DC$, the rays $EA,EB$ coincide and the rays $EC,ED$ coincide; similarly the rays $FA,FD$ coincide and the rays $FB,FC$ coincide. The figure shows one possible order of the points on the four lines; the argument below uses only these facts about rays and does not depend on that order.

A ratio that holds for every cyclic quadrilateral. By the sine rule in triangles $EBC$ and $FDC$, \[\frac{EB}{EC}=\frac{\sin\angle ECB}{\sin\angle EBC},\qquad \frac{FD}{FC}=\frac{\sin\angle FCD}{\sin\angle FDC}.\] Because $E$ lies on line $AB$, the angle $\angle EBC$ equals $\angle ABC$ or $180^\circ-\angle ABC$, so $\sin\angle EBC=\sin\angle ABC$. In the same way $\sin\angle ECB=\sin\angle BCD$, $\sin\angle FCD=\sin\angle BCD$ and $\sin\angle FDC=\sin\angle ADC$. Finally $\sin\angle ADC=\sin\angle ABC$, since $\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ$ in the cyclic quadrilateral. Hence \[\frac{EB}{EC}=\frac{\sin\angle BCD}{\sin\angle ABC}=\frac{FD}{FC}.\qquad(1)\]

(i) $EV$ bisects the angle $BEC$ of triangle $EBC$, so by the angle bisector theorem $\dfrac{CV}{VB}=\dfrac{EC}{EB}$. Likewise $FU$ bisects the angle $DFC$ of triangle $FDC$, so $\dfrac{CU}{UD}=\dfrac{FC}{FD}$. By (1) these ratios are equal: $\dfrac{CV}{VB}=\dfrac{CU}{UD}$. In triangle $CBD$ the points $V\in CB$ and $U\in CD$ therefore divide the two sides from $C$ in the same ratio, and by the converse of the intercept theorem $UV\parallel BD$.

(ii) Since the rays $EB,EA$ coincide and the rays $EC,ED$ coincide, the line $EP$ is also the bisector of $\angle AEC$ in triangle $AEC$, and it meets line $AC$ at $P$. By the angle bisector theorem $\dfrac{AP}{PC}=\dfrac{EA}{EC}$. In the same way $FP$ bisects $\angle AFC$ in triangle $AFC$, so $\dfrac{AP}{PC}=\dfrac{FA}{FC}$. Therefore \[\frac{EA}{EC}=\frac{FA}{FC}.\qquad(2)\] Dividing (1) by (2) gives $\dfrac{EB}{EA}=\dfrac{FD}{FA}=:\lambda$. As the rays $EB,EA$ coincide and the rays $FD,FA$ coincide, this means $\overrightarrow{EB}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{EA}$ and $\overrightarrow{FD}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{FA}$, so \[\overrightarrow{AB}=(1-\lambda)\,\overrightarrow{AE},\qquad \overrightarrow{AD}=(1-\lambda)\,\overrightarrow{AF},\qquad \lambda\ne1\ (\text{as }B\ne A).\] Hence the homothety $h_A$ with centre $A$ and ratio $1-\lambda$ maps $E\mapsto B$ and $F\mapsto D$, and $BD\parallel EF$.

The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ of the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ meet at a point $N$; since $BD\parallel EF$, line $AC$ is not parallel to $EF$ and meets it at a point $M$. The homothety $h_A$ maps line $EF$ to line $BD$ and fixes line $AC$, so it maps $M$ to $N$, and \[\frac{BN}{ND}=\frac{EM}{MF}.\] Next, $C$ is the common point of lines $BF$ and $DE$, and $BD\parallel EF$, so the homothety with centre $C$ that maps $B\mapsto F$ also maps $D\mapsto E$ (line $BD$ goes to the parallel line through $F$, i.e. line $EF$, and line $CD$ is fixed). It fixes line $AC$, so it maps $N$ to $M$, and \[\frac{BN}{ND}=\frac{FM}{ME}.\] Therefore $\dfrac{EM}{MF}=\dfrac{MF}{EM}$, so $EM=MF$: the lines $AC$ and $EF$ meet at the midpoint of $EF$.

Show solution 2 – Similar triangles and a circle on diameter $EF$

Since $E$ lies on lines $AB$ and $DC$ outside both segments, the rays $EA,EB$ coincide and the rays $EC,ED$ coincide; likewise the rays $FA,FD$ coincide and the rays $FB,FC$ coincide. Write $\alpha=\angle BAD$, $\beta=\angle ABC$, $\delta=\angle ADC$, so $\beta+\delta=180^\circ$.

(i) By the power of the point $E$, $EB\cdot EA=EC\cdot ED$, so $\dfrac{EB}{EC}=\dfrac{ED}{EA}$. Similarly, the power of $F$ gives $FD\cdot FA=FC\cdot FB$, so $\dfrac{FD}{FC}=\dfrac{FB}{FA}$. By the sine rule in triangles $EAD$ and $FAB$, which have the same angle $\alpha$ at $A$, \[\frac{ED}{EA}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\angle EDA}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\delta},\qquad \frac{FB}{FA}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\angle FBA}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\beta},\] and $\sin\beta=\sin\delta$. Hence $\dfrac{EB}{EC}=\dfrac{FD}{FC}$. The angle bisector theorem in triangles $EBC$ and $FDC$ gives $\dfrac{CV}{VB}=\dfrac{EC}{EB}=\dfrac{FC}{FD}=\dfrac{CU}{UD}$, so in triangle $CBD$ the points $V,U$ cut $CB,CD$ in the same ratio and $UV\parallel BD$.

Order of the points. As the rays $EB,EA$ and $EC,ED$ coincide, $EP$ is the internal bisector of $\angle AEC$ in triangle $AEC$, so $\dfrac{PA}{PC}=\dfrac{EA}{EC}$; likewise $FP$ is the internal bisector of $\angle AFC$ in triangle $AFC$, so $\dfrac{PA}{PC}=\dfrac{FA}{FC}$. Put \[k=\frac{PA}{PC}=\frac{EA}{EC}=\frac{FA}{FC}.\] Dividing $\dfrac{EB}{EC}=\dfrac{FD}{FC}$ by this gives $\dfrac{EB}{EA}=\dfrac{FD}{FA}$. So $B$ lies between $A$ and $E$ (that is, $EB<EA$) exactly when $D$ lies between $A$ and $F$. Relabelling $A\leftrightarrow C$, $B\leftrightarrow D$ changes neither $E$ nor $F$, nor the angles $\angle BEC$ and $\angle DFC$ (they become $\angle DEA$ and $\angle BFA$, formed by the same rays), nor the line $AC$; it turns “$A$ between $B$ and $E$” into “$C$ between $D$ and $E$”. In the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ the lines $AB$ and $DC$ meet beyond $B$ exactly when they meet beyond $C$ (namely when $E$ is on the far side of line $BC$ from $A$ and $D$). So we may assume that $B$ lies between $A$ and $E$ and $C$ between $D$ and $E$; then $D$ lies between $A$ and $F$, and in the same way $C$ lies between $B$ and $F$. This is the order of the figure; in particular $B\in AE$, $D\in AF$, $C$ is inside triangle $AEF$, and so is $P$, an interior point of segment $AC$.

The two bisectors are perpendicular. Triangle $EAD$ has angles $\alpha$, $\delta$ and $\angle AED=180^\circ-\alpha-\delta$; triangle $FAB$ has angles $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\angle AFB=180^\circ-\alpha-\beta$. The point $P$ lies on segment $AC$, inside triangle $AEF$, so $AEPF$ is a quadrilateral with a reflex angle $360^\circ-\angle EPF$ at $P$. Its angle sum is \[\alpha+\tfrac12(180^\circ-\alpha-\delta)+\tfrac12(180^\circ-\alpha-\beta)+(360^\circ-\angle EPF)=360^\circ,\] which gives $\angle EPF=180^\circ-\tfrac12(\beta+\delta)=90^\circ$.

(ii) Recall $k=\dfrac{PA}{PC}=\dfrac{EA}{EC}=\dfrac{FA}{FC}$. First, $k\ne1$: otherwise $EA=EC$ and $FA=FC$, the bisectors $EP$ and $FP$ would both be the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, and they could not be perpendicular.

Since $k\ne1$, the external bisector of $\angle AEC$ meets line $AC$ at a point $Q$ with $\dfrac{QA}{QC}=k$ (external bisector theorem), and $Q$ is the only point of line $AC$ outside segment $AC$ with this ratio. The external bisector of $\angle AFC$ meets line $AC$ at a point with the same ratio $\dfrac{FA}{FC}=k$, so it also passes through $Q$. Internal and external bisectors are perpendicular, so $\angle PEQ=\angle PFQ=90^\circ$, and $E,F$ lie on the circle $\omega$ with diameter $PQ$. The centre of $\omega$ is the midpoint of $PQ$, a point of line $AC$.

On the other hand $P$ is on $\omega$ and $\angle EPF=90^\circ$, so the chord $EF$ is also a diameter of $\omega$; its midpoint is the centre of $\omega$. Hence the midpoint of $EF$ lies on line $AC$. Lines $AC$ and $EF$ are different (line $EF$ does not pass through $A$, since $E\in AB$ and $F\in AD$ are not collinear with $A$), so they meet exactly at the midpoint of $EF$.

Show solution 3 – An oblique reflection in the line AC

Let $R$ be the circumradius of $ABCD$.

Step 1: $AB\cdot BC=AD\cdot CD$. The lines $EB$ and $EC$ are the lines $AB$ and $CD$, so the point $P$ of the bisector of $\angle BEC$ is equidistant from the lines $AB$ and $CD$. As $P$ lies on line $AC$, its distance to line $AB$ is $PA\sin\angle BAC$ and its distance to line $CD$ is $PC\sin\angle DCA$ (the sine of the angle between the two lines, which does not depend on the side of $A$ or $C$ on which $P$ lies). By the extended sine rule in the circumcircle, $\sin\angle BAC=\frac{BC}{2R}$ and $\sin\angle DCA=\frac{AD}{2R}$. Hence \[PA\cdot BC=PC\cdot AD .\] In the same way, $P$ lies on the bisector of $\angle DFC$, formed by the lines $AD$ and $BC$, so $PA\sin\angle DAC=PC\sin\angle BCA$, that is \[PA\cdot CD=PC\cdot AB .\] Neither $PA$ nor $PC$ is $0$ (if $PA=0$, the first equation gives $PC\cdot AD=0$, impossible), so dividing the two equations gives $\frac{BC}{CD}=\frac{AD}{AB}$, i.e. $AB\cdot BC=AD\cdot CD$.

Step 2: the midpoint of $BD$ lies on $AC$. Since $\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ$, the two angles have the same sine, so \[[ABC]=\tfrac12AB\cdot BC\sin\angle ABC=\tfrac12AD\cdot CD\sin\angle ADC=[ADC].\] The triangles $ABC$ and $ADC$ share the base $AC$, so $B$ and $D$ are at the same distance from line $AC$; they lie on opposite sides of it (the quadrilateral is convex). Hence the midpoint $N$ of $BD$ lies on line $AC$.

Step 3: an oblique reflection. Put $\vec d=\overrightarrow{BD}$; it is not parallel to $AC$. Every point $X$ of the plane can be written uniquely as $X=Q+t\vec d$ with $Q$ on line $AC$ and $t$ real. Define $\sigma(X)=Q-t\vec d$. This map $\sigma$ is affine, so it maps lines to lines and preserves intersections of lines; it fixes every point of $AC$ and $\sigma(\sigma(X))=X$. Since $B=N-\frac12\vec d$ and $D=N+\frac12\vec d$, the map $\sigma$ exchanges $B$ and $D$, and it fixes $A$, $C$ and $P$.

Therefore $\sigma$ maps line $AB$ to line $AD$ and line $CD$ to line $CB$, so it maps $E=AB\cap CD$ to $AD\cap CB=F$; as $\sigma$ is an involution, it also maps $F$ to $E$. Then it maps line $PF$ to line $PE$ and line $CD$ to line $CB$, so it maps $U=PF\cap CD$ to $PE\cap CB=V$.

Finally, for any point $X=Q+t\vec d$ we have $\overrightarrow{X\sigma(X)}=-2t\vec d$, parallel to $BD$, and the midpoint of $X$ and $\sigma(X)$ is $Q$, which lies on $AC$.

(i) Applied to $X=U$: $\overrightarrow{UV}$ is parallel to $BD$. (Here $U\ne V$: otherwise $U$ would lie on $AC$ and on $CD$, so $U=C$, and then $C$ would lie on line $PF$; but line $PF$ meets line $AC$ only at $P\ne C$.) So $UV\parallel DB$.

(ii) Applied to $X=E$: the midpoint of $EF$ lies on $AC$. The line $EF$ is not the line $AC$ (the point $E$ is on line $AB$ and $E\ne A$, so $E$ is not on $AC$), hence the lines $AC$ and $EF$ meet exactly at the midpoint of $EF$.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ be a sequence defined by $a_1=33$ and for $n\ge1$ \[a_{n+1}=a_n+\frac{1}{\left\lfloor a_n+\frac12\right\rfloor}.\] Which integers appear in the sequence?

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the values of $a_n$ for the first few steps in exact fractions. Where is the first boundary at which the denominator in the increment changes?
Hint 2: key idea
Group terms in the intervals $[m-\tfrac12,m+\tfrac12)$, where the increment is constant. If a block starts a distance $\delta$ above its lower endpoint, count how many steps are needed to leave it when $\delta<1/m$ and when $\delta=1/m$. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Compute the overshoot from the first block and track it until the block whose step equals that overshoot. In every block, test whether its midpoint is reached by an integer number of steps. Repeat the midpoint test after the overshoot resets.
Common trap
The upper endpoint of a block belongs to the next block, so equality at the boundary changes the next increment.
Show the answer

$33$ and all even integers $\ge 66$

Show the solution – Blocks between consecutive half-integers

Write $r(x)=\left\lfloor x+\frac12\right\rfloor$; this is the integer nearest to $x$, and $r(x)=m$ exactly when $m-\frac12\le x<m+\frac12$. The recursion is $a_{n+1}=a_n+\dfrac1{r(a_n)}$; the sequence is increasing.

Key lemma. Suppose $a_k=m-\frac12+\delta$ for an integer $m\ge1$ and a number $\delta$ with $0\le\delta\le\frac1m$. As long as the terms stay below $m+\frac12$ they round to $m$, so \[a_{k+j}=m-\tfrac12+\delta+\tfrac jm\qquad\text{while these values are }<m+\tfrac12 .\]

  • If $\delta<\frac1m$: this holds for $j=0,1,\dots,m-1$ (since $\delta+\frac{m-1}m<1$), and then $a_{k+m}=(m+1)-\frac12+\delta$.

  • If $\delta=\frac1m$: it holds for $j=0,1,\dots,m-2$, and then $a_{k+m-1}=m+\frac12=(m+1)-\frac12+0$.

Call the terms of the sequence in $[m-\frac12,m+\frac12)$ the block of $m$. All of them lie in this interval, so the only integer that can occur in the block of $m$ is $m$ itself, and it occurs if and only if $\delta+\frac jm=\frac12$ for an allowed $j$.

Following the sequence. The block of $33$ starts at $a_1=33$: the terms are $33+\frac j{33}$ for $j=0,1,\dots,16$ (all $<33.5$), and $a_{18}=33+\frac{17}{33}=33\frac12+\frac1{66}$. The only integer here is $33$.

So the block of $34$ starts at $34-\frac12+\frac1{66}$, with $\delta=\frac1{66}<\frac1{34}$. By the lemma, each block of $m=34,35,\dots,65$ starts at $m-\frac12+\frac1{66}$ (the same $\delta$ carries over), and $m$ appears in it only if $\frac jm=\frac12-\frac1{66}=\frac{16}{33}$, i.e. $33j=16m$. Since $\gcd(16,33)=1$ this forces $33\mid m$, impossible for $34\le m\le65$. So no integer from $34$ to $65$ appears.

The block of $66$ starts at $66-\frac12+\frac1{66}$, now with $\delta=\frac1{66}=\frac1m$. Its terms are $66-\frac12+\frac{j+1}{66}$ for $j=0,\dots,64$; the value $66$ occurs for $j+1=33$. The block ends with $a=66\frac12$ exactly, so the block of $67$ starts with $\delta=0$.

From then on $\delta=0<\frac1m$ for every block, so the block of each $m\ge67$ starts at $m-\frac12$ and consists of $m-\frac12+\frac jm$, $j=0,\dots,m-1$. The value $m$ appears if and only if $\frac jm=\frac12$ for some $j\le m-1$, that is, if and only if $m$ is even (take $j=\frac m2$).

The sequence passes through every block, so the integers that appear are exactly $33$ and the even integers from $66$ on: \[\boxed{33,\ 66,\ 68,\ 70,\ 72,\ \dots}\]

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Process Termination and Reachability

In an $n\times n$ grid, you are allowed to choose $k$ squares, including the top right and bottom left squares, and colour them red. The remaining squares are coloured blue. Thereafter, you are allowed to repeatedly choose any row or column and reverse the colours of all the squares in the row or column, respectively, until all the squares are red. What is the minimum value of $k$ for this to be possible?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try reversing the same row twice, then try reversing a row and a column in either order. What determines whether the colour of their intersection ultimately changes?
Hint 2: key idea
Give each row and column a bit recording whether it is flipped an odd number of times. Use those bits to describe exactly which cells must start red and translate the two corner requirements into equalities between row and column bits. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
Count the red cells in terms of the numbers of rows and columns with bit $1$. Handle a constant row or column bit pattern separately, and for mixed patterns use $(u-1)(v-1)\ge0$ on each product in the count. Look for a bit pattern attaining equality there while obeying the two corner equalities.
Common trap
When $n=1$, the two required corners coincide.
Show the answer

$2n-2$ (for $n\ge2$; for $n=1$ the two corners coincide and $k=1$)

Show solution 1 – Which rows and columns are flipped

Number the rows $1,\dots,n$ from the top and the columns $1,\dots,n$ from the left, so the top right square is $(1,n)$ and the bottom left square is $(n,1)$. If $n=1$, these are the same square; it must be red, and then the grid is already all red, so the minimum is $k=1$. From now on $n\ge2$.

Which colourings can be turned all red. Flipping the same line twice changes nothing, and the order of flips does not matter (each square only counts how many times it has been reversed). So a finished sequence of moves is described by $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ (row $i$ reversed an odd number of times or not) and $y_j\in\{0,1\}$ (the same for column $j$); square $(i,j)$ changes colour exactly when $x_i+y_j$ is odd. All squares end red exactly when the squares that change colour are exactly the blue ones, i.e. \[(i,j)\text{ is red at the start}\iff x_i=y_j .\] Conversely, for any choice of $x_i,y_j$ the set $\{(i,j):x_i=y_j\}$ can be made all red by flipping the rows with $x_i=1$ and the columns with $y_j=1$.

Counting. Let $p$ be the number of rows with $x_i=1$ and $q$ the number of columns with $y_j=1$. The number of red squares is \[k=pq+(n-p)(n-q).\] The corners give $x_1=y_n$ and $x_n=y_1$.

  • If $p\in\{0,n\}$, all $x_i$ are equal, so every column is entirely red or entirely blue. Columns $1$ and $n$ contain the red corners, so $k\ge2n$. The case $q\in\{0,n\}$ is the same with rows.

  • If $1\le p\le n-1$ and $1\le q\le n-1$, then using $uv\ge u+v-1$ for $u,v\ge1$ (equivalent to $(u-1)(v-1)\ge0$), \[k\ge(p+q-1)+\bigl((n-p)+(n-q)-1\bigr)=2n-2 .\]

So $k\ge2n-2$.

Construction. Take $x_1=1$, $x_2=\dots=x_n=0$, $y_1=0$, $y_2=\dots=y_n=1$. The red squares are the first row without $(1,1)$ and the first column without $(1,1)$: $2n-2$ squares, including $(1,n)$ and $(n,1)$. Flipping row $1$ and columns $2,\dots,n$ makes everything red. Hence the minimum is $\boxed{2n-2}$ for $n\ge2$ (and $1$ for $n=1$).

Show solution 2 – An invariant of $2\times2$ rectangles

Number the rows from the top and the columns from the left; the given corners are $(1,n)$ and $(n,1)$. If $n=1$, these are the same square; it must be red, and then the grid is already all red, so the minimum is $k=1$. From now on $n\ge2$.

Invariant. Take two rows $i,i'$ and two columns $j,j'$. A move reverses either none or exactly two of the four squares $(i,j),(i,j'),(i',j),(i',j')$, so the parity of the number of red squares among these four never changes. At the end all four are red, so at the start every such “rectangle” contains an even number of red squares. Consequently, for every row $i$ and columns $j,j'$: squares $(i,j),(i,j')$ have equal colours exactly when $(1,j),(1,j')$ do. In other words, every row has the same colour pattern as row $1$ or the exactly opposite pattern.

Lower bound. Let row $1$ contain $r$ red squares; $r\ge1$ since $(1,n)$ is red. Let $t$ be the number of rows equal to row $1$ (including row $1$), so $t\ge1$, and the other $n-t$ rows are opposite to it. The number of red squares is \[k=tr+(n-t)(n-r).\]

  • If $r=n$: each row is all red or all blue; rows $1$ and $n$ contain red squares, so $k\ge2n$.

  • If $t=n$: all rows equal row $1$, so column $1$ has the colour of $(n,1)$ in every row and is red, and column $n$ is red as well; thus $k\ge2n$.

  • Otherwise $1\le r\le n-1$ and $1\le t\le n-1$, and with $uv\ge u+v-1$ for $u,v\ge1$, \[k\ge(t+r-1)+(2n-t-r-1)=2n-2 .\]

The bound is attained. Colour red the squares $(1,2),\dots,(1,n)$ and $(2,1),\dots,(n,1)$, and everything else blue; this is $2n-2$ squares including both corners. Reverse row $1$: now $(1,1)$ is red and $(1,2),\dots,(1,n)$ are blue, so column $1$ is entirely red and each column $2,\dots,n$ is entirely blue. Then reverse each of the columns $2,\dots,n$; every square is now red. So the minimum is $\boxed{2n-2}$ for $n\ge2$ (and $1$ for $n=1$).

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Monovariants and Directional Change

The number $1$ is written $10$ times on a whiteboard. Every minute, Simon chooses two distinct numbers $a$ and $b$ on the whiteboard which may be of equal value, erases them, and writes down two new numbers $a+\frac1b$ and $b+\frac1a$.

  • (a) Show that, regardless of Simon's sequence of operations, after $1000$ minutes there will be at least one number on the board which is greater than $20$.

  • (b) Show that there is some sequence of operations for Simon to ensure that after $1000$ minutes, all numbers on the board are less than $21$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try operating on two equal entries and then on two unequal entries. Compare how both entries change, and record what happens when the equal pair starts at a large value.
Hint 2: key idea
Track the sum of squares of the entries and expand its change in one move. The terms $a/b$ and $b/a$ can be bounded together by AM–GM, independently of which pair was chosen. (LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Add the one-move bounds and compare with the total possible if every entry stays at most $20$. For the construction, update five disjoint pairs in each round so all ten entries are equal again at the end of the round. Square the resulting one-variable recurrence and bound the added reciprocal squares separately for early and late rounds.
Common trap
Simon chooses two separate entries, which may carry the same value.
Show the solution – Sum of squares as a monovariant

(a) Let $Q$ be the sum of the squares of the ten numbers. All numbers stay positive. When $a,b$ are replaced by $a+\frac1b$ and $b+\frac1a$, the change of $Q$ is \[\Bigl(a+\frac1b\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(b+\frac1a\Bigr)^2-a^2-b^2=2\Bigl(\frac ab+\frac ba\Bigr)+\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}>4,\] because $\frac ab+\frac ba\ge2$ by AM–GM and the last two terms are positive. At the start $Q=10$, so after $1000$ minutes $Q>10+4\cdot1000=4010$. If every number were at most $20$, then $Q\le10\cdot400=4000$, a contradiction. Hence some number exceeds $20$.

(b) Simon works in rounds of five minutes: in each round he splits the ten numbers into five pairs and operates on each pair once. If all ten numbers are equal to $x$ at the start of a round, then every operation replaces $x,x$ by $x+\frac1x,x+\frac1x$, so after the round all ten numbers equal $x+\frac1x$. Starting from $x_0=1$, after $k$ rounds all numbers equal $x_k$, where \[x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac1{x_k},\qquad x_{k+1}^2=x_k^2+2+\frac1{x_k^2}.\] After $1000$ minutes, i.e. $200$ rounds, every number equals $x_{200}$. From the second formula, $x_k^2\ge1+2k$ for all $k$, and \[x_{200}^2=1+400+\sum_{k=0}^{199}\frac1{x_k^2}\le401+\sum_{k=0}^{199}\frac1{2k+1}.\] The ten terms with $k\le9$ are each at most $1$, and the $190$ terms with $k\ge10$ are each at most $\frac1{21}$. Hence \[x_{200}^2\le401+10+\frac{190}{21}<401+10+10=421<441=21^2,\] so after $1000$ minutes all numbers are equal to $x_{200}<21$.

Problem 5

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Higher Power Residues

Determine all primes $p$ such that for any positive integer $n$, one can find positive integers $a_1,a_2,a_3$ such that \[(a_1-a_2)(a_2-a_3)(a_3-a_1)\equiv n\pmod p.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Name two of the three differences and express the third using them. Check whether any pair of residue classes can arise as those two differences by choosing sufficiently large positive representatives for the original entries.
Hint 2: key idea
Write the second difference as a multiple of the first, so varying the first scales the product by a cube. For primes with a nontrivial cube subgroup, ask which cube classes are met by $t(t+1)$ as the multiplier $t$ varies. (LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences)
Hint 3: approach
Handle the smallest primes directly and use the bijectivity of cubing when $3$ is coprime to $p-1$. For the larger primes with three cube classes, encode their counts by the cubic character sum $J=\sum_a\chi(a)\chi(1-a)$ and expand $|J|^2$ by grouping pairs according to $a/b$ and $(1-a)/(1-b)$. Compare its resulting size with the lower bound that would follow if one class count vanished, and test any small primes left by the comparison.
Common trap
In the expansion of $|J|^2$, the pair of ratios $(1,1)$ represents every admissible pair with $a=b$.
Show the answer

all primes except $2$ and $7$

Show the solution – Cubic residues and a Jacobi sum

Reformulation. Put $x=a_1-a_2$ and $y=a_2-a_3$; then $a_3-a_1=-(x+y)$ and the product equals $-xy(x+y)$. Any residues $x,y$ modulo $p$ occur (take $a_3$ a large positive integer, then $a_2=a_3+y$, $a_1=a_2+x$ with suitable representatives). Since $n\mapsto-n$ permutes the residues, the condition is: every residue modulo $p$ is of the form $f(x,y)=xy(x+y)$. The residue $0$ is always obtained ($x=0$).

$p=2$ fails. Among $a_1,a_2,a_3$ two have the same parity, so the product is even; $n=1$ is never reached.

$p=3$ works. $(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(1,2,3)$ gives $(-1)(-1)(2)=2$, and $(3,2,1)$ gives $(1)(1)(-2)=-2\equiv1$.

$p\equiv2\pmod3$ works. Then $\gcd(3,p-1)=1$, so cubing is a bijection of the residues: if $u^3\equiv v^3\not\equiv0$, then $(u/v)^3\equiv1$, and the order of $u/v$ divides $\gcd(3,p-1)=1$, so $u\equiv v$. With $y=x$ we get $f(x,x)=2x^3$, and since $2$ is invertible ($p$ odd), $2x^3$ takes every value.

From now on $p\equiv1\pmod3$. Fix a primitive root $g$. The nonzero cubes form the subgroup $C_0=\{g^{3k}\}$, and the nonzero residues split into the three classes $C_r=\{g^{3k+r}\}$, $r=0,1,2$. Note $-1=(-1)^3\in C_0$. For $x\ne0$ and $y=tx$, \[f(x,tx)=x^3\,t(t+1).\] If $t(t+1)\in C_r$, then $x^3t(t+1)$ runs over all of $C_r$ as $x$ varies (the quotient of two elements of $C_r$ is a cube). So $f$ takes every nonzero value if and only if \[t(t+1)\ \text{meets each of the three classes } C_0,C_1,C_2\ \text{as } t \text{ runs over the residues.}\qquad(\ast)\]

$p=7$ fails. Here $C_0=\{1,6\}$. For $t=1,2,3,4,5$ we get $t(t+1)\equiv2,6,5,6,2$, which lie in $\{2,5\}\cup\{1,6\}$ and never in the class $\{3,4\}$. So $f$ never takes the values $3,4$; since the product equals $-f$ and $-3\equiv4$, $-4\equiv3$, the product never takes them either, and, e.g., $n=3$ is impossible.

$p\equiv1\pmod 3$, $p\ge13$ works. Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and define $\chi(0)=0$, $\chi(a)=\omega^r$ for $a\in C_r$. Then $\chi(ab)=\chi(a)\chi(b)$, $|\chi(a)|=1$ and $\overline{\chi(a)}=\chi(a^{-1})$ for $a\ne0$, $\chi(-1)=1$, and since each class has $\frac{p-1}3$ elements, \[\sum_{a\ne0}\chi(a)=\tfrac{p-1}3(1+\omega+\omega^2)=0,\qquad \sum_{a\ne0}\chi(a)^2=\tfrac{p-1}3(1+\omega^2+\omega^4)=0.\] Let $N_r$ be the number of $t$ with $t(t+1)\in C_r$; then $N_0+N_1+N_2=p-2$, and \[J:=\sum_{t}\chi(t)\chi(t+1)=N_0+N_1\omega+N_2\omega^2 .\] Replacing $t$ by $-t$ and using $\chi(-1)=1$, also $J=\sum_a\chi(a)\chi(1-a)$.

Claim: $|J|^2=p$. We have \[|J|^2=\sum_{a,b\notin\{0,1\}}\chi(a)\chi(1-a)\overline{\chi(b)\chi(1-b)}=\sum_{a,b\notin\{0,1\}}\chi\Bigl(\frac ab\Bigr)\chi\Bigl(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\Bigr).\] Group the pairs $(a,b)$ by $(u,v)=\bigl(\frac ab,\frac{1-a}{1-b}\bigr)$, where $u,v\ne0$. Given $(u,v)$, the pairs with $a=bu$ and $1-a=(1-b)v$ satisfy $1-v=b(u-v)$. If $u=v=1$, then $a=b$ and there are $p-2$ such pairs. If $u=v\ne1$ there are none. If $u\ne v$, then $b=\frac{1-v}{u-v}$ is determined; it is admissible ($b\ne0,1$, and then $a=bu\ne0,1$) exactly when $u\ne1$ and $v\ne1$. Hence \[|J|^2=(p-2)+\sum_{\substack{u,v\ne0,1\\u\ne v}}\chi(u)\chi(v)=(p-2)+\Bigl(\sum_{u\ne0,1}\chi(u)\Bigr)^2-\sum_{u\ne0,1}\chi(u)^2=(p-2)+(-1)^2-(-1)=p .\]

Conclusion. Suppose $(\ast)$ fails, say $N_r=0$. Then $J=\omega^{r+1}(N_{r+1}+N_{r+2}\,\omega)$ (indices mod $3$), and with $a=N_{r+1}$, $b=N_{r+2}$, $a+b=p-2$: \[p=|J|^2=|a+b\omega|^2=a^2-ab+b^2=\tfrac14(a+b)^2+\tfrac34(a-b)^2\ge\tfrac{(p-2)^2}4 .\] This gives $p^2-8p+4\le0$, i.e. $p\le4+2\sqrt3<8$, contradicting $p\ge13$. So $(\ast)$ holds and every residue is reached.

Answer. The primes $p\equiv1\pmod3$ below $13$ are just $7$, so the condition holds for $\boxed{\text{every prime }p\ne2,7}$.
