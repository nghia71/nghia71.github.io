Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Find all real numbers $x>0$, $y>0$, $z>0$ satisfying \[\begin{cases} x(x-3)+2y(y-3)-9z=0,\\ x(2x-9)-12y+z(z-15)=0,\\ -6x+y(y-9)+2z(z-6)=0.\end{cases}\]

Show the answer

$(x,y,z)=(10,5,10)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Eliminate z, then a quartic)

Call the three equations (1), (2), (3). Expanding, \[(1)+(2)-2\cdot(3):\quad (x^2-3x+2y^2-6y-9z)+(2x^2-9x-12y+z^2-15z)-2(-6x+y^2-9y+2z^2-12z)=3x^2-3z^2 .\] So $3x^2-3z^2=0$, and since $x,z>0$ we get $z=x$. Then (3) is a consequence of (1) and (2) (it equals $\frac12[(1)+(2)]$ when $z=x$), and the system becomes \[x^2-12x+2y^2-6y=0,\qquad 3x^2-24x-12y=0 .\] The second equation gives $y=\dfrac{x^2-8x}{4}$. Substituting into the first and multiplying by $8$: \[8x^2-96x+(x^2-8x)^2-12(x^2-8x)=0\iff x^4-16x^3+60x^2=0\iff x^2(x-6)(x-10)=0 .\] As $x>0$, $x=6$ or $x=10$. For $x=6$ we get $y=-3<0$, rejected. For $x=10$ we get $y=5$ and $z=10$.

Check: $10\cdot 7+2\cdot5\cdot2-90=0$, $\ 10\cdot 11-60+10\cdot(-5)=0$, $\ -60+5\cdot(-4)+2\cdot10\cdot4=0$. Hence the only solution is $\boxed{(x,y,z)=(10,5,10)}$.

Solution 2 (Combinations with no linear terms)

Call the equations (1), (2), (3). Every equation is “quadratic part $=$ linear part”; we look for combinations $\lambda(1)+\mu(2)+\nu(3)$ in which the linear terms cancel, because then only a relation between squares is left.

The coefficients of $x$, $y$, $z$ in $\lambda(1)+\mu(2)+\nu(3)$ are $-3\lambda-9\mu-6\nu$, $-6\lambda-12\mu-9\nu$, $-9\lambda-15\mu-12\nu$. They all vanish for $(\lambda,\mu,\nu)=(1,1,-2)$, and indeed \[(1)+(2)-2\cdot(3)=3x^2-3z^2=0,\] so $z=x$ (both are positive). With $z=x$, (1) and (2) become \[(1')\ \ x^2-12x+2y^2-6y=0,\qquad (2')\ \ 3x^2-24x-12y=0,\] and (3) is then automatically true, since it equals $\frac12[(1)+(2)]$. Again we cancel the linear terms: $2\cdot(1')-(2')$ gives \[-x^2+4y^2=0,\] so $x=2y$ (both positive). Putting $x=2y$ into $(2')$: $12y^2-48y-12y=12y(y-5)=0$, so $y=5$, $x=z=10$.

Substituting back shows that $(10,5,10)$ satisfies all three equations, so the answer is $\boxed{(x,y,z)=(10,5,10)}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Find all real numbers $a$, $b$, $c$ such that each of the polynomials $x^2-3x+a$ and $x^2+x+b$ has two distinct real roots, and these roots are roots of the polynomial $x^3-x^2+cx+4$.

Show the answer

$a=2,\ b=-2,\ c=-4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting roots and Vieta)

Let $r_1\ne r_2$ be the roots of $x^2-3x+a$ and $s_1\ne s_2$ the roots of $x^2+x+b$; all four are roots of $f(x)=x^3-x^2+cx+4$. By Vieta, $r_1+r_2=3$ and $s_1+s_2=-1$, so $\{r_1,r_2\}\ne\{s_1,s_2\}$.

The set $T=\{r_1,r_2,s_1,s_2\}$ consists of roots of the cubic $f$, so it has at most $3$ elements. It contains two different $2$-element sets, so it has at least $3$ elements. Hence $|T|=3$: the two quadratics have exactly one common root $t$, and \[T=\{t,\ 3-t,\ -1-t\}\] consists of three distinct roots of $f$. A cubic has at most three roots, so these are all the roots of $f$, each simple. By Vieta for $f$, their sum is $1$: \[t+(3-t)+(-1-t)=1\ \Longrightarrow\ t=1 .\] So the roots of $f$ are $1,2,-2$, and $x^2-3x+a=(x-1)(x-2)$, $x^2+x+b=(x-1)(x+2)$, $f(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x+2)=x^3-x^2-4x+4$. Thus $a=2$, $b=-2$, $c=-4$.

Conversely, for these values the quadratics $x^2-3x+2$ and $x^2+x-2$ have the distinct real roots $1,2$ and $1,-2$, all roots of $x^3-x^2-4x+4$. The answer is $\boxed{a=2,\ b=-2,\ c=-4}$.

Solution 2 (Divisibility and remainders)

Let $f(x)=x^3-x^2+cx+4$. The polynomial $x^2-3x+a$ has two distinct roots $r\ne r'$, both roots of $f$, so $(x-r)(x-r')=x^2-3x+a$ divides $f$. We compute the remainder of $f$ modulo $x^2-3x+a$, using $x^2\equiv 3x-a$: \[x^3\equiv 3x^2-ax\equiv 3(3x-a)-ax=(9-a)x-3a,\] \[f(x)\equiv (9-a)x-3a-(3x-a)+cx+4=(6-a+c)x+(4-2a).\] The remainder must be the zero polynomial, so $a=2$ and $c=a-6=-4$. Then \[f(x)=x^3-x^2-4x+4=(x-1)(x^2-4)=(x-1)(x-2)(x+2).\] Now $x^2+x+b$ must have two distinct roots among $1,2,-2$ whose sum is $-1$. The only such pair is $\{1,-2\}$, so $b=1\cdot(-2)=-2$.

Finally, the discriminants $9-4\cdot2=1$ and $1+4\cdot2=9$ are positive, and the roots $1,2$ and $1,-2$ are roots of $f$. The answer is $\boxed{a=2,\ b=-2,\ c=-4}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

Find all integers $n\ge 2$ such that each of the numbers \[2^2\cdot n-1,\quad 2^3\cdot n-1,\quad \dots,\quad 2^n\cdot n-1\] is a prime number.

Show the answer

$n=2$ and $n=3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Prime factors of n-1 and of 2n-1)

Write $q_k=2^k n-1$ for $2\le k\le n$. For $n=2$: $q_2=7$; for $n=3$: $q_2=11$, $q_3=23$. All are prime, so $n=2$ and $n=3$ work. From now on let $n\ge4$ and suppose all $q_k$ are prime. Note $q_k\ge q_2=4n-1>2n-1$.

Step 1: $n-1$ is a power of $2$. Suppose an odd prime $p$ divides $n-1$. Then $3\le p\le n-1$, so $k=p-1$ satisfies $2\le k\le n-2$. Since $n\equiv1\pmod p$ and, by Fermat's little theorem, $2^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$, \[q_{p-1}=2^{p-1}n-1\equiv 1\cdot1-1=0\pmod p .\] But $q_{p-1}>n-1\ge p$, so $q_{p-1}$ is not prime, a contradiction. Hence $n=2^s+1$, and $n\ge4$ forces $s\ge2$.

Step 2: a prime factor of $2n-1$. Let $r$ be a prime factor of $2n-1=2^{s+1}+1$; $r$ is odd. Let $e$ be the smallest positive integer with $2^e\equiv1\pmod r$ (it exists by Fermat's theorem). Since $2^{s+1}\equiv-1\pmod r$, we have $2^{2s+2}\equiv1$, so $e\le 2s+2$; also $e\ge2$ because $2\not\equiv1\pmod r$.

Because $2n\equiv1\pmod r$, \[q_{e+1}=2^{e}\cdot 2n-1\equiv 2^e-1\equiv0\pmod r .\] If $e+1\le n$, then $q_{e+1}$ is one of our numbers, divisible by $r$ and larger than $2n-1\ge r$, hence not prime. Therefore $e+1>n$, i.e. $e\ge n=2^s+1$. Combined with $e\le 2s+2$: \[2^s+1\le 2s+2\iff 2^s\le 2s+1,\] which holds only for $s\le2$. So $s=2$ and $n=5$; but then $q_3=39=3\cdot13$ is not prime.

Hence no $n\ge4$ works, and the answer is $\boxed{n=2\text{ and }n=3}$.

Solution 2 (Fermat on n-1, then residues mod 3 and 5)

Let $q_k=2^kn-1$ ($2\le k\le n$). By direct check, $n=2$ ($q_2=7$) and $n=3$ ($q_2=11$, $q_3=23$) work. Let $n\ge4$ and assume all $q_k$ are prime.

$n-1$ has no odd prime factor. If an odd prime $p$ divides $n-1$, then $2\le p-1\le n-2$, and by Fermat's little theorem $q_{p-1}=2^{p-1}n-1\equiv 1-1=0\pmod p$, while $q_{p-1}>n-1\ge p$. So $q_{p-1}$ is composite, a contradiction. Hence $n=2^s+1$ with $s\ge2$, so $n\ge5$.

$s$ even. Then $2^s\equiv1\pmod 3$, so $n\equiv2\pmod3$ and \[q_3=8n-1\equiv 8\cdot2-1=15\equiv0\pmod 3 .\] Since $q_3>3$ and $3\le n$, this is a contradiction.

$s$ odd (so $s\ge3$ and $n\ge9$). The powers of $2$ modulo $5$ repeat with period $4$: $2,4,3,1$. For odd $s$, $2^s\equiv2$ or $3\pmod5$, so $n=2^s+1\equiv3$ or $4\pmod 5$.

  • If $n\equiv4$: $q_2=4n-1\equiv 16-1\equiv0\pmod5$.

  • If $n\equiv3$: $q_5=32n-1\equiv 2\cdot3-1\equiv0\pmod5$ (and $5\le n$, so $q_5$ is on the list).

In both cases a number on the list is a multiple of $5$ larger than $5$, a contradiction.

So the answer is $\boxed{n=2\text{ and }n=3}$.

Remark. The case $n=2^s+1$ can also be finished by a factorisation: for $s\ge3$ the exponent $k=s-1$ lies in $[2,n]$ and \[2^{s-1}(2^s+1)-1=2^{2s-1}+2^{s-1}-1=(2^s-1)(2^{s-1}+1),\] a product of two factors greater than $1$; the case $s=2$ ($n=5$) fails because $q_3=39$. This is the finish used in the official solution.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle $(O)$ such that the ray $DA$ meets the ray $CB$ at a point $P$. Let $X$ be an arbitrary point on the side $CD$. Let $J$, $K$, $L$ be the circumcenters of the triangles $ADX$, $BCX$, $PAB$, respectively.

  • (a) Prove that $O$, $J$, $K$, $L$ lie on a circle.

  • (b) Let $H$ be the orthocenter of triangle $JOK$. Prove that the distance from $H$ to $CD$ is equal to $LP$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Angle chase through the Miquel point, then oblique projection)

Write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $(J)$, $(K)$, $(L)$ denote the circles $ADX$, $BCX$, $PAB$.

Perpendicular lines. $O$ and $J$ both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, so $OJ\perp AD$. Likewise $OK\perp BC$ and $OL\perp AB$.

The common point $Q$. Let $Q$ be the second common point of $(J)$ and $(K)$ (if the circles are tangent at $X$, take $Q=X$ and read the line $QX$ as the common tangent). From the cyclic quadrilaterals $ADXQ$ and $BCXQ$, and since $D$, $X$, $C$ are collinear, \[\measuredangle(QA,QB)=\measuredangle(QA,QX)+\measuredangle(QX,QB)=\measuredangle(DA,DC)+\measuredangle(CD,CB)=\measuredangle(DA,CB)=\measuredangle(PA,PB).\] Hence $P$, $A$, $B$, $Q$ are concyclic: $Q$ lies on $(L)$ too.

(a) $AQ$ is the common chord of $(J)$ and $(L)$, so $JL\perp AQ$; similarly $KL\perp BQ$. Rotating both lines of an angle by $90^\circ$ does not change it, so \[\measuredangle(JO,JL)=\measuredangle(AD,AQ)=\measuredangle(AP,AQ),\qquad \measuredangle(KO,KL)=\measuredangle(BC,BQ)=\measuredangle(BP,BQ).\] The right-hand sides are equal because $P,A,B,Q$ are concyclic. Hence $\measuredangle(JO,JL)=\measuredangle(KO,KL)$, and $O$, $J$, $K$, $L$ lie on a circle.

(b) In triangle $JOK$ the altitude from $J$ is perpendicular to $OK$, hence parallel to $BC$; the altitude from $K$ is parallel to $AD$. So $H$ is the intersection of the line through $J$ parallel to $BC$ and the line through $K$ parallel to $AD$. (Part (a) is not needed here.)

Take coordinates with $D=(0,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ and $P$ above the $x$-axis. Let $\delta=\angle PDC$, $\gamma=\angle PCD$, so $\angle DPC=180^\circ-\gamma-\delta$. Put $X=(x,0)$, $a=DA$, $b=CB$, and let $u=(\cos\delta,\sin\delta)$, $w=(-\cos\gamma,\sin\gamma)$ be the unit vectors from $D$ towards $A$ and from $C$ towards $B$.

Since $ABCD$ is cyclic, $PA\cdot PD=PB\cdot PC$, so triangles $PAB$ and $PCD$ are similar with ratio $k=\frac{PA}{PC}=\frac{PB}{PD}=\frac{AB}{CD}$. Also, by the law of sines in $PDC$, \[PD=\frac{c\sin\gamma}{\sin(\gamma+\delta)},\qquad PC=\frac{c\sin\delta}{\sin(\gamma+\delta)} .\]

The centres. $J=(\frac x2,h_J)$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $DA$, i.e. $J\cdot u=\frac a2$, so $x\cos\delta+2h_J\sin\delta=a$. Likewise $K=(\frac{c+x}2,h_K)$ with $(K-C)\cdot w=\frac b2$, i.e. $(c-x)\cos\gamma+2h_K\sin\gamma=b$.

Oblique projection. The line through $J$ parallel to $BC$ meets $CD$ at $J_1=(\frac x2+h_J\cot\gamma,\,0)$, and the line through $K$ parallel to $AD$ meets $CD$ at $K_2=(\frac{c+x}2-h_K\cot\delta,\,0)$. Using the two relations above, \[2\sin\gamma\sin\delta\,(x_{J_1}-x_{K_2})=-c\sin\gamma\sin\delta+\cos\gamma\,(a-x\cos\delta)+\cos\delta\,(b-(c-x)\cos\gamma)=a\cos\gamma+b\cos\delta-c\cos(\gamma-\delta).\] The point $X$ has disappeared. Now $a=PD-PA=PD-k\,PC$ and $b=PC-k\,PD$, so \[a\cos\gamma+b\cos\delta=(PD\cos\gamma+PC\cos\delta)-k\,(PC\cos\gamma+PD\cos\delta).\] By the formulas for $PD$, $PC$ and the identities $\sin2\gamma+\sin2\delta=2\sin(\gamma+\delta)\cos(\gamma-\delta)$ and $\sin\delta\cos\gamma+\sin\gamma\cos\delta=\sin(\gamma+\delta)$, \[PD\cos\gamma+PC\cos\delta=c\cos(\gamma-\delta),\qquad PC\cos\gamma+PD\cos\delta=c .\] Therefore $a\cos\gamma+b\cos\delta-c\cos(\gamma-\delta)=-kc=-AB$, and \[J_1K_2=\frac{AB}{2\sin\gamma\sin\delta}.\]

Conclusion. The triangle $HK_2J_1$ has $HK_2\parallel PD$, $HJ_1\parallel PC$ and $K_2J_1$ on line $DC$, so it is homothetic to triangle $PDC$ (with $K_2\leftrightarrow D$, $J_1\leftrightarrow C$, $H\leftrightarrow P$). Since $K_2$ lies to the right of $J_1$ while $D$ lies to the left of $C$, the ratio is $-J_1K_2/DC$: the point $H$ lies on the other side of $CD$ from $P$, and its distance to $CD$ is $J_1K_2/DC$ times that of $P$. The distance from $P$ to $CD$ is $PD\sin\delta=\dfrac{c\sin\gamma\sin\delta}{\sin(\gamma+\delta)}$, hence \[d(H,CD)=\frac{J_1K_2}{c}\cdot\frac{c\sin\gamma\sin\delta}{\sin(\gamma+\delta)}=\frac{AB}{2\sin(\gamma+\delta)}=\frac{AB}{2\sin\angle APB}=LP,\] the last step by the law of sines in triangle $PAB$, whose circumradius is $LP$. This proves (b).

Solution 2 (Spiral similarity, then the Steiner line)

Write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$ modulo $180^\circ$. Since $O$ and $J$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, $OJ\perp AD$; likewise $OK\perp BC$.

(a) Let $Q$ be the second common point of the circles $(ADX)$ and $(BCX)$. As in Solution 1, $\measuredangle(QA,QB)=\measuredangle(DA,DC)+\measuredangle(CD,CB)=\measuredangle(PA,PB)$, so $Q$ also lies on the circle $(PAB)$.

Let $\theta=\measuredangle(AQ,AD)$. Using the concyclic quadruples $QADX$, $QXCB$ and the collinear triples $D,X,C$ and $D,A,P$: \[\theta=\measuredangle(AQ,AD)=\measuredangle(XQ,XD)=\measuredangle(XQ,XC)=\measuredangle(BQ,BC),\qquad \theta=\measuredangle(AQ,AP).\] So the chord $QD$ of circle $(J)$, the chord $QC$ of circle $(K)$ and the chord $QP$ of circle $(L)$ are each seen from a point of their own circle under the same directed angle $\theta$. The corresponding central angles are therefore all equal to $2\theta$ (as rotations), and the isosceles triangles $QDJ$, $QCK$, $QPL$ are directly similar. Consequently the spiral similarity centred at $Q$ that sends $D$ to $J$ also sends $C$ to $K$ and $P$ to $L$, so triangle $JKL$ is directly similar to triangle $DCP$, and \[\measuredangle(LJ,LK)=\measuredangle(PD,PC).\] On the other hand $OJ\perp PD$ and $OK\perp PC$, so $\measuredangle(OJ,OK)=\measuredangle(PD,PC)$. Hence $\measuredangle(LJ,LK)=\measuredangle(OJ,OK)$ and $O,J,K,L$ are concyclic.

(b) Let $Y$, $Z$ be the reflections of $L$ in the lines $OJ$ and $OK$.

Locating $Y$ and $Z$. Let $u$ be the unit vector from $D$ towards $P$ and $M$ the midpoint of $AD$. The line $OJ$ passes through $M$ and is perpendicular to $u$, so the reflection in it is $V\mapsto V-2\big((V-M)\cdot u\big)u$. The projection of $L$ on line $AP$ is the midpoint of $AP$ (as $LA=LP$), so $(L-D)\cdot u=DA+\frac{AP}2=\frac{DA+DP}2$, while $(M-D)\cdot u=\frac{DA}2$. Hence $(L-M)\cdot u=\frac{DP}2$ and \[Y=L-DP\cdot u=L-(P-D),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \overrightarrow{DY}=\overrightarrow{PL}.\] In the same way $\overrightarrow{CZ}=\overrightarrow{PL}$. So $Y$ and $Z$ are the images of $D$ and $C$ under the translation by $\overrightarrow{PL}$, and the line $YZ$ is parallel to $CD$.

$PL\perp CD$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $(L)$ at $P$. By the tangent–chord angle and because $ABCD$ is cyclic, \[\measuredangle(t,PA)=\measuredangle(BP,BA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(DC,DA)=\measuredangle(DC,PA),\] so $t\parallel CD$. Since $PL\perp t$, we get $PL\perp CD$. Therefore $DY\perp CD$, $CZ\perp CD$ and $DY=CZ=PL$: $DCZY$ is a rectangle and every point of line $YZ$ is at distance $PL$ from $CD$.

The Steiner line. By (a), $L$ lies on the circumcircle of triangle $JOK$. For a point on the circumcircle of a triangle, its reflections in the three side lines lie on one line, and this line (the Steiner line) passes through the orthocenter. Hence $H$ lies on the line $YZ$, and \[d(H,CD)=PL .\]

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

A rook makes $63$ moves on an $8\times 8$ chessboard, passing through every square exactly once; in each move it goes from a square to a square sharing a side with it. Number the squares from $1$ to $64$ in the order in which the rook visits them (the starting square gets the number $1$). Let $n$ be the largest difference between the numbers of two squares that share a side. What is the smallest possible value of $n$?

Show the answer

$15$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The first complete line)

$n=15$ is possible. Go through the board column by column: down the first column, up the second, down the third, and so on (see the figure). In column $j$ the numbers are $8(j-1)+1,\dots,8j$. Vertical neighbours differ by $1$. In row $i$ (counted from the top), the squares in columns $j$ and $j+1$ carry $8(j-1)+i$ and $8j+9-i$ if $j$ is odd, and $8(j-1)+9-i$ and $8j+i$ if $j$ is even; the differences are $17-2i$ and $2i-1$, at most $15$. So the largest difference is $15$ (attained by $1$ and $16$).

$n\ge15$ always. Call a row or a column a line. Let $S_m$ be the set of squares with numbers $1,\dots,m$ and $U_m$ the set of the others. Let $t$ be the smallest number such that $S_t$ contains a complete line $\ell$. After $63$ moves only one square is missing, so seven rows are complete; hence $t\le 63$ and $U_t\ne\varnothing$. Rotating the board, we may assume $\ell$ is a row. Note that the square numbered $t$ lies in $\ell$ (otherwise $\ell$ would already be complete in $S_{t-1}$).

$\ell$ is an edge row. The squares $t+1,\dots,64$ form a rook path, so $U_t$ is connected; it does not meet $\ell$, so it lies entirely on one side of $\ell$. If $\ell$ were not an edge row, the rows on the other side would be complete in $S_t$ but would not contain the square $t$, so they would be complete in $S_{t-1}$, contradicting the choice of $t$. Reflecting the board if necessary, $\ell$ is the bottom row and $U_t$ lies in the top seven rows.

Every column meets $U_t$. Let the square $t$ be in column $c_0$. The square $t+1$ is adjacent to it and lies in $U_t$, so it is the square just above, in column $c_0$. A column that is complete in $S_t$ would have become complete exactly at step $t$ (by minimality), so it would contain square $t$, i.e. be column $c_0$; but column $c_0$ contains $t+1\in U_t$. So no column is complete in $S_t$.

The far column. The rook path $t+1,t+2,\dots,64$ visits both edge columns $1$ and $8$. Let $e$ be the one it reaches for the first time later, and $e'$ the other one. Each move changes the column by at most $1$; first reaching $e'$ takes at least $|c_0-e'|$ moves from square $t+1$, and going from there to column $e$ takes at least $7$ more. So every square of $U_t$ in column $e$ has number at least \[t+1+|c_0-e'|+7 .\] Let $u$ be the lowest square of $U_t$ in column $e$ and $s$ the square just below it (it exists because $u$ is not in the bottom row). By the choice of $u$, $s\in S_t$, and the path $s,s+1,\dots,t$ goes from column $e$ to column $c_0$, so the number of $s$ is at most $t-|c_0-e|$. Hence the neighbours $s$ and $u$ differ by at least \[1+7+|c_0-e'|+|c_0-e|=8+|c_0-1|+|8-c_0|=8+7=15 .\] Therefore $n\ge15$, and the smallest possible value is $\boxed{15}$.

Solution 2 (Opposite edges are filled at separate times)

Construction. Go down the first column, up the second, down the third, and so on (the snake in the figure of Solution 1). Vertical neighbours differ by $1$. Take horizontal neighbours in columns $j$ and $j+1$, in the $i$th row counted from the end where the rook passes from column $j$ to column $j+1$ ($1\le i\le8$). Between them the rook makes $i-1$ moves to finish column $j$, one move across, and $i-1$ moves back along column $j+1$, so their numbers differ by $2i-1\le15$, with equality for $i=8$. So the largest difference is exactly $15$.

Lower bound. Suppose some numbering has all neighbour differences at most $14$.

Claim: no number of the bottom row lies between the smallest and the largest number of the top row. Take two adjacent squares of the top row, with numbers $p<q$; then $q-p\le14$. The rook goes from square $p$ to square $q$ in $q-p\le14$ moves. If on the way it visited the bottom row, it would need at least $7$ moves down and $7$ moves up, plus at least one horizontal move (the two squares are in different columns), that is at least $15$ moves. So none of the numbers $p,p+1,\dots,q$ belongs to the bottom row. The top row is a chain of $7$ adjacent pairs; consecutive pairs share a square, so the intervals $[p,q]$ of these pairs overlap and their union is an interval containing all numbers of the top row, i.e. it contains $[m_T,M_T]$, where $m_T$ and $M_T$ are the smallest and largest numbers of the top row. This proves the claim.

By symmetry, no number of the top row lies in $[m_B,M_B]$ (the range of the bottom row). If the bottom row had numbers both below $m_T$ and above $M_T$, then $[m_B,M_B]$ would contain the whole top row, which is impossible. So either every top number is smaller than every bottom number, or every top number is larger. In the same way, every number of the left column is smaller than every number of the right column, or every one is larger.

Reflecting the board in a horizontal axis and/or a vertical axis (this keeps all the conditions), we may assume that the bottom row numbers are larger than the top row numbers, and the left column numbers are larger than the right column numbers. Let $A$ be the number of the top-left corner and $B$ the number of the bottom-right corner. Comparing the top row with the bottom row gives $A<B$; comparing the left column with the right column gives $A>B$. This contradiction shows that some difference is at least $15$.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{15}$.
