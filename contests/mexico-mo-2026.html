Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments

A list of positive integers sorted from least to greatest is regia if all the numbers appearing in it are formed only by two digits $1\le a<b\le 9$ such that $b-a>1$. For example, \[2,\ 4,\ 24,\ 444\] is a regia list of size $4$ with $a=2$, $b=4$.

A regia list is special if the difference between any two consecutive numbers in it is a prime number. Determine the maximum value of $k$ for which there exists a special regia list of size $k$ in which all of its numbers have $4$ digits or fewer.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Group the entries of an increasing list by their number of digits. Compare two entries of the same length by subtracting their corresponding digits.
Hint 2: key idea
Every same-length difference is divisible by $b-a$. If that difference is prime, investigate how the two numbers can differ in their units digit and whether any higher digit can change. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Reduce the difference modulo $10$, then use the largest place value where the digits differ to show what the prime-gap pair must look like. Test whether three consecutive entries could share one length. For attainment, choose a small prime digit gap and try joining the permitted pairs across successive lengths, checking those joining gaps for primality.
Common trap
A gap between numbers of different lengths needs its own primality check.
Show the answer

$k=8$

Show the solution – Equal lengths force a difference of exactly $b-a$

Consider a special regia list with digits $a<b$, put $d=b-a\ge 2$, and assume all its numbers have at most $4$ digits. Since consecutive differences are primes, the list is strictly increasing.

Key step. Let $x<y$ be two numbers of the list with the same number $L$ of digits, and write $x=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}x_i10^i$, $y=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}y_i10^i$ with all digits $x_i,y_i\in\{a,b\}$. Then $y_i-x_i\in\{-d,0,d\}$, so \[y-x=d\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\varepsilon_i10^i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{-1,0,1\}.\] In particular $d\mid y-x$. Now suppose that $y-x$ is a prime $p$. Since $d\ge2$ divides the prime $p$, we get $p=d$ and therefore $\sum_{i}\varepsilon_i10^i=1$. Reducing modulo $10$ gives $\varepsilon_0\equiv1\pmod{10}$, so $\varepsilon_0=1$ and $\sum_{i\ge1}\varepsilon_i10^i=0$. If some $\varepsilon_i$ with $i\ge1$ were nonzero, take the largest such index $j$; then \[\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{j}\varepsilon_i10^i\Bigr|\ge 10^j-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}10^i>0,\] a contradiction. Hence $\varepsilon_i=0$ for $i\ge1$: $x$ and $y$ agree in every digit except the last one, which is $a$ in $x$ and $b$ in $y$.

Upper bound. Because the list is increasing, the numbers with a given number of digits form a block of consecutive terms. Suppose some block had three terms $x<y<z$, consecutive in the list. Their differences $y-x$ and $z-y$ are primes, so by the key step the last digit of $y$ is $b$ (comparing $x,y$) and also $a$ (comparing $y,z$), which is impossible. So each block has at most $2$ terms. There are only $4$ possible lengths ($1,2,3,4$ digits), hence $k\le 8$.

Example with $k=8$. Take $a=1$, $b=4$ (so $b-a=3>1$) and the list \[1,\ 4,\ 11,\ 14,\ 111,\ 114,\ 1111,\ 1114 .\] The consecutive differences are $3,7,3,97,3,997,3$, all prime. So the maximum is $k=\boxed{8}$.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation · Basic Inversion Setup

Let $ABCD$ be a rectangle with $AB>BC$. Let $E$ and $P$ be the intersections of $AB$ and $BD$, respectively, with the perpendicular to $BD$ through $C$. Let $X$ and $Y$ be the intersections of $AP$ with the circle through the points $B$, $E$, and $D$. Prove that $B$ is the center of the circle through the points $C$, $X$, and $Y$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the right angle at $C$ in triangle $BCD$ and the altitude foot $P$. Compare $BP\cdot BD$ with a square of a side.
Hint 2: key idea
Invert about $B$ with radius $BC$. Use the two right-triangle similarities to find the images of $D$ and $E$, then determine the image of the circle through $B,E,D$. (LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation)
Hint 3: approach
Use $AB>BC$ to locate $E$ on $AB$ and verify the required product for its image. Compare the image line with $AP$ and see how inversion acts on their intersection pair $X,Y$. Check whether the two points could be exchanged by inversion given that $B$ is outside their line.
Common trap
A point and its inverse lie on the same ray from $B$, which restricts how inversion can act on the intersection pair.
Show solution 1 – Inversion centred at B

Let $\Gamma$ be the circle through $B,E,D$ and let $\omega$ be the circle with centre $B$ and radius $BC$. Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $B$ and radius $BC$, so $\Phi$ sends a point $Z\neq B$ to the point $Z'$ of ray $BZ$ with $BZ\cdot BZ'=BC^2$; the points fixed by $\Phi$ are exactly the points of $\omega$.

$\Phi(D)=P$. In triangle $BCD$ the angle at $C$ is right and $CP$ is the altitude to the hypotenuse $BD$, so $P$ lies on segment $BD$ and the right triangles $BPC$ and $BCD$ (common angle at $B$) are similar. Hence $BP/BC=BC/BD$, that is, $BP\cdot BD=BC^2$.

Where $E$ lies. Let $\ell$ be the line $CP$, perpendicular to $BD$ at $P$. A point is on the same side of $\ell$ as $B$ exactly when its projection onto line $BD$ is on the same side of $P$ as $B$. In the right triangle $BCD$ we also have $DP\cdot DB=DC^2$, so $BP=BC^2/BD<CD^2/BD=DP$ (here $AB>BC$ is used), that is, $BP<\tfrac12BD$. By the central symmetry of the rectangle, the projection $Q$ of $A$ onto $BD$ satisfies $DQ=BP$, so $BQ=BD-BP>BP$: the point $Q$ is beyond $P$ as seen from $B$. Hence $A$ and $B$ are on opposite sides of $\ell$, and $E$ lies strictly between $A$ and $B$. The chord of the rectangle cut out by $\ell$ is the segment $CE$, and it contains the point $P$ of the diagonal $BD$; so $P$ lies on segment $CE$.

$\Phi(E)=A$. In triangle $EBC$ the angle at $B$ is right, and since $CE\perp BD$ (with $P$ on segment $CE$), \[\angle BEC=90^\circ-\angle BCE=90^\circ-\angle BCP=\angle PBC=\angle DBC .\] So the right triangles $EBC$ (right angle at $B$) and $BCD$ (right angle at $C$) are similar, with $BE/BC=BC/CD$. Since $CD=AB$, this gives $BE\cdot BA=BC^2$.

The image of $\Gamma$. The circle $\Gamma$ passes through the centre $B$, so $\Phi$ maps $\Gamma$ (without $B$) onto a line, namely the line through $\Phi(E)=A$ and $\Phi(D)=P$. Thus $\Phi(\Gamma)=AP$ and, as $\Phi$ is its own inverse, $\Phi(AP)=\Gamma$. Note that $B$ is not on line $AP$: otherwise that line would be $AB$, and $P$ would lie on both $AB$ and $BD$, forcing $P=B$, which is false since $BP=BC^2/BD>0$.

Conclusion. Let $X$ be one of the two common points of $\Gamma$ and line $AP$, and $X'=\Phi(X)$. As $X\in\Gamma$, $X'\in\Phi(\Gamma)=AP$; as $X\in AP$, $X'\in\Phi(AP)=\Gamma$. So $X'\in\{X,Y\}$. If $X'=Y\ne X$, then $B$, $X$, $Y$ would be collinear (since $X'$ lies on ray $BX$), so line $AP=XY$ would pass through $B$, which we excluded. Therefore $X'=X$, which means $BX^2=BC^2$. In the same way $BY=BC$.

Hence $BC=BX=BY$: the points $C,X,Y$ lie on $\omega$, and the circle through them is $\omega$, whose centre is $B$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates: line AP is the radical line of the two circles

Put $B$ at the origin with the $x$-axis along ray $BA$ and the $y$-axis along ray $BC$. With $AB=p$ and $BC=q$ (so $p>q>0$) we have \[B=(0,0),\quad A=(p,0),\quad C=(0,q),\quad D=(p,q).\] The points $E$ and $P$. Line $BD$ has direction $(p,q)$, so the perpendicular to $BD$ through $C$ is $px+qy=q^2$. Setting $y=0$ gives $E=\bigl(\tfrac{q^2}{p},0\bigr)$. The point $P$ is $t(p,q)$ with $t(p^2+q^2)=q^2$, so \[P=\Bigl(\frac{pq^2}{p^2+q^2},\ \frac{q^3}{p^2+q^2}\Bigr).\] The circle $\Gamma$ through $B,E,D$. A circle through the origin has an equation $x^2+y^2+ux+vy=0$. The point $E$ gives $\frac{q^4}{p^2}+u\frac{q^2}{p}=0$, so $u=-\frac{q^2}{p}$. The point $D$ gives $p^2+q^2-q^2+vq=0$, so $v=-\frac{p^2}{q}$. Hence \[\Gamma:\quad x^2+y^2=\frac{q^2}{p}\,x+\frac{p^2}{q}\,y .\] The line $AP$. Consider the line \[\ell:\quad \frac{q^2}{p}\,x+\frac{p^2}{q}\,y=q^2 .\] The point $A=(p,0)$ lies on $\ell$, since $\frac{q^2}{p}\cdot p=q^2$. The point $P$ lies on $\ell$, since \[\frac{q^2}{p}\cdot\frac{pq^2}{p^2+q^2}+\frac{p^2}{q}\cdot\frac{q^3}{p^2+q^2}=\frac{q^4+p^2q^2}{p^2+q^2}=q^2 .\] As $A\ne P$, the line $AP$ is $\ell$.

Conclusion. If $Z=(x,y)$ is one of the points $X,Y$, then $Z$ lies on $\Gamma$ and on $\ell$, so \[BZ^2=x^2+y^2=\frac{q^2}{p}\,x+\frac{p^2}{q}\,y=q^2=BC^2 .\] Thus $BX=BY=BC$, so $C$, $X$, $Y$ lie on the circle with centre $B$ and radius $BC$, and this is the circle through $C,X,Y$. Its centre is $B$.

Show solution 3 – Powers of A and P: line AP is the radical axis

Put $w=AB$ and $h=BC$, so $w>h>0$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle through $B,E,D$ and let $\omega$ be the circle with centre $B$ and radius $h$; it passes through $C$. For a point $Z$ we write $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(Z)$ and $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)$ for its powers with respect to the two circles; recall $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)=BZ^2-h^2$, and if a line through $Z$ meets a circle at $U,V$, the power of $Z$ is $ZU\cdot ZV$ when $Z$ is outside segment $UV$ and $-ZU\cdot ZV$ when $Z$ is inside it.

Locating $E$ and $P$. Let $\mathbf u=\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\mathbf v=\overrightarrow{BC}$, so $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0$, $|\mathbf u|=w$, $|\mathbf v|=h$ and $\overrightarrow{BD}=\mathbf u+\mathbf v$. The point $E$ of line $AB$ is $B+s\mathbf u$ with $(s\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=0$, that is, $sw^2-h^2=0$. So $s=h^2/w^2\in(0,1)$: the point $E$ lies strictly between $A$ and $B$, with $BE=h^2/w$ and $AE=w-h^2/w$. The point $P$ of line $BD$ is $B+t(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)$ with $(t(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=0$, that is, $t(w^2+h^2)=h^2$. So $t\in(0,1)$: the point $P$ lies strictly between $B$ and $D$, and \[BP\cdot BD=t\,BD^2=h^2 .\]

The power of $A$. Line $AB$ meets $\Gamma$ at $E$ and $B$, and $A$ lies outside segment $EB$. Hence \[\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(A)=AE\cdot AB=\Bigl(w-\frac{h^2}{w}\Bigr)w=w^2-h^2=AB^2-h^2=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(A).\]

The power of $P$. Line $BD$ meets $\Gamma$ at $B$ and $D$, and $P$ lies inside segment $BD$. Hence \[\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(P)=-PB\cdot PD=-BP\,(BD-BP)=BP^2-BP\cdot BD=BP^2-h^2=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(P).\]

The radical axis. The circles $\Gamma$ and $\omega$ are not concentric: $B$ is the centre of $\omega$ but lies on $\Gamma$. So the points having equal powers with respect to them form a line, their radical axis. The distinct points $A$ and $P$ both lie on it, so the radical axis is the line $AP$. Since $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(P)=-PB\cdot PD<0$, the point $P$ is inside $\Gamma$, so line $AP$ really meets $\Gamma$ at two distinct points $X$ and $Y$. Each of them has power $0$ with respect to $\Gamma$, hence power $0$ with respect to $\omega$: it lies on $\omega$. Thus $BX=BY=BC=h$.

The three points determine $\omega$. The point $C$ is not on line $AP$. Indeed, if it were, $P$ would be the intersection of $AC$ and $BD$, the centre of the rectangle, so $BP=\frac12BD$ and $BP\cdot BD=\frac12BD^2=\frac12(w^2+h^2)>h^2$, contradicting $BP\cdot BD=h^2$. Hence $C,X,Y$ are three different points of $\omega$, the circle through them is $\omega$, and its centre is $B$.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

In the game of the “Lake of the Ducks”, there are $n+1$ islands $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$, each with a different duck. Patricio plays by building $n$ bridges connecting pairs of islands, such that for each $i$ with $1\le i\le n$, there exists a unique $j<i$ such that there is a bridge joining island $a_i$ with island $a_j$.

An operation consists of choosing a bridge and swapping the ducks on its islands. A move consists of performing $n$ operations, one after another, using each of the $n$ bridges exactly once. Patricio wins the game if, for the construction he made, there exist two moves $A$ and $B$ such that applying $A$ twice in a row results in the same arrangement of ducks as applying move $B$ once.

Determine, for each $n$, all possible constructions with which Patricio wins the game. (Two constructions are equal if they join the same pairs of islands, regardless of the order in which the bridges were built.)

Hint 1: understand the problem
Orient each bridge from its larger label to its smaller label. What does following these arrows tell you about the connectedness of the bridge graph?
Hint 2: key idea
Treat a bridge swap as a transposition and prove by deleting a leaf that a move on a tree is a single cycle through all vertices. For one fixed bridge, split the tree at that bridge and count how many vertices a move can send from one side to the other. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Test the cycle structure of a squared move for the two parities of the vertex count. If a bridge has at least two vertices on each side, compare its crossing count for a move and for the square of that move using consecutive blocks in the cycle. Identify the remaining possible tree shapes, test which cycles their edge orders can realize, and impose the original label rule.
Common trap
Operations are performed in a specified order, so fix a convention for composing their permutations.
Show the answer

For odd $n$ there is no winning construction. For even $n$ there are exactly two: all bridges $a_0a_i$ ($1\le i\le n$), or the bridge $a_0a_1$ together with the bridges $a_1a_i$ ($2\le i\le n$); that is, the stars centred at $a_0$ or at $a_1$.

Show the solution – Moves are long cycles, and every bridge cuts a cycle into two arcs

Setting up. Islands are vertices and bridges are edges of a graph $T$ with $N=n+1$ vertices and $n$ edges. Every $a_i$ ($i\ge1$) is joined to some $a_j$ with $j<i$, so by induction every island is connected to $a_0$: $T$ is a connected graph with $N$ vertices and $N-1$ edges, i.e. a tree.

For a bridge $\{u,v\}$ let $(u\,v)$ be the transposition of the islands $u,v$. If a move uses the bridges in the order $\tau_1,\tau_2,\dots,\tau_n$, the duck that starts on island $x$ ends on island $\pi(x)$, where $\pi=\tau_n\circ\dots\circ\tau_2\circ\tau_1$. We identify the move with this permutation $\pi$ of the islands. Applying move $A$ twice moves the duck from $x$ to $A(A(x))$, so Patricio wins exactly when $A\circ A=B$ for two moves $A,B$. Below, “a product of the edges of a tree” means a composition of the transpositions of all its edges, each used once, in some order.

Lemma 1. For every tree with $N\ge2$ vertices, every product of its edges is an $N$-cycle (a single cycle through all vertices).

Proof. Induction on $N$. For $N=2$ the product is one transposition, a $2$-cycle. Let $N\ge3$, let $\ell$ be a leaf with neighbour $u$, and write the product as $\pi=\beta\circ(u\,\ell)\circ\alpha$, where $\alpha,\beta$ are products of the other edges (in the given orders); they do not move $\ell$. Since $\beta(\ell)=\ell$, we have $\beta\circ(u\,\ell)=(w\,\ell)\circ\beta$ with $w=\beta(u)$, so \[\pi=(w\,\ell)\circ\sigma,\qquad \sigma=\beta\circ\alpha .\] Here $\sigma$ is a product of the edges of the tree $T-\ell$, so by induction it is a cycle through all vertices except $\ell$, and it fixes $\ell$. Let $y$ be the vertex with $\sigma(y)=w$. Then $\pi(y)=\ell$, $\pi(\ell)=w$, and $\pi(z)=\sigma(z)$ for all other $z$. So $\pi$ is the cycle of $\sigma$ with $\ell$ inserted between $y$ and $w$: an $N$-cycle. $\square$

Lemma 2. Let $\pi$ be a product of the edges of a tree, let $\{u,v\}$ be an edge, and let $U\ni u$ and $V\ni v$ be the vertex sets of the two components left after deleting this edge. Then exactly one $x\in U$ satisfies $\pi(x)\in V$.

Proof. Write $\pi=\beta\circ(u\,v)\circ\alpha$, where $\alpha,\beta$ are products of the other edges. Every other edge has both ends in $U$ or both ends in $V$, so $\alpha$ and $\beta$ map $U$ onto $U$ and $V$ onto $V$. For $x\in U$ we have $\alpha(x)\in U$; the transposition $(u\,v)$ sends $\alpha(x)$ into $V$ exactly when $\alpha(x)=u$; and $\beta$ keeps the side. So $\pi(x)\in V$ if and only if $x=\alpha^{-1}(u)$, which is one element of $U$. $\square$

If $\pi=(c_0\,c_1\,\dots\,c_{N-1})$ is an $N$-cycle (with $\pi(c_i)=c_{i+1}$, indices modulo $N$), each maximal run of consecutive $c_i$ lying in $U$ ends with an index $i$ such that $c_i\in U$, $c_{i+1}\in V$. By Lemma 2 there is exactly one such index, so $U$ is a block of consecutive vertices $c_i,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{i+s-1}$ of the cycle.

Odd $n$: Patricio never wins. Then $N$ is even. Any move $A=(c_0\,c_1\,\dots\,c_{N-1})$ is an $N$-cycle by Lemma 1, and $A\circ A$ sends $c_i$ to $c_{i+2}$, so it splits into the two cycles $(c_0\,c_2\,\dots\,c_{N-2})$ and $(c_1\,c_3\,\dots\,c_{N-1})$. But every move $B$ is an $N$-cycle, so $A\circ A\neq B$.

Even $n$, a star: Patricio wins. Let all bridges join a centre $c$ to the other islands. Given any $N$-cycle $(c\;y_1\;y_2\;\dots\;y_n)$, use the bridges in the order $\{c,y_1\},\{c,y_2\},\dots,\{c,y_n\}$. The duck on $c$ goes to $y_1$ at the first operation and is never moved again; the duck on $y_j$ stays until operation $j$, goes to $c$, and operation $j+1$ (if $j<n$) takes it to $y_{j+1}$, where it stays. So the move is exactly the cycle $c\to y_1\to y_2\to\dots\to y_n\to c$: every $N$-cycle is a move. Now take any move $A=(c_0\,c_1\,\dots\,c_{N-1})$. Since $N$ is odd, $2$ is invertible modulo $N$, so $c_0,c_2,c_4,\dots,c_{2(N-1)}$ (indices mod $N$) are all different, and $A\circ A$ is the $N$-cycle $(c_0\,c_2\,c_4\,\dots)$. Hence $A\circ A$ is a move $B$, and Patricio wins.

Even $n$, not a star: Patricio loses. Take a longest path $x_0x_1\dots x_L$ in $T$. If $L\le2$, then $T$ is a star: for $L=1$ it is a single edge, and for $L=2$ a vertex at distance at least $2$ from $x_1$ would, through its neighbour on the way to $x_1$, extend to a path with at least $3$ edges. Hence $L\ge3$. The edge $\{x_1,x_2\}$ splits the tree into parts $U\ni x_1,x_0$ and $V\ni x_2,x_3$, each with at least $2$ vertices. Suppose $A\circ A=B$ for moves $A,B$, and write $A=(c_0\,c_1\,\dots\,c_{N-1})$. By Lemma 2 applied to $A$, the set $U$ is a block $\{c_i,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{i+s-1}\}$ with $2\le s\le N-2$, and $V$ is the rest. The move $B=A\circ A$ sends $c_j$ to $c_{j+2}$. Then both $c_{i+s-2}$ and $c_{i+s-1}$ lie in $U$ (as $s\ge2$), and their images $c_{i+s}$ and $c_{i+s+1}$ lie in $V$ (as $N-s\ge2$). So two elements of $U$ are sent into $V$ by the move $B$, contradicting Lemma 2 for $B$.

Which stars are allowed. In a star with centre $a_c$, every island $a_i$ with $i\ge1$, $i\ne c$, has $a_c$ as its only neighbour, and the rule requires that neighbour to have a smaller index: $c<i$. So $c=0$ or $c=1$, and both work: the bridges $a_0a_1,a_0a_2,\dots,a_0a_n$, or $a_0a_1,a_1a_2,\dots,a_1a_n$ (for $n=2$ these are the two possible constructions).

Answer. For odd $n$ no construction wins. For even $n$ Patricio wins exactly with the two stars: all bridges from $a_0$, or the bridge $a_0a_1$ and all bridges from $a_1$ to $a_2,\dots,a_n$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots$ be a sequence of positive integers such that $a_1=1$ and, for each integer $n\ge 1$, \[\bigl((a_{n+1}-a_1)^2+1\bigr)\bigl((a_{n+1}-a_2)^2+1\bigr)\cdots\bigl((a_{n+1}-a_n)^2+1\bigr)=(a_1^2+1)(a_2^2+1)\cdots(a_n^2+1).\] Determine all possible values of $a_{2026}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compute the constraint on the next term after a few short initial segments. In each case, compare its distances to the previous terms with the previous terms themselves.
Hint 2: key idea
Use induction, assuming the earlier entries are consecutive positive integers. Compare the products for a candidate after the whole initial segment and for a candidate inside it, using that $d^2+1$ increases with nonnegative integer $d$. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Test the first integer beyond the initial segment by reindexing the factors, then compare every larger candidate factor by factor. For an internal candidate, split the distances on its left and right into two initial products and cancel a common product with the target. Pair the remaining factors with the shorter initial product, keeping track of the extra positive factor.
Common trap
The factor from a candidate equal to a previous term is $1$, and it must be included in the comparison.
Show the answer

$a_{2026}=2026$

Show solution 1 – Induction with a factor-by-factor comparison

We prove that $a_n=n$ for every $n$; then the only possible value is $a_{2026}=\boxed{2026}$. For $r\ge0$ write $Q_r=\prod_{d=1}^{r}(d^2+1)$ (with $Q_0=1$).

Clearly $a_1=1$. Suppose $a_j=j$ for $1\le j\le n$. Then $x=a_{n+1}$ is a positive integer with $g(x)=C$, where \[g(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl((x-j)^2+1\bigr),\qquad C=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(j^2+1)=Q_n .\] $x=n+1$ works: $g(n+1)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl((n+1-j)^2+1\bigr)=\prod_{d=1}^{n}(d^2+1)=C$.

$x\ge n+2$ is impossible: for each $j\le n$ we have $x-j>n+1-j\ge1$, so $(x-j)^2+1>(n+1-j)^2+1$, and multiplying, $g(x)>g(n+1)=C$.

$1\le x\le n$ is impossible: the factor with $j=x$ equals $1$, the factors with $j<x$ are $d^2+1$ for $d=x-j=1,\dots,x-1$, and those with $j>x$ are $d^2+1$ for $d=j-x=1,\dots,n-x$. So $g(x)=Q_{x-1}\,Q_{n-x}$, while \[C=Q_n=Q_{n-x}\prod_{d=n-x+1}^{n}(d^2+1).\] The last product has $x$ factors. Its first factor is at least $2$, and its other factors are $(n-x+1+e)^2+1$ for $e=1,\dots,x-1$, each larger than $e^2+1$. Hence $\prod_{d=n-x+1}^{n}(d^2+1)\ge 2Q_{x-1}>Q_{x-1}$, and $C>g(x)$.

So $a_{n+1}=n+1$, which completes the induction. Conversely, the computation $g(n+1)=C$ shows that $a_n=n$ satisfies the condition for every $n$, so such a sequence exists, and $a_{2026}=\boxed{2026}$.

Show solution 2 – The ratio of consecutive values

We show by induction that $a_n=n$ for all $n$. Clearly $a_1=1$. Suppose $a_j=j$ for $1\le j\le n$, and let \[g(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl((x-j)^2+1\bigr),\qquad C=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(j^2+1).\] Then $a_{n+1}$ is a positive integer $x$ with $g(x)=C$. All factors of $g$ are positive. Substituting $i=j-1$ in $g(x+1)$ gives $g(x+1)=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl((x-i)^2+1\bigr)$, which shares the factors $i=1,\dots,n-1$ with $g(x)$. Therefore \[\frac{g(x+1)}{g(x)}=\frac{x^2+1}{(x-n)^2+1},\] and since $x^2-(x-n)^2=n(2x-n)$, we get $g(x+1)<g(x)$ for $x<\frac n2$ and $g(x+1)>g(x)$ for $x>\frac n2$.

Moreover $g(0)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(j^2+1)=C$ and $g(n+1)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl((n+1-j)^2+1\bigr)=C$. On the integers:

  • $g(0)>g(1)>\dots>g(\lceil n/2\rceil)$ (each step starts at an integer $x<\frac n2$), so $g(x)<C$ for $1\le x\le\lceil n/2\rceil$;

  • $g(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1)<\dots<g(n)<g(n+1)<g(n+2)<\dots$ (each step starts at an integer $x>\frac n2$), so $g(x)<C$ for $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\le x\le n$ and $g(x)>C$ for $x\ge n+2$.

Since $\lceil n/2\rceil\ge\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, these ranges cover $1\le x\le n$. Thus the only positive integer with $g(x)=C$ is $x=n+1$, and $a_{n+1}=n+1$.

The sequence $a_n=n$ does satisfy the condition (that is the equality $g(n+1)=C$ above), so it is the unique such sequence and $a_{2026}=\boxed{2026}$.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $m$ and $k$ be positive integers. There is a $k\times 2m$ grid in which the top row has $m$ blue grasshoppers followed by $m$ red grasshoppers, one in each cell. Each grasshopper can jump to any of the cells immediately below it (the cell in the same column or one of the two cells at its sides, if they exist), as shown below.

After some jumps, in the bottom row there are $m$ red grasshoppers followed by $m$ blue grasshoppers, one in each cell. It is also known that every cell of the grid was touched by some grasshopper. Determine the smallest possible value of $k$ for each $m$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Track the column of one grasshopper as it descends. How far can it move sideways between two fixed rows?
Hint 2: key idea
First check when a red grasshopper can reach column $1$. Then use the requirement that every cell is touched to examine the occupant of column $1$ in row $m$, and track how far that occupant must still travel to its final color block. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Combine the travel before and after that cell to get a lower bound on the number of rows. For construction, try exchanging each disjoint adjacent blue-red pair while all other grasshoppers jump straight down, following how an alternating middle block grows and later shrinks. Verify one occupant per cell in every row and count the required transitions.
Common trap
The number of jumps between two rows is the difference of their row indices.
Show the answer

$k=2m$

Show solution 1 – One corner cell and a construction

Number the rows $1,\dots,k$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,2m$ from left to right. Each grasshopper starts in row $1$, ends in row $k$, and each jump takes it to the next row and changes its column by at most $1$. At the end, the blue grasshoppers are in columns $m+1,\dots,2m$ and the red ones in columns $1,\dots,m$. We show that the smallest $k$ is $2m$.

Lower bound. If $k\le m$, the grasshopper in the bottom-left cell $(k,1)$ at the end is red; it started in a column $\ge m+1$ and made $k-1\le m-1$ jumps, so its final column is at least $m+1-(k-1)\ge2$, a contradiction. So $k\ge m+1$, and the cell in row $m$, column $1$ exists. Some grasshopper $G$ touched this cell.

  • $G$ is not red: a red grasshopper starts in a column $\ge m+1$, and after the $m-1$ jumps that bring it to row $m$ it is in a column $\ge m+1-(m-1)=2$.

  • So $G$ is blue. From column $1$ in row $m$ it makes $k-m$ more jumps and ends in a column $\ge m+1$. Hence $1+(k-m)\ge m+1$, that is, $k\ge 2m$.

Construction for $k=2m$. Write B for blue and R for red, and describe each row by its colours from left to right. We want the rows $1,2,\dots,2m$ to be, for $t=0,1,\dots,2m-1$ (row $t+1$): \[\text{row } t+1=\begin{cases}\mathrm{B}^{\,m-t}\,(\mathrm{RB})^{t}\,\mathrm{R}^{\,m-t}, & 0\le t\le m,\\[2pt] \mathrm{R}^{\,t-m}\,(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t}\,\mathrm{B}^{\,t-m}, & m\le t\le 2m-1\end{cases}\] (both formulas give $(\mathrm{RB})^m$ for $t=m$). Row $1$ is $\mathrm{B}^m\mathrm{R}^m$ and row $2m$ is $\mathrm{R}^m\mathrm{B}^m$. To go from row $t+1$ to row $t+2$:

  • if $t<m$, write row $t+1$ as $\mathrm{B}^{m-t-1}\cdot(\mathrm{BR})^{t+1}\cdot\mathrm{R}^{m-t-1}$; in each of the $t+1$ adjacent pairs BR the two grasshoppers exchange columns (the blue one jumps down-right, the red one down-left), and every other grasshopper jumps straight down. The result is $\mathrm{B}^{m-t-1}(\mathrm{RB})^{t+1}\mathrm{R}^{m-t-1}$, which is row $t+2$;

  • if $m\le t\le 2m-2$, write row $t+1$ as $\mathrm{R}^{t-m}\cdot\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{BR})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathrm{B}^{t-m}$ and exchange the two grasshoppers of each pair BR in the middle; the result is $\mathrm{R}^{t-m}\cdot\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathrm{B}^{t-m}=\mathrm{R}^{t-m+1}(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}^{t-m+1}$, which is row $t+2$.

Every jump goes to a cell directly below or diagonally below, and in every row the $2m$ grasshoppers stand in $2m$ different cells, so every cell of the $2m\times 2m$ grid is touched. (The figure shows $m=3$; the shaded cell is the cell in row $m$, column $1$.) Hence the smallest value is $k=\boxed{2m}$.

Show solution 2 – Rows are permutations: tracking the blue grasshoppers in order

Number the rows $1,\dots,k$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,2m$ from left to right. Each grasshopper starts in row $1$, ends in row $k$, and each jump takes it to the next row, changing its column by at most $1$. We show that the smallest $k$ is $2m$.

Each row is filled exactly once. Every grasshopper touches exactly one cell of each row. A row has $2m$ cells, all touched, and there are $2m$ grasshoppers, so each cell of a row is touched by exactly one grasshopper. For a row $r<k$, let $f(c)$ be the column in row $r+1$ of the grasshopper that was in column $c$ of row $r$. Then $f$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,2m\}$ with $|f(c)-c|\le1$.

Claim: if $f(c)=c+1$, then $f(c+1)=c$. The set $f(\{1,\dots,c\})$ lies in $\{1,\dots,c+1\}$, has $c$ elements and contains $c+1$, so it misses exactly one element of $\{1,\dots,c\}$. That element is $f(c')$ for some $c'>c$, and since $|f(c')-c'|\le1$ we must have $c'=c+1$ and $f(c+1)=c$. $\square$

So between consecutive rows, some disjoint pairs of neighbouring grasshoppers exchange columns and all others go straight down.

Blue positions. Let $b_1(r)<b_2(r)<\dots<b_m(r)$ be the columns of the blue grasshoppers in row $r$; so $b_j(1)=j$, and $b_1(k)=m+1$ because at the end the blues fill columns $m+1,\dots,2m$.

  • (i) $b_j(r+1)\le b_j(r)+1$: the blues in columns $b_1(r),\dots,b_j(r)$ go to $j$ different columns, all at most $b_j(r)+1$.

  • (ii) If $b_{j+1}(r)=b_j(r)+1$, then $b_j(r+1)\le b_j(r)$. Indeed, put $c=b_j(r)$. The blues in columns $b_1(r),\dots,b_{j-1}(r)$ go to $j-1$ different columns at most $b_{j-1}(r)+1\le c$. If the blue in column $c$ does not move right, it gives a $j$-th blue in a column $\le c$. If it moves to $c+1$, then by the claim the blue in column $c+1$ moves to $c$, again giving a $j$-th blue in a column $\le c$. Either way $b_j(r+1)\le c$.

A speed limit. Let $F_j(r)=\max(j,\ r-1+2j-m)$. We prove $b_j(r)\le F_j(r)$ for all $j$ and $r$, by induction on $r$. For $r=1$, $b_j(1)=j\le F_j(1)$. Assume it for row $r$ and all $j$. Note that $F_j(r+1)=F_j(r)+1$, except when $r-1+2j-m<j$, i.e. $r\le m-j$, in which case $F_j(r+1)=F_j(r)=j$.

  • If $F_j(r+1)=F_j(r)+1$, then by (i) $b_j(r+1)\le b_j(r)+1\le F_j(r+1)$.

  • If $r\le m-j$ and $b_j(r)<j$, then $b_j(r+1)\le b_j(r)+1\le j=F_j(r+1)$.

  • If $r\le m-j$ and $b_j(r)=j$: then $j<m$, and $j=b_j(r)<b_{j+1}(r)\le F_{j+1}(r)=\max(j+1,\ r+1+2j-m)=j+1$ (because $r\le m-j$). So $b_{j+1}(r)=b_j(r)+1$, and (ii) gives $b_j(r+1)\le j=F_j(r+1)$.

Lower bound. For $j=1$ and $r=k$: $m+1=b_1(k)\le\max(1,\ k+1-m)$, so $k+1-m\ge m+1$, that is, $k\ge 2m$.

Construction for $k=2m$. Write B for blue and R for red, and describe each row by its colours from left to right. We want the rows $1,2,\dots,2m$ to be, for $t=0,1,\dots,2m-1$ (row $t+1$): \[\text{row } t+1=\begin{cases}\mathrm{B}^{\,m-t}\,(\mathrm{RB})^{t}\,\mathrm{R}^{\,m-t}, & 0\le t\le m,\\[2pt] \mathrm{R}^{\,t-m}\,(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t}\,\mathrm{B}^{\,t-m}, & m\le t\le 2m-1\end{cases}\] (both formulas give $(\mathrm{RB})^m$ for $t=m$). Row $1$ is $\mathrm{B}^m\mathrm{R}^m$ and row $2m$ is $\mathrm{R}^m\mathrm{B}^m$. To go from row $t+1$ to row $t+2$:

  • if $t<m$, write row $t+1$ as $\mathrm{B}^{m-t-1}\cdot(\mathrm{BR})^{t+1}\cdot\mathrm{R}^{m-t-1}$; in each of the $t+1$ adjacent pairs BR the two grasshoppers exchange columns (the blue one jumps down-right, the red one down-left), and every other grasshopper jumps straight down. The result is $\mathrm{B}^{m-t-1}(\mathrm{RB})^{t+1}\mathrm{R}^{m-t-1}$, which is row $t+2$;

  • if $m\le t\le 2m-2$, write row $t+1$ as $\mathrm{R}^{t-m}\cdot\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{BR})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathrm{B}^{t-m}$ and exchange the two grasshoppers of each pair BR in the middle; the result is $\mathrm{R}^{t-m}\cdot\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}\cdot\mathrm{B}^{t-m}=\mathrm{R}^{t-m+1}(\mathrm{RB})^{2m-t-1}\mathrm{B}^{t-m+1}$, which is row $t+2$.

Every jump goes to a cell directly below or diagonally below, and in every row the $2m$ grasshoppers stand in $2m$ different cells, so every cell of the $2m\times 2m$ grid is touched. (The figure shows $m=3$.) Hence the smallest value is $k=\boxed{2m}$.

Show solution 3 – The middle cut can be crossed only every other step

Number the rows $1,\dots,k$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,2m$ from left to right. Each grasshopper jumps from each row to the next, changing its column by at most $1$; so it touches exactly one cell in each row. We show that the smallest $k$ is $2m$.

Each transition is a set of disjoint neighbour swaps. A row has $2m$ cells, all touched, and there are $2m$ grasshoppers, each touching one cell of the row; so every row holds each grasshopper in exactly one cell. Fix a row $r<k$ and let $f(c)$ be the column in row $r+1$ of the grasshopper standing in column $c$ of row $r$. Then $f$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,2m\}$ with $|f(c)-c|\le1$. Suppose $f$ is not the identity and let $c$ be the smallest column with $f(c)\ne c$. The columns $1,\dots,c-1$ are fixed, so $f(c)\ne c-1$, and therefore $f(c)=c+1$. Column $c$ of row $r+1$ must be reached by someone; the columns before $c$ are taken by their own grasshoppers, so it is reached from column $c+1$: $f(c+1)=c$. Removing columns $c,c+1$ and repeating the argument from the left, we see that $f$ exchanges some disjoint pairs of neighbouring columns and fixes all others.

Lower bound. Call the columns $1,\dots,m$ the left half and let $\rho(r)$ be the number of red grasshoppers in the left half in row $r$. Then $\rho(1)=0$ and $\rho(k)=m$. A neighbour swap changes $\rho$ only if it exchanges the columns $m$ and $m+1$ and the two grasshoppers there have different colours. If, in row $r$, column $m$ is blue and column $m+1$ is red and they swap, then $\rho$ grows by $1$ (call this a forward crossing); if column $m$ is red and column $m+1$ blue and they swap, $\rho$ drops by $1$. In every other case $\rho(r+1)=\rho(r)$. Hence at least $m$ of the $k-1$ transitions are forward crossings. After a forward crossing from row $r$ to row $r+1$, column $m$ of row $r+1$ holds a red grasshopper and column $m+1$ a blue one, so the transition from row $r+1$ to row $r+2$ is not a forward crossing. Thus no two forward crossings occur in consecutive transitions, and $m$ of them need at least $2m-1$ transitions: $k-1\ge 2m-1$, that is, $k\ge 2m$.

A schedule with $k=2m$. Name the blue grasshoppers $B_1,\dots,B_m$ and the red ones $R_1,\dots,R_m$ from left to right, so in row $1$ the grasshopper $B_i$ is in column $i$ and $R_j$ in column $m+j$. The transitions are numbered $t=1,\dots,2m-1$ (transition $t$ leads from row $t$ to row $t+1$). We decree that $B_i$ and $R_j$ exchange columns at transition \[t=m-i+j ,\] and that, at each transition, every grasshopper not taking part in a scheduled exchange jumps straight down. For row $r$ define \[\beta_i(r)=i+\min\bigl(m,\max(0,\,r-1-m+i)\bigr),\qquad \gamma_j(r)=m+j-\min\bigl(m,\max(0,\,r-j)\bigr).\] These count the scheduled exchanges before row $r$: $B_i$ is scheduled at the transitions $m-i+1,\dots,2m-i$ (one for each $j$), so before row $r$ it has made $\min(m,\max(0,r-1-m+i))$ exchanges, each moving it one column to the right; $R_j$ is scheduled at the transitions $j,\dots,m-1+j$, so it has made $\min(m,\max(0,r-j))$ exchanges, each moving it one column to the left.

Claim: for $r=1,\dots,2m$, in row $r$ every $B_i$ stands in column $\beta_i(r)$, every $R_j$ in column $\gamma_j(r)$, and these $2m$ columns are all different. For $r=1$ this is the starting position. Assume it for row $t\le 2m-1$. If $B_i$ and $R_j$ are scheduled at transition $t=m-i+j$, then $r=t$ gives $\beta_i(t)=i+\min(m,j-1)=i+j-1$ and $\gamma_j(t)=m+j-\min(m,m-i)=i+j$, so they stand in neighbouring columns $i+j-1$ and $i+j$, the blue one on the left. For a fixed $t$ the scheduled pairs are $(i,\ i+t-m)$; increasing $i$ by $1$ moves the pair $\{i+j-1,i+j\}$ two columns to the right, so these pairs of columns are disjoint. Hence transition $t$ is a legal set of disjoint neighbour swaps (each blue jumps down-right, each red down-left, everyone else straight down), it keeps the columns of row $t+1$ all different, and every grasshopper's number of exchanges goes up by one exactly when it was scheduled, which gives the formulas for row $t+1$.

In row $2m$ we get $\beta_i(2m)=i+\min(m,\,m-1+i)=m+i$ and $\gamma_j(2m)=m+j-\min(m,\,2m-j)=j$. So the bottom row holds $R_1,\dots,R_m$ in columns $1,\dots,m$ and $B_1,\dots,B_m$ in columns $m+1,\dots,2m$: $m$ red grasshoppers followed by $m$ blue ones. Every jump is straight down or diagonal, and every row is completely filled, so every cell of the $2m\times 2m$ grid is touched.

Hence the smallest value is $k=\boxed{2m}$.

Problem 6

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

Two distinct positive integers $a,b$ are called friends if \[a+b+1\mid ab-1.\] Prove that if an integer has exactly one friend, then that friend has an even number of friends.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For fixed $a$, set $d=a+b+1$ and express $b$ in terms of $d$. Reduce $ab-1$ modulo $d$ to find a condition involving $a$ alone.
Hint 2: key idea
Associate friends of $a$ with sufficiently large divisors of $a^2+a+1$. Pair divisors on either side of its square root, and check which divisor would incorrectly produce $b=a$. (LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Treat $a=1$ separately, then use the divisor count to translate having one friend into a restriction on $a^2+a+1$ and identify that friend. Factor the analogous expression for the friend's own divisor count and check coprimality of its factors. Locate the remaining factor between consecutive squares to control the parity of its divisor count.
Common trap
A number is not its own friend, so the divisor producing $b=a$ must be excluded.
Show the solution – Friends of a correspond to large divisors of $a^2+a+1$

Friendship is symmetric. Throughout, $\tau(N)$ is the number of positive divisors of $N$, and $N_a=a^2+a+1$.

Step 1: reformulation. For positive integers $a\ne b$ put $d=a+b+1$. Modulo $d$ we have $b\equiv-(a+1)$, so \[ab-1\equiv -a(a+1)-1=-(a^2+a+1)\pmod d .\] Hence $a$ and $b$ are friends if and only if $a+b+1\mid N_a$. Setting $b=d-a-1$, the friends of $a$ correspond one-to-one to the divisors $d$ of $N_a$ with $d\ge a+2$ (this makes $b\ge1$) and $d\ne 2a+1$ (this makes $b\ne a$).

Step 2: counting friends. The divisors of $N_a$ come in pairs $\{d,\,N_a/d\}$. Since $a^2<N_a<(a+1)^2$, $N_a$ is not a square, so $d\ne N_a/d$; the two members cannot both be $\le a$ (their product would be $\le a^2$) nor both be $\ge a+1$ (their product would be $\ge(a+1)^2$). Also $a+1\nmid N_a$, because $N_a=a(a+1)+1$. So in each pair exactly one member is $\ge a+2$, and $N_a$ has exactly $\tau(N_a)/2$ divisors $\ge a+2$. Finally, $2a+1\mid N_a$ would give $2a+1\mid 4N_a=(2a+1)^2+3$, so $2a+1\mid3$ and $a=1$. Therefore \[\#\{\text{friends of }a\}=\frac{\tau(a^2+a+1)}{2}\quad\text{for } a\ge2,\] while $a=1$ has no friends ($N_1=3$, and its only divisor $\ge3$ is $3=2\cdot1+1$).

Step 3: the unique friend. Let $a$ have exactly one friend $b$. Then $a\ge2$ and $\tau(N_a)=2$, so $N_a=p$ is a prime. The only divisor of $p$ that is $\ge a+2$ is $p$ itself, so $b=p-a-1=a^2$.

Step 4: friends of $b=a^2$. We have \[N_b=a^4+a^2+1=(a^2+a+1)(a^2-a+1)=p\,M,\qquad M=a^2-a+1 .\] A common divisor of $p$ and $M$ divides $p-M=2a$; it is odd (as $p=a(a+1)+1$ is odd), so it divides $a$, hence it divides $p-a(a+1)=1$. So $\gcd(p,M)=1$ and $\tau(N_b)=\tau(p)\,\tau(M)=2\tau(M)$. Since $b=a^2\ge4$, Step 2 gives \[\#\{\text{friends of }b\}=\frac{\tau(N_b)}{2}=\tau(M).\] For $a\ge2$ we have $(a-1)^2<a^2-a+1<a^2$ (the differences are $a>0$ and $a-1>0$), so $M$ is not a perfect square. Its divisors therefore split into pairs $\{e,M/e\}$ with $e\ne M/e$, and $\tau(M)$ is even. Hence $b$ has an even number of friends.
