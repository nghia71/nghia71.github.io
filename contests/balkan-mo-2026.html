Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

A set $S$ of positive real numbers is called Aristotelian if for any $x,y,z\in S$ satisfying $x<y<z$, we have \[\frac{z-x}{y}\in S.\] Find all integers $n\ge 4$ for which there exists an Aristotelian set with exactly $n$ elements.

Show the answer

$n=4$ only

Show the solution

Solution (Two families of quotients)

$n=4$ works. Let $t\approx0.7549$ be the positive root of $t^3+t^2=1$, i.e. $1-t^2=t^3$, and $S=\{t^3,t^2,t,1\}$. The four triples give \[\frac{t-t^3}{t^2}=\frac{1-t^2}{t}=t^2,\quad \frac{1-t^3}{t^2}=\frac{t^2}{t^2}=1,\quad\frac{1-t^3}{t}=t,\quad\frac{1-t^2}{t}=t^2,\] all in $S$ (here $1-t^3=t^2$ because $t^3+t^2=1$). So $S$ is Aristotelian.

Remark. There is also a rational example, $S=\{\frac59,\frac23,1,\frac{11}9\}$: the four triples give \[\frac{1-\frac59}{\frac23}=\frac23,\quad\frac{\frac{11}9-\frac59}{\frac23}=1,\quad\frac{\frac{11}9-\frac59}{1}=\frac23,\quad\frac{\frac{11}9-\frac23}{1}=\frac59.\]

$n\ge5$ is impossible. Let $S=\{s_1<s_2<\dots<s_n\}$ be Aristotelian, $n\ge5$. Consider the two families \[F_1=\Bigl\{\frac{s_n-s_1}{s_j}:2\le j\le n-1\Bigr\},\qquad F_3=\Bigl\{\frac{s_n-s_i}{s_{n-1}}:1\le i\le n-2\Bigr\}.\] Each consists of $n-2$ distinct elements of $S$ ($F_1$ is decreasing in $j$, $F_3$ decreasing in $i$). The smallest element of $F_1$ and the largest element of $F_3$ are the same number $\frac{s_n-s_1}{s_{n-1}}=:s_a$; let $s_b=\frac{s_n-s_1}{s_2}$ be the largest element of $F_1$. Since $F_3\subseteq\{s_1,\dots,s_a\}$, we get $a\ge n-2$; since $F_1\subseteq\{s_a,\dots,s_b\}$ and $b\le n$, we get $n-a+1\ge n-2$, i.e. $a\le3$. Hence $n\le5$.

Let $n=5$. Then $a=3$, $b=5$, $F_1=\{s_3,s_4,s_5\}$ and $F_3=\{s_1,s_2,s_3\}$, and matching the orders ($F_1$ is decreasing in $j$, so $j=2,3,4$ give $s_5,s_4,s_3$; $F_3$ is decreasing in $i$, so $i=1,2,3$ give $s_3,s_2,s_1$): \[\frac{s_5-s_1}{s_2}=s_5,\quad\frac{s_5-s_1}{s_3}=s_4,\quad\frac{s_5-s_1}{s_4}=s_3,\quad\frac{s_5-s_2}{s_4}=s_2,\quad\frac{s_5-s_3}{s_4}=s_1.\] Subtracting the last three in pairs: $s_2-s_1=s_4(s_3-s_2)$ and $s_3-s_2=s_4(s_2-s_1)$, so $s_2-s_1=s_4^2(s_2-s_1)$ and $s_4=1$. Then $s_1,s_2,s_3$ is an arithmetic progression ($s_1+s_3=2s_2$), $s_5=s_1+s_3=2s_2$ and $s_3=s_2s_5$.

The triple $(s_2,s_3,s_5)$ gives $\frac{s_5-s_2}{s_3}=\frac{s_2}{s_3}\in S$, a number less than $1=s_4$. It is not $s_2$ (as $s_3\ne1$) and not $s_1$ (as $s_1s_3<s_2^2<s_2$ by AM–GM and $s_2<1$), so $s_2/s_3=s_3$, i.e. $s_2=s_3^2$. With $w=s_3$: $s_5=s_3/s_2=1/w$ and $s_5=2s_2=2w^2$, so $w^3=\frac12$, and $S=\{2w^2-w,\ w^2,\ w,\ 1,\ 2w^2\}$. Since $0.79^3<\frac12<0.8^3$, we have $0.79<w<0.8$.

Finally the triple $(s_1,s_2,s_3)$ gives $v=\frac{s_3-s_1}{s_2}=\frac{2w-2w^2}{w^2}=\frac2w-2=4w^2-2$ (using $\frac1w=2w^2$). But \[v-s_1=2w^2+w-2>2(0.79)^2+0.79-2>0,\qquad s_2-v=2-3w^2>2-3(0.8)^2>0,\] because $2w^2+w-2$ is increasing and $2-3w^2$ is decreasing in $w>0$. So $s_1<v<s_2$ and $v\notin S$, a contradiction. Hence no Aristotelian set has $5$ or more elements, and the answer is \[\boxed{n=4}\]

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A $2n\times 2n$ board is tiled with $2\times 1$ and $1\times 2$ dominoes. To pivot a domino is to select one of its two unit squares and rotate the entire domino by $90^\circ$ clockwise, $90^\circ$ anticlockwise, or $180^\circ$ about the centre of that unit square. Prove that it is always possible to simultaneously pivot every domino such that, after all pivots have been performed, the dominoes still tile the board.

Show the solution

Solution (Keep the black square)

Colour the board like a chessboard. Every domino covers one black and one white square.

Pivots and tilings. Pivoting a domino about one of its squares keeps that square and moves the other square to one of the other three neighbours of the kept square (a $90^\circ$ turn gives the two side neighbours, a $180^\circ$ turn the opposite one). So it suffices to find a second tiling $T'$ of the board with no domino in common with the given tiling $T$: then pivot every domino of $T$ about its black square $s$, so that it becomes the domino of $T'$ containing $s$. This is a legal pivot (the new partner is a neighbour of $s$ different from the old one, because $T$ and $T'$ share no domino), and since every domino of $T'$ contains exactly one black square, the new dominoes are exactly the dominoes of $T'$, which tile the board.

Construction of $T'$. Cut the $2n\times2n$ board into $n^2$ blocks of $2\times2$ squares. A $2\times2$ block cannot contain both a horizontal and a vertical domino of $T$ (two such dominoes inside four squares would overlap). Tile each block by two dominoes of the orientation that $T$ does not use inside this block (either orientation if $T$ has no domino inside it). The resulting tiling $T'$ shares no domino with $T$: every domino of $T'$ lies inside a block and has an orientation that no domino of $T$ inside that block has. This finishes the proof.

Remark (another way to choose the pivots). The dominoes of $T$ and of $T'$ together form disjoint cycles $s_1s_2\dots s_{2r}$ whose edges alternate between $T$ ($s_1s_2$, $s_3s_4$, …) and $T'$ ($s_2s_3$, …, $s_{2r}s_1$). Pivoting every domino $s_{2i-1}s_{2i}$ about $s_{2i}$ also turns $T$ into $T'$; on each cycle the squares $s_{2i}$ have one colour, so this is the same construction with the colour chosen cycle by cycle. It shows a little more: for every tiling $T''$ sharing no domino with $T$, some simultaneous pivot of all dominoes of $T$ produces exactly $T''$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram with $\angle DAB<90^\circ$ and $AB<AD$. Let $H$ be the orthocentre of $\triangle BCD$ and $H'$ be the reflection of $H$ over line $BD$. Line $AH$ intersects the lines $BD$, $CD$, and $BC$ at $E$, $F$, and $G$ respectively. Prove that the circumcircles of $\triangle HEH'$ and $\triangle CFG$ are tangent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The Miquel point lies on line $C^*E$)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(p,q)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circle $BCD$, $C^*$ the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite to $C$, and $\ell$ the line $AH$ (which contains $E,F,G$).

Step 1: $\ell\parallel CC^*$, and $H'\in\omega$, $H'\in CH$. Since $CC^*$ is a diameter, $C^*D\perp CD$ and $C^*B\perp CB$; also $BH\perp CD$ and $DH\perp BC$. So $BH\parallel C^*D$ and $DH\parallel C^*B$: $BHDC^*$ is a parallelogram, and the midpoint of $HC^*$ is the midpoint of $BD$, which is also the midpoint of $AC$. Hence $AHCC^*$ is a parallelogram and $\ell=AH\parallel CC^*$. Next, $CH\perp BD$, so the reflection in $BD$ maps line $CH$ to itself and $H'\in CH$; and the reflection of the orthocentre in a side lies on the circumcircle, so $H'\in\omega$.

Degenerate positions. (a) $\ell\ne CC^*$; this is where $AB<AD$ is used. Otherwise $A$, $C$ and the centre $Z$ of $\omega$ are collinear. $Z$ is not the midpoint of $BD$ (else $\angle BCD=90^\circ$, but $\angle BCD=\angle DAB<90^\circ$), and $Z$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BD$, so line $AC$, which passes through the midpoint of $BD$, would be this bisector; then $CB=CD$, i.e. $AD=AB$. Hence $C\notin\ell$, so $F,G\ne C$ and $C^*\notin\ell$ (in particular $C^*\ne E$). (b) $\ell$ does not pass through $B$ or $D$. If $B\in\ell$, then $\ell=AB\parallel CD$, so $CC^*=CD$, $\angle CBD=90^\circ$, and in triangle $ABD$ (right-angled at $D$) $AB>AD$, a contradiction. If $D\in\ell$, then similarly $\angle BDC=90^\circ$ and $H=E=H'=D$, so the triangle $HEH'$ of the statement does not exist. (c) The statement also presupposes that $\ell$ meets $BD$, i.e. $\ell\nparallel BD$; this fails for some shapes (for example $AB=1$, $AD=\sqrt2$, $\angle DAB=\arccos\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}$), and then there is nothing to prove, so we assume it.

Step 2: the point $M$. Let $M$ be the second intersection of line $C^*E$ with $\omega$ ($M=C^*$ if this line is tangent to $\omega$). Then $M\ne C$, since $E\notin CC^*$ by (a); and $M\notin\ell$, since line $C^*E$ meets $\ell$ only at $E$, and $E\in\omega$ would put $E$ in $\omega\cap BD=\{B,D\}$, excluded by (b). If $M=C^*$, the line $MC^*$ below means the tangent at $C^*$, and the inscribed angle steps hold by the tangent–chord theorem. Since $CC^*$ is a diameter, $MC\perp ME$. Then, using $C^*\in ME$, inscribed angles in $\omega$ and $CC^*\parallel\ell$, \[\measuredangle(MD,ME)=\measuredangle(MD,MC^*)=\measuredangle(CD,CC^*)=\measuredangle(FD,\ell)=\measuredangle(FD,FE),\] so $M$ lies on circle $DEF$. Consequently \[\measuredangle(MF,MC)=\measuredangle(MF,MD)+\measuredangle(MD,MC)=\measuredangle(EF,ED)+\measuredangle(BD,BC)=\measuredangle(\ell,BC)= \measuredangle(GF,GC),\] so $M$ lies on circle $CFG$. (Thus $M$ is the Miquel point of the lines $BC$, $CD$, $DB$, $\ell$.)

Step 3: $M$ lies on circle $HEH'$. Using $C^*\in ME$, $H'\in\omega$, $H'\in CH$ and $CC^*\parallel\ell$: \[\measuredangle(MH',ME)=\measuredangle(MH',MC^*)=\measuredangle(CH',CC^*)=\measuredangle(HH',\ell)=\measuredangle(HH',HE).\]

Step 4: a tangency criterion. Let $\Gamma_1$, $\Gamma_2$ be circles through a point $M$ meeting a line $\ell\not\ni M$ at $E,H$ and at $F,G$. The tangents $t_1,t_2$ at $M$ satisfy $\measuredangle(t_1,ME)=\measuredangle(HM,\ell)$ and $\measuredangle(t_2,MF)=\measuredangle(GM,\ell)$ (tangent–chord angle), so \[\measuredangle(t_1,t_2)=\measuredangle(HM,\ell)+\measuredangle(ME,MF)-\measuredangle(GM,\ell)=\measuredangle(ME,MF)-\measuredangle(MG,MH).\] Hence the circles are tangent at $M$ if and only if $\measuredangle(ME,MF)=\measuredangle(MG,MH)$.

Step 5: the angles agree. From circle $DEF$: $\measuredangle(ME,MF)=\measuredangle(DE,DF)=\measuredangle(DB,DC)$. From circle $HEH'M$ and the reflection in $BD$ (which maps $\ell=HE$ to $H'E$, and $HH'\perp BD$): \[\measuredangle(ME,MH)=\measuredangle(H'E,H'H)=\measuredangle(H'E,BD)+\measuredangle(BD,HH')=\measuredangle(BD,\ell)+90^\circ,\] and with $\measuredangle(MC,ME)=90^\circ$ this gives $\measuredangle(MC,MH)=\measuredangle(BD,\ell)$. From circle $CFG$, $\measuredangle(MG,MC)=\measuredangle(FG,FC)=\measuredangle(\ell,DC)$. Therefore \[\measuredangle(MG,MH)=\measuredangle(\ell,DC)+\measuredangle(BD,\ell)=\measuredangle(DB,DC)=\measuredangle(ME,MF).\] By Step 4, the circles $HEH'$ and $CFG$, which both pass through $M$, are tangent at $M$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates along line $AH$ and signed radii)

We assume, as the statement does, that the points $E,F,G$ exist and that the two triangles are non-degenerate.

Set-up. Take $AB$ as the unit of length and coordinates $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $D=(u,v)$, $C=(1+u,v)$ with $v>0$. Then $\angle DAB<90^\circ$ gives $u>0$, and $AB<AD$ gives $w=u^2+v^2>1$. Put \[p=v^2,\quad J=w-u,\quad M=p-uJ=(1-u)w,\quad N=M+J,\quad W=w-1,\quad T=w+1-2u.\] Here $J>0$ (as $u<\sqrt w<w$), $W>0$, and $T=(u-1)^2+v^2=BD^2>0$. We shall use the identities \[N-p=(1-u)J,\quad J-p=u(u-1),\quad M-p=-uJ,\quad M-J=-uW,\quad (1-u)J-p=-uT,\] \[p+J^2=wT,\qquad pW^2+N^2=wT^2,\qquad p(w+1)-uN=wT,\] each checked by expanding in $u$ and $v$.

The orthocentre. The altitude of triangle $BCD$ from $B$ is perpendicular to $CD$, which is horizontal, so it is the line $x=1$. The altitude from $D$ is perpendicular to $BC$, whose direction is $(u,v)$; its points satisfy $u(x-u)+v(y-v)=0$, and $x=1$ gives $y=\frac{w-u}{v}$. Hence $H=(1,h)$ with $h=\frac Jv>0$, and $1+h^2=\frac{p+J^2}{p}=\frac{wT}{p}$.

New coordinates. The vectors $(1,h)$ and $(-h,1)$ are perpendicular and have the same length $\sqrt{1+h^2}$. Write every point as $X(1,h)+Y(-h,1)$; then $(X,Y)$ are Cartesian coordinates in which all lengths are divided by the same factor $\sqrt{1+h^2}$. Circles stay circles and the tangency of two circles is unaffected, so from now on we work in $(X,Y)$. The line $\ell=AH$ is the $X$-axis, and its point $(x,hx)$ has $X=x$; in particular $H=(1,0)$. A vector $(a,b)$ has new components $\frac{(a+hb,\ b-ha)}{1+h^2}$. This gives:

  • $BD$, direction $(u-1,v)$: new components proportional to $(W,\ N/v)$, since $u-1+hv=u-1+J=W$ and $v-h(u-1)=\frac{p-(u-1)J}{v}=\frac Nv$; its slope is $\frac{N}{vW}$.

  • $CD$, direction $(1,0)$: slope $-h$.

  • $BC$, direction $(u,v)$: components proportional to $(w,\ M/v)$, since $u+hv=w$ and $v-hu=\frac Mv$; its slope is $\frac{M}{vw}=\frac{1-u}{v}$.

  • $C$: $Y_C=\frac{v-h(1+u)}{1+h^2}=\frac{M-J}{v(1+h^2)}=-\frac{uW}{v(1+h^2)}\ne0$.

The points $E,F,G$. On $\ell$, $F$ is the point $(x,hx)$ with $hx=v$, so $f=X_F=\frac vh=\frac pJ$. For $G$, solve $(x,hx)=(1+\lambda u,\lambda v)$: then $\lambda(v-hu)=h$, so $\lambda=\frac{J}{M}$ and $g=1+\frac{uJ}{M}=\frac pM$. For $E$, solve $(x,hx)=(1+\lambda(u-1),\lambda v)$: then $\lambda\frac Nv=h$, so $\lambda=\frac JN$ and $e=1+\frac{(u-1)J}{N}=\frac pN$. As $G$ and $E$ exist, $M\ne0$ and $N\ne0$; in particular $u\ne1$. By the identities, $e\ne1$, $f\ne1$, $g\ne1$ and $f\ne g$, so $E,F,G$ all differ from $H$ and $F\ne G$. Put \[r=1-e,\qquad s=f-g=\frac{p(M-J)}{JM}=-\frac{puW}{JM};\] both are non-zero.

The circle $\Gamma_1=(HEH')$. The reflection in $BD$ swaps $H$ and $H'$, so the centre $O_1$ lies on $BD$. It also lies on the perpendicular bisector of the chord $HE$ of the $X$-axis, so $X_{O_1}=\frac{1+e}2$. Starting from $E=(e,0)$ on $BD$ and moving along $BD$ by $\frac r2$ in $X$ changes $Y$ by $\frac r2\cdot\frac N{vW}$. Hence \[2O_1=\Bigl(1+e,\ \frac{rN}{vW}\Bigr),\qquad 4R_1^2=4\,O_1E^2=r^2\Bigl(1+\frac{N^2}{pW^2}\Bigr)=\frac wp\Bigl(\frac{rT}W\Bigr)^2,\] using $pW^2+N^2=wT^2$.

The circle $\Gamma_2=(CFG)$. A circle through $(f,0)$ and $(g,0)$ has an equation $(X-f)(X-g)+Y^2-\beta Y=0$ for some $\beta$, its centre is $O_2=\bigl(\frac{f+g}2,\frac\beta2\bigr)$ and $4R_2^2=s^2+\beta^2$. Since $C$ lies on line $CD$ through $F$ (slope $-h$) and on line $BC$ through $G$ (slope $\frac{1-u}v$), \[X_C-f=-\frac{Y_C}{h},\qquad X_C-g=\frac{vY_C}{1-u}.\] Substituting $C$ into the equation and dividing by $Y_C\ne0$: \[\beta=Y_C\Bigl(1-\frac{v}{h(1-u)}\Bigr)=Y_C\cdot\frac{(1-u)J-p}{(1-u)J}=\frac{uW}{v(1+h^2)}\cdot\frac{uT}{(1-u)J}=\frac{u^2Wp}{vMJ}=-\frac{us}{v},\] using $1+h^2=\frac{wT}p$ and $(1-u)w=M$. Therefore \[2O_2=\Bigl(f+g,\ -\frac{us}{v}\Bigr),\qquad 4R_2^2=s^2\Bigl(1+\frac{u^2}{p}\Bigr)=\frac wp\,s^2.\]

The distance of the centres. Write $2(O_1-O_2)=(d_X,d_Y)$ with $d_X=1+e-f-g$ and $d_Y=\frac{rN}{vW}+\frac{us}{v}$. Expanding (an identity in $e,f,g$), \[\frac{d_X^2-r^2-s^2}{2}=(1-f)(e-g)+(1-g)(e-f).\] From the identities, $1-f=\frac{u(u-1)}J$, $e-g=-\frac{pJ}{NM}$, $1-g=-\frac{uJ}M$, $e-f=-\frac{pM}{NJ}$, so the right-hand side is \[\frac{up}{N}\Bigl(1-\frac{u-1}{M}\Bigr)=\frac{up}{N}\cdot\frac{(1-u)(w+1)}{M}=\frac{up(w+1)}{Nw}=-rs\,\frac{w+1}{W},\] where the last step uses $rs=\frac{(1-u)J}{N}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{puW}{JM}\Bigr)=-\frac{puW}{Nw}$. For the $Y$-components, $d_Y^2$ equals $\frac{r^2N^2}{pW^2}+\frac{u^2s^2}{p}+2rs\frac{uN}{pW}$. Adding everything and using $p(w+1)-uN=wT$, \[4\,O_1O_2^2=4R_1^2+4R_2^2+2rs\,\frac{uN-p(w+1)}{pW}=\frac wp\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{rT}{W}\Bigr)^2+s^2-2\,\frac{rT}{W}\,s\Bigr]=\frac wp\Bigl(\frac{rT}{W}-s\Bigr)^2.\]

Conclusion. Put $\rho_1=\frac{rT}W$, $\rho_2=s$ and $k=\frac12\sqrt{w/p}$. Then $R_1=k|\rho_1|$, $R_2=k|\rho_2|$ and $O_1O_2=k|\rho_1-\rho_2|$, with $\rho_1,\rho_2\ne0$. If $\rho_1,\rho_2$ have opposite signs, $O_1O_2=R_1+R_2$ and the circles are externally tangent. If they have the same sign, $O_1O_2=|R_1-R_2|$. The circles are distinct, because $H\in\Gamma_1$ while $\Gamma_2$ meets the $X$-axis only at $F$ and $G$, whose $X$-coordinates $f,g$ differ from $1$. So in this case they are internally tangent. In both cases the circumcircles of $HEH'$ and $CFG$ are tangent.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer. Initially, the number $1$ is written $n$ times on a blackboard. An operation consists of choosing two numbers $a$ and $b$ currently on the blackboard, not both zero, and replacing them with the numbers \[\frac{(a-b)^2}{a+b}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{4ab}{a+b}.\] Determine all integers $n$ for which it is possible, after a finite number of operations, for the number $n$ to appear on the blackboard.

Show the answer

the powers of two, $n=2^k$ ($k\ge1$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Divisibility by an odd prime cannot be created)

Note that $\frac{(a-b)^2}{a+b}+\frac{4ab}{a+b}=\frac{(a+b)^2}{a+b}=a+b$, so the sum of the numbers is always $n$. All numbers on the board are rational and non-negative (if $a,b\ge0$ are rational and not both $0$, so are the two new numbers). If $n$ appears, the other $n-1$ numbers are therefore $0$.

Powers of two work. Applied to two equal numbers $x,x$ the operation gives $0$ and $2x$. If $n=2^k$, pair the $2^k$ ones into $2^{k-1}$ pairs and turn each pair into $0,2$; then pair the twos, and so on. After $k$ rounds the board shows $2^k=n$.

Other $n$ fail. Let $p$ be an odd prime dividing $n$. Call a rational number $p$-divisible if it is $0$ or, in lowest terms, has a numerator divisible by $p$. Sums, differences and products of $p$-divisible numbers are $p$-divisible; a product of two of them is even “$p^2$-divisible”, and if $x^2$ is $p^2$-divisible then $x$ is $p$-divisible.

Lemma. If an operation turns $a,b$ into $a',b'$ and both $a'$ and $b'$ are $p$-divisible, then $a$ and $b$ are $p$-divisible.

Proof. $s=a+b=a'+b'$ is $p$-divisible and non-zero, and $(a-b)^2=a's$ is a product of two $p$-divisible numbers, so $a-b$ is $p$-divisible. Hence $2a=s+(a-b)$ and $2b=s-(a-b)$ are $p$-divisible, and since $p$ is odd, so are $a$ and $b$. $\square$

Suppose $n$ appears. Then the board shows $n,0,\dots,0$, and all numbers on it are $p$-divisible. By the lemma (the numbers not involved in an operation do not change), if all numbers are $p$-divisible after an operation, they were all $p$-divisible before it. Going back step by step, the initial board $1,1,\dots,1$ would consist of $p$-divisible numbers, which is false. Hence $n$ has no odd prime factor.

The answer is \[\boxed{n=2^k,\ k\ge1}\]

Solution 2 (Going backwards with the numbers $nm/2^j$)

As in Solution 1, the sum of the numbers is always $n$, all numbers on the board are rational and non-negative, a board showing $n$ is $n,0,\dots,0$, and powers of two work (the operation turns $x,x$ into $0,2x$).

Call a number good if it equals $\frac{nm}{2^j}$ for some integers $m\ge0$, $j\ge0$. Finitely many good numbers can be written with one common $j$ (multiply $m$ by a power of $2$).

Lemma. If an operation turns $a,b$ into $a',b'$ and $a',b'$ are good, then $a$ and $b$ are good.

Proof. Write $a'=\frac{nm_1}{2^j}$, $b'=\frac{nm_1'}{2^j}$, and $s=a+b=a'+b'=\frac{nm_2}{2^j}$ with $m_2=m_1+m_1'$; here $s>0$, as $a,b$ are not both $0$. Since $a'=\frac{(a-b)^2}{s}$, \[(a-b)^2=a's=\frac{n^2m_1m_2}{4^j}.\] So $\sqrt{m_1m_2}=\frac{2^j|a-b|}{n}$ is rational, and the integer $m_1m_2$ is the square of an integer $r\ge0$. Then $|a-b|=\frac{nr}{2^j}$, and \[\{a,b\}=\Bigl\{\frac{s+|a-b|}2,\ \frac{s-|a-b|}2\Bigr\}=\Bigl\{\frac{n(m_2+r)}{2^{j+1}},\ \frac{n(m_2-r)}{2^{j+1}}\Bigr\},\] where $m_2-r\ge0$ because the smaller of $a,b$ is non-negative. So $a$ and $b$ are good. $\square$

Suppose $n$ appears. The board $n,0,\dots,0$ consists of good numbers ($n=\frac{n\cdot1}{2^0}$, $0=\frac{n\cdot0}{2^0}$). The numbers not involved in an operation do not change, so by the lemma every earlier board consists of good numbers, the initial one included: $1=\frac{nm}{2^j}$, i.e. $nm=2^j$. Thus $n$ divides a power of $2$, so it is a power of $2$, and $n\ge2$. Hence \[\boxed{n=2^k,\ k\ge1}\]
