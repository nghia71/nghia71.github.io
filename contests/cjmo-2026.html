J1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Bounding Integer Solutions

Let $a,b,c$ be distinct positive integers with $a+b+c=29$.

What is the smallest possible value of $a^2+b^2+c^2$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try triples of distinct positive integers whose entries are near the average. Compare what happens to the sum of squares when one entry moves farther away.
Hint 2: key idea
Write each integer as $10$ plus a deviation. Expanding the squares, use the fixed total to see which terms stay constant across all triples. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Check whether three distinct deviations of absolute value at most one could have the required sum. If one deviation must be larger, use distinctness to bound the combined squares of the other two. Look for a triple of deviations that makes those bounds tight.
Common trap
The deviations may be zero or negative even though the original integers are positive.
Show the answer

$\boxed{285}$

Show solution 1 – Deviations from $10$

Write $a=10+u$, $b=10+v$, $c=10+w$. Then $u,v,w$ are distinct integers with $u+v+w=29-30=-1$, and \[a^2+b^2+c^2=300+20(u+v+w)+u^2+v^2+w^2=280+u^2+v^2+w^2 .\] The only three distinct integers with all absolute values at most $1$ are $-1,0,1$, whose sum is $0\ne-1$. Hence one of $u,v,w$ has absolute value at least $2$, so its square is at least $4$; the other two are distinct, so their squares add up to at least $0+1=1$. Thus $u^2+v^2+w^2\ge5$ and $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge285$. Equality holds for $(u,v,w)=(-2,0,1)$, i.e. $\{a,b,c\}=\{8,10,11\}$, and indeed $8+10+11=29$ and $64+100+121=285$. The smallest value is $\boxed{285}$.

Show solution 2 – Gaps between the numbers

Let $a<b<c$ and put $p=b-a\ge1$, $q=c-b\ge1$. The identity \[3(a^2+b^2+c^2)=(a+b+c)^2+(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\] gives $a^2+b^2+c^2=\dfrac{841+p^2+q^2+(p+q)^2}{3}$, so we must minimise $F=p^2+q^2+(p+q)^2$. Also $29=a+b+c=3a+2p+q$, so $2p+q\equiv29\equiv2\pmod 3$.

The pairs with $F<26$ are $(1,1)$ $(F=6)$, $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ $(F=14)$, and $(2,2)$ $(F=24)$; every other pair with $p,q\ge1$ has $\max(p,q)\ge3$ and $F\ge 1+9+16=26$. Among these, $2p+q$ equals $3,4,5,6$ respectively, and only $(p,q)=(2,1)$ has $2p+q\equiv2\pmod 3$. So $F\ge14$, and $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge\frac{841+14}{3}=285$. For $(p,q)=(2,1)$ we get $3a=29-5$, $a=8$, so $(a,b,c)=(8,10,11)$ attains $285$. The answer is $\boxed{285}$.

J2

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Addition and Balance Relations

Consider a trapezoid $ABCD$, where sides $AB$, $CD$ are parallel. Points $W,X,Y,Z$ are given such that $BCWX$ and $ADYZ$ form rhombi, and the interiors of the three quadrilaterals $ABCD$, $BCWX$, and $ADYZ$ are disjoint.

Show that the distance between the centers of $BCWX$ and $ADYZ$ is at most half the perimeter of $ABCD$.

Remark: The center of a rhombus is the intersection of its diagonals.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the two rhombus centres and the midpoints of the nonparallel sides of the trapezoid. Draw a route from one centre to the other through the two midpoints.
Hint 2: key idea
Each centre is the midpoint of a rhombus diagonal. In each rhombus, use midpoint vectors to relate the segment from that centre to the midpoint of the shared side to an equal rhombus side. (LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Express the two end segments of your route using the side lengths of the rhombi. Express its middle segment as the trapezoid midline, taking the base vectors in the same direction. Set up a comparison between the straight-line distance and the length of this route.
Common trap
For the midline vector, use $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{DC}$ so the parallel base vectors point the same way.
Show solution 1 – Through the midpoints of the legs

Let $M$ and $N$ be the centers of $BCWX$ and $ADYZ$, and let $K$ and $L$ be the midpoints of $BC$ and $AD$. We use vectors.

Since $BCWX$ is a rhombus (vertices in this order), its center is the midpoint of the diagonal $CX$, so \[\vec{KM}=\tfrac12(C+X)-\tfrac12(B+C)=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{BX},\qquad KM=\tfrac12BX=\tfrac12BC .\] In the same way the center of $ADYZ$ is the midpoint of $DZ$, so $\vec{LN}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{AZ}$ and $LN=\tfrac12AD$.

The segment $LK$ is the midline of the trapezoid: $\vec{LK}=\tfrac12(B+C)-\tfrac12(A+D)=\tfrac12\bigl(\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{DC}\bigr)$. Because $AB\parallel CD$ and $ABCD$ is a (convex) trapezoid, the vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{DC}$ point in the same direction, so $LK=\tfrac12(AB+CD)$.

By the triangle inequality along the broken line $N\to L\to K\to M$, \[MN\le NL+LK+KM=\tfrac12AD+\tfrac12(AB+CD)+\tfrac12BC=\tfrac12(AB+BC+CD+DA),\] which is half the perimeter of $ABCD$.

Show solution 2 – Through the vertices $D,C,B,A$

Let $M$ and $N$ be the centers of $BCWX$ and $ADYZ$. The center of a rhombus is the midpoint of each diagonal; in $BCWX$ the diagonals are $BW$ and $CX$, in $ADYZ$ they are $AY$ and $DZ$. Hence $M=\tfrac12(C+X)$ and $N=\tfrac12(D+Z)$, and \[\overrightarrow{NM}=\tfrac12\bigl(\overrightarrow{DC}+\overrightarrow{ZX}\bigr),\qquad \overrightarrow{ZX}=\overrightarrow{ZA}+\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BX}.\] Now $ZA=AD$ and $BX=BC$ (sides of the rhombi). By the triangle inequality, \[2\,MN\le DC+ZA+AB+BX=CD+AD+AB+BC,\] so $MN$ is at most half the perimeter of $ABCD$. (This argument uses neither $AB\parallel CD$ nor the disjointness of the interiors, so the bound holds for any quadrilateral with rhombi $BCWX$ and $ADYZ$.)
