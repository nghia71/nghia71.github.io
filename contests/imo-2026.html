Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with \[\gcd(m,n)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}.\] He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so.

  • (a) Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$.

  • (b) Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

(Note that $\gcd(x,y)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of positive integers $x$ and $y$, and $\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)$ denotes the least common multiple of $x$ and $y$.)

Show the answer

proof; $M=\prod_p p^{g_p}$, where $g_p$ is the greatest common divisor of the exponents of $p$ in the $2026$ initial numbers

Show the solution

Solution (The Euclidean algorithm on the exponents)

For a prime $p$ and a positive integer $x$ let $v_p(x)$ be the exponent of $p$ in $x$. If $v_p(m)=a$ and $v_p(n)=b$, then \[v_p\bigl(\gcd(m,n)\bigr)=\min(a,b),\qquad v_p\Bigl(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}\Bigr)=\max(a,b)-\min(a,b)=|a-b|.\] So for every prime a move replaces the pair of exponents $(a,b)$ by $(\min(a,b),|a-b|)$: one step of the subtractive Euclidean algorithm.

(a) The process stops with exactly one number greater than $1$. Let $\Pi$ be the product of all numbers on the board and $N$ the number of entries greater than $1$. A move replaces $m,n$ by two numbers with product $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=mn/\gcd(m,n)$.

  • If $\gcd(m,n)\ge2$, then $\Pi$ is divided by $\gcd(m,n)$; since $\Pi$ stays a positive integer, this happens at most $\log_2\Pi_0$ times, where $\Pi_0$ is the initial product.

  • If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, the two new numbers are $1$ and $mn$, so $\Pi$ is unchanged and $N$ decreases by $1$. As $N\le2026$, there are at most $2025$ such moves in a row.

Hence the number of moves is finite (at most $2026(\log_2\Pi_0+1)$). When no move is possible, at most one entry is greater than $1$. On the other hand, a move is made only when $N\ge2$, and at least one of the two new numbers is greater than $1$ (if $\gcd(m,n)=1$, then $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)/\gcd(m,n)=mn>1$). So $N$ never drops from $2$ to $0$, and since $N=2026$ at the start, $N\ge1$ always. Therefore the final board contains exactly one integer $M>1$, and all other entries are $1$.

(b) $M$ does not depend on the choices. For each prime $p$ let $g_p$ be the greatest common divisor of the $2026$ exponents $v_p(t)$, $t$ running over the entries of the board (with $\gcd(0,\dots,0)=0$). We claim that $g_p$ never changes. Indeed, an integer $d$ divides both $a$ and $b$ if and only if it divides both $\min(a,b)$ and $|a-b|$ (the larger of $a,b$ equals $\min(a,b)+|a-b|$), so \[\gcd\bigl(\min(a,b),\,|a-b|\bigr)=\gcd(a,b),\] and the other $2024$ exponents are not touched by the move.

On the final board the exponents of $p$ are $0,0,\dots,0,v_p(M)$, whose greatest common divisor is $v_p(M)$. Hence $v_p(M)=g_p$ for every prime $p$, that is, \[M=\prod_{p}p^{\,g_p},\] where $g_p$ is computed from the initial board. This number depends only on the initial $2026$ integers and not on the moves, which proves (b).

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle and let points $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively. Let points $K$ and $L$ be chosen inside triangles $BMC$ and $BNC$, respectively, such that $K$ lies inside the angle $LBA$, $L$ lies inside the angle $ACK$, and \[\angle KBA=\angle ACL,\quad \angle LBK=\angle LNC,\quad \angle LCK=\angle BMK.\] Let $O$ be the circumcentre of triangle $AKL$. Prove that $OM=ON$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Three circles and a radical axis)

Throughout, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$, and $\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega)$ the power of the point $X$ with respect to the circle $\omega$. Let $A,B,C$ be labelled counterclockwise.

Step 1: the points $R$, $S$ and three circles. Since $K$ lies inside triangle $BMC$, hence inside triangle $ABC$, the line $BK$ meets the side $AC$ at an interior point $S$; similarly the line $CL$ meets the side $AB$ at an interior point $R$. The point $K$ lies on the segment $BS$ and $L$ on the segment $CR$.

The position conditions say that the rays $BC,BL,BK,BA$ follow each other counterclockwise around $B$, and the rays $CA,CL,CK,CB$ counterclockwise around $C$. Also, $L$ is inside triangle $BNC$ and $K$ is inside triangle $BMC$, so the rotation from $NL$ to $NC$ and the rotation from $MB$ to $MK$ are counterclockwise as well. Therefore the three given equalities of angles are equalities of directed angles: \[\measuredangle(BK,BA)=\measuredangle(CA,CL),\qquad \measuredangle(BL,BK)=\measuredangle(NL,NC),\qquad \measuredangle(CL,CK)=\measuredangle(MB,MK).\] Written with the points $R,S$, the first one is $\measuredangle(BS,BR)=\measuredangle(CS,CR)$, so \[B,\ C,\ S,\ R\ \text{lie on a circle }\omega .\] Let $O_1$ be its centre. The third equality is $\measuredangle(CR,CK)=\measuredangle(MR,MK)$, so \[C,\ M,\ K,\ R\ \text{lie on a circle }\omega_K .\] (If $R=M$, the equality says that the circle through $C,M,K$ is tangent to $AB$ at $M$; all statements below remain true with this circle as $\omega_K$.) In the same way the second equality shows that $B,N,L,S$ lie on a circle $\omega_L$.

Step 2: the midpoint $S'$ of $AS$ lies on $\omega_K$. The power of $A$ with respect to $\omega$ gives $AR\cdot AB=AS\cdot AC$. Hence \[\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega_K)=AM\cdot AR=\tfrac12AB\cdot AR=\tfrac12AS\cdot AC=AS'\cdot AC .\] The line $AC$ meets $\omega_K$ at $C$ and at a second point $X$ of the ray $AC$ with $AX\cdot AC=\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega_K)$; thus $AX=AS'$ and $X=S'$. Symmetrically, the midpoint $R'$ of $AR$ lies on $\omega_L$.

Step 3: the half-size copy of $\omega$. Let $\omega'$ be the image of $\omega$ under the homothety with centre $A$ and ratio $\frac12$. It passes through the images $M,N,R',S'$ of $B,C,R,S$, and its centre $O'$ is the midpoint of $AO_1$. In particular \[O'M=O'N .\] We shall prove that $O'$ is the circumcentre of triangle $AKL$; this finishes the proof.

Step 4: $O'K=O'A$. For a point $X$ put $f(X)=\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega')-\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega_K)$. In coordinates the power of $X$ with respect to a circle is $|X|^2$ plus a linear function of $X$, so $f$ is a linear (affine) function of $X$. The circles $\omega'$ and $\omega_K$ both pass through $M$ and $S'$, so $f(M)=f(S')=0$ and $f$ vanishes on the whole line $MS'$. Consequently $f$ is constant on every line parallel to $MS'$. Now $MS'$ is a midline of triangle $ABS$, so $MS'\parallel BS$, and $K$ lies on $BS$. Hence \[f(K)=f(B).\] Since $K\in\omega_K$, the left side is $\operatorname{pow}(K,\omega')$. For the right side we use the line $AB$: it meets $\omega'$ at $M,R'$ and $\omega_K$ at $M,R$, and the points $R,R',M$ all lie on the ray $BA$ with $BR'=BR+RR'=BR+\tfrac12AR$. Therefore \[f(B)=BM\cdot BR'-BM\cdot BR=BM\cdot\tfrac12AR=\tfrac14AB\cdot AR .\] On the other hand $\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega')=AM\cdot AR'=\tfrac12AB\cdot\tfrac12AR=\tfrac14AB\cdot AR$. So \[\operatorname{pow}(K,\omega')=\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega'),\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad O'K=O'A .\]

Step 5: conclusion. The same argument with the roles of $B,M,K,R,S'$ and $C,N,L,S,R'$ exchanged (circles $\omega'$ and $\omega_L$, which share the points $N,R'$, and $NR'\parallel CR$) gives $O'L=O'A$. Thus $O'$ is equidistant from $A,K,L$, so $O'=O$, and $OM=ON$ by Step 3.

Solution 2 (The same circles, a metric finish: a right angle at K)

This solution sets up the same circles as Solution 1 and then finishes with a computation instead of the linearity of powers. $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle between lines, modulo $180^\circ$; $A,B,C$ are labelled counterclockwise.

The circles. Since $K$ lies inside triangle $BMC$, hence inside triangle $ABC$, the line $BK$ meets the side $AC$ at an interior point $S$, and $K$ lies on the segment $BS$; similarly the line $CL$ meets the side $AB$ at an interior point $R$, and $L$ lies on the segment $CR$. The position conditions say that the rays $BC,BL,BK,BA$ follow each other counterclockwise around $B$ and the rays $CA,CL,CK,CB$ counterclockwise around $C$; also the rotation from $NL$ to $NC$ and the rotation from $MB$ to $MK$ are counterclockwise ($L$ is inside triangle $BNC$, $K$ inside triangle $BMC$). So the three given equalities are equalities of directed angles: \[\measuredangle(BS,BR)=\measuredangle(CS,CR),\qquad \measuredangle(BL,BS)=\measuredangle(NL,NC),\qquad \measuredangle(CR,CK)=\measuredangle(MR,MK).\] The first one shows that $B,C,S,R$ lie on a circle $\omega$; let $O_1$ be its centre and $\rho$ its radius. The third one shows that $C,M,K,R$ lie on a circle $\omega_K$ (if $R=M$, it says that the circle $CMK$ is tangent to $AB$ at $M$; take this circle as $\omega_K$). In the same way the second one shows that $B,N,L,S$ lie on a circle $\omega_L$.

The midpoint $S'$ of $AS$ lies on $\omega_K$. The power of $A$ with respect to $\omega$ gives $AR\cdot AB=AS\cdot AC$, so \[\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega_K)=AM\cdot AR=\tfrac12AB\cdot AR=\tfrac12AS\cdot AC=AS'\cdot AC .\] The line $AC$ meets $\omega_K$ at $C$ and at a second point $X$ of the ray $AC$ with $AX\cdot AC=\operatorname{pow}(A,\omega_K)$; hence $AX=AS'$ and $X=S'$. In the same way the midpoint $R'$ of $AR$ lies on $\omega_L$.

Claim. $\angle AKO_1=90^\circ$.

Proof. The point $K$ lies inside $\omega$ on the chord $BS$, and $A$ lies outside $\omega$, so \[O_1K^2=\rho^2-KB\cdot KS,\qquad O_1A^2=\rho^2+AR\cdot AB .\] By the converse of the Pythagorean theorem the claim is equivalent to $AK^2+O_1K^2=O_1A^2$, that is, to \[AK^2-KB\cdot KS=AB\cdot AR.\tag{1}\] Put a coordinate on the line $BS$ with $B$ at $0$ and $S$ at $s>0$, and let $K$ be at $x$ $(0<x<s)$. Let the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BS$ be at $a$, and let $h$ be the distance from $A$ to $BS$. Then \[AK^2=h^2+(x-a)^2,\qquad AB^2=h^2+a^2,\qquad KB\cdot KS=x(s-x).\] The line $BS$ meets $\omega_K$ at $K$ and at a second point $K_2$ with coordinate $y$ (with $y=x$ in case of tangency). Two facts determine $x+y$ and $xy$:

  • $MS'$ is a midline of triangle $ABS$, so $MS'\parallel BS$. Thus $MS'$ and $KK_2$ are parallel chords of $\omega_K$; they have the same perpendicular bisector, so their midpoints have the same projection on $BS$. The points $M,S'$ project to $\frac a2$ and $\frac{a+s}2$, hence $\frac{x+y}2=\frac a2+\frac s4$, i.e. $x+y=a+\frac s2$.

  • The power of $B$ with respect to $\omega_K$ is $xy=BM\cdot BR=\frac12AB\cdot BR$.

Therefore \[AK^2-KB\cdot KS=h^2+a^2+2x^2-2ax-sx=AB^2+2x\Bigl(x-a-\frac s2\Bigr)=AB^2-2xy=AB^2-AB\cdot BR=AB\cdot AR,\] which is (1). $\square$

The same argument with $C,N,L,R,R',\omega_L$ in place of $B,M,K,S,S',\omega_K$ gives $\angle ALO_1=90^\circ$. Hence $K$ and $L$ lie on the circle with diameter $AO_1$, so this is the circumcircle of $AKL$ and $O$ is the midpoint of $AO_1$. Finally $OM$ is a midline of triangle $ABO_1$ and $ON$ is a midline of triangle $ACO_1$, so \[OM=\tfrac12O_1B=\tfrac12\rho=\tfrac12O_1C=ON .\]

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Liu Bang and Xiang Yu have a stick of length $1$ and want to divide it between themselves. Liu Bang marks at most $n$ points on the stick, and then Xiang Yu marks at most $n$ points on the stick. The marked points are distinct. Then, the stick is cut at all marked points, creating a number of pieces. Afterwards, they take turns claiming any unclaimed piece of the stick, with Liu Bang going first. Each player's goal is to maximise the total length of their own pieces. For each $n$, determine the largest value $c$ such that Liu Bang may guarantee a total length of at least $c$, regardless of Xiang Yu's play.

Show the answer

$c=\dfrac{2^n}{2^{n+1}-1}$

Show the solution

Solution (Subset sums for Xiang Yu, powers of two for Liu Bang)

Let $u=\dfrac1{2^{n+1}-1}$. We prove that the answer is $c=\dfrac{1+u}2=\dfrac{2^n}{2^{n+1}-1}$.

The claiming phase. Let the pieces after cutting be $b_1\ge b_2\ge\dots\ge b_m$ and put \[D=b_1-b_2+b_3-b_4+\cdots\qquad(\text{the alternating sum}).\] Lemma 1. With best play Liu Bang obtains exactly $b_1+b_3+b_5+\dots=\frac{1+D}2$.

If Liu always takes the largest unclaimed piece, his $j$-th piece is at least $b_{2j-1}$, because only $2j-2$ pieces were claimed before; so he gets at least $b_1+b_3+\cdots$. Conversely let Xiang always take the unclaimed piece with the smallest index. We claim that Liu's $j$-th largest piece has index at least $2j-1$. Otherwise Liu holds $j$ pieces among $b_1,\dots,b_{2j-2}$. When he takes the last of these, he has made at least $j-1$ earlier choices, so Xiang has already chosen at least $j-1$ times, each time while a piece of index $\le 2j-2$ was still free; hence all these choices of Xiang have index $\le 2j-2$. That is $j+(j-1)>2j-2$ pieces of index $\le2j-2$, a contradiction. So Liu gets at most $b_1+b_3+\cdots$. Since the two sums $b_1+b_3+\cdots$ and $b_2+b_4+\cdots$ add up to $1$ and differ by $D$, the lemma follows.

So Liu wants $D$ large and Xiang wants $D$ small. We also need:

Lemma 2. $0\le D\le b_1+\dots+b_m$, and adding two equal pieces to a collection does not change $D$.

The first part is clear from $D=(b_1-b_2)+(b_3-b_4)+\cdots$ . Two equal pieces are neighbours in the sorted order, so they get opposite signs, and every later piece moves by two places and keeps its sign.

Xiang Yu can force $D\le u$. Let Liu's pieces be $a_1,\dots,a_m$ with $m\le n+1$. If $m\le n$, Xiang cuts every piece at its midpoint; the pieces then come in equal pairs and $D=0$. Let $m=n+1$. For a set $I$ of pieces write $\sigma(I)$ for its total length. The $2^{n+1}$ numbers $\sigma(I)$ lie in $[0,1]$ and include $0$ and $1$; listed in increasing order they have $2^{n+1}-1$ consecutive gaps with total $1$, so two different sets $I',J'$ satisfy $0\le\sigma(I')-\sigma(J')\le u$. Removing the common pieces we get disjoint sets $I,J$, not both empty, with \[0\le\sigma(I)-\sigma(J)\le u .\] Xiang now cuts as follows.

  • Imagine the pieces of $I$ laid end to end on $[0,\sigma(I)]$ and the pieces of $J$ laid end to end on $[0,\sigma(J)]\subseteq[0,\sigma(I)]$. Let $U$ be the set of all endpoints of these pieces that lie in the interval $(0,\sigma(J)]$. Xiang cuts every piece of $I$ and of $J$ at all points of $U$ in its interior (positions measured in the layout). The pieces of $J$ have $|J|$ right endpoints in $(0,\sigma(J)]$, and at most $|I|-1$ endpoints of pieces of $I$ lie in $(0,\sigma(J))$ (the last endpoint is $\sigma(I)\ge\sigma(J)$). Each point of $U$ is an endpoint of a piece in one of the two layouts and is interior to at most one piece of the other layout, so it causes at most one cut. So at most $|I|+|J|-1$ cuts are made.

  • Every piece outside $I\cup J$ is cut at its midpoint: $n+1-|I|-|J|$ cuts.

In total at most $n$ points are marked. Afterwards both layouts are divided on $[0,\sigma(J)]$ by the same points, so the parts of the $I$-pieces inside $[0,\sigma(J)]$ and the parts of the $J$-pieces are equal in pairs; the halves of the other pieces are equal in pairs too. What remains are the parts of the $I$-pieces in $[\sigma(J),\sigma(I)]$, of total length $\sigma(I)-\sigma(J)$. By Lemma 2, $D\le\sigma(I)-\sigma(J)\le u$, and by Lemma 1 Liu gets at most $\frac{1+u}2$.

Liu Bang can force $D\ge u$. Liu marks $n$ points so that the pieces have lengths \[2^n u,\ 2^{n-1}u,\ \dots,\ 2u,\ u\qquad(\text{their sum is }(2^{n+1}-1)u=1).\] Let Xiang mark at most $n$ further points, and let $b_1\ge\dots\ge b_m$ be the final pieces; $m\le 2n+1$. Pair them as $(b_1,b_2),(b_3,b_4),\dots$; if $m$ is odd, $b_m$ stays alone. Then $D$ is the sum of the differences $b_{2i-1}-b_{2i}$ of the pairs, plus $b_m$ if $m$ is odd.

Consider the graph $H$ whose vertices are Liu's $n+1$ pieces; for every pair we draw an edge between the two Liu pieces that contain the two members of the pair (a loop if they come from the same Liu piece). The graph has $n+1$ vertices and $\lfloor m/2\rfloor\le n$ edges. If every connected component had at least as many edges as vertices, there would be at least $n+1$ edges; so some component $T$ has fewer edges than vertices, i.e. $T$ is a tree (without loops). Colour the vertices of $T$ with signs $\varepsilon=\pm1$ so that adjacent vertices get opposite signs, and consider \[\Sigma=\sum_{v\in T}\varepsilon(v)\cdot(\text{length of }v).\] Each Liu piece is the sum of its final parts. A final part of a piece of $T$ is either the lonely piece $b_m$, or it belongs to a pair whose other member lies in a piece of $T$ with the opposite sign. Hence $\Sigma$ is a sum of terms $\pm(b_{2i-1}-b_{2i})$, one for each pair inside $T$, and possibly $\pm b_m$; so $|\Sigma|\le D$. On the other hand $\Sigma/u$ is a sum $\pm2^{i_1}\pm2^{i_2}\pm\cdots$ of distinct powers of two (at least one), which is not zero because it is an odd multiple of the smallest of these powers. So $|\Sigma|\ge u$, and therefore $D\ge u$. By Lemma 1 Liu gets at least $\frac{1+u}2$.

Answer. Both bounds together give \[\boxed{c=\frac{2^n}{2^{n+1}-1}}\]

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Shan-Yu and Mulan are playing a game. Let $\theta$ be an angle with $0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ known to both players. Initially, Shan-Yu makes a paper triangle $\mathcal{T}$ with measurements of his choice. Then, they repeatedly perform the following steps:

  • If $\mathcal{T}$ has at least one angle measuring exactly $\theta$, then the game stops and Mulan wins.

  • Otherwise, Mulan chooses a point $P$ on the perimeter of $\mathcal{T}$, different from its three vertices. She then makes a straight cut from $P$ to the opposite vertex of $\mathcal{T}$, splitting it into two triangles.

  • Shan-Yu discards one of the two triangles. The remaining triangle becomes the new $\mathcal{T}$.

For which real values of $\theta$ can Mulan guarantee her victory in finitely many steps, no matter how Shan-Yu plays?

Show the answer

exactly for $\theta=\dfrac{180^\circ}{m}$ with an integer $m\ge2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Multiples of the angle)

Answer: Mulan can guarantee a win exactly when $\theta=\dfrac{180^\circ}{m}$ for some integer $m\ge2$.

Call an angle good if it equals $k\theta$ for a positive integer $k$, and call a triangle safe if none of its angles is good.

What a cut does. Let the triangle be $ABC$ with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$, and let Mulan cut along $AP$ with $P$ on $BC$. Put $\alpha_1=\angle BAP$, $\alpha_2=\angle PAC$, $\varphi=\angle APB$. The two pieces have angles \[(\alpha_1,\ \beta,\ \varphi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\alpha_2,\ \gamma,\ 180^\circ-\varphi),\] where $\alpha_1+\alpha_2=\alpha$, $\varphi=\alpha_2+\gamma$ and $180^\circ-\varphi=\alpha_1+\beta$ (exterior angles). As $P$ runs over the open side $BC$, the angle $\varphi$ takes every value strictly between $\gamma$ and $180^\circ-\beta=\gamma+\alpha$.

If $\theta\ne180^\circ/m$ for every integer $m\ge 2$, Shan-Yu can avoid defeat. First, safe triangles exist: choose angles $x,y$ with $0<x,y$, $x+y<180^\circ$ such that none of $x$, $y$, $180^\circ-x-y$ is a multiple of $\theta$ (only countably many values are forbidden for $x$, and then for $y$). Shan-Yu starts with a safe triangle and keeps the triangle safe for ever; a safe triangle has no angle $\theta$, so Mulan never wins.

Suppose the current triangle is safe and Mulan cuts as above. If both pieces were unsafe, then one of $\alpha_1,\varphi$ is good and one of $\alpha_2,180^\circ-\varphi$ is good ($\beta,\gamma$ are not good). There are four cases.

  • $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ good: then $\alpha=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$ is good, impossible.

  • $\alpha_1$ and $180^\circ-\varphi=\alpha_1+\beta$ good: then $\beta$ is a positive difference of two multiples of $\theta$, so $\beta$ is good, impossible.

  • $\varphi=\alpha_2+\gamma$ and $\alpha_2$ good: then $\gamma$ is good, impossible.

  • $\varphi$ and $180^\circ-\varphi$ good: then $180^\circ=\varphi+(180^\circ-\varphi)=m\theta$ with an integer $m\ge2$, excluded.

So at least one piece is safe, and Shan-Yu keeps it.

If $\theta=180^\circ/m$ with $m\ge2$, Mulan wins. (i) A triangle with a good angle is won for Mulan. We use induction on $k$, where $k\theta$ is the good angle. For $k=1$ she has won. For $k\ge2$ she cuts from the vertex with angle $k\theta$, dividing this angle into $\theta$ and $(k-1)\theta$ (the cutting ray meets the opposite side at an interior point). One piece has an angle $\theta$, the other an angle $(k-1)\theta$; whichever Shan-Yu keeps, Mulan wins by induction.

(ii) From a safe triangle Mulan can reach (i) in one cut. It suffices to find a vertex, say $A$, such that the interval $(\gamma,\gamma+\alpha)$ contains a multiple $j\theta$ of $\theta$: cutting with $\varphi=j\theta$, the pieces have the angles $j\theta$ and $180^\circ-j\theta=(m-j)\theta$ at $P$, both good, because $0<j\theta<180^\circ$ gives $1\le j\le m-1$.

Write the angles in units of $\theta$: $x=\alpha/\theta$, $y=\beta/\theta$, $z=\gamma/\theta$, so $x+y+z=m$ and none of $x,y,z$ is an integer. Let $\{t\}$ denote the fractional part. The sum $\{x\}+\{y\}+\{z\}$ is an integer strictly between $0$ and $3$, so it is $1$ or $2$.

  • If it is $2$, then $\{z\}+\{x\}=2-\{y\}>1$, so $\lfloor z+x\rfloor>\lfloor z\rfloor$; moreover $\lfloor z+x\rfloor<z+x$ because $z+x=m-y$ is not an integer. So the interval $(z,z+x)$ contains the integer $\lfloor z+x\rfloor$.

  • If it is $1$ and some angle is larger than $\theta$, name it $\alpha$; then $x>1$ and the interval $(z,z+x)$ has length greater than $1$, so it contains an integer.

  • If it is $1$ and $x,y,z<1$, then $m=x+y+z=\{x\}+\{y\}+\{z\}=1$, contradicting $m\ge2$.

In the first two cases the integer $j$ found satisfies $\gamma<j\theta<\gamma+\alpha$, as needed. (For $m=2$ this cut is an altitude.)

Together, (i) and (ii) show that Mulan wins after at most $m-1$ cuts, whatever the initial triangle is.

Conclusion. Mulan can guarantee victory if and only if \[\boxed{\theta=\frac{180^\circ}{m}\ \text{ for an integer }m\ge2},\] i.e. $\theta\in\{90^\circ,60^\circ,45^\circ,36^\circ,30^\circ,\dots\}$.

Solution 2 (Cut along an altitude first)

Answer: Mulan can guarantee a win exactly when $\theta=\dfrac{180^\circ}{m}$ for some integer $m\ge2$.

Call an angle good if it equals $k\theta$ for a positive integer $k$, and call a triangle safe if none of its angles is good. If Mulan cuts a triangle $ABC$ (angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$) along $AP$, with $P$ an interior point of $BC$, and $\alpha_1=\angle BAP$, $\alpha_2=\angle PAC$, $\varphi=\angle APB$, the two pieces have the angles $(\alpha_1,\beta,\varphi)$ and $(\alpha_2,\gamma,180^\circ-\varphi)$, where $\varphi=\alpha_2+\gamma$ and $180^\circ-\varphi=\alpha_1+\beta$ (exterior angles).

If $\theta\ne180^\circ/m$ for every integer $m\ge2$, Shan-Yu avoids defeat. Safe triangles exist: choose $x$ and then $y$, with $x,y>0$ and $x+y<180^\circ$, such that none of $x,y,180^\circ-x-y$ is a multiple of $\theta$ (each time only countably many values are forbidden). Shan-Yu starts with a safe triangle. If the current triangle is safe and both pieces of a cut were unsafe, one of $\alpha_1,\varphi$ and one of $\alpha_2,180^\circ-\varphi$ would be good. Then: $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ good makes $\alpha=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$ good; $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_1+\beta$ good makes $\beta$ (a positive difference of multiples of $\theta$) good; $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_2+\gamma$ good makes $\gamma$ good; $\varphi$ and $180^\circ-\varphi$ good gives $180^\circ=m\theta$ with an integer $m\ge2$. All four are impossible, so one piece is safe and Shan-Yu keeps it. A safe triangle has no angle equal to $\theta$, so Mulan never wins.

If $\theta=180^\circ/m$ with $m\ge2$, Mulan wins. (i) A triangle with a good angle $k\theta$ is won for Mulan, by induction on $k$: for $k=1$ she has won; for $k\ge2$ she cuts the angle $k\theta$ into $\theta$ and $(k-1)\theta$, and each piece has a good angle with a smaller multiplier ($1$ or $k-1$).

(ii) The altitude. From any triangle, Mulan first cuts along the altitude from the vertex of the largest angle. The other two angles are acute (two angles of a triangle cannot both be at least $90^\circ$), so the foot $H$ of this altitude is an interior point of the opposite side, and both pieces are right-angled at $H$. If $m=2$, then $90^\circ=\theta$ and Mulan has already won. Let $m\ge3$.

(iii) The second cut. Let $HUV$ be the piece that Shan-Yu keeps, with the right angle at $H$, named so that $\beta=\angle HUV\le45^\circ\le\angle HVU=90^\circ-\beta$. There is a multiple $k\theta$ in the interval $(45^\circ,90^\circ]$: for $m=3$ take $60^\circ$; for $m\ge4$ we have $\theta\le45^\circ$, and an interval of the form $(t,t+45^\circ]$ contains a multiple of any $\theta\le45^\circ$. Mulan cuts from $H$ to a point $P$ of the open segment $UV$ and puts $\varphi=\angle HPV$. In triangle $HPV$, \[\varphi=180^\circ-(90^\circ-\beta)-\angle PHV=90^\circ+\beta-\angle PHV,\] and as $P$ runs over the open segment $UV$, the angle $\angle PHV$ takes every value in $(0^\circ,90^\circ)$; so $\varphi$ takes every value in $(\beta,\beta+90^\circ)$. Since $\beta\le45^\circ<k\theta\le90^\circ<\beta+90^\circ$, she can choose $P$ with $\varphi=k\theta$. Then the piece $HPV$ has the angle $k\theta$ at $P$ and the piece $HPU$ has the angle $180^\circ-k\theta=(m-k)\theta$ at $P$, where $1\le k\le m-1$ because $0<k\theta<180^\circ$. Both pieces have a good angle, and by (i) Mulan wins.

Conclusion. Mulan can guarantee victory if and only if \[\boxed{\theta=\frac{180^\circ}{m}\ \text{ for an integer }m\ge2}.\]

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Let $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ be the set of positive real numbers. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ such that \[\sqrt{\frac{x^2+f(y)^2}{2}}\ge\frac{f(x)+y}{2}\ge\sqrt{xf(y)}\] for every $x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x+c$ for a constant $c\ge0$

Show the solution

Solution (Orbits are arithmetic progressions)

Answer: $f(x)=x+c$, where $c\ge0$ is a constant.

These functions work. For $f(x)=x+c$ put $Y=y+c>0$. Then $\frac{f(x)+y}2=\frac{x+Y}2$ and the two inequalities read \[\sqrt{\frac{x^2+Y^2}2}\ \ge\ \frac{x+Y}2\ \ge\ \sqrt{xY},\] which are the QM–AM and AM–GM inequalities for the positive numbers $x$ and $Y$.

Step 1: $f(f(y))=2f(y)-y$. Put $x=f(y)$. The left and the right expression both become $f(y)$, so $f(y)\ge\frac{f(f(y))+y}2\ge f(y)$, hence $f(f(y))+y=2f(y)$.

Step 2: orbits. Let $g(y)=f(y)-y$. Step 1 says $f(f(y))-f(y)=f(y)-y$. Applying this to $y,f(y),f(f(y)),\dots$ we see that the iterates $y,\ f(y),\ f(f(y)),\dots$ form an arithmetic progression with difference $g(y)$: \[f^{k}(y)=y+k\,g(y)\qquad(k=0,1,2,\dots).\] All iterates are positive, so $g(y)\ge0$. Moreover $g(f^k(y))=g(y)$ for all $k$.

Step 3: a consequence of the left inequality. Let $x>0$ and let $Y=f(y)$ be a value of $f$; put $\delta=g(x)-g(y)$. Since $f(x)+y=(x+g(x))+(Y-g(y))=x+Y+\delta$, the squared left inequality $2(x^2+Y^2)\ge(f(x)+y)^2$ becomes \[(x-Y)^2\ \ge\ 2\delta\,(x+Y)+\delta^2 .\tag{$*$}\]

Step 4: $g$ takes at most one positive value. Suppose $g(x_0)=p>q=g(y_0)>0$. For integers $j\ge0$, $k\ge1$ put $x=x_0+jp$ and $Y=y_0+kq=f(y)$ with $y=y_0+(k-1)q$. By Step 2, $g(x)=p$ and $g(y)=q$, so $(*)$ holds with $\delta=p-q>0$. Take $j$ so large that $x>y_0+q$, and choose $k\ge1$ with $Y\le x<Y+q$. Then $(*)$ gives \[q^2>(x-Y)^2\ge2(p-q)(x+Y)>2(p-q)\,x_0+2(p-q)\,jp,\] which is false for large $j$. So two different positive values are impossible.

Step 5: $g$ cannot take both $0$ and a positive value. Suppose $g(y)=0$ for some $y$ and $g(x)=D>0$ for some $x$; call the points of the first kind fixed (then $f(y)=y$) and those of the second kind shifted. By Step 4 every positive number is fixed or shifted. If $y$ is fixed and $x$ is shifted, $(*)$ with $Y=y=f(y)$ and $\delta=D$ gives $(x-y)^2\ge2D(x+y)+D^2>D^2$, so \[|x-y|>D\qquad\text{for every fixed }y\text{ and every shifted }x. \tag{$**$}\] A shifted point $x$ cannot be smaller than a fixed point $y$: the numbers $x,x+D,x+2D,\dots$ are all shifted (Step 2), and one of them lies in $(y-D,y]$, contradicting $(**)$. Hence every fixed point is smaller than every shifted point. Let $s$ be the supremum of the fixed points (it is finite, because the shifted point $x$ is an upper bound of the fixed points); then every number greater than $s$ is shifted, and every positive number smaller than $s$ is fixed (a number smaller than some fixed point is not shifted). Choose a fixed point $y$ with $\max(0,s-\frac D4)<y<s$ and the shifted point $x=s+\frac D4$: then $0<x-y\le\frac D2$, contradicting $(**)$.

Conclusion. By Steps 4 and 5 the function $g$ is constant, $g\equiv c$ with $c\ge0$, so $f(x)=x+c$. Together with the verification above, the solutions are exactly \[\boxed{f(x)=x+c\ \text{ with a constant }c\ge0}.\]

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots$ be an infinite sequence of positive integers greater than $1$. Suppose that for all positive integers $n$, the number $a_{n+1}$ is the smallest positive integer greater than $a_n$ such that $\gcd(a_{n+1},a_i)>1$ for every $i=1,2,\ldots,n$. Prove that there exist positive integers $T$ and $L$ such that \[a_{n+T}=a_n+L\] for every positive integer $n$.

(Note that $\gcd(x,y)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of positive integers $x$ and $y$.)

Show the solution

Solution (Large primes are never needed)

Let $S=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots\}$ be the set of terms and, for an integer $x>1$, let $P(x)$ be the set of primes dividing $x$. Two integers have a common divisor greater than $1$ exactly when their prime sets meet.

Step 1: a description of $S$. (1) Any two elements of $S$ have a common prime factor (the later one was chosen that way).

(2) If an integer $x>a_1$ satisfies $\gcd(x,s)>1$ for all $s\in S$ with $s<x$, then $x\in S$. Indeed, let $a_m$ be the largest term smaller than $x$. Then $x$ is an integer greater than $a_m$ sharing a factor with each of $a_1,\dots,a_m$, so by minimality $a_{m+1}\le x$; and $a_{m+1}\ge x$ by the choice of $m$. Thus $x=a_{m+1}$.

Call a finite set $R$ of primes good if $R$ meets $P(s)$ for every $s\in S$. By (1) and (2), for every integer $x\ge a_1$: \[x\in S\iff P(x)\text{ is good}.\tag{3}\] A set containing a good set is good, and every good set is the prime set of some element of $S$ (take a product of powers of its primes that is at least $a_1$).

Step 2: the key lemma. Let $t\in S$ and let $p>a_1$ be a prime dividing $t$. Then $P(t)\setminus\{p\}$ is good.

Suppose not, and for this prime $p$ let $t$ be the smallest element of $S$ divisible by $p$ for which $R=P(t)\setminus\{p\}$ is not good. Note that $R\ne\emptyset$: otherwise $t$ is a power of $p$, and $\gcd(t,a_1)>1$ forces $p\mid a_1$, impossible as $p>a_1$.

Since $R$ is not good, there are elements $s\in S$ with $P(s)\cap R=\emptyset$. Let $s$ be one of them. As $P(s)$ meets $P(t)=R\cup\{p\}$, we have $p\mid s$; and $s\ne t$ because $R\neq\emptyset$. If $s<t$, then by the minimality of $t$ the set $P(s)\setminus\{p\}$ is good, so it meets $P(t)$; but a common element would lie in $P(s)\cap R=\emptyset$. Hence $s>t$. We have shown: \[\text{every }s\in S\text{ with }s<t\text{ has a prime factor in }R.\tag{4}\] Write $t=p^e m$ with $p\nmid m$; then $P(m)=R$ and $m\ge2$. Let $q$ be a prime factor of $m$, let $f\ge0$ be the largest integer with $q^f<p^e$, and put $w=mq^f$. Then $P(w)=R$, \[w<mp^e=t,\qquad w=\frac{m}{q}\,q^{f+1}\ \ge\ q^{f+1}\ \ge\ p^e\ >\ a_1 .\] By (4), $w$ has a common factor with every element of $S$ smaller than $w$, so $w\in S$ by (2). But then, by (1), every element of $S$ has a prime factor in $P(w)=R$, i.e. $R$ is good. Contradiction.

(The published solution proves the same statement for primes $p>a_1^2$; the weaker threshold $p>a_1$ is enough here.)

Step 3: only the primes up to $a_1$ matter. Let $Q$ be the set of primes not exceeding $a_1$ and $L$ their product. We claim that for every integer $x\ge a_1$ \[x\in S\iff P(x)\cap Q\text{ is good}.\tag{5}\] If $P(x)\cap Q$ is good, so is the larger set $P(x)$, and $x\in S$ by (3). Conversely let $x\in S$, so $P(x)$ is good. Remove the primes greater than $a_1$ from $P(x)$ one at a time: if the current set $R'$ is good and contains a prime $p>a_1$, choose $t\in S$ with $P(t)=R'$ (Step 1) and apply the lemma: $R'\setminus\{p\}$ is good. After finitely many steps we get that $P(x)\cap Q$ is good.

Step 4: periodicity. For a prime $p\in Q$ we have $p\mid x\iff p\mid x+L$, hence $P(x)\cap Q=P(x+L)\cap Q$, and by (5) \[x\in S\iff x+L\in S\qquad\text{for all integers }x\ge a_1 .\] Let $T$ be the number of elements of $S$ in the interval $[a_1,a_1+L)$; $T\ge1$ because $a_1$ is one of them. The map $x\mapsto x+L$ is an increasing bijection from $S\cap[a_1+jL,a_1+(j+1)L)$ onto $S\cap[a_1+(j+1)L,a_1+(j+2)L)$ for every $j\ge0$. So each of these blocks contains exactly $T$ terms, and the $r$-th term of a block plus $L$ is the $r$-th term of the next block. This is exactly \[a_{n+T}=a_n+L\qquad\text{for all }n\ge1 ,\] with $L=\prod_{p\le a_1}p$ and $T=|S\cap[a_1,a_1+L)|$.
