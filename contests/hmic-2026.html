Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Let $m$ and $n$ be even positive integers. Suppose we have a tiling of an $m\times n$ rectangle with $mn/2$ non-overlapping dominoes ($1\times 2$ or $2\times 1$). Prove that there exists another tiling of the rectangle with dominoes that shares no dominoes with the original tiling.

Show the solution – Retile each 2 by 2 block

Since $m$ and $n$ are even, the rectangle splits into $\frac{mn}4$ blocks of size $2\times2$, made of rows $2r-1,2r$ and columns $2s-1,2s$. Each block can be tiled in two ways: by its two horizontal dominoes or by its two vertical dominoes.

Fix a block. A domino of the original tiling that lies inside the block is one of its two horizontal or two vertical dominoes. A horizontal one and a vertical one inside the same block always share a cell, so the original tiling cannot contain both. Hence the original tiling contains no vertical domino of the block, or no horizontal domino of the block.

In the first case tile the block by its two vertical dominoes, otherwise by its two horizontal dominoes. Doing this in every block gives a tiling of the whole rectangle. Each of its dominoes lies inside a block and has the orientation that the original tiling does not use inside that block, so it is not a domino of the original tiling. This is the required tiling.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Fermat and Euler Type Results

Prove that there exist infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that $n+100$ divides $3^n-2^n-1$.

Show the solution – Take n + 100 = 101q

Construction. Let $q\ne101$ be a prime with $q\equiv1\pmod{100}$ and put \[n=101q-100,\qquad\text{so}\qquad n+100=101q .\] Modulo $101$. Since $q\equiv1\pmod{100}$, we have $n=101q-100\equiv q\equiv1\pmod{100}$. By Fermat's little theorem $3^{100}\equiv2^{100}\equiv1\pmod{101}$, so $3^n\equiv3$ and $2^n\equiv2$, and $3^n-2^n-1\equiv0\pmod{101}$.

Modulo $q$. Here $q>100$, so $q\nmid6$, and $n=101(q-1)+1\equiv1\pmod{q-1}$. By Fermat's little theorem $3^n\equiv3$ and $2^n\equiv2\pmod q$, so $3^n-2^n-1\equiv0\pmod q$.

As $101$ and $q$ are distinct primes, $n+100=101q$ divides $3^n-2^n-1$. Different primes $q$ give different $n$, so it remains to know that there are infinitely many primes $q\equiv1\pmod{100}$. This is a case of Dirichlet's theorem; here is a short self-contained proof.

Lemma. Let $\Phi(x)=x^{40}-x^{30}+x^{20}-x^{10}+1$ (the $100$th cyclotomic polynomial). If a prime $r$ divides $\Phi(a)$ for an integer $a$ and $r\nmid 10a$, then $r\equiv1\pmod{100}$.

Proof. Multiplying out, $(x^{10}+1)\Phi(x)=x^{50}+1$. Hence $a^{50}\equiv-1\pmod r$, so $a^{100}\equiv1$ and, as $r\ne2$, $a^{50}\not\equiv1$. The order $d$ of $a$ modulo $r$ therefore divides $100$ but not $50$, so $d\in\{4,20,100\}$. If $d=4$ or $d=20$, then $a^{20}\equiv1$, so $a^{10}\equiv\pm1$, and $a^{10}\equiv1$ would give $a^{50}\equiv1$; thus $a^{10}\equiv-1$ and $\Phi(a)\equiv1+1+1+1+1=5\pmod r$, so $r\mid5$, contradicting $r\nmid10$. So $d=100$, and $100\mid r-1$ by Fermat's little theorem. $\square$

If $q_1,\dots,q_s$ were all the primes $\equiv1\pmod{100}$, take $a=10\,q_1\cdots q_s$. Then $\Phi(a)\equiv\Phi(0)=1\pmod a$ and $\Phi(a)>1$, so $\Phi(a)$ has a prime factor $r$ with $r\nmid a$. By the lemma $r\equiv1\pmod{100}$, and $r$ differs from all $q_i$, a contradiction. Hence there are infinitely many such primes, and infinitely many $n$ with $n+100\mid3^n-2^n-1$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Angle Bisector and Median Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a scalene triangle with circumcenter $O$ and symmedian point $K$. Points $X$ and $Y$ lie on the interiors of sides $\overline{AC}$ and $\overline{AB}$, respectively, such that $XY\parallel BC$. Suppose that the circumcircles of triangles $AXB$ and $AYC$ meet segment $\overline{BC}$ at points $D\ne B$ and $E\ne C$, respectively. Prove that $D$, $E$, $X$, and $Y$ lie on a circle whose center lies on line $OK$.

(The symmedian point of triangle $ABC$ is the intersection of the reflections of the $B$-median and $C$-median across the angle bisectors of $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ACB$, respectively.)

Show solution 1 – Antiparallels and the cosine circle

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and $AX:AC=AY:AB=t$ with $0<t<1$. We write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for directed angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$.

Concyclic. From the circles $ABDX$ and $AYEC$, \[\measuredangle(DX,DE)=\measuredangle(DX,DB)=\measuredangle(AX,AB)=\measuredangle(AC,AB),\qquad \measuredangle(EY,EC)=\measuredangle(AY,AC)=\measuredangle(AB,AC).\] Since $XY\parallel BC$, \[\measuredangle(YX,YE)=\measuredangle(CB,YE)=-\measuredangle(EY,EC)=\measuredangle(AC,AB)=\measuredangle(DX,DE),\] so $D,E,X,Y$ lie on a circle $\omega_t$. (This uses $D\ne E$. If $D=E$, which happens for one value of $t$ when $\angle A$ is acute, there are only three distinct points $D,X,Y$, not collinear since $D$ lies on $BC$ and line $XY$ is parallel to $BC$ at positive distance, and $\omega_t$ is their circumcircle; everything below applies to it unchanged, because it only uses the circle through $D$, $X$ and $Y$.) Let $Z_t$ be its centre.

Two lines through $Z_t$ with fixed directions. The tangent $\tau_C$ to the circumcircle at $C$ satisfies $\measuredangle(\tau_C,CB)=\measuredangle(AC,AB)=\measuredangle(DX,CB)$ (tangent–chord angle), so $DX\parallel\tau_C$, i.e. $DX\perp OC$. Hence $Z_t$ lies on the line $\ell_t$ through the midpoint of $DX$ parallel to $OC$. It also lies on the perpendicular bisector $m_t$ of $XY$, which is perpendicular to $BC$. These directions are different, since $OC\perp BC$ is impossible.

$Z_t$ moves on a line. The power of $C$ with respect to circle $ABDX$ gives $CD\cdot CB=CX\cdot CA=(1-t)b^2$, so \[X=A+t(C-A),\quad Y=A+t(B-A),\quad D=C+(1-t)\frac{b^2}{a^2}(B-C).\] These formulas define points $X_t,Y_t,D_t$ for every real $t$, all depending affinely on $t$; so do the midpoints of $D_tX_t$ and $X_tY_t$. Define $\ell_t$, $m_t$ and $Z_t=\ell_t\cap m_t$ for all real $t$ by the same recipe. Intersecting two lines of fixed non-parallel directions through points depending affinely on $t$ means solving a linear system with a fixed matrix and an affine right-hand side, so $Z_t=Z_1+(t-1)V$ for a fixed vector $V$; for $0<t<1$ this $Z_t$ is the centre of $\omega_t$.

$t=1$. Then $X_1=D_1=C$ and $Y_1=B$, so $\ell_1$ is the line through $C$ parallel to $OC$, namely line $OC$, and $m_1$ is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. Hence $Z_1=O$.

A parameter with $Z_{t_0}=K$. For a point $P$ of the $B$-median, triangles $PBA$ and $PBC$ have equal areas, so the distances of $P$ to lines $BA$ and $BC$ are in the ratio $a:c$; reflecting in the bisector of $\angle B$ swaps these two lines, so $d(K,BA):d(K,BC)=c:a$. In the same way $d(K,CA):d(K,CB)=b:a$, hence $d(K,BC):d(K,CA):d(K,AB)=a:b:c$. Write $d(K,BC)=\mu a$, $d(K,CA)=\mu b$, $d(K,AB)=\mu c$ with $\mu>0$; $K$ lies inside the triangle. Let $R$ be the circumradius.

Draw through $K$ the parallel to $\tau_C$. The points $A$, $B$ and $K$ lie strictly on the same side of $\tau_C$, and the distance to $\tau_C$ grows linearly from $0$ along each of the rays $CA$ and $CB$; so the parallel meets rays $CB$ and $CA$ at points $D'$ and $X'$, and since $K$ lies inside the angle $ACB$, $K$ is on segment $D'X'$. Triangle $CD'X'$ is similar to $CAB$ ($\angle CD'X'=\angle A$, $\angle CX'D'=\angle B$). Then \[KD'=\frac{d(K,BC)}{\sin A}=\frac{\mu a}{\sin A}=2R\mu,\qquad KX'=\frac{d(K,CA)}{\sin B}=\frac{\mu b}{\sin B}=2R\mu\] by the law of sines. In the same way the parallel through $K$ to the tangent at $B$ meets rays $BC$, $BA$ at $E'$, $Y'$, with $K$ on segment $E'Y'$ and $KE'=KY'=2R\mu$. So $K$ is the common midpoint of $D'X'$ and $E'Y'$; the half-turn about $K$ maps line $BC$, which contains $D'$ and $E'$, to a line parallel to $BC$ containing $X'$ and $Y'$. Let $t_0$ be defined by $X'=A+t_0(C-A)$; the parallel to $BC$ through $X'$ meets line $AB$ at $A+t_0(B-A)$, so $Y'=A+t_0(B-A)$, and $CD'\cdot CB=CX'\cdot CA$ (similar triangles) gives $D'=D_{t_0}$. Now $K$ is the midpoint of $D_{t_0}X_{t_0}$, so $K\in\ell_{t_0}$, and $KX'=KY'$, so $K\in m_{t_0}$ when $X'\ne Y'$. (If $X'=Y'$, both equal $A$ and $t_0=0$; then $D'=E'$ as well, so $CD'+BE'=a$ with $CD'=\frac{b^2}a$, $BE'=\frac{c^2}a$ gives $a^2=b^2+c^2$, $\angle A=90^\circ$, and $CD'=\frac{b^2}a=b\cos C$ shows that $D'$ is the foot of the altitude from $A$. So $K$, the midpoint of $AD'$, lies on that altitude, which is $m_0$.) Thus $Z_{t_0}=K$. Moreover $t_0\ne1$, because $X'=C$ would put $K$ on $\tau_C$ itself.

Conclusion. $K\ne O$: otherwise $O$ would be inside the triangle with $d(O,BC)=R\cos A$ etc., and $R\cos A:R\cos B:R\cos C=a:b:c=\sin A:\sin B:\sin C$ would give $\cot A=\cot B=\cot C$, an equilateral triangle. From $O=Z_1$ and $K=Z_{t_0}=O+(t_0-1)V$ we get $V=\frac{K-O}{t_0-1}$, so \[Z_t=O+\frac{t-1}{t_0-1}\,(K-O)\] lies on line $OK$ for every $t$. Hence the circle through $D,E,X,Y$ has its centre on line $OK$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(a,0)$, $A=(p,h)$ with $h>0$, where $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, so $p^2+h^2=c^2$ and $p=\frac{a^2+c^2-b^2}{2a}$. Let $AX:AC=AY:AB=t\in(0,1)$ and $s=1-t$; put $S=b^2+c^2$ and $\Sigma=a^2+b^2+c^2$.

The four points. $X=A+t(C-A)$ and $Y=A+t(B-A)$ both have height $sh$, and the midpoint of $XY$ has abscissa $sp+t\frac a2=\frac a2+s\left(p-\frac a2\right)=\frac a2+s\frac{c^2-b^2}{2a}$. The power of $C$ with respect to circle $ABDX$ gives $CD\cdot CB=CX\cdot CA=sb^2$, and similarly $BE\cdot BC=sc^2$, so \[D=\Bigl(a-\frac{sb^2}a,0\Bigr),\qquad E=\Bigl(\frac{sc^2}a,0\Bigr),\] and the midpoint of $DE$ has abscissa $\frac a2+s\frac{c^2-b^2}{2a}$ as well.

Concyclic. So the vertical line $x=x_s$, with $x_s=\frac a2+s\frac{c^2-b^2}{2a}$, passes through the midpoints of the horizontal segments $XY$ and $DE$, and the reflection in it swaps $X\leftrightarrow Y$ and $D\leftrightarrow E$ (it fixes $D=E$ if these points coincide). The perpendicular bisector of $XD$ is not vertical ($X$ is above $BC$, $D$ on it), so it meets the line $x=x_s$ at a point $Z$ with $ZX=ZD$; by the symmetry also $ZY=ZX$ and $ZE=ZD$. Hence $D,E,X,Y$ lie on a circle with centre $Z=(x_s,y_s)$.

The centre. Half the lengths of $XY$ and $DE$ are $u=\frac{ta}2$ and $w=\frac12\bigl(a-\frac{sS}a\bigr)$ (up to sign). From $ZX^2=ZD^2$: $u^2+(y_s-sh)^2=w^2+y_s^2$, so $y_s=\frac{s^2h^2+u^2-w^2}{2sh}$. Since $ta=a-sa$, \[u^2-w^2=\tfrac14\Bigl[(a-sa)^2-\bigl(a-\tfrac{sS}a\bigr)^2\Bigr]=\tfrac s4\Bigl(\tfrac Sa-a\Bigr)\Bigl(2a-s\bigl(a+\tfrac Sa\bigr)\Bigr),\] and therefore \[y_s=\frac{S-a^2}{4h}+s\Bigl(\frac h2-\frac{S^2-a^4}{8a^2h}\Bigr).\] $O$ and $K$. The circumcentre is $O=\bigl(\frac a2,y_O\bigr)$ with $\frac{a^2}4+y_O^2=(p-\frac a2)^2+(h-y_O)^2$, which gives $y_O=\frac{p^2+h^2-pa}{2h}=\frac{c^2-pa}{2h}=\frac{S-a^2}{4h}$. Hence \[Z-O=s\,(d_x,d_y),\qquad d_x=\frac{c^2-b^2}{2a},\quad d_y=\frac h2-\frac{S^2-a^4}{8a^2h}.\] The symmedian point has distances to the side lines proportional to $a,b,c$: a point of the $B$-median has distances to lines $BA$, $BC$ in the ratio $a:c$ (for every point $P$ of the median, triangles $PBA$ and $PBC$ have equal areas), the reflection in the bisector of $\angle B$ swaps the two lines, so $d(K,BA):d(K,BC)=c:a$, and likewise at $C$. Hence the areas $[KBC]:[KCA]:[KAB]$ are $a^2:b^2:c^2$ and $K=\frac{a^2A+b^2B+c^2C}{\Sigma}=\Bigl(\frac{a^2p+c^2a}{\Sigma},\frac{a^2h}{\Sigma}\Bigr)$. Using $a^2p=\frac a2(a^2+c^2-b^2)$, \[K_x-\frac a2=\frac{a\bigl(\frac{a^2+c^2-b^2}2+c^2-\frac{\Sigma}2\bigr)}{\Sigma}=\frac{a(c^2-b^2)}{\Sigma}=\frac{2a^2}{\Sigma}\,d_x,\] \[\frac{2a^2}{\Sigma}\,d_y=\frac{a^2h}{\Sigma}-\frac{(S-a^2)(S+a^2)}{4\Sigma h}=\frac{a^2h}{\Sigma}-\frac{S-a^2}{4h}=K_y-y_O,\] because $S+a^2=\Sigma$. So $K-O=\frac{2a^2}{\Sigma}(d_x,d_y)$, where $d_x\ne0$ as the triangle is scalene ($b\ne c$). Therefore $Z-O=\frac{s\Sigma}{2a^2}(K-O)$, and the centre $Z$ lies on line $OK$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

Let $n\ge 4$ be an integer. Alice selects $n$ pairwise distinct points $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_n$ in the plane. In a single query, Bob selects four pairwise distinct integers $i$, $j$, $k$, and $\ell$, each between $1$ and $n$ inclusive, and sends them to Alice; Alice then responds “yes” if the lines $A_iA_j$ and $A_kA_\ell$ are parallel or coincide, and “no” otherwise. In terms of $n$, determine the minimum number of queries Bob needs to determine whether all $n$ points are collinear, regardless of how the points are positioned.

Show the answer

$3$ if $n=4$, and $n-2$ if $n\ge5$

Show the solution – Linear algebra for the lower bound, a path for the upper bound

A query is determined by the two disjoint pairs $\{i,j\}$ and $\{k,\ell\}$; we write it $(ij\,|\,k\ell)$.

Lower bound $n-2$ (all $n\ge4$). Let Alice answer “yes” to every query; this is consistent with all points collinear, so Bob, to be sure of collinearity, must reach a moment when “yes” to all queries asked forces collinearity. Suppose Bob has asked $q\le n-3$ queries. Look for points $A_i=(i,y_i)$. They are pairwise distinct, no line $A_iA_j$ is vertical, and the answer to $(ij\,|\,k\ell)$ is “yes” iff the slopes agree: \[\frac{y_j-y_i}{j-i}=\frac{y_\ell-y_k}{\ell-k}.\] This is a homogeneous linear equation in $(y_1,\dots,y_n)$, so the $q$ queries define a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$ of dimension at least $n-q\ge3$. The points are collinear iff $y_i=\alpha+\beta i$ for constants $\alpha,\beta$, and these vectors form a subspace of dimension $2$. So some solution $y$ is not of this form: its points are distinct, not collinear, and answer “yes” to every query asked. Bob cannot yet decide, so he needs at least $n-2$ queries.

$n=4$. There are only three queries: $(12|34)$, $(13|24)$, $(14|23)$. Any two of them are answered “yes” by a parallelogram whose two pairs of opposite sides are the two queried pairs of pairs (for instance, for $(12|34)$ and $(13|24)$ take $A_1=(0,0)$, $A_2=(1,0)$, $A_3=(0,1)$, $A_4=(1,1)$; the other cases are relabellings). So two queries do not suffice. Three do: suppose all three answers are “yes”. If three of the points are collinear on a line $g$, the fourth point $A_j$ and any of the three, $A_i$, form a pair whose complementary pair lies on $g$, so $A_iA_j\parallel g$ and $A_j\in g$: all four are collinear. Otherwise no three are collinear; then $A_1A_2\parallel A_3A_4$ and $A_1A_3\parallel A_2A_4$ make $A_1A_2A_4A_3$ a parallelogram, whose diagonals $A_1A_4$ and $A_2A_3$ meet at its centre and are neither parallel nor equal, contradicting $(14|23)$. So for $n=4$ the answer is $3$.

Upper bound $n-2$ for $n\ge5$. Bob asks the $n-3$ queries $(12\,|\,j,j{+}1)$ for $j=3,\dots,n-1$ and the query $(23\,|\,45)$, in total $n-2$ queries (all indices in each are distinct). If some answer is “no”, the points are not collinear. If all answers are “yes”, then every line $A_jA_{j+1}$, $1\le j\le n-1$, is parallel to or equal to $A_1A_2$ (for $j=2$ via $A_4A_5$). Consecutive lines $A_{j-1}A_j$ and $A_jA_{j+1}$ are parallel and share $A_j$, so they coincide; hence all points lie on line $A_1A_2$.

Answer. The minimum number of queries is $\boxed{3}$ for $n=4$ and $\boxed{n-2}$ for $n\ge5$.

Problem 5

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Finite Difference and Discrete Polynomial Behavior

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Determine, in terms of $n$, the number of ordered $n$-tuples $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ of real numbers such that

  • $0\le a_i<1$ for all $1\le i\le n$, and

  • $a_1\cdot1^k+a_2\cdot2^k+\dots+a_n\cdot n^k$ is an integer for all nonnegative integers $k$.

Show the answer

$1!\cdot2!\cdots(n-1)!=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(j-i)$

Show solution 1 – A lattice and the Vandermonde determinant

Only $k\le n-1$ matter. Let $F(x)=(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-n)$, monic with integer coefficients. For $k\ge n$, division gives $x^k=F(x)G(x)+H(x)$ with $G,H\in\mathbb Z[x]$ and $\deg H<n$. As $F(i)=0$ for $1\le i\le n$, writing $H(x)=\sum_{j<n}h_jx^j$, \[\sum_i a_i\,i^k=\sum_i a_iH(i)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_j\sum_i a_i\,i^j,\] an integer combination of the sums for $k=0,\dots,n-1$. So the condition is equivalent to $Va\in\mathbb Z^n$, where $V$ is the $n\times n$ matrix with entries $V_{ki}=i^k$ ($0\le k\le n-1$, $1\le i\le n$) and $a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)^{T}$.

Counting. $V$ is a Vandermonde matrix, $\det V=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(j-i)\ne0$. Let $\Lambda=\{a\in\mathbb R^n: Va\in\mathbb Z^n\}=V^{-1}\mathbb Z^n$. Since $V$ has integer entries, $\mathbb Z^n\subseteq\Lambda$. Every coset $a+\mathbb Z^n$ of $\mathbb Z^n$ in $\Lambda$ has exactly one representative in $[0,1)^n$ (take fractional parts), so the required number is the index $[\Lambda:\mathbb Z^n]$. Multiplication by $V$ maps $\Lambda$ isomorphically onto $\mathbb Z^n$ and $\mathbb Z^n$ onto $V\mathbb Z^n$, so \[[\Lambda:\mathbb Z^n]=[\mathbb Z^n:V\mathbb Z^n]=|\det V|,\] the last equality being the standard fact that an integer matrix with nonzero determinant has image of index $|\det V|$ (bring $V$ to Smith normal form). Finally \[\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(j-i)=\prod_{j=2}^{n}(j-1)!=1!\,2!\cdots(n-1)!.\] The number of $n$-tuples is $\boxed{1!\cdot2!\cdots(n-1)!}$ (for $n=1$ the empty product $1$: only $a_1=0$).

Show solution 2 – A triangular system

Let $P_0(x)=1$ and $P_j(x)=(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-j)$ for $j\ge1$. These polynomials are monic with integer coefficients, of degrees $0,1,2,\dots$, so they form a basis of $\mathbb Z[x]$ over $\mathbb Z$: every integer polynomial is an integer combination of them (subtract a multiple of $P_{\deg}$ to lower the degree). Moreover $P_j(i)=0$ for $1\le i\le j$.

Reformulation. Put $s_j=\sum_{i=1}^n a_iP_j(i)$. Each $P_j$ is an integer combination of powers $x^k$, so the condition implies $s_j\in\mathbb Z$ for all $j$. Conversely, each $x^k$ is an integer combination of the $P_j$, and $P_j$ vanishes at $1,\dots,n$ for $j\ge n$, so $\sum_ia_ii^k$ is an integer combination of $s_0,\dots,s_{n-1}$. Hence the condition is equivalent to $s_0,\dots,s_{n-1}\in\mathbb Z$, where \[s_j=\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}a_i\,\frac{(i-1)!}{(i-1-j)!}=j!\,a_{j+1}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{n}a_i\,\frac{(i-1)!}{(i-1-j)!}.\] Counting. Choose $a_n,a_{n-1},\dots,a_1$ in this order. When $a_{j+2},\dots,a_n$ are already chosen, the condition $s_j\in\mathbb Z$ reads $j!\,a_{j+1}\in\mathbb Z-r_j$ for a fixed real $r_j$; as $a_{j+1}$ runs over $[0,1)$, $j!\,a_{j+1}$ runs over $[0,j!)$, which contains exactly $j!$ numbers of $\mathbb Z-r_j$. So $a_{j+1}$ has exactly $j!$ choices, whatever the earlier choices were, and the total number is \[\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}j!=\boxed{1!\cdot2!\cdots(n-1)!}.\]
