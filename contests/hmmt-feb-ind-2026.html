Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

A line intersects the graph of $y=x^2+\frac{2}{x}$ at three distinct points. Given that the $x$-coordinates of two of the points are $6$ and $7$, respectively, compute the $x$-coordinate of the third point.

Show the answer

$-\frac{1}{21}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vieta on a cubic)

Let the line be $y=mx+c$. A point of the graph with abscissa $x\ne 0$ lies on the line exactly when \[x^2+\frac2x=mx+c\iff x^3-mx^2-cx+2=0 .\] The three intersection points have three distinct abscissas, all nonzero, and each is a root of this cubic; a cubic has at most three roots, so the three abscissas $6$, $7$, $r$ are exactly its roots. By Vieta's formulas the product of the roots is $-2$, so \[6\cdot 7\cdot r=-2,\qquad r=\boxed{-\frac{1}{21}}.\]

Solution 2 (Equal slopes)

Write $f(x)=x^2+\frac2x$. For $u\ne v$ (both nonzero) the slope of the chord between the graph points over $u$ and $v$ is \[\frac{f(u)-f(v)}{u-v}=\frac{u^2-v^2}{u-v}+\frac{\frac2u-\frac2v}{u-v}=u+v-\frac{2}{uv}.\] The three points lie on one line, so the chords over $\{6,7\}$, $\{6,r\}$ and $\{7,r\}$ have the same slope: \[6+r-\frac{2}{6r}=7+r-\frac{2}{7r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1=\frac{2}{7r}-\frac{1}{3r}=-\frac{1}{21r}.\] Hence $r=\boxed{-\frac{1}{21}}$. (Check: both slopes equal $13-\frac1{21}$.)

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Modular Equations and Solutions

Compute the second smallest positive integer $n$ such that

  • $n$ is divisible by $101$, and

  • the decimal representation of $n$ contains the number $2026$ as a consecutive sequence of digits.

Show the answer

$420261$

Show the solution

Solution (Check by number of digits)

Since $101\cdot 99=9999$, we have $10^4\equiv 1$ and $10^2\equiv -1\pmod{101}$; also $2026=20\cdot101+6\equiv 6$ and $10^5=10\cdot 10^4\equiv 10\pmod{101}$.

Four or five digits. The only $4$-digit candidate is $2026\equiv 6$, not a multiple. A $5$-digit candidate is $2026d$ or $d2026$ with a digit $d$ ($d\ne 0$ in the second form). Now $2026d=10\cdot 2026+d\equiv 60+d$, which is never $0$ for $0\le d\le 9$; and $d2026=d\cdot 10^4+2026\equiv d+6$, also never $0$. So no multiple of $101$ below $10^5$ works.

Six digits. The block $2026$ sits in one of three places.

  • $\overline{2026ab}=202600+\overline{ab}$ and $202600=101\cdot 2005+95$, so we need $\overline{ab}\equiv 6$, i.e. $\overline{ab}=06$: the number $202606=101\cdot 2006$.

  • $\overline{a2026b}=a\cdot 10^5+20260+b\equiv 10a+60+b$, so $10a+b\equiv 41$; with $1\le a\le 9$, $0\le b\le 9$ the value $10a+b$ lies in $[10,99]$, so $10a+b=41$: the number $420261=101\cdot 4161$.

  • $\overline{ab2026}=\overline{ab}\cdot 10^4+2026\equiv \overline{ab}+6$, so $\overline{ab}=95$: the number $952026=101\cdot 9426$.

So the multiples of $101$ containing $2026$, in increasing order, begin $202606<420261<\cdots$, and the second smallest is $\boxed{420261}$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

Compute the sum of all positive integers $n$ such that $n$ has at least $6$ positive integer divisors and the $6$th largest divisor of $n$ is $6$.

Show the answer

$48$

Show the solution

Solution (Divisors below $n/6$)

Pair each divisor $d$ of $n$ with $n/d$. This reverses the order of the divisors, so the $6$th largest divisor is $6$ exactly when $6\mid n$ and exactly five divisors of $n$ are larger than $6$; equivalently, exactly five divisors $e$ of $n$ satisfy $e<n/6$ (the divisors larger than $6$ are the numbers $n/e$ with $e<n/6$).

Small $n$. If $n\le 36$, then $n/6\le 6$, so the divisors $e<n/6$ are among $1,2,3,4,5$; five of them would force $1,2,3,4,5\mid n$, i.e. $60\mid n$, impossible. So $n>36$ and $n/6>6$.

Now $1,2,3,6$ are four divisors below $n/6$, so there is exactly one more divisor $e<n/6$, and $e\notin\{1,2,3,6\}$.

  • If $4\mid n$, then $e=4$, so $5\nmid n$ and no divisor lies strictly between $6$ and $n/6$. Since $12\mid n$, we need $12\ge n/6$, i.e. $n\le 72$. The multiples of $12$ in $(36,72]$ not divisible by $5$ are $48$ and $72$. For $72$ the divisor $8$ lies below $n/6=12$, too many. For $48$: $n/6=8$ and the divisors below $8$ are exactly $1,2,3,4,6$. So $n=48$ works.

  • If $5\mid n$ but $4\nmid n$, then $e=5$ and $30\mid n$; the divisor $10$ must satisfy $10\ge n/6$, so $n\le 60$, i.e. $n=60$, but $4\mid 60$. No solutions.

  • If $4\nmid n$ and $5\nmid n$, write $n=6m$ with $m=n/6>6$; then $m$ is odd and $5\nmid m$, and exactly one divisor $e$ of $n$ lies strictly between $6$ and $m$. If $m$ is a prime $p$, the divisors of $6p$ below $p$ are only $1,2,3,6$: too few. Otherwise $m=qr$ with $q$ the smallest prime factor of $m$ and $r\ge q\ge 3$, $q\ne 5$. If $q\ge 7$, both $q$ and $2q$ are divisors in $(6,m)$ (as $2q<qr$). If $q=3$: $r=3$ gives $n=54$, whose divisors below $9$ are only $1,2,3,6$; $r=5$ is excluded; and $r\ge 7$ gives two divisors $r<2r$ in $(6,3r)$. No solutions.

Hence $n=48$ is the only such integer, and the sum is $\boxed{48}$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Elimination and Reduction

Let $a$, $b$, and $c$ be pairwise distinct complex numbers such that \[\begin{aligned} a^2+ab+b^2&=3(a+b),\\ a^2+ac+c^2&=3(a+c),\\ b^2+bc+c^2&=5(b+c)+1.\end{aligned}\] Compute $a$.

Show the answer

$\frac72$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two roots of one quadratic)

The first two equations say that $t=b$ and $t=c$ both satisfy \[t^2+at+a^2=3a+3t,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad t^2+(a-3)t+(a^2-3a)=0 .\] Since $b\ne c$, they are the two roots of this quadratic, so by Vieta \[b+c=3-a,\qquad bc=a^2-3a .\] The third equation is $(b+c)^2-bc=5(b+c)+1$, which becomes \[(3-a)^2-(a^2-3a)=5(3-a)+1\iff 9-3a=16-5a\iff a=\tfrac72 .\] Conversely, for $a=\frac72$ take $b,c$ to be the roots of $t^2+\frac12t+\frac74=0$; they are non-real and distinct (the discriminant is $\frac14-7<0$), hence also different from $a$, and the computation above runs backwards, so all three equations hold. Therefore $a=\boxed{\frac72}$.

Solution 2 (Subtract, then symmetric functions)

Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $a(b-c)+(b^2-c^2)=3(b-c)$; dividing by $b-c\ne 0$, \[a+b+c=3 .\] Then the first equation reads $a^2+ab+b^2=(a+b+c)(a+b)=(a+b)^2+c(a+b)$, i.e. $-ab=ca+cb$, so \[ab+bc+ca=0 .\] Hence $b+c=3-a$ and $bc=-a(b+c)=-a(3-a)$. The third equation is $(b+c)^2-bc=5(b+c)+1$: \[(3-a)^2+a(3-a)=5(3-a)+1\iff 3(3-a)=16-5a\iff a=\tfrac72 .\] This value is attained: with $b+c=-\frac12$, $bc=\frac74$ the numbers $b,c$ are distinct non-real conjugates, all three equations hold (each step above is reversible once $a+b+c=3$ and $ab+bc+ca=0$), and $a,b,c$ are pairwise distinct. So $a=\boxed{\frac72}$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Fermat and Euler Type Results

Compute the largest positive integer $n$ such that \[n\ \text{divides}\ \bigl(\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor\bigr)!^{\,n!}+450.\]

Show the answer

$1230$

Show the solution

Solution (Small primes and large primes)

Let $m=\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$, so $m^2\le n<(m+1)^2$, and let $N=m!^{\,n!}+450$.

Primes $p\le m$. If $p^k\,\|\,n$ with $p\le m$, then $p\mid m!$ and $n!\ge n\ge k$, so $p^k\mid m!^{\,n!}$. Hence $p^k\mid n\mid N$ forces $p^k\mid 450=2\cdot 3^2\cdot 5^2$.

Primes $p>m$. Then $p\ge m+1>\sqrt n$, so $p^2>n$ and $p$ divides $n$ at most once; also two such primes cannot both divide $n$ (their product would exceed $n$). For such a $p$ we have $p\nmid m!$ and $p-1\mid n!$ (as $p-1<p\le n$), so by Fermat $m!^{\,n!}\equiv 1\pmod p$, and $p\mid N$ means $p\mid 451=11\cdot 41$.

So $n=d$ or $n=dp$ with $d\mid 450$ and $p\in\{11,41\}$, $p>\sqrt n$.

  • $n=d\mid 450$ gives $n\le 450$.

  • $n=11d$ with $11>\sqrt{11d}$ gives $d<11$, so $n\le 110$.

  • $n=41d$ with $41>\sqrt{41d}$ gives $d<41$; the largest divisor of $450$ below $41$ is $30$, so $n\le 1230$.

Finally $n=1230=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot41$ works: $m=\lfloor\sqrt{1230}\rfloor=35$, so $2,3,5\mid 35!$ and $N\equiv 450\equiv 0$ modulo $2$, $3$ and $5$; and $41\nmid 35!$, $40\mid 1230!$, so $N\equiv 1+450=451=11\cdot 41\equiv 0\pmod{41}$. Thus the answer is $\boxed{1230}$.

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

The numbers $1, 2, \ldots, 2100$ are written on a board. Every second, Mark takes two numbers on the board, $a$ and $b$, erases them, and replaces them with $\gcd(a,b)$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$. Mark stops once any move he makes will not change the numbers written on the board. Compute the number of divisors of the $2026$th smallest positive integer written on the board when he finishes.

Show the answer

$3840$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Exponents are sorted prime by prime)

The process stops. If $a\nmid b$ and $b\nmid a$, then $g=\gcd(a,b)$ and $l=\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ satisfy $gl=ab$ and $g<\min(a,b)$, so $g+l>a+b$ (for a fixed product, the sum grows as the two numbers spread apart). So every move that changes the board increases the sum of the numbers, and every number always divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,2100)$; hence only finitely many changing moves are possible. Moves that change nothing do not affect the board, so Mark finishes as soon as he has made enough changing moves (he certainly finishes if he never wastes a move while a changing move exists), and the question concerns the board at that moment.

The final board. A move on $a,b$ changes nothing exactly when one divides the other. So when Mark finishes the $2100$ numbers form a chain under divisibility: sorted increasingly, each divides the next.

An invariant. Fix a prime $p$. Replacing $a,b$ by $\gcd(a,b),\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ replaces the exponents $v_p(a),v_p(b)$ by their minimum and maximum, so the multiset of $p$-exponents of the numbers on the board never changes. In the final chain the $p$-exponents increase along the chain, so the $k$th smallest final number has, for every prime $p$, the $k$th smallest exponent of $p$ among $v_p(1),\dots,v_p(2100)$.

The $2026$th number. It is the $75$th largest ($2100-2026+1=75$). Among $1,\dots,2100$ exactly $\lfloor 2100/p^e\rfloor$ numbers have $v_p\ge e$, so the $75$th largest exponent of $p$ is the largest $e$ with $\lfloor 2100/p^e\rfloor\ge 75$, i.e. with $p^e\le 28$. This gives $2^4,3^3,5^2$ and $7,11,13,17,19,23$ to the first power, so the number is \[2^4\cdot 3^3\cdot 5^2\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 19\cdot 23,\] with $5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot 2^6=\boxed{3840}$ divisors.

Solution 2 (Counting multiples of prime powers)

The process stops and ends in a chain. If $a\nmid b$ and $b\nmid a$, the pair $\gcd(a,b)<\min(a,b)$, $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ has the same product and a larger sum than $a,b$; all numbers stay divisors of $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,2100)$, so there are finitely many changing moves. Moves that change nothing leave the board as it is, so whenever Mark finishes (and he certainly does if he never wastes a move while a changing move exists) he has made finitely many changes and no move changes the board any more. So at the end any two numbers are comparable under divisibility: the final numbers $c_1\le c_2\le\dots\le c_{2100}$ satisfy $c_1\mid c_2\mid\cdots\mid c_{2100}$.

An invariant. For a prime power $q=p^e$ let $N_q$ be the number of entries divisible by $q$. A move on $a,b$ keeps $N_q$: $q$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ iff it divides $a$ or $b$, and divides $\gcd(a,b)$ iff it divides both. So $N_q=\lfloor 2100/q\rfloor$ forever.

Reading off $c_{2026}$. In the chain, the entries divisible by $q$ form a final segment $c_j,\dots,c_{2100}$ of length $N_q$. So $q\mid c_{2026}$ iff the last $75$ entries $c_{2026},\dots,c_{2100}$ are all divisible by $q$, iff $\lfloor 2100/q\rfloor\ge 75$, iff $q\le 28$. Therefore \[c_{2026}=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,28)=2^4\cdot3^3\cdot5^2\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\cdot19\cdot23,\] whose number of divisors is $5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2^6=\boxed{3840}$.

Problem 7

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Elementary Symmetric Substitution

Positive real numbers $x$, $y$, and $z$ satisfy the following equations: \[\begin{aligned} xyz&=3,\\ (x-y)(y-z)(z-x)&=4,\\ (x+y)(y+z)(z+x)&=40.\end{aligned}\] Compute the minimum possible value for $x$.

Show the answer

$\sqrt[3]{\frac{3-\sqrt6}{3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{3+\sqrt6}}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Ratios of the variables)

Put $L=(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)$ and $K=(x-y)(y-z)(z-x)$. Expanding, \[L+K=2(xy^2+yz^2+zx^2)+2xyz,\qquad L-K=2(x^2y+y^2z+z^2x)+2xyz .\] With $L=40$, $K=4$, $xyz=3$ this gives $xy^2+yz^2+zx^2=19$ and $x^2y+y^2z+z^2x=15$. Divide by $xyz=3$ and set $u=\frac xy$, $v=\frac yz$, $w=\frac zx$ (so $uvw=1$): \[u+v+w=\frac yz+\frac zx+\frac xy=\frac{19}{3},\qquad uv+vw+wu=\frac1u+\frac1v+\frac1w=\frac xz+\frac yx+\frac zy=5 .\] So $u,v,w$ are the roots of $3t^3-19t^2+15t-3=(3t-1)(t^2-6t+3)$, namely $\frac13$ and $3\pm\sqrt6$, in some order.

Conversely, for any assignment of these three numbers to $u,v,w$ the positive reals $x=\sqrt[3]{3u/w}$, $y=x/u$, $z=y/v$ satisfy $xyz=3$ (since $x^3=xyz\cdot\frac xy\cdot\frac xz$), and the two sums above, hence $L=40$ and $K=4$. Since $x^3=3\cdot\frac{u}{w}$ with $u\ne w$ taken from $\{\frac13,3-\sqrt6,3+\sqrt6\}$, the minimum is at $u=\frac13$, $w=3+\sqrt6$: \[x^3=\frac{1}{3+\sqrt6}=\frac{3-\sqrt6}{3},\qquad x_{\min}=\boxed{\sqrt[3]{\frac{3-\sqrt6}{3}}}\approx 0.568 .\]

Solution 2 (Divide each factor by a variable)

Let $u=\frac xy$, $v=\frac yz$, $w=\frac zx$, so $uvw=1$. Dividing the two cubic equations by $xyz=3$, with $\frac{x+y}{y}=1+u$, $\frac{y+z}{z}=1+v$, $\frac{z+x}{x}=1+w$ (and the same with minus signs), \[(1+u)(1+v)(1+w)=\frac{40}{3},\qquad (u-1)(v-1)(w-1)=\frac43 .\] Write $s=u+v+w$ and $q=uv+vw+wu$. Expanding with $uvw=1$: $2+s+q=\frac{40}3$ and $1-q+s-1=\frac43$, so $s=\frac{19}3$ and $q=5$. Thus $u,v,w$ are the roots of $t^3-\frac{19}{3}t^2+5t-1$; since $t=\frac13$ is a root, the polynomial is $\frac13(3t-1)(t^2-6t+3)$, and the roots are $\frac13$, $3-\sqrt6\approx0.55$, $3+\sqrt6\approx5.45$.

Now $x^3=xyz\cdot\frac xy\cdot\frac xz=3\cdot\frac uw$. Every ordering of the three roots as $(u,v,w)$ gives positive reals $x,y,z$ (via $x^3=3u/w$, $y=x/u$, $z=y/v$) satisfying all three equations, because the equations depend only on $s$, $q$ and $uvw$. So the possible values of $x^3$ are the numbers $3u/w$ with $u\ne w$ from the root set, and the smallest is \[3\cdot\frac{1/3}{3+\sqrt6}=\frac1{3+\sqrt6}.\] Hence the minimum of $x$ is $\boxed{\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{3+\sqrt6}}}$.

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Telescoping Sums and Products

Let $a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots$ be the unique sequence of nonnegative integers less than $397$ with $a_0=1$ and \[a_{n+1}(a_n+1)^2\equiv a_n \pmod{397}\] for all nonnegative integers $n$. Given that $a_{2026}=9$, compute the remainder when $a_0+a_1+\cdots+a_{2026}$ is divided by $397$.

Show the answer

$279$

Show the solution

Solution (Reciprocals telescope)

Work modulo the prime $p=397$. If some $a_n$ were $0$, then $a_{n+1}(a_n+1)^2\equiv 0$ gives $a_{n+1}=0$, and all later terms would vanish, contradicting $a_{2026}=9$. So every $a_n$ ($0\le n\le 2026$) is invertible, and for $n\le 2025$ also $a_n+1\not\equiv 0$ (otherwise $a_n\equiv 0$). Hence \[\frac1{a_{n+1}}\equiv\frac{(a_n+1)^2}{a_n}=a_n+2+\frac1{a_n},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a_n+2\equiv\frac1{a_{n+1}}-\frac1{a_n}.\] Summing over $n=0,\dots,2025$ telescopes: \[\sum_{n=0}^{2025}a_n+2\cdot 2026\equiv\frac1{a_{2026}}-\frac1{a_0}=\frac19-1 .\] Since $9\cdot 44=396\equiv-1$, we have $\frac19\equiv-44$. Therefore \[\sum_{n=0}^{2026}a_n\equiv -44-1-4052+9=-4088\equiv -4088+11\cdot 397=279\pmod{397},\] and the remainder is $\boxed{279}$.

Problem 9

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Discrete Summation Identities

Compute \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-\lfloor 101k/1\rfloor}+2^{-\lfloor 101k/2\rfloor}+\cdots+2^{-\lfloor 101k/100\rfloor}\right).\]

Show the answer

$50-\frac{50}{2^{101}-1}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (How often each exponent occurs)

Group the terms by the exponent: the sum is $\sum_{n\ge1}c_n2^{-n}$, where $c_n$ is the number of pairs $(j,k)$ with $1\le j\le 100$, $k\ge1$ and $\lfloor 101k/j\rfloor=n$, i.e. $nj\le 101k<(n+1)j$. For fixed $j$ the interval $[nj,nj+j)$ has length $j<101$, so it contains at most one multiple of $101$; thus $c_n$ counts the $j$ for which $[nj,nj+j)$ contains a multiple of $101$. With $r$ the remainder of $nj$ mod $101$, this happens iff $r=0$ or $r>101-j$.

Pair $j$ with $j'=101-j$. Then $nj'\equiv -nj$, so its remainder is $r'=0$ if $r=0$ and $r'=101-r$ otherwise; $j'$ counts iff $r=0$ or $101-r>j$, i.e. $r<101-j$. Hence:

  • if $101\mid n$ (so $r=0$), both $j$ and $j'$ count;

  • if $r=101-j$, i.e. $101\mid (n+1)j$, i.e. $101\mid n+1$, neither counts;

  • otherwise exactly one of them counts.

With $50$ pairs, $c_n=100$ if $101\mid n$, $c_n=0$ if $101\mid n+1$, and $c_n=50$ otherwise. Therefore \[\sum_{n\ge1}c_n2^{-n}=50\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}+50\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-101m}-50\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-101m+1}=50-50\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-101m}=\boxed{50-\frac{50}{2^{101}-1}} .\]

Solution 2 (Complementary floor sequences)

Fix $j\in\{1,\dots,50\}$ and put $j'=101-j$. We show that, among the numbers $\lfloor 101k/j\rfloor$ and $\lfloor 101k/j'\rfloor$ ($k\ge1$), each positive integer $N$ occurs once, except that multiples of $101$ occur twice and numbers $\equiv -1\pmod{101}$ do not occur.

For $x>0$, the number of $k\ge1$ with $\lfloor 101k/j\rfloor\le N$, i.e. $k<\frac{(N+1)j}{101}$, is $\lceil x\rceil-1$ with $x=\frac{(N+1)j}{101}$; for $j'$ it is $\lceil (N+1)-x\rceil-1$. If $x$ is not an integer, the total is $\lceil x\rceil+\lceil N+1-x\rceil-2=N$; if $x$ is an integer, i.e. $101\mid N+1$ (as $\gcd(j,101)=1$), the total is $N-1$. So the number of terms $\le N$ is $F(N)=N-[\,101\mid N+1\,]$, and the number of terms equal to $N$ is \[F(N)-F(N-1)=1-[\,101\mid N+1\,]+[\,101\mid N\,].\] Summing $2^{-N}$ over the terms of the pair $\{j,j'\}$ therefore gives \[\sum_{N\ge1}2^{-N}+\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-101m}-\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-(101m-1)}=1-\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-101m}=1-\frac1{2^{101}-1}.\] The $100$ values of $j$ form $50$ such pairs, so the whole sum is $50\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{101}-1}\right)=\boxed{50-\frac{50}{2^{101}-1}}$.

Problem 10

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Quadratic Residues

Let \[S=\sum_{k=0}^{2026}k\binom{2k}{k}2^k.\] Compute the remainder when $S$ is divided by $2027$. (Note that $2027$ is prime.)

Show the answer

$289$

Show the solution

Solution (Central binomials modulo $p$)

Let $p=2027$ and $h=\frac{p-1}2=1013$, so $2h\equiv-1\pmod p$.

Step 1: $\binom{2k}{k}\equiv(-4)^k\binom hk\pmod p$ for $0\le k\le p-1$. For $k>h$ we have $2k\ge p>k$, so $p$ divides $(2k)!$ but not $(k!)^2$, and both sides are $0$ (since $\binom hk=0$). For $k\le h$, \[\binom{2k}{k}=\frac{2^k\cdot 1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k-1)}{k!},\qquad 2i-1\equiv-(p-2i+1)=-2(h+1-i),\] so $1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1)\equiv(-2)^k\,h(h-1)\cdots(h-k+1)$ and $\binom{2k}k\equiv 2^k(-2)^k\binom hk$, as claimed ($k!$ is invertible mod $p$).

Step 2: the sum. Hence, with $\binom hk=0$ for $k>h$, \[S\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{h}k\binom hk(-8)^k=h\cdot(-8)\cdot(1-8)^{h-1}=-8h\cdot 7^{h-1},\] using $\sum_k k\binom hk y^k=hy(1+y)^{h-1}$ and that $h-1$ is even. Since $2h\equiv-1$, $-8h\equiv 4$, so $S\equiv 4\cdot 7^{h-1}=\frac47\cdot 7^{h}$.

Step 3: Euler's criterion. $7^h\equiv\left(\frac{7}{2027}\right)$. Both $7$ and $2027$ are $\equiv 3\pmod 4$, so quadratic reciprocity gives $\left(\frac{7}{2027}\right)=-\left(\frac{2027}{7}\right)=-\left(\frac{4}{7}\right)=-1$ (as $2027=7\cdot289+4$). Jacopo is rolling a fair $4$-sided die with faces labeled $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$. He starts with a score of $0$. Every time he rolls a face with label $i$, he adds $i$ to his score, and then replaces the label of that face with $0$. Compute Jacopo's expected score after $4$ rolls.

Show the answer

$\frac{875}{128}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Linearity of expectation)

Face $i$ contributes $i$ to the score exactly once if it is rolled at least once in the $4$ rolls (its label then becomes $0$), and $0$ otherwise. The chance that face $i$ is never rolled is $\left(\frac34\right)^4=\frac{81}{256}$. By linearity, \[E=\sum_{i=1}^4 i\left(1-\frac{81}{256}\right)=10\cdot\frac{175}{256}=\boxed{\frac{875}{128}} .\]

Solution 2 (Count distinct faces, then use symmetry)

The score is the sum of the original labels of the distinct faces that appeared. Let $D$ be the number of distinct faces among the $4$ rolls. Given $D=d$, every set of $d$ faces is equally likely by symmetry, so each face is in the set with probability $\frac d4$ and the conditional expected score is $\frac d4(1+2+3+4)=\frac{5d}{2}$. Hence $E=\frac52\,E[D]$.

Counting the $256$ roll sequences by $D$: $D=1$: $4$; $D=2$: $\binom42(2^4-2)=84$; $D=3$: $\binom43\cdot\binom42\cdot 3!=144$; $D=4$: $4!=24$. So \[E[D]=\frac{4+168+432+96}{256}=\frac{700}{256},\qquad E=\frac52\cdot\frac{700}{256}=\boxed{\frac{875}{128}} .\]

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Permutations and Arrangements

The numbers $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$, $6$, and $7$ are written on a blackboard in some order. Jacob repeatedly swaps numbers at adjacent positions on the blackboard until the numbers are sorted in ascending order. Compute the number of initial orderings for which it is possible that the number $4$ was included in a swap at most once.

Show the answer

$324$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversions involving 4)

Call $x$ in conflict with $4$ if $x>4$ stands left of $4$ or $x<4$ stands right of $4$ initially. An adjacent swap changes the relative order of only the two numbers swapped, and the final order is sorted, so each conflicting $x$ must be swapped with $4$ an odd number of times, in particular at least once; each swap involving $4$ handles one partner. So $4$ is in at least as many swaps as conflicts. Conversely bubble sort (swap adjacent pairs that are out of order) swaps each out-of-order pair exactly once, so $4$ can be in exactly that many swaps. We count orderings with at most one conflict.

Let $4$ be in position $i$ with $a$ larger numbers before it. Then $i-1-a$ smaller numbers are before it and $3-(i-1-a)$ smaller numbers after it, so the number of conflicts is $a+(4-i+a)=2a+4-i$.

  • $0$ conflicts: $i=4$, $a=0$: left part $\{1,2,3\}$, right part $\{5,6,7\}$: $3!\cdot3!=36$.

  • $1$ conflict with $a=0$: $i=3$; two of $1,2,3$ on the left, the rest on the right: $\binom32\cdot2!\cdot4!=144$.

  • $1$ conflict with $a=1$: $i=5$; the left holds $1,2,3$ and one of $5,6,7$: $3\cdot4!\cdot2!=144$.

(There are no other cases: at most three smaller numbers precede $4$, so $i-1-a\le3$, i.e. $i\le a+4$, and the number of conflicts is $2a+4-i\ge a$. So at most one conflict forces $a\le1$; for $a=0$ the count $4-i\le1$ with $i\le4$ gives $i\in\{3,4\}$, and for $a=1$ the count $6-i\le1$ with $i\le5$ gives $i=5$.) Total: $36+144+144=\boxed{324}$.

Solution 2 (Insert 4 into the other six)

As in any sorting by adjacent swaps, $4$ must be swapped at least once with every number it is out of order with (a larger number to its left or a smaller one to its right), and bubble sort achieves exactly this many swaps for $4$, because it swaps each out-of-order pair once. So we count orderings in which $4$ is out of order with at most one number.

Start from an ordering of $1,2,3,5,6,7$ and insert $4$ into one of the $7$ gaps. In the leftmost gap $4$ is out of order with the three small numbers: count $3$. Moving $4$ one gap to the right past a small number lowers the count by $1$; past a large number it raises it by $1$. So, reading the six numbers as steps D (small) and U (large), the count at gap $g$ is $3-(\#D-\#U)$ among the first $g$ steps, and we need $\#D-\#U\ge 2$ there.

Count pairs (step pattern with three D's and three U's, gap $g$) with this property: $g=2$: first two steps DD, then any arrangement of one D and three U's: $4$; $g=3$: DDD then UUU: $1$; $g=4$: three D's and one U in the first four, then UU: $4$; other $g$ give a difference of at most $1$. That is $9$ pairs, and each pattern corresponds to $3!\cdot3!$ orderings of the six numbers. Total $9\cdot 36=\boxed{324}$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure · Expectation Arguments

Sarunyu has a stick of length $1$ with one endpoint marked in red. Every minute, he picks one of his sticks uniformly at random and breaks it into two halves of equal length. Compute the expected length of the stick with the red endpoint after $5$ minutes.

Show the answer

$\frac{63}{256}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Independent halving factors)

During minute $t$ ($t=1,\dots,5$) there are $t$ sticks, one of which carries the red endpoint, and the chosen stick is uniform among them. If the red stick is chosen it is halved (the red end stays on one half); otherwise its length is unchanged. So the red stick's length is multiplied by a factor $F_t$ with $P(F_t=\frac12)=\frac1t$, $P(F_t=1)=1-\frac1t$. Whatever happened in the first $t-1$ minutes, exactly one of the $t$ sticks is red and it is chosen with probability $\frac1t$; since this conditional law does not depend on the history, $F_t$ is independent of $F_1,\dots,F_{t-1}$. Hence \[E\Bigl[\prod_{t=1}^5F_t\Bigr]=\prod_{t=1}^{5}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{2t}\Bigr)=\frac12\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac56\cdot\frac78\cdot\frac9{10}=\frac{945}{3840}=\boxed{\frac{63}{256}} .\]

Solution 2 (A random point stays with the red end)

Pick a point $U$ uniformly on the original stick, independent of everything. The expected length of the red stick equals the probability that $U$ lies on the red stick at the end (condition on the final red stick, whose length is exactly the conditional probability).

Track the event that $U$ is still on the red stick. Suppose after $t-1$ minutes it is, and condition on the current sticks: then $U$ is uniform on the red stick. In minute $t$ (with $t$ sticks present) the red stick is chosen with probability $\frac1t$, and then $U$ stays with the red half with probability $\frac12$. So the event survives minute $t$ with probability $1-\frac1t\cdot\frac12$, and after a survival $U$ is again uniform on the (new) red stick. Multiplying, \[P=\prod_{t=1}^5\frac{2t-1}{2t}=\frac{1\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot9}{2\cdot4\cdot6\cdot8\cdot10}=\frac{\binom{10}{5}}{4^5}=\frac{252}{1024}=\boxed{\frac{63}{256}} .\]

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Explicit Bijections

Let $S$ be the set of positive integer divisors of $10^9$. Compute the number of subsets $T$ of $S$ such that

  • for every element $s$ of $S$, exactly one of $s$ and $10^9/s$ is in $T$, and

  • for every element $t$ of $T$, all positive integer divisors of $t$ are in $T$.

Show the answer

$252$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Column heights)

Write each divisor as $2^a5^b$ with $0\le a,b\le 9$, so $S$ is a $10\times10$ grid and $10^9/s$ corresponds to $(9-a,9-b)$. The second condition says $T$ is closed downward: if $(a,b)\in T$ then $(a',b')\in T$ whenever $a'\le a$, $b'\le b$. Such a set is described by the column heights $h_a=\#\{b:(a,b)\in T\}$: column $a$ of $T$ is $\{0,\dots,h_a-1\}$, and $10\ge h_0\ge h_1\ge\dots\ge h_9\ge 0$.

The first condition says $(a,b)\in T\iff(9-a,9-b)\notin T$, i.e. $b<h_a\iff 9-b\ge h_{9-a}$, i.e. $b<h_a\iff b\le 9-h_{9-a}$, i.e. $h_a=10-h_{9-a}$ for all $a$.

So $T$ is determined by $h_0\ge\dots\ge h_4$, with $h_5,\dots,h_9$ forced to be $10-h_4,\dots,10-h_0$; the whole sequence is nonincreasing iff $h_4\ge 10-h_4$, i.e. $h_4\ge5$. The number of nonincreasing sequences $10\ge h_0\ge\dots\ge h_4\ge 5$ (five terms from six values) is $\binom{5+6-1}{5}=\boxed{252}$.

Solution 2 (A symmetric staircase)

Draw the divisors $2^a5^b$ ($0\le a,b\le9$) as unit cells of a $10\times10$ board. A set $T$ closed under taking divisors is a “staircase” anchored at the corner cell $(0,0)$, and its boundary with the complement is a lattice path of $20$ unit steps (ten in each direction) across the board from one side corner to the opposite one; different sets give different paths and every such path gives such a set.

The map $s\mapsto 10^9/s$ is the half-turn of the board about its centre. The first condition says exactly that the half-turn maps $T$ onto the complement of $T$ (no cell is fixed, as the centre is not a cell centre). The half-turn maps the boundary of $T$ to the boundary of its image, so the condition holds iff the boundary path is mapped to itself. Rotating a monotone path by $180^\circ$ and reading it from its new start reverses the sequence of steps and keeps each step's type, so the path is invariant iff its step sequence is a palindrome.

A palindrome of length $20$ is determined by its first $10$ steps, which must contain $5$ steps of each kind (so that the whole path has $10$ of each). Hence there are $\binom{10}{5}=\boxed{252}$ sets $T$.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

Derek currently owes $\pi$ units of a currency called Money of Indiscrete Type, or MIT for short. Every day, the following happens:

  • He flips a fair coin to decide how much of his debt to pay. If he flips heads, he decreases his debt by $1$ MIT. If he flips tails, he decreases his debt by $2$ MITs. If Derek's debt ever becomes nonpositive, Derek becomes debt-free.

  • Afterwards, his remaining debt doubles.

Compute the probability that Derek ever becomes debt-free. (MITs are continuous, so the debt is never rounded.)

Show the answer

$\frac{4-\pi}{2}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A self-similar probability)

Let $p(x)$ be the probability of becoming debt-free from debt $x>0$.

If $x\le 2$, a tails frees him; a heads (when $x>1$) leads to debt $2(x-1)\le 2$ again. So from any debt in $(0,2]$ he stays in $(0,2]$ until the first tails, which comes eventually with probability $1$: $p(x)=1$ for $0<x\le2$. If $x\ge4$, the new debt is $2(x-1)>4$ or $2(x-2)\ge4$, so it never drops below $4$: $p(x)=0$.

For $2<x<4$ put $v=\frac{4-x}{2}\in(0,1)$ and $P(v)=p(x)$; extend by $P(v)=0$ for $v\le0$ and $P(v)=1$ for $v\ge1$, matching the two facts above. A tails gives debt $2x-4$, i.e. new $v'=2v$; a heads gives debt $2x-2$, i.e. $v'=2v-1$. So \[P(v)=\tfrac12P(2v)+\tfrac12P(2v-1)\qquad(0<v<1).\] If $v<\frac12$ this reads $P(v)=\frac12P(2v)$; if $v\ge\frac12$ it reads $P(v)=\frac12+\frac12P(2v-1)$. Writing $v=0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots$ in binary, both cases say $P(v)=\frac{b_1}{2}+\frac12P(T v)$, where $Tv=0.b_2b_3\ldots$ (when $2v=1$ or $2v-1=0$ the boundary values $P(1)=1$, $P(0)=0$ give the same). Iterating $N$ times, \[P(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{b_i}{2^i}+\frac{1}{2^N}P(T^Nv),\] and letting $N\to\infty$ (as $0\le P\le1$) gives $P(v)=v$. For $x=\pi$: $p(\pi)=\boxed{\frac{4-\pi}{2}}\approx 0.429$.

Solution 2 (Comparing with a random binary number)

Let $v=\frac{4-\pi}{2}\approx0.429$. While the debt $x$ is in $(2,4)$, write $v_n=\frac{4-x}{2}$ after $n$ days. A tails changes the debt to $2x-4$, i.e. $v\mapsto 2v$; a heads changes it to $2x-2$, i.e. $v\mapsto 2v-1$. If the debt reaches $(0,2]$ (that is, $v_n\ge1$) he is eventually debt-free with probability $1$ (from $x\le2$ every tails frees him and every heads keeps the debt at most $2$). If the debt reaches $[4,\infty)$ (that is, $v_n\le0$) it never goes below $4$ again, so he is never free.

Let $h_i=1$ if day $i$ is heads and $0$ if tails, and let $U=0.h_1h_2h_3\ldots$ in binary: $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. By induction, as long as the process is in the middle range, \[v_n=2^n\bigl(v-0.h_1h_2\ldots h_n\bigr).\] So the middle range persists while $0.h_1\ldots h_n<v<0.h_1\ldots h_n+2^{-n}$, i.e. while $v$ lies inside the dyadic interval of length $2^{-n}$ that contains $U$. The first time it does not, either $v\ge$ the right end (win, $v_n\ge1$) or $v\le$ the left end (loss, $v_n\le0$). Since $v$ is irrational it differs from $U$ almost surely, and then he wins exactly when $U<v$. Therefore the probability is $P(U<v)=v=\boxed{\frac{4-\pi}{2}}$.

Problem 7

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Let $S$ be the set of vertices of a right prism whose bases are regular decagons $A_1A_2\ldots A_{10}$ and $B_1B_2\ldots B_{10}$. A plane, not passing through any vertex of $S$, partitions the vertices of $S$ into two sets (one of which may be empty), one of which is $M$. Compute the number of possible sets $M$ that can arise out of such a partition.

Show the answer

$1574$

Show the solution

Solution (Reduce to caps of one decagon)

Reading of the statement. We count the sets $M$ of vertices lying in one of the two open half-spaces bounded by the plane, including $M=\emptyset$ and $M=S$ (a plane may miss the prism altogether). This is the reading of the official answer; if both parts must be nonempty, subtract $2$ to get $1572$.

Let $V_1,\dots,V_{10}$ be the vertices of a regular decagon in the plane $z=0$, with $B_i=(V_i,0)$ and $A_i=(V_i,h)$. An open half-space is $\{(v,z):u\cdot v+\gamma z>c\}$ with $u\in\mathbb R^2$, $\gamma,c\in\mathbb R$ not all of $u,\gamma$ zero. The set $M$ of vertices on one side of a plane avoiding all vertices is such a half-space intersected with $S$. Its top part is $\{i:u\cdot V_i>c-\gamma h\}$ and its bottom part is $\{i:u\cdot V_i>c\}$.

Call a subset $X$ of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ a $u$-cap if $X=\{i:u\cdot V_i>t\}$ for some real $t$ ($\emptyset$ and the full set are $u$-caps for every $u$). Since $c-\gamma h$ and $c$ can be chosen independently, the possible $M$ correspond exactly to ordered pairs (top part, bottom part) $=(X,Y)$ such that $X$ and $Y$ are $u$-caps for one common $u$ (if $u=0$, both parts are $\emptyset$ or everything, which is also covered); thresholds avoiding the finitely many values $u\cdot V_i$ give a plane through no vertex. For a fixed $u$ the caps are nested, so $X\subseteq Y$ or $Y\subseteq X$.

Which caps occur. Put the vertices at angles $36^\circ i$. A cap with $k$ elements, $1\le k\le 9$, is a set of $k$ consecutive vertices (an arc). For an arc with middle direction $\mu$, its end vertices are at angular distance $18(k-1)^\circ$ from $\mu$ and the nearest vertices outside it at $18(k+1)^\circ$. If $u$ points in direction $\mu+\delta$, with $\delta$ the signed shortest angular displacement from $\mu$ (so $|\delta|\le180^\circ$), the arc is a cap iff every arc vertex is closer in angle to $u$ than every outside vertex, i.e. $18(k-1)+|\delta|<18(k+1)-|\delta|$, i.e. $|\delta|<18^\circ$. So each arc has an open window of directions of width $36^\circ$ centred at its middle direction, and two arcs are caps for a common $u$ iff their windows overlap, i.e. their middle directions differ by less than $36^\circ$.

Counting. There are $90$ arcs with $1\le k\le9$, plus $\emptyset$ and the full set.

  • $X=Y$: $92$ pairs.

  • $X\subsetneq Y$, both proper nonempty arcs: let $Y$ have $j$ vertices and $X$ have $i<j$ vertices at offset $t$ inside $Y$ ($0\le t\le j-i$). The middle directions differ by $36\,|t-\frac{j-i}{2}|$ degrees, which is less than $36$ for exactly one $t$ if $j-i$ is even and two values if $j-i$ is odd. For fixed $j$ the number of $X$ is $\sum_{d=1}^{j-1}(1\text{ or }2)$, which is $2,3,5,6,8,9,11,12$ for $j=2,\dots,9$, total $56$; with $10$ choices of $Y$ for each size, $560$ pairs.

  • $X=\emptyset$ with $Y\ne\emptyset$: $91$; $Y$ the full set with $X$ a proper nonempty arc: $90$.

So there are $560+91+90=741$ nested pairs with $X\subsetneq Y$, the same number with $Y\subsetneq X$, and the total is $92+2\cdot741=\boxed{1574}$. This includes the pairs $(\emptyset,\emptyset)$ and $(\text{all},\text{all})$, i.e. $M=\emptyset$ and $M=S$, which come from planes missing the prism.

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

A regular hexagon with side length $4$ is subdivided into a lattice of $96$ equilateral triangles of side length $1$. Let $S$ be the set of all vertices of this lattice. Compute the number of nondegenerate triangles with vertices in $S$ that contain the center of the hexagon strictly in their interior.

Show the answer

$6992$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the triples in a half-plane)

Let $O$ be the centre. The $61$ points of $S$ are $O$ and $60$ others, and $S$ is symmetric under the half-turn about $O$. Group the $60$ points by the ray from $O$ through them. Using unit vectors $e_1,e_2$ at $60^\circ$, the points in the closed sector between $e_1$ and $e_2$ are $pe_1+qe_2$ with $p,q\ge0$ and $p+q\le4$; the ray through such a point is determined by the primitive vector $(p,q)/\gcd(p,q)$, and a ray whose primitive vector has $p+q=m$ contains $\lfloor 4/m\rfloor$ points of $S$. In each of the six sectors (counting the ray along $e_1$ but not the one along $e_2$) the rays are: $e_1$ with $4$ points; $e_1+e_2$ with $2$ points; $2e_1+e_2$, $e_1+2e_2$, $3e_1+e_2$, $e_1+3e_2$ with $1$ point each. So there are $36$ rays: $6$ with $4$ points, $6$ with $2$ points and $24$ with $1$ point ($24+12+24=60$), and the opposite ray of each ray carries the same number of points.

A triangle whose vertices are in $S$ contains $O$ strictly inside iff $O$ is not a vertex and the three vertices do not lie in one closed half-plane bounded by a line through $O$. (If $O$ is strictly inside, every line through $O$ has vertices strictly on both sides. If $O$ is outside the closed triangle, a line strictly separating $O$ from the triangle, translated to pass through $O$, leaves the triangle in one closed half-plane; if $O$ is on the boundary, the line of an edge through $O$ does this.) Degenerate triples lie on a line, hence in such a half-plane, so the answer is $\binom{60}{3}$ minus the number $Z$ of triples of non-centre points lying in a closed half-plane through $O$.

For such a triple, call a ray $\rho$ through one of its points a start if all three points lie in the closed half-plane swept counterclockwise from $\rho$ through $180^\circ$. Each such triple has exactly one start, except triples lying on one line through $O$ with points on both opposite rays, which have two. For a ray $\rho$ with $m$ points, the points strictly inside that half-plane or on the opposite ray number $\frac{60-2m}{2}+m=30$ (half of the points off the line, by symmetry, plus the opposite ray). The triples with start $\rho$ are those using at least one point of $\rho$ and the rest from these $30$: $\binom{m+30}{3}-\binom{30}{3}$. Thus \[Z=6\cdot1924+6\cdot900+24\cdot435-156=27228,\qquad \binom{60}{3}-Z=34220-27228=\boxed{6992}.\]

Using unit vectors $e_1,e_2$ at $60^\circ$, the points in the closed sector between $e_1$ and $e_2$ are $pe_1+qe_2$ with $p,q\ge0$ and $p+q\le4$; the ray through such a point is determined by the primitive vector $(p,q)/\gcd(p,q)$, and a ray whose primitive vector has $p+q=m$ contains $\lfloor 4/m\rfloor$ points of $S$. Call a triple of non-centre points spread if its three rays are distinct. A spread triple is of exactly one of three kinds: (i) the triangle contains $O$ strictly inside (all three angular gaps between consecutive rays are less than $180^\circ$); (ii) two of the rays are opposite; (iii) all three rays lie in an open half-plane (one gap exceeds $180^\circ$), a narrow triple. Triples that are not spread never contain $O$ strictly inside (two vertices on one ray put $O$ outside or on an edge line), so the answer is the number $T$ of kind (i).

So $T+3T=31832-3864=27968$ and $T=\boxed{6992}$.

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots$ be a sequence of finite nonempty sets of positive integers. Given that $|A_i\cap A_j|=\gcd(i,j)$ for all (not necessarily distinct) positive integers $i$ and $j$, compute the minimum possible value of \[\sum_{d\mid 250}\max A_d,\] where the sum ranges over all positive integer divisors $d$ of $250$.

(For a finite nonempty set $S$, we define $\max S$ as the largest element of $S$.)

Show the answer

$499$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pair up the divisors)

Taking $i=j$ gives $|A_i|=i$. For divisors $d$ of $250=2\cdot5^3$ group the eight sets as \[A_1,\quad (A_2,A_5),\quad (A_{10},A_{25}),\quad (A_{50},A_{125}),\quad A_{250}.\] A set of $k$ positive integers has maximum at least $k$, so $\max A_1\ge1$ and $\max A_{250}\ge250$. For a pair $(A_{2\cdot5^k},A_{5^{k+1}})$ with $k=0,1,2$, the union has \[2\cdot5^k+5^{k+1}-\gcd(2\cdot5^k,5^{k+1})=6\cdot5^k\] elements, so the larger of the two maxima is at least $6\cdot5^k$, while the other is at least the size of its own set, which is at least $\min(2\cdot5^k,5^{k+1})=2\cdot5^k$. So each pair contributes at least $8\cdot5^k$, and \[\sum_{d\mid250}\max A_d\ge 1+8+40+200+250=499 .\] Construction. For every positive integer $d$ choose a block $B_d$ of $\varphi(d)$ positive integers, all blocks disjoint, with \[B_1=\{1\},\ B_2=\{2\},\ B_5=\{3,\dots,6\},\ B_{10}=\{7,\dots,10\},\ B_{25}=\{11,\dots,30\},\] \[B_{50}=\{31,\dots,50\},\ B_{125}=\{51,\dots,150\},\ B_{250}=\{151,\dots,250\},\] and all other blocks above $250$. Let $A_n=\bigcup_{d\mid n}B_d$. Then $|A_i\cap A_j|=\sum_{d\mid\gcd(i,j)}\varphi(d)=\gcd(i,j)$; here $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$ because writing each fraction $\frac jn$ ($1\le j\le n$) in lowest terms $\frac ad$ gives, for each $d\mid n$, exactly the $\varphi(d)$ numerators $1\le a\le d$ coprime to $d$. The maxima over $d\mid250$ are $1,2,6,10,30,50,150,250$, with sum $\boxed{499}$.

Solution 2 (The sets are forced to be unions of blocks)

Structure. From $i=j$ we get $|A_i|=i$. If $d\mid n$ then $|A_d\cap A_n|=d=|A_d|$, so $A_d\subseteq A_n$. For any $i,j$ the set $A_{\gcd(i,j)}$ lies in $A_i\cap A_j$ and has the same size, so $A_i\cap A_j=A_{\gcd(i,j)}$. Restrict to $d\mid250$ and let $U=A_{250}$. For $x\in U$ the divisors $d$ of $250$ with $x\in A_d$ are closed under $\gcd$, so they have a least element $\ell(x)$ (in the divisibility order), and $x\in A_n\iff\ell(x)\mid n$ for every $n\mid250$. With $B_d=\{x:\ell(x)=d\}$ we get $A_n=\bigcup_{d\mid n}B_d$ (disjoint) for $n\mid250$, so $\sum_{d\mid n}|B_d|=|A_n|=n$. Taking $n=1,2,5,10,25,50,125,250$ in turn and subtracting the sizes of the blocks of the proper divisors already found gives $|B_d|=1,1,4,4,20,20,100,100$ (this is $\varphi(d)$).

Optimisation. Now list the $250$ elements of $U$ in increasing order. For $n\mid250$, if the largest element of $A_n$ is the $k$th in this list, then $\max A_n\ge k$ (it exceeds $k-1$ distinct positive integers), and $k\ge|A_n|$ because all of $A_n$ has appeared by then. For a pair $(2\cdot5^k,5^{k+1})$, the set whose largest element comes later in the list has all of $A_{2\cdot5^k}\cup A_{5^{k+1}}$ listed by then, so its maximum is at least $|A_{2\cdot5^k}\cup A_{5^{k+1}}|=6\cdot5^k$, and the other maximum is at least the size of its own set. So \[\sum\max A_d\ge 1+(2+6)+(10+30)+(50+150)+250=499,\] and listing the blocks in the order $B_1,B_2,B_5,B_{10},B_{25},B_{50},B_{125},B_{250}$ (positions $1$; $2$; $3$–$6$; $7$–$10$; $11$–$30$; $31$–$50$; $51$–$150$; $151$–$250$) gives the maxima $1,2,6,10,30,50,150,250$. The remaining sets $A_n$, $n\nmid250$, are built from further disjoint blocks of sizes $\varphi(d)$ placed above $250$, which keeps $|A_i\cap A_j|=\sum_{d\mid\gcd(i,j)}\varphi(d)=\gcd(i,j)$. The minimum is $\boxed{499}$.

Problem 10

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Implicit or Structural Correspondence

Let $S$ be the set of all ordered pairs $(x,y)$ of nonnegative integers $0\le x\le 19$ and $0\le y\le 2$. Compute the number of permutations $(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \ldots, (x_{60},y_{60})$ of the elements of $S$ such that

  • $y_1=2$ and $y_{60}=0$;

  • for all nonnegative integers $1\le i\le 59$, exactly one of the following holds:

    • $x_i=x_{i+1}$ and $|y_i-y_{i+1}|=1$,

    • $y_i=y_{i+1}$ and $x_i-x_{i+1}$ is $-1$ or $19$.

Show the answer

$20460$

Show the solution

Solution (Unroll the cylinder)

Think of the pairs as cells of a cylinder: $20$ columns $x\in\mathbb Z/20$ and rows $y=0,1,2$. Allowed steps are up or down within a column, or one column to the right ($x\to x+1$, with $19\to0$). We count Hamiltonian paths from row $2$ to row $0$. By rotating the columns, the number of paths starting in column $c$ does not depend on $c$; we count paths starting at $(0,2)$ and multiply by $20$. Write $n=20$.

Unrolling. Lift the path to the strip $\mathbb Z\times\{0,1,2\}$, starting at $(0,2)$; it moves right $h$ times and ends in column $h$. Call $P$ the lifted path and $P_k=P+(kn,0)$ its translates. Since the path visits every cell of the cylinder once, the $P_k$ are pairwise disjoint and cover every lattice point of the strip. Each $P_k$ meets each column of its range in one vertical run (it never returns to a column). If $P_k$ and $P_{k+1}$ both meet a column, one run lies above the other, and the order cannot change from a column to the next (the two horizontal steps would cross). $P_{k+1}$ starts at the top cell of column $(k+1)n$, so $P_{k+1}$ is above $P_k$ throughout. Consequently, for $0\le u<n$ the column $u$ is cut, top to bottom, into $c(u)=\lfloor (h-u)/n\rfloor+1$ intervals, and the run of $P$ in column $u+jn$ is the $(j+1)$st interval from the top. Conversely, any $h$ and any such cuts give a Hamiltonian path, provided consecutive runs connect: each run is entered at one end of its interval and left at the other, the first run is entered at row $2$, the exit row of each run is the entry row of the next, and the last run is left at row $0$.

Write $h=qn+s$ with $0\le s<n$. Then $c(u)=q+1$ for $u\le s$ and $c(u)=q$ for $u>s$. Every column must be covered and has only $3$ cells, so every $c(u)\in\{1,2,3\}$; hence either $q=0$ and $s=n-1$, i.e. $h=n-1$ (all $c=1$), or $q=1$ ($c=2$ for $u\le s$, $c=1$ for $u>s$), or $q=2$ ($c=3$ for $u\le s$, $c=2$ for $u>s$, $s\le n-2$, since all $c=3$ would allow no vertical step at all). A column cut in two parts is of type $\mathsf A$ (parts $\{2\}$, $\{0,1\}$) or $\mathsf B$ (parts $\{1,2\}$, $\{0\}$).

$h=n-1$. Every run is a full column, so the exit rows alternate $0,2,0,\dots$; the last ($n$th) run exits at row $2$ since $n$ is even. Impossible.

$q=1$. The runs are: tops of columns $0..s$, then full columns $s+1..n-1$ (there are $f=n-1-s$ of them), then bottoms of columns $0..s$. In the top phase the row stays $2$ through $\mathsf A$ and switches $2\leftrightarrow1$ through $\mathsf B$; in the bottom phase it stays $0$ through $\mathsf B$ and switches $0\leftrightarrow1$ through $\mathsf A$. A full column must be entered at row $0$ or $2$, and the bottom phase entered at row $0$ or $1$. If $f\ge1$: the top phase must end at row $2$ (even number of $\mathsf B$'s); then $f$ must be odd to reach row $0$; then the bottom phase starts and must end at row $0$ (even number of $\mathsf A$'s). An $\mathsf A$ needs an even number of $\mathsf B$'s before it (top phase) and a $\mathsf B$ needs an even number of $\mathsf A$'s before it (bottom phase). Read the word in $\mathsf A,\mathsf B$ of length $m=s+1$ from the left: whenever both counts so far are even and the next letter is $X$, the letter after it (if any) must again be $X$, since the other letter would see an odd number of $X$'s before it. So the word is a product of blocks $\mathsf{AA}$ and $\mathsf{BB}$, possibly followed by one single last letter; the single letter is excluded because the total numbers of $\mathsf A$'s and of $\mathsf B$'s are both even. Hence $m$ is even; but $m=n-f$ is odd. No paths. If $f=0$ ($m=n$): the top phase ends at row $1$ (odd number of $\mathsf B$'s), the bottom phase starts at row $1$ and ends at $0$ (odd number of $\mathsf A$'s); now an $\mathsf A$ needs an even number of $\mathsf B$'s before it and a $\mathsf B$ an odd number of $\mathsf A$'s before it. Tracking the parities $(\#\mathsf A,\#\mathsf B)\bmod 2$: from $(0,0)$ only $\mathsf A$, to $(1,0)$; from $(1,0)$ either $\mathsf A$ back to $(0,0)$ or $\mathsf B$ to $(1,1)$; from $(1,1)$ only $\mathsf B$ back to $(1,0)$. Walks of length $n$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ on this path of three states: every odd-numbered step lands on $(1,0)$, every even-numbered step chooses $(0,0)$ or $(1,1)$, and the last must be $(1,1)$: $2^{n/2-1}$ words.

$q=2$. Columns $0..s$ are three singletons, used by the three laps; the other $m=n-1-s\ge1$ columns have types $\mathsf A/\mathsf B$. Lap $1$ runs along row $2$ through columns $0..s$, then through the tops of columns $s+1..n-1$, and must end at row $1$ (to continue along the middle singletons); lap $2$ runs along row $1$, through the bottoms, and must end at row $0$; lap $3$ runs along row $0$. These are exactly the conditions of the case $f=0$ above for a word of length $m$, giving $2^{m/2-1}$ words for even $m$ and none for odd $m$. Summing over $m=2,4,\dots,n-2$: $1+2+\dots+2^{n/2-2}=2^{n/2-1}-1$.

Total. Paths from $(0,2)$: $2^{n/2-1}+2^{n/2-1}-1=2^{n/2}-1=1023$. Over all $20$ starting columns: $20\cdot1023=\boxed{20460}$.

Solution 1 (Diagonals through the centre)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(12,0)$, $C=(12,16)$, $D=(0,16)$. A rhombus is a parallelogram, so its diagonals $WY$ and $XZ$ bisect each other. The midpoint of $WY$ has $y$-coordinate $8$ (as $W$, $Y$ lie on $y=0$, $y=16$) and the midpoint of $XZ$ has $x$-coordinate $6$; so both are the centre $(6,8)$ of the rectangle. Equal sides $WX=XY$: $a^2+b^2=(16-b)^2+(12-a)^2$, i.e. $3a+4b=50$.

The points $B$, $C$ lie outside the circle (beyond $P$ on ray $AP$, beyond $Q$ on ray $AQ$), so their powers are positive; hence $K$ is on the same side of $B$ as $M$ and on the same side of $C$ as $M$, i.e. $K$ lies on segment $BC$, and \[BP\cdot BA=BM\cdot BK,\qquad CQ\cdot CA=CM\cdot CK .\] Adding, with $BM=CM=4$ and $BK+KC=8$: $x(1+x)+x(3+x)=32$, i.e. $x^2+2x-16=0$, so $BP=x=\boxed{\sqrt{17}-1}$.

Solution 2 (Mollweide)

Let $a=BC=8$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ the angles. In triangle $BPM$, $\angle BMP=\frac\alpha2$ and $\angle BPM=180^\circ-\beta-\frac\alpha2$, so by the law of sines \[\frac{BP}{BM}=\frac{\sin\frac\alpha2}{\sin(\beta+\frac\alpha2)} .\] Since $\beta+\frac\alpha2=90^\circ+\frac{\beta-\gamma}{2}$, the denominator is $\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2$, and Mollweide's formula \[\frac{b+c}{a}=\frac{\sin\beta+\sin\gamma}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{2\sin\frac{\beta+\gamma}2\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2}{2\sin\frac\alpha2\cos\frac\alpha2}=\frac{\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}{2}}{\sin\frac\alpha2}\] (law of sines, sum-to-product, and $\sin\frac{\beta+\gamma}2=\cos\frac\alpha2$) turns this into \[BP=BM\cdot\frac{a}{b+c}=\frac{32}{b+c}.\] By the same computation in triangle $CQM$, $CQ=\frac{32}{b+c}$ too. Writing $x=BP=CQ$, we have $c=1+x$, $b=3+x$, so \[x=\frac{32}{4+2x}\iff x^2+2x-16=0\iff x=\sqrt{17}-1 .\] So $BP=\boxed{\sqrt{17}-1}$. (Check: the sides $\sqrt{17}$, $\sqrt{17}+2$, $8$ form a triangle.)

Problem 7

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Isosceles and Equilateral Rigidity

Let $ABC$ be an isosceles triangle with $AB=AC$. Points $P$ and $Q$ are located inside triangle $ABC$ such that $BP=PQ=QC$. Suppose that $\angle BAP=20^\circ$, $\angle PAQ=46^\circ$, and $\angle QAC=26^\circ$. Compute the measure of $\angle APC$.

Show the answer

$74^\circ$

Show the solution

Solution (Two equilateral triangles)

Here $\angle BAC=20^\circ+46^\circ+26^\circ=92^\circ$, so $\angle ABC=\angle ACB=44^\circ$.

Step 1: an equilateral triangle. Rotate about $A$ by $92^\circ$ so that $C$ goes to $B$, and let $Q'$ be the image of $Q$. Then $BQ'=CQ$, $AQ'=AQ$ and $\angle Q'AB=\angle QAC=26^\circ$, with $Q'$ on the other side of $AB$ from $C$. So $\angle Q'AP=26^\circ+20^\circ=46^\circ=\angle QAP$, and triangles $Q'AP$ and $QAP$ are congruent (SAS), giving $PQ'=PQ$. Hence $BP=PQ'=Q'B$ $(=PQ=QC)$: triangle $BPQ'$ is equilateral, and $\angle Q'BP=60^\circ$ is made up of $\angle Q'BA$ and $\angle ABP$ (the rays $BQ'$ and $BP$ lie on opposite sides of $BA$).

Step 2: rotate about $B$. Let $B^*$ be the point on the same side of $AB$ as $C$ with $ABB^*$ equilateral. The rotation about $B$ by $60^\circ$ towards $C$ maps $A\mapsto B^*$ and, by Step 1, $Q'\mapsto P$. So triangle $BB^*P$ is congruent to $BAQ'$: $\angle BB^*P=\angle BAQ'=26^\circ$, and $P$ is on the same side of $BB^*$ as $A$ (the image of the side of $AB$ containing $Q'$). Therefore $\angle AB^*P=60^\circ-26^\circ=34^\circ$. Also $\angle B^*AP=60^\circ-20^\circ=40^\circ$, so in triangle $AB^*P$ \[\angle APB^*=180^\circ-40^\circ-34^\circ=106^\circ .\]

Step 3: a cyclic quadrilateral. $AB^*=AB=AC$ and $\angle B^*AC=92^\circ-60^\circ=32^\circ$, so triangle $AB^*C$ is isosceles with $\angle ACB^*=\angle AB^*C=74^\circ$. The points $P$ ($\angle BAP=20^\circ<60^\circ$) and $C$ ($92^\circ>60^\circ$) lie on opposite sides of line $AB^*$, and $\angle APB^*+\angle ACB^*=106^\circ+74^\circ=180^\circ$, so $A,P,B^*,C$ are concyclic. Finally $P$ and $B^*$ are on the same side of $AC$, so they see the chord $AC$ under equal angles: \[\angle APC=\angle AB^*C=\boxed{74^\circ}.\]

Problem 8

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with orthocenter $H$. The internal angle bisector of $\angle BAC$ meets the circumcircles of triangles $ABH$, $ACH$, and $ABC$ again at points $P$, $Q$, and $M$, respectively. Suppose that points $A$, $P$, $Q$, and $M$ are distinct and lie on the internal angle bisector of $\angle BAC$ in that order. Given that $AP=4$, $AQ=5$, and $BC=7$, compute $AM$.

Show the answer

$\sqrt{69}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Law of sines in three triangles)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles of $ABC$ and $R$ its circumradius. The reflection of $H$ in $AB$ lies on the circumcircle, so the circle $ABH$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $AB$ and also has radius $R$.

$AP$. In circle $ABH$, $AB=2R\sin\angle APB$; since $AB=2R\sin\gamma$, $\angle APB$ is $\gamma$ or $180^\circ-\gamma$. As a point $X$ moves along the ray from $A$ through $M$, the angle $\angle AXB$ decreases strictly, and $\angle AMB=\angle ACB=\gamma$; since $P\ne M$, we get $\angle APB=180^\circ-\gamma$. Then $\angle ABP=\gamma-\frac\alpha2$ (in particular $\gamma>\frac\alpha2$) and \[AP=2R\sin\Bigl(\gamma-\frac\alpha2\Bigr).\] In the same way $AQ=2R\sin(\beta-\frac\alpha2)$. In triangle $ABM$, $\angle AMB=\gamma$ and $\angle ABM=\beta+\frac\alpha2=90^\circ+u$ with $u=\frac{\beta-\gamma}2$, so $AM=\frac{AB\sin(90^\circ+u)}{\sin\gamma}=2R\cos u$.

Sum and difference. Since $\beta-\frac\alpha2=90^\circ-\alpha+u$ and $\gamma-\frac\alpha2=90^\circ-\alpha-u$, \[AQ-AP=2R\bigl[\cos(\alpha-u)-\cos(\alpha+u)\bigr]=4R\sin\alpha\sin u=2\,BC\sin u,\] \[AQ+AP=4R\cos\alpha\cos u=2\,AM\cos\alpha .\] So $\sin u=\frac1{14}$ and $\cos\alpha=\frac{9}{2AM}$. Also $\sin\alpha=\frac{BC}{2R}=\frac{7\cos u}{AM}$, with $\cos^2u=\frac{195}{196}$. From $\sin^2\alpha+\cos^2\alpha=1$: \[AM^2=49\cdot\frac{195}{196}+\frac{81}4=\frac{195+81}{4}=69,\qquad AM=\boxed{\sqrt{69}} .\]

Solution 2 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Let $O$ be the circumcentre, $R$ the circumradius, and $d$ the unit vector along the bisector from $A$ towards $M$. A line $A+td$ meets a circle with centre $Z$ through $A$ again at $t=2(Z-A)\cdot d$.

The circle $ABH$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $AB$ (the reflection of $H$ in $AB$ lies on the circumcircle), so its centre is the reflection of $O$ in $AB$; as $OA=OB$, $AOBZ$ is a rhombus and $Z=A+B-O$. Hence (all lengths measured along $d$) \[AP=2(B-O)\cdot d,\qquad AQ=2(C-O)\cdot d,\qquad AM=2(O-A)\cdot d .\] Difference. $AQ-AP=2(C-B)\cdot d=2\cdot7\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between $\overrightarrow{BC}$ and $d$; so $\cos\varphi=\frac1{14}$.

Sum. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and $e$ the unit normal to $BC$ pointing away from $A$. Then $N-O=R\cos\alpha\,e$ (with $\alpha=\angle BAC$), and $e\cdot d=\sin\varphi>0$ since $d$ crosses $BC$ towards $M$. So $AP+AQ=4(N-O)\cdot d=4R\cos\alpha\sin\varphi$.

$AM$. $M$ is the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$, so $M=O+Re$. From $M=A+AM\,d$: $AM=(M-O)\cdot d-(A-O)\cdot d=R\sin\varphi+\frac{AM}2$, so $AM=2R\sin\varphi$.

Therefore $\cos\alpha=\frac{AP+AQ}{2AM}=\frac{9}{2AM}$ and $\sin\alpha=\frac{BC}{2R}=\frac{7\sin\varphi}{AM}$, with $\sin^2\varphi=1-\frac1{196}=\frac{195}{196}$. Then $\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha=1$ gives $AM^2=\frac{81}4+\frac{195}4=69$, so $AM=\boxed{\sqrt{69}}$.

Problem 9

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be triangle with incenter $I$ and incircle $\omega$. The circumcircle of triangle $BIC$ intersects $\omega$ at points $E$ and $F$. Suppose that $\Gamma\neq\omega$ is a circle passing through $E$ and $F$ and tangent to lines $AB$ and $AC$. If $AB=5$, $AC=7$, and $\Gamma$ has twice the radius of $\omega$, compute $BC$.

Show the answer

$3+\sqrt{11}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Distances along the bisector)

Let $a=BC$, $b=7$, $c=5$, $r$ the inradius, $\alpha=\angle A$, $\sigma=\sin\frac\alpha2$, and let $L$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$; it lies on the bisector $AI$. Since $\angle LBI=\angle LBC+\angle CBI=\frac\alpha2+\frac\beta2$ and $\angle LIB=\angle IAB+\angle IBA=\frac\alpha2+\frac\beta2$, we get $LB=LI$, and likewise $LC=LI$; so the circle $BIC$ has centre $L$ and radius $LI$. Put coordinates on line $AI$ with $I$ at $0$ and $L$ at $\delta=LI>0$, and $A$ at $-m$, $m=AI=\frac r\sigma$.

Every circle through $E$ and $F$ has its centre on the line of centres $IL$, i.e. on line $AI$; a circle with centre at coordinate $z$ on this line and radius $\rho$ passes through $E,F$ iff its power function agrees on the common chord, which lies at coordinate $x_0$ with $x_0^2-r^2=x_0^2-2\delta x_0$ (powers with respect to $\omega$ and to circle $BIC$), i.e. $x_0=\frac{r^2}{2\delta}$, and $E,F=(x_0,\pm\sqrt{r^2-x_0^2})$. The condition is $(x_0-z)^2+r^2-x_0^2=\rho^2$, i.e. \[z^2-2zx_0+r^2=\rho^2 .\] A circle tangent to lines $AB,AC$ with centre on line $AI$ and radius $2r$ is the image of $\omega$ under a homothety at $A$ of ratio $\pm2$: centre at $z=m$ or $z=-3m$. For $z=-3m$: $9m^2+3m\frac{r^2}{\delta}=3r^2$ is impossible as $m>r$. For $z=m$: $m^2-\frac{mr^2}{\delta}=3r^2$, so $\delta=\frac{mr^2}{m^2-3r^2}=\frac{r\sigma}{1-3\sigma^2}$.

Now use $\delta=LI=LB=2R\sigma$ (law of sines in the circumcircle, $\angle LAB=\frac\alpha2$; $R$ the circumradius) and $r=4R\sigma\sin\frac\beta2\sin\frac\gamma2$. The latter holds because $a=r\bigl(\cot\frac\beta2+\cot\frac\gamma2\bigr)=\frac{r\sin\frac{\beta+\gamma}2}{\sin\frac\beta2\sin\frac\gamma2}=\frac{r\cos\frac\alpha2}{\sin\frac\beta2\sin\frac\gamma2}$ (tangent lengths from $B$ and $C$) and $a=2R\sin\alpha=4R\sigma\cos\frac\alpha2$. So \[1-3\sigma^2=2\sigma\sin\tfrac\beta2\sin\tfrac\gamma2=\sigma\Bigl(\cos\tfrac{\beta-\gamma}2-\sigma\Bigr)\iff 1-2\sigma^2=\sigma\cos\tfrac{\beta-\gamma}{2}.\] By Mollweide's formula $\frac{b+c}a=\frac{\sin\beta+\sin\gamma}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{2\cos\frac\alpha2\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2}{2\sigma\cos\frac\alpha2}$ (law of sines and sum-to-product), $\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2=\frac{(b+c)\sigma}{a}$, so $\cos\alpha=\frac{(b+c)(1-\cos\alpha)}{2a}$, i.e. $\cos\alpha=\frac{6}{a+6}$. The law of cosines gives $a^2=74-\frac{420}{a+6}$, i.e. \[a^3+6a^2-74a-24=(a+12)(a^2-6a-2)=0,\] so $BC=a=\boxed{3+\sqrt{11}}$.

Solution 2 (Power functions of a pencil)

Let $a=BC$, $s$ the semiperimeter, $r$ the inradius, $L$ the midpoint of arc $BC$, $I_A$ the $A$-excenter, and $K$ the circle $BIC$ (centre $L$; it passes through $I$ and $I_A$). For a circle $\kappa$ write $\operatorname{pow}_\kappa(X)=|XZ|^2-\rho^2$. Circles through the two points $E,F$ of $\omega\cap K$ form a pencil: $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma=\lambda\operatorname{pow}_\omega+(1-\lambda)\operatorname{pow}_K$ for some real $\lambda$. (Each power function is $|X|^2$ plus an affine function of $X$. The centre of $\Gamma$ lies on the line $IL$ of the other two centres, and $I\ne L$, so it equals $\lambda I+(1-\lambda)L$ for some $\lambda$; then $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma-\lambda\operatorname{pow}_\omega-(1-\lambda)\operatorname{pow}_K$ has no $|X|^2$ term and no linear term, so it is a constant, and it vanishes at $E$.)

$\Gamma$ is tangent to $AB$, $AC$ and its centre is on the line of centres $IL=AI$, so it is the image of $\omega$ under the homothety at $A$ of ratio $2$ or $-2$; call its centre $G_\pm=A\pm2(I-A)$. Evaluate at $I$ and at $A$, using $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(I)=-r^2$, $\operatorname{pow}_K(I)=0$, $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(A)=(s-a)^2$, $\operatorname{pow}_K(A)=AI\cdot AI_A=bc$ and $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(A)=4(s-a)^2$ (tangent length doubled). (Here $A,I,I_A$ lie on the bisector, with $A$ outside $K$; and $AI\cdot AI_A=bc$ because the triangles $ABI$ and $AI_AC$ are similar: $\angle BAI=\angle I_AAC=\frac\alpha2$ and $\angle AIB=180^\circ-\frac\alpha2-\frac\beta2=90^\circ+\frac\gamma2=\angle ACI_A$.) Put $t=\frac{(s-a)^2}{r^2}$, so $AI^2=r^2(1+t)$. By Heron's formula and $r=\frac{[ABC]}s$, $r^2=\frac{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{s}$, so $t=\frac{s(s-a)}{(s-b)(s-c)}$ and $AI^2=(s-a)^2+r^2=\frac{(s-a)\,(s(s-a)+(s-b)(s-c))}{s}=\frac{bc(s-a)}s$, since $s(s-a)+(s-b)(s-c)=2s^2-(a+b+c)s+bc=bc$.

Ratio $+2$. $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(I)=AI^2-4r^2=-\lambda r^2$ gives $\lambda=3-t$. Then at $A$: $4(s-a)^2=(3-t)(s-a)^2+(t-2)bc$, i.e. $(1+t)(s-a)^2=(t-2)bc$, i.e. $AI^2\,t=(t-2)\,bc$. Using these values of $AI^2$ and $t$: \[\frac{s-a}{s}=1-\frac2t\iff\frac as=\frac2t\iff a(s-a)=2(s-b)(s-c).\] Ratio $-2$. Here $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(I)=9AI^2-4r^2$, so $\lambda=-5-9t$ and the equation at $A$ becomes $9AI^2t=(6+9t)bc$, i.e. $\frac{AI^2}{bc}>1$, impossible since $\frac{AI^2}{bc}=\frac{s-a}{s}<1$.

With $b=7$, $c=5$: $s-a=\frac{12-a}2$, $s-b=\frac{a-2}2$, $s-c=\frac{a+2}2$, and $a(12-a)=a^2-4$, i.e. $a^2-6a-2=0$. So $BC=\boxed{3+\sqrt{11}}$.

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with centroid $G$ and circumcenter $O$. Suppose that the orthocenter of triangle $AGO$ lies on line $BC$. Given that $AB=11$ and $AC=13$, compute $BC$.

Show the answer

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates centred at the midpoint of $BC$)

Let $N$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and put $N=(0,0)$, $B=(-m,0)$, $C=(m,0)$ with $2m=BC$, and $A=(p,q)$, $q>0$. Then $G=\bigl(\frac p3,\frac q3\bigr)$ and $O=(0,k)$ with $k=\frac{p^2+q^2-m^2}{2q}$ (from $OA=OB$). Let $X=(x,0)$ be the orthocenter of $AGO$, assumed on line $BC$.
