Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a triangle such that $\angle BAC=60^\circ$ and $AB<AC$. Let $O$ and $I$ be the circumcenter and the incenter of triangle $ABC$, respectively, and let $T$ be the orthocenter of triangle $BIC$.

  • (a) Prove that the four points $B$, $C$, $O$ and $I$ lie on the same circle.

  • (b) Prove that the lines $BC$, $OI$ and $AT$ are concurrent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A rhombus, a parallelogram and three radical axes)

Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, with radius $R$, and let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of $\omega$ not containing $A$. We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

(a) Since $\angle BAC=60^\circ<90^\circ$, the center $O$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $A$, and $\angle BOC=2\angle BAC=120^\circ$. Also $\angle BIC=90^\circ+\tfrac12\angle BAC=120^\circ$, and $I$ lies inside the triangle, on the same side of $BC$ as $A$. Two points on the same side of $BC$ that see $BC$ under the same angle lie on one circle through $B$ and $C$; so $B$, $C$, $O$, $I$ are concyclic.

More precisely, this circle is centered at $N$: by the incenter lemma $NB=NC=NI$, and $NB=2R\sin\angle BAN=2R\sin30^\circ=R=NO$ because $N\in\omega$. Call this circle $\Gamma$ (center $N$, radius $R$).

(b) Step 1: $T$ lies on $\omega$ and $NOTI$ is a parallelogram. The quadrilateral $NBOC$ has $NB=BO=OC=CN=R$, so it is a rhombus and $\vec{NB}+\vec{NC}=\vec{NO}$. The circumcenter of triangle $BIC$ is $N$, and for a triangle with circumcenter $N$ the orthocenter is $N+\vec{NB}+\vec{NI}+\vec{NC}$ (Sylvester's relation). Hence \[\vec{NT}=\vec{NO}+\vec{NI},\qquad\text{that is}\qquad \vec{OT}=\vec{NI}\ \text{ and }\ \vec{IT}=\vec{NO}.\] So $NOTI$ is a parallelogram; in particular $OT=NI=R$, so $T\in\omega$, and $OT\parallel NI$, $IT\parallel NO$.

Step 2: $A$, $O$, $I$, $T$ are concyclic. The points $A$, $I$, $N$ are collinear (the bisector of angle $A$ passes through $N$). Using the parallelogram, and then the isosceles triangle $OAN$ ($OA=ON=R$), \[\angle(TO,TI)=\angle(NI,NO)=\angle(NA,NO)=\angle(AO,AN)=\angle(AO,AI),\] so $A$, $O$, $I$, $T$ lie on one circle $\Delta$. (Here $O\ne I$ because $AB\ne AC$, and $T\ne A$: otherwise $\vec{OA}=\vec{NI}$ would make $OA\parallel AN$, putting $O$ on line $AN$ and forcing $AB=AC$.)

Step 3: radical center. The circles $\omega$, $\Gamma$, $\Delta$ are distinct ($I\in\Gamma\cap\Delta$ lies inside $\omega$; $A\in\Delta$ is not on $\Gamma$ since $NA>NI=R$). Their pairwise radical axes are \[\omega,\Gamma:\ BC,\qquad \Gamma,\Delta:\ OI,\qquad \omega,\Delta:\ AT .\] The lines $BC$ and $OI$ are not parallel: the perpendicular bisector of the chord $OI$ of $\Gamma$ passes through $N$; if $OI\parallel BC$, it would be the line through $N$ perpendicular to $BC$, which is the line $NO$, so $O$ would lie on the perpendicular bisector of $OI$, i.e. $O=I$, impossible. Hence the three radical axes meet at the radical center, and $BC$, $OI$, $AT$ are concurrent.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers for the key circle)

Let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of the circumcircle $\omega$ not containing $A$, and $R$ the circumradius.

(a) $\angle BOC=2\cdot60^\circ=120^\circ$ and $\angle BIC=90^\circ+30^\circ=120^\circ$, and $O$, $I$ lie on the same side of $BC$ (the side of $A$, since $\angle A$ is acute), so $B$, $C$, $O$, $I$ are concyclic. In fact $NB=NC=NI$ (incenter lemma) and $NB=2R\sin 30^\circ=R=NO$, so this circle $\Gamma$ has center $N$ and radius $R$.

(b) Use complex numbers with $N=0$ and $R=1$. Then $B$, $C$, $O$, $I$ are on the unit circle and $OB=OC=1$, so the triangles $NBO$ and $NOC$ are equilateral; rotating the picture we may take \[B=1,\qquad O=\zeta,\qquad C=\zeta^2,\qquad \zeta=e^{i\pi/3},\] and $I=z$ with $|z|=1$. Note $1+\zeta^2=\zeta$.

Since $N$ is the circumcenter of triangle $BIC$, its orthocenter is $T=B+I+C=z+\zeta$.

The point $A$ lies on the ray $NI$ beyond $I$ and on $\omega$ (center $O$, radius $1$): $A=tz$ with $t>1$ and $|tz-\zeta|=1$, that is $t^2-t(z\bar\zeta+\bar z\zeta)=0$, so $t=z\bar\zeta+\bar z\zeta$ and \[A=z^2\bar\zeta+\zeta .\] Put $w=z\bar\zeta$ (so $|w|=1$). Then \[\frac{A-O}{T-O}=\frac{z^2\bar\zeta}{z}=w,\qquad \frac{A-I}{T-I}=\frac{z^2\bar\zeta+\zeta-z}{\zeta}=w^2-w+1,\] and the cross-ratio \[\frac{A-O}{T-O}:\frac{A-I}{T-I}=\frac{w}{w^2-w+1}=\frac{1}{w+\bar w-1}\] is real. Hence $A$, $O$, $I$, $T$ are concyclic (they are distinct: $O\ne I$ as $AB\ne AC$, and $T\ne A$ since $w\ne1$, i.e. $I\ne O$). Call this circle $\Delta$. Also $|T-O|=|z|=1$, so $T\in\omega$.

Now $\omega$ contains $A,T,B,C$; $\Gamma$ contains $B,C,O,I$; $\Delta$ contains $A,O,I,T$. These circles are distinct ($I$ is inside $\omega$, and $|A|=t=2\operatorname{Re}(w)>1$ shows $A\notin\Gamma$), so their radical axes $BC$ (for $\omega,\Gamma$), $OI$ (for $\Gamma,\Delta$) and $AT$ (for $\omega,\Delta$) are concurrent or parallel. They are not parallel: $OI\parallel BC$ would mean that $\zeta$ and $z$ are symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, which is the line through $0$ and $\zeta$; that forces $z=\zeta$, i.e. $I=O$. Therefore $BC$, $OI$ and $AT$ are concurrent.

Problem 2

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Congruence Lifting and Refinement

Let $p$ be an odd prime. Determine the smallest positive integer $k$ such that there exists a positive integer $n$ for which, when the binomial coefficients $\binom{n}{0},\binom{n}{1},\dots,\binom{n}{k}$ are divided by $p$, every possible remainder $0,1,\dots,p-1$ occurs at least once.

Remark: $\binom{a}{b}=0$ if $a<b$.

Show the answer

$k=\dfrac{3(p-1)}{2}$

Show the solution

Solution (Lucas's theorem and counting residues)

We use Lucas's theorem: if $n=\sum n_ip^i$ and $j=\sum j_ip^i$ are written in base $p$, then $\binom nj\equiv\prod_i\binom{n_i}{j_i}\pmod p$. We also use, for $0\le j\le p-1$, \[\binom{p-1}{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\frac{p-i}{i}\equiv(-1)^j,\qquad \binom{p-2}{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\frac{p-1-i}{i}\equiv(-1)^j\,(j+1)\pmod p\quad(j\le p-2).\]

Lower bound. Suppose $n$ and $k$ work and $k<\frac{3(p-1)}2$. Then $k<2p$, so every $j\le k$ is $j=j_0+pj_1$ with $0\le j_0\le p-1$ and $j_1\in\{0,1\}$. Let $n_0,n_1$ be the last two base-$p$ digits of $n$. By Lucas, \[\binom nj\equiv\binom{n_0}{j_0}\ (j_1=0),\qquad \binom nj\equiv n_1\binom{n_0}{j_0}\ (j_1=1).\] First, $k\ge p$. Otherwise only $j_1=0$ occurs; the remainder $0$ requires some $j>n_0$, so $n_0\le p-2$, and the nonzero remainders are among $\binom{n_0}{0},\dots,\binom{n_0}{n_0}$, which by the symmetry $\binom{n_0}{j}=\binom{n_0}{n_0-j}$ take at most $\lfloor n_0/2\rfloor+1\le\frac{p-1}2<p-1$ values. So not all $p-1$ nonzero remainders appear.

Now $p\le k\le\frac{3p-5}{2}$. (For $p=3$ this range is empty, so we may assume $p\ge5$.) The nonzero remainders come from the set $S_0$ of nonzero values $\binom{n_0}{j_0}$ ($0\le j_0\le p-1$) and the set $S_1$ of nonzero values $n_1\binom{n_0}{j_0}$ with $0\le j_0\le k-p$; clearly $|S_1|\le k-p+1\le\frac{p-3}2$.

  • If $n_0\le p-2$: as above $|S_0|\le\lfloor n_0/2\rfloor+1\le\frac{p-1}2$ (recall $p-2$ is odd), so $|S_0|+|S_1|\le p-2$.

  • If $n_0=p-1$: $S_0\subseteq\{1,-1\}$, so $|S_0|+|S_1|\le2+\frac{p-3}2=\frac{p+1}2<p-1$.

Either way fewer than $p-1$ nonzero remainders occur, a contradiction. Hence $k\ge\frac{3(p-1)}2$.

Construction. Take $n=p^2-2$, whose base-$p$ digits are $n_0=p-2$, $n_1=p-1$, and $k=p+\frac{p-3}2=\frac{3(p-1)}2$.

  • $j=0,1,\dots,p-1$: $\binom nj\equiv\binom{p-2}{j}$, which is $0$ for $j=p-1$ and $(-1)^j(j+1)$ for $j\le p-2$. For even $j=2s$ this is $2s+1$, and for odd $j=2s+1$ it is $-(2s+2)\equiv p-2-2s$. So we get $0$ and every odd residue $1,3,\dots,p-2$.

  • $j=p+j_0$ with $0\le j_0\le\frac{p-3}2$: $\binom nj\equiv(p-1)(-1)^{j_0}(j_0+1)\equiv(-1)^{j_0+1}(j_0+1)$. For odd $j_0$ this is $j_0+1$, giving the even residues $e\le\frac{p-1}2$; for even $j_0$ it is $-(j_0+1)\equiv p-1-j_0$, giving the even residues $e\ge p-1-\frac{p-3}{2}=\frac{p+1}2$. Together these are all even residues $2,4,\dots,p-1$.

So all remainders $0,1,\dots,p-1$ occur among $\binom n0,\dots,\binom nk$, and the smallest $k$ is $\boxed{\dfrac{3(p-1)}{2}}$.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

For each permutation $a=(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots,a_{2026})$ of $(1,2,3,\dots,2026)$, consider the following numbers: \[\begin{aligned} &a_1+2a_2+3a_3+\dots+2025a_{2025}+2026a_{2026},\\ &a_2+2a_3+3a_4+\dots+2025a_{2026}+2026a_1,\\ &\qquad\vdots\\ &a_{2026}+2a_1+3a_2+\dots+2025a_{2024}+2026a_{2025}. \end{aligned}\] Define $f(a)$ to be the number of distinct values among these $2026$ sums. What is the minimum possible value of $f(a)$ as $a$ ranges over all permutations of $(1,2,3,\dots,2026)$?

Show the answer

$64$

Show the solution

Solution (Consecutive sums form a closed walk)

Let $N=2026$ and read indices modulo $N$. The $t$-th sum (for $t=0,1,\dots,N-1$) is $S_t=\sum_{i=1}^{N}i\,a_{i+t}$. Shifting the index, \[S_{t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}i\,a_{i+t+1}=\sum_{j=2}^{N+1}(j-1)a_{j+t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j-1)a_{j+t}+N a_{t+1}=S_t-\frac{N(N+1)}2+Na_{t+1}.\] Put $d_t=2a_{t+1}-(N+1)$ and $U_t=2S_t/N$. Then $U_{t+1}-U_t=d_t$. Because $a$ is a permutation of $1,\dots,N$ and $N+1=2027$, the numbers $d_0,\dots,d_{N-1}$ are exactly $\pm1,\pm3,\dots,\pm2025$, each once, and $U_N=U_0$. So $U_0,U_1,\dots,U_N=U_0$ is a closed walk on the integers whose $N$ steps are these $N$ distinct odd numbers, and $f(a)$ is the number of distinct points it visits.

Lower bound. The steps are odd, so consecutive points have opposite parity. Let the visited set consist of $e$ even and $o$ odd numbers, $e+o=f(a)$. Every step joins an even point $u$ and an odd point $v$; the pair $\{u,v\}$ can carry at most two steps, namely $v-u$ (from $u$ to $v$) and $u-v$ (from $v$ to $u$), because all steps are distinct. Hence $N\le2eo$, so $eo\ge1013$. As $eo\le\frac{(e+o)^2}{4}$, we get $f(a)^2\ge4052>63^2$, so $f(a)\ge64$.

Construction. Let $E=\{0,2,4,\dots,62\}$ and $O=\{63+64q: q=0,1,\dots,31\}$ ($32$ numbers each). The differences $v-u$ ($v\in O$, $u=2r\in E$) are $64q+(63-2r)$, i.e. every odd number from $1$ to $2047$ exactly once. For each odd $d\le2025$ let $\{u_d,v_d\}$ be the pair with $v_d-u_d=d$; these $1013$ pairs form a connected graph on the $64$ points: every $u\in E$ is joined to $63$ (difference $63-u$), every $63+64q$ with $q\le30$ is joined to $0$ (difference at most $1983$), and $2047$ is joined to $62$ (difference $1985$).

Replace each pair $\{u,v\}$ by the two arrows $u\to v$ and $v\to u$. In this directed graph every point has as many outgoing as incoming arrows and the graph is connected, so it has a closed walk using every arrow exactly once (an Euler circuit). It has $2026$ steps, and the step along $u\to v$ is $v-u$; so the steps are exactly $\pm1,\pm3,\dots,\pm2025$, each once, and the walk visits all $64$ points. Let $d_0,d_1,\dots,d_{N-1}$ be the steps of this walk in order and put $a_{t+1}=\frac{2027+d_t}{2}$; this is a permutation of $1,\dots,2026$. Its numbers $U_t=2S_t/N$ satisfy $U_{t+1}-U_t=d_t$, so they are the points of the walk shifted by a constant. Hence the sums $S_t=\frac N2U_t$ take exactly $64$ distinct values.

Hence the minimum of $f(a)$ is $\boxed{64}$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

For every positive integer $n$, define \[f(n)=\left\lfloor\sqrt{(n+1)^3}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\sqrt{n^3}\right\rfloor .\] Prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that $f(n+1)<f(n)$.

Show the solution

Solution (Around an integer cube)

Let $m\ge3$ be odd and $n=m^2-1$. Then $\sqrt{(n+1)^3}=m^3$ is an integer, and we need $\lfloor\sqrt{n^3}\rfloor$ and $\lfloor\sqrt{(n+2)^3}\rfloor$, i.e. the integer parts of $\sqrt{(m^2\mp1)^3}$.

Claim. $m^3-\frac{3m}2<\sqrt{(m^2-1)^3}<m^3-\frac{3m}2+\frac12$ and $m^3+\frac{3m}2<\sqrt{(m^2+1)^3}<m^3+\frac{3m}2+\frac12$.

All four bounds are positive, so it suffices to compare squares: \[\Bigl(m^3-\tfrac{3m}2\Bigr)^2=m^6-3m^4+\tfrac94m^2<m^6-3m^4+3m^2-1=(m^2-1)^3,\] because $\frac34m^2>1$; \[\Bigl(m^3-\tfrac{3m}2+\tfrac12\Bigr)^2=m^6-3m^4+m^3+\tfrac94m^2-\tfrac32m+\tfrac14>(m^2-1)^3\iff m^3-\tfrac34m^2-\tfrac32m+\tfrac54>0,\] true for $m\ge2$; \[\Bigl(m^3+\tfrac{3m}2\Bigr)^2=m^6+3m^4+\tfrac94m^2<m^6+3m^4+3m^2+1=(m^2+1)^3;\] \[\Bigl(m^3+\tfrac{3m}2+\tfrac12\Bigr)^2=m^6+3m^4+m^3+\tfrac94m^2+\tfrac32m+\tfrac14>(m^2+1)^3\iff m^3-\tfrac34m^2+\tfrac32m-\tfrac34>0,\] true for $m\ge1$.

Since $m$ is odd, $\frac{3m}2=\frac{3m-1}2+\frac12$ with $\frac{3m\pm1}{2}$ integers. The claim gives \[\Bigl\lfloor\sqrt{(m^2-1)^3}\Bigr\rfloor=m^3-\frac{3m+1}2,\qquad \Bigl\lfloor\sqrt{(m^2+1)^3}\Bigr\rfloor=m^3+\frac{3m-1}2,\] because the first number lies in $\bigl(m^3-\frac{3m+1}2+\frac12,\ m^3-\frac{3m+1}2+1\bigr)$ and the second in $\bigl(m^3+\frac{3m-1}2+\frac12,\ m^3+\frac{3m-1}2+1\bigr)$. Therefore \[f(n)=m^3-\Bigl(m^3-\frac{3m+1}2\Bigr)=\frac{3m+1}2,\qquad f(n+1)=\Bigl(m^3+\frac{3m-1}2\Bigr)-m^3=\frac{3m-1}2<f(n).\] This holds for every odd $m\ge3$, so infinitely many $n$ (namely $n=8,24,48,\dots$) satisfy $f(n+1)<f(n)$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity · also Level 2, Day 2, Problem 6

On a $100\times100$ chessboard, $50$ squares have been numbered in advance as shown in the figure: the square in column $2k-1$ and row $2k-1$ (counted from the bottom-left corner) carries the number $k$, for $k=1,2,\dots,50$.

Esmeralda wants to write positive integers in some of the empty squares. She wants the final board to satisfy the following condition:

No two squares that share a side have different numbers written in them.

What is the maximum number of additional squares Esmeralda can fill while satisfying this condition?

Show the answer

$9756$ (construction proved; the matching upper bound is not proved here)

Show the solution

Solution (Partial: reformulation and a construction with 9756 squares)

This is partial progress: the construction is complete, the matching bound is not proved.

Reformulation. After Esmeralda writes her numbers, consider the filled squares and join two of them when they share a side. All squares of one connected piece carry the same number. So two numbered squares with different numbers must lie in different pieces, and conversely, if the $50$ numbered squares lie in $50$ different pieces of the set of filled squares, she can write in every piece the number of its numbered square (and any number in pieces without one). Hence the answer is $10000-50-F$, where $F$ is the least number of squares that must stay empty so that the $50$ numbered squares are pairwise separated.

Construction with $F=194$. Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,100$ from the bottom left, so number $k$ is at $(2k-1,2k-1)$. Leave empty the two squares $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$ next to number $1$, and for $k=2,\dots,49$ the four squares sharing a side with $(2k-1,2k-1)$. These $2+4\cdot48=194$ squares are distinct (the numbered squares are two steps apart along the diagonal). Numbers $1,\dots,49$ are then cut off from everything else, and number $50$ lies in the one remaining large piece. So Esmeralda fills \[10000-50-194=9756\] squares (the figure shows the same pattern on a $10\times10$ board with $5$ numbers).

A reduction of the bound. Write $(x,y)$ for the square in column $x$ and row $y$. The squares with $|x-y|=1$ are exactly the neighbours of the numbered squares: the neighbours of number $k$ are $(2k-1\pm1,\,2k-1)$ and $(2k-1,\,2k-1\pm1)$, and every square with $|x-y|=1$ is of this form. There are $2+4\cdot49=198$ of them, and each is adjacent to exactly one numbered square. Call those with $y=x+1$ upper and those with $x=y+1$ lower neighbour squares; let $\mathcal U$ be the squares with $y-x\ge2$ and $\mathcal L$ those with $x-y\ge2$. An upper neighbour square is adjacent only to its numbered square, to a square $(2k,2k)$, and to squares of $\mathcal U$; similarly for lower ones and $\mathcal L$.

Let $f_U,f_L$ be the numbers of filled upper and lower neighbour squares and $e_U,e_L$ the numbers of empty squares in $\mathcal U,\mathcal L$. Then the number of empty squares is at least $(198-f_U-f_L)+e_U+e_L$. A filled neighbour square lies in the piece of its own number, so filled upper neighbour squares of different numbers must be in different pieces even when we only use paths inside $\mathcal U$. Hence the bound $194$ follows from the

Lemma. If upper neighbour squares of different numbers are never joined by a path of filled squares inside $\mathcal U$, then $f_U\le e_U+2$

and its mirror image ($f_L\le e_L+2$), since then at least $198-4=194$ squares are empty.

What is missing. A proof of the Lemma. Integer programming confirms it on the analogous $2m\times2m$ boards for $m\le9$ (even using only the L-shaped paths that go up a column of $\mathcal U$ and then right along a row), and on the full problem the exact minimum $4m-6$ for $m=2,\dots,7$; this supports the answer $9756$, but it is not a proof.

Problem 6

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle. Let $M$ be the midpoint of side $BC$, and let $D$ and $E$ be points on sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively, such that $MD=ME$. The line through $D$ perpendicular to $AB$ intersects the line through $E$ perpendicular to $AC$ at $P$. Let $R$ and $S$ be the reflections of $P$ across $AB$ and $AC$, respectively. The lines $BR$ and $CS$ intersect at $Q$. Let $K$ and $L$ be the orthogonal projections of $Q$ onto $AB$ and $AC$, respectively. Prove that $DL=EK$.

Show the solution

Solution (Distances to the two sides as coordinates)

Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$, $c=\cos\alpha$, $s=\sin\alpha$. For a point $Z$ let $x(Z)$ be its signed distance to line $AB$ (positive on the side of $C$) and $y(Z)$ its signed distance to line $AC$ (positive on the side of $B$). These are affine functions of $Z$, and every point is determined by $(x,y)$. We assume $P$ lies on neither line $AB$ nor line $AC$ (otherwise $R=P$ or $S=P$ and the construction degenerates).

Three facts. Put $A$ at the origin, let $u,v$ be the unit vectors along $AB$, $AC$, and let $n$, $m$ be the unit normals of $AB$, $AC$ pointing to $C$, $B$. Then $u\cdot m=s$ and $n\cdot m=-c$.

  • (i) If $Z=du+x\,n$, then $y=Z\cdot m=ds-xc$. So the foot of $Z$ on $AB$ is the point at signed position $\frac{y+xc}{s}$ from $A$ along $AB$; similarly the foot on $AC$ is at position $\frac{x+yc}{s}$ along $AC$.

  • (ii) For $U$ at position $p$ on line $AB$ and $V$ at position $q$ on line $AC$: $UV^2=p^2+q^2-2pqc$.

  • (iii) The reflection of $Z$ in $AB$ is $Z-2x\,n$, with coordinates $(-x,\ y+2cx)$; the reflection in $AC$ has coordinates $(x+2cy,\ -y)$.

Coordinates of the points. Let $P=(x,y)$, $Q=(X,Y)$, and $h_B=y(B)>0$, $h_C=x(C)>0$, so $B=(0,h_B)$ and $C=(h_C,0)$. By (iii), $R=(-x,\,y+2cx)$. As $Q=B+\lambda(R-B)$, we get $X=-\lambda x$ and $Y=h_B+\lambda(y+2cx-h_B)$; eliminating $\lambda=-X/x$: \[Xy+xY+2cxX=h_B(x+X).\tag{1}\] In the same way, $S=(x+2cy,-y)$ and $Q\in CS$ give \[Xy+xY+2cyY=h_C(y+Y).\tag{2}\]

The hypothesis $MD=ME$. With $D$ at position $d=\frac{y+xc}{s}$ on $AB$ and $E$ at $e=\frac{x+yc}{s}$ on $AC$, and $M=(\frac{h_C}2,\frac{h_B}2)$, whose projections on $AB$, $AC$ are at $m_1=\frac{h_B+ch_C}{2s}$, $m_2=\frac{h_C+ch_B}{2s}$ by (i), \[MD^2-ME^2=(d^2-2dm_1)-(e^2-2em_2).\] Now $d^2-e^2=\frac{(y+xc)^2-(x+yc)^2}{s^2}=y^2-x^2$ and $2(dm_1-em_2)=\frac{(y+xc)(h_B+ch_C)-(x+yc)(h_C+ch_B)}{s^2}=yh_B-xh_C$. Hence \[MD=ME\iff \Phi:=x^2-y^2-xh_C+yh_B=0 .\]

The goal $DL=EK$. $K$ and $L$ are at positions $\frac{Y+Xc}{s}$ on $AB$ and $\frac{X+Yc}{s}$ on $AC$. By (ii), as above, \[DL^2-EK^2=(y^2-x^2)+(X^2-Y^2)-2c\,\frac{(y+xc)(X+Yc)-(x+yc)(Y+Xc)}{s^2}=-\Psi,\] where $\Psi:=(x^2-y^2)-(X^2-Y^2)+2c(Xy-xY)$.

The key identity. Write $\sigma=Xy+xY$. Multiply $\Phi$ by $(x+X)(y+Y)$ and replace $h_C(y+Y)$ and $h_B(x+X)$ using (2) and (1): \[(x+X)(y+Y)\Phi=(x^2-y^2)(x+X)(y+Y)-x(x+X)(\sigma+2cyY)+y(y+Y)(\sigma+2cxX).\] Since $(x+X)(y+Y)=xy+XY+\sigma$ and $x(x+X)-y(y+Y)=x^2-y^2+xX-yY$, the right side equals \[(x^2-y^2)(xy+XY)-\sigma(xX-yY)+2cxy(Xy-xY).\] Finally $\sigma(xX-yY)=(Xy+xY)(xX-yY)=xy(X^2-Y^2)+XY(x^2-y^2)$, so \[(x+X)(y+Y)\,\Phi=xy\bigl[(x^2-y^2)-(X^2-Y^2)+2c(Xy-xY)\bigr]=xy\,\Psi .\] By hypothesis $\Phi=0$, and $xy\ne0$; therefore $\Psi=0$, i.e. $DL^2=EK^2$, and $DL=EK$.

Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Monovariants

Maria owns $n$ islands. Some pairs of distinct islands are connected by shipping routes, along which ships travel in both directions. Each island belongs to exactly two routes, and no two routes share the same pair of islands. A cycle is a ship's journey that starts and ends at the same island without repeating any islands or routes, except for the initial island. In the route map below, there are cycles $F-G-K-F$, $A-B-J-D-A$ and $C-E-H-I-C$. Cycles that differ only in their starting island and direction of travel are considered identical. For example, the cycles $F-G-K-F$ and $G-F-K-G$ are identical.

Maria wants to modify the routes so that a ship can travel from any island to any other island using the routes. Each month, she chooses four distinct islands $X,Y,Z,W$ such that the routes $X-Y$ and $Z-W$ exist, but the routes $X-Z$ and $Y-W$ do not. The routes $X-Y$ and $Z-W$ are then removed and replaced with the new routes $X-Z$ and $Y-W$.

  • (a) Consider the case $n=11$, with the route map shown above. Maria makes the following substitutions:

    Month 1Month 2Month 3
    Routes removed$F-G,\ H-E$$I-C,\ G-K$$A-B,\ F-H$
    Routes added$F-H,\ G-E$$I-G,\ C-K$$A-F,\ B-H$

    How many cycles are there after each substitution?

  • (b) Explain why, for any $n$ and in any month of the reorganization, the routes can be partitioned into cycles so that every route and every island belongs to exactly one of these cycles.

  • (c) For an arbitrary initial route map with $n=2026$ and $314$ cycles, what is the minimum number of months Maria needs to achieve her goal?

Show the answer

(a) $2,\ 2,\ 1$ cycles; (c) $313$ months

Show the solution

Solution (Following the cycles)

(a) At the start there are $3$ cycles: $FGK$, $ABJD$, $CEHI$.

  • Month 1: removing $F-G$ and $H-E$ leaves the paths $F-K-G$ and $H-I-C-E$; the new routes $F-H$ and $G-E$ join them into the single cycle $F-K-G-E-C-I-H-F$. There are $\boxed{2}$ cycles (this one and $ABJD$).

  • Month 2: both removed routes $I-C$ and $G-K$ lie on the $7$-cycle. Removing them leaves the paths $G-E-C$ and $K-F-H-I$; adding $C-K$ and $I-G$ gives the single cycle $G-E-C-K-F-H-I-G$. Still $\boxed{2}$ cycles.

  • Month 3: $A-B$ lies on $ABJD$ and $F-H$ on the $7$-cycle. Removing them leaves the paths $A-D-J-B$ and $F-K-C-E-G-I-H$; adding $A-F$ and $B-H$ gives one cycle through all $11$ islands. Now there is $\boxed{1}$ cycle.

(b) A substitution does not change how many routes each island belongs to: $X$ loses $X-Y$ and gains $X-Z$, and similarly for $Y$, $Z$, $W$. The new routes did not exist before, so no pair of islands gets two routes. Hence at every moment each island belongs to exactly two routes. Now start at any island and keep leaving each island by the route we did not arrive on. Since there are finitely many islands, we eventually revisit an island; the first repeated island must be the starting one (any other island already has both of its routes used), so we have traced a cycle. Remove it (its islands use up both of their routes) and repeat with the remaining islands. This partitions all islands and routes into cycles.

(c) Maria's goal is a single cycle through all $2026$ islands (a connected map in which every island has two routes is one cycle).

Each month the number of cycles drops by at most $1$. If the two removed routes lie on different cycles, those two cycles become two paths, and the two new routes join them into one cycle: the number drops by exactly $1$. If both lie on the same cycle, removing them cuts it into two paths, and the two new routes produce one or two cycles from them; other cycles are untouched. So the number never drops by more than $1$, and going from $314$ cycles to $1$ takes at least $313$ months.

$313$ months suffice. While there are at least two cycles, choose a route $X-Y$ on one cycle and $Z-W$ on another. The routes $X-Z$ and $Y-W$ do not exist (no route joins different cycles), so the move is allowed, and it merges the two cycles. After $313$ such months a single cycle remains.

The minimum is $\boxed{313}$ months.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Slope, Parallelism, and Perpendicularity

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with orthocenter $H$. Let $P$ and $Q$ be points on the lines $AB$ and $AC$, respectively, such that $\angle PHQ=90^\circ$. Let $X$ be a point such that $BX\parallel PH$ and $CX\parallel QH$. Prove that $PQ\perp HX$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Dot products from the orthocenter)

Use vectors with origin $H$, and write $\vec A,\vec B,\dots$ for position vectors. Because $H$ is the orthocenter, $\vec A\cdot(\vec B-\vec C)=0$ and $\vec B\cdot(\vec C-\vec A)=0$, so \[\vec A\cdot\vec B=\vec B\cdot\vec C=\vec C\cdot\vec A=:k .\] The point $X$. The lines through $B$ parallel to $PH$ and through $C$ parallel to $QH$ are perpendicular (as $PH\perp QH$), so they meet at one point $X$, and \[\vec X=\vec B+s\vec P=\vec C+t\vec Q\quad\text{for some reals } s,t .\] Since $\vec P\cdot\vec Q=0$ ($\angle PHQ=90^\circ$), \[\vec X\cdot\vec P=(\vec C+t\vec Q)\cdot\vec P=\vec C\cdot\vec P,\qquad \vec X\cdot\vec Q=(\vec B+s\vec P)\cdot\vec Q=\vec B\cdot\vec Q .\] Both are equal to $k$. As $P$ is on line $AB$, $\vec P=\vec A+\lambda(\vec B-\vec A)$, so $\vec C\cdot\vec P=k+\lambda(k-k)=k$; likewise $\vec Q=\vec A+\mu(\vec C-\vec A)$ gives $\vec B\cdot\vec Q=k$. (Geometrically: $HC\perp AB$, so $\vec C\cdot\vec P$ does not depend on the position of $P$ on $AB$.)

Therefore $\vec X\cdot(\vec P-\vec Q)=k-k=0$, i.e. $HX\perp PQ$. (If $X=H$ the statement is empty; otherwise this is the required perpendicularity.)

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $BC$ on the $x$-axis and the altitude from $A$ on the $y$-axis: $A=(0,a)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a>0$, $b<0<c$. The altitude from $B$ is perpendicular to $AC$, whose direction is $(c,-a)$, so it is $c(x-b)-ay=0$; at $x=0$ it gives the orthocenter $H=(0,h)$ with $h=-\frac{bc}{a}>0$.

Points of line $AB$ are $P=(\lambda b,\ (1-\lambda)a)$ and of line $AC$ are $Q=(\mu c,\ (1-\mu)a)$. Write $p=\vec{HP}=(\lambda b,(1-\lambda)a-h)$ and $q=\vec{HQ}=(\mu c,(1-\mu)a-h)$; the hypothesis is $p\cdot q=0$.

$X=B+sp=C+tq$, and $\vec{HX}=\vec{HB}+sp=\vec{HC}+tq$ with $\vec{HB}=(b,-h)$, $\vec{HC}=(c,-h)$. Then \[\vec{HX}\cdot p=\vec{HC}\cdot p=c\lambda b-h(1-\lambda)a+h^2,\qquad \vec{HX}\cdot q=\vec{HB}\cdot q=b\mu c-h(1-\mu)a+h^2 .\] Using $ha=-bc$: $\vec{HC}\cdot p=\lambda bc+(1-\lambda)bc+h^2=bc+h^2$, and in the same way $\vec{HB}\cdot q=bc+h^2$. Hence $\vec{HX}\cdot(p-q)=0$. Since $p-q=\vec{QP}$, this says $HX\perp PQ$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 33: Radicals and Irrational Expressions · Bounding Irrational Values

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Find the minimum value of $c$, depending on $n$, such that, for all $x\ge c$, we have \[\lfloor x\sqrt{n}\rfloor>\lfloor x\sqrt{n-1}\rfloor>\lfloor x\sqrt{n-2}\rfloor>\dots>\lfloor x\sqrt{2}\rfloor>\lfloor x\sqrt{1}\rfloor .\]

Show the answer

$c=\dfrac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}$ (for $n\ge2$)

Show the solution

Solution (The last bad interval of each pair)

For $n=1$ there is no inequality to check, so let $n\ge2$; only $x>0$ matters.

Fix $k\ge1$. The inequality $\lfloor x\sqrt{k+1}\rfloor>\lfloor x\sqrt k\rfloor$ fails exactly when both numbers lie in the same interval $[m,m+1)$ for some integer $m\ge0$, i.e. \[\frac{m}{\sqrt k}\le x<\frac{m+1}{\sqrt{k+1}} .\] This interval is nonempty iff $m\sqrt{k+1}<(m+1)\sqrt k$, i.e. $m(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k)<\sqrt k$, i.e. \[m<\sqrt k(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt k)=k+\sqrt{k^2+k}.\] Since $k<\sqrt{k^2+k}<k+\frac12$, the largest such $m$ is $2k$. So the set of bad $x$ for the pair $(k,k+1)$ is contained in $\bigl[0,\frac{2k+1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\bigr)$ and contains the nonempty interval $\bigl[2\sqrt k,\frac{2k+1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\bigr)$.

Let $g(k)=\frac{2k+1}{\sqrt{k+1}}$. From $(2k+1)^2=4k(k+1)+1$ we get $g(k)^2=4k+\frac1{k+1}$, which is increasing in $k$. So among the pairs $k=1,\dots,n-1$ the bad sets all lie below $g(n-1)=\frac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}$, and the last pair has bad points arbitrarily close to it from below.

Consequently, for every $x\ge\frac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}$ all $n-1$ inequalities hold, while for every $c<\frac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}$ some $x\in\bigl[c,\frac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}\bigr)$ breaks the chain. The minimum is \[\boxed{c=\frac{2n-1}{\sqrt n}} .\]

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking · Strategic Residues

Agnes wrote $2027$ distinct prime numbers around a circle. Is it possible that all $2027$ sums of adjacent prime pairs around the circle are perfect squares?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (Squares modulo 4 and 8)

No. Suppose it were possible.

Odd primes only. If all $2027$ primes were odd, every adjacent sum would be even, hence a square divisible by $4$. Then neighbours would have $p+q\equiv0\pmod4$, so residues $1$ and $3$ modulo $4$ would alternate around the circle. An odd cycle cannot be $2$-coloured alternately, a contradiction.

The prime $2$ is used. It appears once (the primes are distinct). Its two neighbours $p,q$ are odd and $2+p$, $2+q$ are odd squares, which are $\equiv1\pmod8$; so $p\equiv q\equiv7\pmod8$, in particular $p\equiv q\equiv3\pmod4$. The other $2026$ primes are odd and form a path from $p$ to $q$ along the circle; as before adjacent ones have sum $\equiv0\pmod4$, so their residues modulo $4$ alternate. A path with $2026$ (an even number of) vertices has endpoints of different residues, contradicting $p\equiv q\pmod4$.

So it is impossible.

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Comparative Constraints

  • (a) Find all real numbers $a$ such that \[\sqrt{12x+13}\ge ax+a+1\quad\text{for all }x\in[-1,1].\]

  • (b) Prove that \[\sqrt{12x+13}+\sqrt{12y+13}-1\ge(x+y+1)^2\quad\text{for all }x,y\in[-1,1].\]

Show the answer

(a) $a\le2$; (b) proof

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A line under a concave curve)

(a) Let $g(x)=\sqrt{12x+13}$ on $[-1,1]$; $g(-1)=1$ and $g(1)=5$. The right side $\ell_a(x)=a(x+1)+1$ is the line through $(-1,1)$ with slope $a$.

If the inequality holds, then at $x=1$: $5\ge2a+1$, so $a\le2$.

Conversely let $a\le2$. For $x\in[-1,1]$ we have $\ell_a(x)\le\ell_2(x)=2x+3$ (since $x+1\ge0$), and \[g(x)\ge2x+3\iff12x+13\ge4x^2+12x+9\iff x^2\le1,\] using $2x+3>0$. So $g(x)\ge\ell_a(x)$ on $[-1,1]$. The answer is $\boxed{a\le2}$.

(b) By (a) with $a=2$, $\sqrt{12x+13}\ge2x+3$ and $\sqrt{12y+13}\ge2y+3$. With $s=x+y+1\in[-1,3]$, \[\sqrt{12x+13}+\sqrt{12y+13}-1\ge2(x+y)+5=2s+3\ge s^2,\] because $s^2-2s-3=(s-3)(s+1)\le0$ for $-1\le s\le3$. Equality holds for $x=y=1$ and for $x=y=-1$.

Remark. The AoPS transcription prints $\sqrt{13x+12}$. That expression is undefined for $-1\le x<-\frac{12}{13}$, and (b) would fail at $x=y=-\frac{12}{13}$ (the left side is $-1$). The official paper has $\sqrt{12x+13}$, as solved here.

Solution 2 (Rationalizing the slope)

(a) Write $g(x)=\sqrt{12x+13}$. At $x=-1$ both sides equal $1$, so only $-1<x\le1$ matters, and there the inequality $g(x)\ge a(x+1)+1$ is equivalent to \[a\le\frac{g(x)-1}{x+1}=\frac{g(x)^2-1}{(x+1)\bigl(g(x)+1\bigr)}=\frac{12(x+1)}{(x+1)\bigl(g(x)+1\bigr)}=\frac{12}{g(x)+1}.\] Since $g$ is increasing, $\frac{12}{g(x)+1}$ is decreasing on $(-1,1]$; its least value is $\frac{12}{g(1)+1}=\frac{12}{6}=2$, attained at $x=1$. Hence the inequality holds for all $x\in[-1,1]$ exactly when $\boxed{a\le2}$.

(b) The case $a=2$ of (a) gives $g(x)\ge2x+3$ and $g(y)\ge2y+3$. Adding, the left side of (b) is at least $2(x+y)+5$, and \[2(x+y)+5-(x+y+1)^2=4-(x+y)^2\ge0,\] because $|x+y|\le2$. This proves (b). Equality needs $|x+y|=2$, i.e. $x=y=1$ or $x=y=-1$, and both of these give equality.
