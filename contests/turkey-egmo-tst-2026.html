Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Consider a sequence of positive integers $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n$ such that for every integer $1\le i\le n$, the number $a_{i+1}$ equals the number of indices $j$ with $1\le j\le n$ satisfying $\gcd(a_i,a_j)=1$ (where $a_{n+1}=a_1$). Determine all possible values of $n$.

Show the answer

$n=1$ and $n=2$

Show the solution

Solution (The next term is a function of the current term)

For a positive integer $t$ let \[ c(t)=\#\{\,j:1\le j\le n,\ \gcd(t,a_j)=1\,\}. \] The condition says $a_{i+1}=c(a_i)$ for all $i$ (indices mod $n$). The key point is that $c$ depends only on the value $a_i$.

All terms are distinct. Since $a_{i+1}=c(a_i)$ and $a_{n+1}=a_1$, the sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ is the orbit of $a_1$ under the map $c$ and returns to $a_1$ after $n$ steps. Let $p$ be the least period of this orbit; then $p\mid n$, the values $a_1,\dots,a_p$ are distinct, and the $n$ terms consist of these $p$ values, each repeated $r=n/p$ times. Consequently, for every $t$, \[ c(t)=r\cdot\#\{\,s\le p:\gcd(t,a_s)=1\,\}, \] so every term $a_{i+1}=c(a_i)$ is a multiple of $r$. If $r\ge2$, any two terms have a common divisor $r\ge2$, so $c(a_i)=0$ for every $i$, hence $a_{i+1}=0$, which is not a positive integer. Hence $r=1$: the $n$ terms are distinct.

They form $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Each term is a count of indices, so $1\le a_i\le n$. Being $n$ distinct values in $\{1,\dots,n\}$, they are exactly $1,2,\dots,n$. Hence $c(t)$ is the number of $j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ coprime to $t$, and $c$ must be a bijection of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (it maps the set of values onto itself, $a_i\mapsto a_{i+1}$).

Only $n\le2$. If $n\ge4$, then $c(2)=c(4)$ (both count the odd numbers in $\{1,\dots,n\}$), so $c$ is not injective. If $n=3$, then $c(2)=\#\{1,3\}=2=\#\{1,2\}=c(3)$, again impossible.

Examples. For $n=1$, $a_1=1$ works ($\gcd(1,1)=1$, so the count is $1$). For $n=2$, $(a_1,a_2)=(1,2)$ works: $a_1=1$ is coprime to both terms, giving $a_2=2$, and $a_2=2$ is coprime only to $a_1$, giving $a_3=1=a_1$.

So the possible values are $\boxed{n\in\{1,2\}}$.

Remark. A different ending, once the terms are known to be $1,\dots,n$ and $c$ is a bijection: since $c(1)=n$ and $c(t)\ge1$ for all $t$ (the term $1$ is coprime to $t$), some $t\le n$ has $c(t)=1$, i.e. $t$ has a common factor with each of $2,\dots,n$. For $n\ge3$, $t$ is then divisible by $2$ and by the largest prime $q\le n$, which is odd; so $t\ge2q>n$ by Bertrand's postulate, a contradiction. The argument with $c(2)=c(4)$ above avoids Bertrand's postulate.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Real numbers are written at the vertices of a regular $n$-gon, and the sum of all written numbers is $0$.

For any two distinct vertices, let the numbers at these vertices be $x$ and $y$. Let $S$ be the sum of the numbers written at the vertices lying strictly on one side of the line joining these two vertices, and let $T$ be the sum of the numbers written at the vertices lying strictly on the other side.

If \[|x-y|\ge|S-T|,\] we call this pair of vertices a good pair. Find the minimum possible value of the number of good pairs.

Show the answer

$n-1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Prefix sums and inversions of a cyclic permutation)

Label the vertices $V_0,\dots,V_{n-1}$ in circular order with numbers $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$, and let \[ P_k=a_0+a_1+\dots+a_{k-1}\qquad(0\le k\le n),\qquad P_0=P_n=0 . \]

Reformulation. Take $i<j$, $x=a_i$, $y=a_j$; one side contains $V_{i+1},\dots,V_{j-1}$ with sum $S$, the other side has sum $T=-(x+y+S)$. Then $S-T=2S+x+y$ and \[ (x-y)^2-(S-T)^2=(x-y-2S-x-y)(x-y+2S+x+y)=-4(S+x)(S+y). \] As $S+x=P_j-P_i$ and $S+y=P_{j+1}-P_{i+1}$, the pair $\{V_i,V_j\}$ is good iff \[ (P_j-P_i)(P_{j+1}-P_{i+1})\le0 .\tag{$*$} \]

Construction with $n-1$ good pairs. Take $a=(1,1,\dots,1,-(n-1))$, so $P_k=k$ for $0\le k\le n-1$ and $P_n=0$. For $i<j\le n-2$ the product in $(*)$ is $(j-i)^2>0$; for $j=n-1$ it is $(n-1-i)(0-(i+1))<0$. So exactly the $n-1$ pairs $\{V_i,V_{n-1}\}$ are good.

Reduction to distinct prefix sums. The numbers $P_1,\dots,P_{n-1}$ may be prescribed arbitrarily (then $a_k=P_{k+1}-P_k$ with $P_n=0$ has sum $0$). A pair that is not good satisfies the strict inequality $(P_j-P_i)(P_{j+1}-P_{i+1})>0$, which survives small changes of the $P_k$. Perturbing $P_1,\dots,P_{n-1}$ slightly so that $P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}$ become pairwise distinct therefore does not increase the number of good pairs. So it suffices to show that there are at least $n-1$ good pairs when $P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}$ are distinct.

Inversions. Let $\rho(k)\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ be the rank of $P_k$ among $P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}$ (indices mod $n$, so $P_n=P_0$), and define the permutation $\tau$ of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ by $\tau(\rho(k))=\rho(k+1)$. It is a single $n$-cycle $\rho(0)\to\rho(1)\to\dots\to\rho(n-1)\to\rho(0)$. The product in $(*)$ is negative exactly when $\rho(i),\rho(j)$ and $\tau(\rho(i)),\tau(\rho(j))$ are in opposite orders, i.e. when $\{\rho(i),\rho(j)\}$ is an inversion of $\tau$; with distinct $P_k$ it is never zero. So the number of good pairs equals the number of inversions of $\tau$.

Lemma. A permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $c$ cycles has at least $n-c$ inversions. Induction on the number of inversions: with no inversion the permutation is the identity and $c=n$. Otherwise there is an $x$ with $\tau(x)>\tau(x+1)$; then $\tau'=\tau\circ(x\ x{+}1)$ has one inversion fewer, and composing with a transposition merges two cycles or splits one, so $c(\tau')\le c(\tau)+1$. By induction, $\mathrm{inv}(\tau)-1=\mathrm{inv}(\tau')\ge n-c(\tau')\ge n-c(\tau)-1$.

For our $n$-cycle, $c=1$, so there are at least $n-1$ good pairs. The minimum is $\boxed{n-1}$.

Solution 2 (The good pairs form a connected graph)

Label the vertices $V_0,\dots,V_{n-1}$ in circular order, with numbers $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$, and put $P_k=a_0+\dots+a_{k-1}$, so $P_0=P_n=0$; indices of $P$ are taken mod $n$. For $i<j$ let $x=a_i$, $y=a_j$ and let $S$ be the sum of the numbers strictly between them (at $V_{i+1},\dots,V_{j-1}$); then the other side has sum $T=-(x+y+S)$ and \[ (x-y)^2-(S-T)^2=-4(S+x)(S+y)=-4(P_j-P_i)(P_{j+1}-P_{i+1}), \] so $\{V_i,V_j\}$ is good iff $(P_j-P_i)(P_{j+1}-P_{i+1})\le0$.

Example. $a=(1,\dots,1,-(n-1))$ gives $P_k=k$ for $k\le n-1$; the product is $(j-i)^2>0$ for $j\le n-2$ and $-(n-1-i)(i+1)<0$ for $j=n-1$: exactly $n-1$ good pairs.

Bound. The values $P_1,\dots,P_{n-1}$ can be chosen freely (then $a_k=P_{k+1}-P_k$, with $P_n=0$, have sum $0$). A pair that is not good satisfies a strict inequality, which is kept under a small change of the $P_k$; so after a small change making $P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}$ distinct the number of good pairs has not increased. Assume they are distinct. Then $i,j$ form a good pair iff the order of $P_i,P_j$ is opposite to the order of $P_{i+1},P_{j+1}$. Let $G$ be the graph on $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ whose edges are the good pairs.

(i) If $\{i,j\}$ is an edge with $P_i<P_j$, and $P_i<P_l<P_j$, then $l$ is joined to $i$ or to $j$. Indeed $P_{i+1}>P_{j+1}$; if $P_{l+1}<P_{i+1}$ then $\{i,l\}$ is good, and otherwise $P_{l+1}>P_{i+1}>P_{j+1}$ and $\{l,j\}$ is good.

(ii) List the indices as $i_1,\dots,i_n$ with $P_{i_1}<\dots<P_{i_n}$, fix $1\le r<n$ and let $L=\{i_1,\dots,i_r\}$. The shift $k\mapsto k+1\pmod n$ is a single cycle, so it does not map $L$ onto itself; as $L$ and its image have the same size, there are $a\in L$ with $a+1\notin L$ and $b\notin L$ with $b+1\in L$. Then $P_a<P_b$ and $P_{a+1}>P_{b+1}$, so $\{a,b\}$ is an edge. By (i) every index $l$ with $P_a\le P_l\le P_b$ lies in the component of $G$ containing $a$ and $b$. Since $P_a\le P_{i_r}<P_{i_{r+1}}\le P_b$, the indices $i_r$ and $i_{r+1}$ lie in one component of $G$.

This holds for every $r$, so $G$ is connected. A connected graph on $n$ vertices has at least $n-1$ edges, so there are at least $n-1$ good pairs. The minimum is $\boxed{n-1}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with orthocenter $H$ and circumcenter $O$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$, and let $P$ be the second intersection point of the line $AH$ with the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Let $Q$ be the intersection of the line through $A$ perpendicular to $HM$ and the line through $H$ parallel to $BC$. Finally, let $R$ be the intersection point of the ray $HO$ with the circumcircle of triangle $APQ$.

Prove that $|AR|=2|HM|$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 ($R$ is the reflection of $H$ in $O$)

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle, $A'$ the antipode of $A$ on $\Gamma$, and $H'$ the reflection of $H$ in $O$.

Classical facts. Since $\angle ACA'=90^\circ$, $A'C\perp AC$, and also $BH\perp AC$; so $BH\parallel CA'$, and similarly $CH\parallel BA'$. Thus $BHCA'$ is a parallelogram and its diagonals bisect each other: $M$ is the midpoint of $HA'$. The quadrilateral $AHA'H'$ has diagonals $AA'$ and $HH'$ with common midpoint $O$, so it is a parallelogram: \[ AH'\parallel HA'\ \ (\text{the line }HM),\qquad AH'=HA'=2HM . \]

$H'$ lies on the circle $APQ$. As $AQ\perp HM$ and $AH'\parallel HM$, we get $\angle QAH'=90^\circ$. Let $Z$ be the midpoint of $QH'$; then $ZA=ZQ=ZH'$ (Thales). Moreover \[ \vec{OZ}=\tfrac12\bigl(\vec{OQ}+\vec{OH'}\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(\vec{OQ}-\vec{OH}\bigr)=\tfrac12\,\vec{HQ}, \] which is parallel to $BC$. So $Z$ lies on the line $\ell$ through $O$ parallel to $BC$. Since $AP\perp BC$ and $OA=OP$, $\ell$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AP$, so $ZP=ZA$. Hence $A,P,Q,H'$ lie on the circle with centre $Z$. The points $A,P,Q$ are not collinear ($Q\ne H$ lies on the perpendicular to $AP$ at $H$), so this is the circle $APQ$.

$R=H'$. The orthocentre of an acute triangle lies strictly between $A$ and $P$ on the chord $AP$, so $H$ is inside the circle $APQ$ and the ray $HO$ meets this circle in exactly one point. $H'=H+2\,\vec{HO}$ lies on this ray ($H\ne O$, the triangle being scalene) and on the circle, so $R=H'$. Therefore \[ AR=AH'=HA'=2\,HM . \] (The point $Q$ exists: $AQ\perp HM$ is parallel to $BC$ only if $HM\perp BC$, i.e. $M$ on the altitude $AH$, i.e. $AB=AC$.)

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the altitude)

Let the foot of the altitude from $A$ be the origin, $BC$ the $x$-axis, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $b<0<c$, $a>0$, $a^2+bc>0$ (the angle at $A$ is acute) and $b+c\ne0$ (as $AB\ne AC$).

The points. $H=(0,-bc/a)$, since $\vec{BH}\cdot\vec{AC}=(-b)c+(-bc/a)(-a)=0$. Reflecting $H$ in $BC$ gives the second point of the altitude on the circumcircle, so $P=(0,bc/a)$. The circumcentre lies on $x=\tfrac{b+c}2$ and on the perpendicular bisector of $AP$, so $O=\bigl(\tfrac{b+c}2,\tfrac{a^2+bc}{2a}\bigr)$; also $M=\bigl(\tfrac{b+c}2,0\bigr)$ and \[ H'=2O-H=\Bigl(b+c,\ a+\frac{2bc}{a}\Bigr). \] $Q=(q,-bc/a)$ with $\vec{AQ}\cdot\vec{HM}=0$: $q\,\tfrac{b+c}{2}+\bigl(-\tfrac{bc}{a}-a\bigr)\tfrac{bc}a=0$, so $q(b+c)=\dfrac{2bc(a^2+bc)}{a^2}$; note $q\ne0$.

The circle. Every circle through $A$ and $P$ has an equation \[ x^2-2gx+(y-a)\Bigl(y-\frac{bc}{a}\Bigr)=0 , \] since at $x=0$ it must vanish exactly at $y=a$ and $y=bc/a$. For $y=-bc/a$ and for $y=a+2bc/a$ the second term equals $\tfrac{2bc}{a}\bigl(a+\tfrac{bc}a\bigr)=\tfrac{2bc(a^2+bc)}{a^2}=q(b+c)$. Hence

  • $Q$ lies on the circle iff $q^2-2gq+q(b+c)=0$, i.e. $2g=q+b+c$;

  • $H'$ lies on the circle iff $(b+c)^2-2g(b+c)+q(b+c)=0$, i.e. (as $b+c\ne0$) $2g=q+b+c$.

So the circle $APQ$ (with $2g=q+b+c$) passes through $H'$.

Conclusion. $H$ lies strictly inside the chord $AP$, hence inside the circle $APQ$, so the ray $HO$ meets the circle once, and $H'=H+2\vec{HO}$ is that point: $R=H'$. Finally \[ AR^2=(b+c)^2+\Bigl(\frac{2bc}{a}\Bigr)^2=4\Bigl(\bigl(\tfrac{b+c}2\bigr)^2+\bigl(\tfrac{bc}a\bigr)^2\Bigr)=4\,HM^2 , \] so $AR=2HM$.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $0\le x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{1001}\le 1$ be real numbers. Find the maximum possible value of \[\sum_{i=1}^{1001}x_i-\sum_{i=1}^{1001}x_ix_{i+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{1001}x_ix_{i+2},\] where $x_{1002}=x_1$ and $x_{1003}=x_2$.

Show the answer

$333$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Reduce to $0$ and $1$, then count gaps)

Let $F(x)=\sum x_i-\sum x_ix_{i+1}-\sum x_ix_{i+2}$ (indices mod $1001$).

Step 1: we may assume $x_i\in\{0,1\}$. No term of $F$ contains $x_i^2$, so for fixed values of the other variables $F$ is a linear function of $x_i$ on $[0,1]$, and its maximum there is attained at $x_i=0$ or $x_i=1$. Replacing $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{1001}$ one after another by the better endpoint never decreases $F$. So the maximum of $F$ equals its maximum over $\{0,1\}^{1001}$.

Step 2: the $0/1$ case. Let $k$ be the number of ones. Then $F=k-e$, where $e$ is the number of pairs of ones at cyclic distance $1$ or $2$ (each such pair is counted exactly once in $F$, because $1001\ge5$). If $k\le1$ then $F=k\le1$. Let $k\ge2$, and let $g_1,\dots,g_k$ be the numbers of zeros in the $k$ gaps between cyclically consecutive ones, so $\sum g_m=1001-k$. A gap with $g_m\le1$ gives a pair of ones at distance $1$ or $2$, and different gaps give different pairs (for $k=2$ at most one gap is that small, as $g_1+g_2=999$). If $z$ gaps have $g_m\le1$, the other $k-z$ gaps have at least $2$ zeros, so $2(k-z)\le1001-k$. Therefore \[ F\le k-z\le k-\frac{3k-1001}{2}=\frac{1001-k}{2}. \] Together with $F\le k$: if $k\le333$ then $F\le333$; if $k\ge334$ then $F\le\frac{667}2<334$. As $F$ is an integer, $F\le333$.

Equality. Put $x_i=1$ for $i=1,4,7,\dots,997$ and $x_i=0$ otherwise: $333$ ones, any two at cyclic distance at least $3$ (the last gap, from $997$ to $1$, has four zeros), so $F=333$. The maximum is $\boxed{333}$.

Solution 2 (A three-term inequality)

For $a,b,c\in[0,1]$ we have $(1-a)(1-b)(1-c)\ge0$, that is, \[ a+b+c-ab-bc-ca\le1-abc\le1 . \] Apply this to $(x_i,x_{i+1},x_{i+2})$ for $i=1,\dots,1001$ and add. Each $x_i$ occurs three times, each product $x_ix_{i+1}$ twice and each product $x_ix_{i+2}$ once, so with $\Sigma_0=\sum x_i$, $\Sigma_1=\sum x_ix_{i+1}$, $\Sigma_2=\sum x_ix_{i+2}$: \[ 3\Sigma_0-2\Sigma_1-\Sigma_2\le1001 . \] Hence \[ 3F=3\Sigma_0-3\Sigma_1-3\Sigma_2=(3\Sigma_0-2\Sigma_1-\Sigma_2)-\Sigma_1-2\Sigma_2\le1001, \] so $F\le\frac{1001}3<334$ for all admissible $x$. Moreover $F$ is linear in each variable separately (no term contains $x_i^2$), so replacing the variables one at a time by the better of $0$ and $1$ does not decrease $F$; hence the maximum is attained at a point of $\{0,1\}^{1001}$, where $F$ is an integer, and it is at most $333$. For $x_i=1$ when $i\in\{1,4,7,\dots,997\}$ and $x_i=0$ otherwise, there are $333$ ones, no two at cyclic distance $1$ or $2$, so $F=333$. The maximum is $\boxed{333}$.

Solution 3 (A random set of indices)

Let $F(x)=\sum x_i-\sum x_ix_{i+1}-\sum x_ix_{i+2}$, indices taken modulo $1001$. Call two indices close if their cyclic distance is $1$ or $2$. The close pairs are the $1001$ pairs $\{i,i+1\}$ and the $1001$ pairs $\{i,i+2\}$; these $2002$ pairs are all different (because $1001\ge5$), so $F$ contains exactly one product $x_ix_j$ for each close pair $\{i,j\}$.

A random set. Fix $x_1,\dots,x_{1001}\in[0,1]$ and choose a random set $S\subseteq\{1,\dots,1001\}$ by putting each index $i$ into $S$ with probability $x_i$, independently of the others. For a set $S$ let $e(S)$ be the number of close pairs with both indices in $S$. The expected size of $S$ is $\sum x_i$. A close pair $\{i,j\}$ consists of two different indices, so by independence both lie in $S$ with probability $x_ix_j$; adding over all close pairs, the expected value of $e(S)$ is $\sum x_ix_{i+1}+\sum x_ix_{i+2}$. By linearity of expectation, \[ F(x)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,|S|-e(S)\,\bigr]. \]

Every set gives at most $333$. Let $S$ be any set of indices. For each of the $e(S)$ close pairs inside $S$, delete one of its two indices from $S$. At most $e(S)$ indices are deleted, so the remaining set $I$ has $|I|\ge|S|-e(S)$, and $I$ contains no close pair (every close pair of $S$ has lost an index). If $|I|\ge2$, go around the circle and look at the cyclic distances between consecutive elements of $I$: there are $|I|$ of them, each is at least $3$, and they add up to $1001$. Hence $3|I|\le1001$, so $|I|\le333$; this also holds if $|I|\le1$. Therefore $|S|-e(S)\le333$ for every $S$.

Conclusion. An expected value of a quantity that is always at most $333$ is at most $333$, so $F(x)\le333$ for all admissible $x$. For $x_i=1$ when $i\in\{1,4,7,\dots,997\}$ and $x_i=0$ otherwise, there are $333$ ones and no two of them are close (the last gap, from $997$ to $1$, has four zeros), so every product in $F$ vanishes and $F=333$. The maximum is $\boxed{333}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

In a triangle $ABC$ with $|AB|\ne|AC|$ and $\angle BAC\ne 90^\circ$, let $D$ be a point on the line $AC$ lying on the side of $A$ opposite to $C$, chosen so that $|AD|=|AB|$. Let $E$ be the intersection point of the line $AB$ with the line through $C$ perpendicular to $BD$. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of the segments $[CD]$ and $[BE]$, respectively. Let $P$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of the circumcircle of $ABC$ that contains $A$, and let $Q$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ that does not contain $A$. Let $K$ be the intersection point of the lines $CE$ and $BD$, and let $L$ be the intersection point of the lines $PM$ and $QN$. Prove that the lines $KL$ and $MN$ are perpendicular.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates with the bisectors as axes)

Let $b=AC$, $c=AB$, $\alpha=\tfrac12\angle BAC$. By hypothesis $b\ne c$ and $\angle BAC\ne90^\circ$. (Both conditions are needed: if $b=c$ then $P=A=M$ and the line $PM$ is not defined, and if $\angle BAC=90^\circ$ then $K=L$, as we shall see.)

Put $A$ at the origin, the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ as the $x$-axis and the external bisector as the $y$-axis, and let $u=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, $v=(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha)$. Then \[ B=cu,\quad C=bv,\quad D=-cv,\quad M=\tfrac{b-c}{2}\,v . \] $\vec{DB}=c(u+v)=(2c\cos\alpha,0)$ is horizontal, so the line through $C$ perpendicular to $BD$ is $x=b\cos\alpha$; it meets line $AB$ (the points $\lambda u$) at $\lambda=b$. So $E=bu$, $N=\tfrac{b+c}{2}u$, and $K$, the intersection of $x=b\cos\alpha$ with the horizontal line $BD$ ($y=c\sin\alpha$), is $K=(b\cos\alpha,\ c\sin\alpha)$.

Arc midpoints. The circumcircle passes through the origin, so its points satisfy $|X|^2=2X\cdot O$, $O$ the circumcentre. From $B$ and $C$: $u\cdot O=\tfrac c2$, $v\cdot O=\tfrac b2$, so $O=\bigl(\tfrac{b+c}{4\cos\alpha},\tfrac{c-b}{4\sin\alpha}\bigr)$. The internal bisector meets the circle again at $Q$ and the external bisector at $P$: \[ Q=\Bigl(\frac{b+c}{2\cos\alpha},0\Bigr),\qquad P=\Bigl(0,\frac{c-b}{2\sin\alpha}\Bigr). \] (The internal bisector meets the circle again at the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, the external bisector at the midpoint of the arc containing $A$; the latter is distinct from $A$ because $b\ne c$.) Moreover $Q\ne N$ and $P\ne M$: the point $N$ lies on $AB$ and $Q$ on the internal bisector, so $Q=N$ would force $Q=A$; similarly $P=M$ would force $P=A$. So the lines $QN$ and $PM$ are defined. The point $L$. We have $Q\cdot u=\tfrac{b+c}2=N\cdot u$ and $P\cdot v=\tfrac{b-c}2=M\cdot v$. Hence line $QN$ is $\{X:X\cdot u=\tfrac{b+c}2\}$ (perpendicular to $AB$) and line $PM$ is $\{X:X\cdot v=\tfrac{b-c}2\}$ (perpendicular to $AC$), and solving, \[ L=\Bigl(\frac{b}{2\cos\alpha},\ \frac{c}{2\sin\alpha}\Bigr). \] Perpendicularity. Using $1-2\cos^2\alpha=-\cos2\alpha$ and $1-2\sin^2\alpha=\cos2\alpha$, \[ \vec{KL}=\cos2\alpha\Bigl(-\frac{b}{2\cos\alpha},\ \frac{c}{2\sin\alpha}\Bigr),\qquad \vec{MN}=N-M=(c\cos\alpha,\ b\sin\alpha), \] and $\vec{KL}\cdot\vec{MN}=\cos2\alpha\,\bigl(-\tfrac{bc}2+\tfrac{bc}2\bigr)=0$. Since $\cos2\alpha=\cos\angle BAC\ne0$, $K\ne L$, and $KL\perp MN$.

Solution 2 (Feet of the arc midpoints and a difference of squares)

Let $b=AC$ and $c=AB$ (by hypothesis $b\ne c$) and let $\ell$ be the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$. On line $AB$ use the coordinate along ray $AB$ ($B=c$), and on line $AC$ the coordinate along ray $AC$ ($C=b$).

Step 1: $D$, $E$, $M$, $N$. $D=-c$ on line $AC$. The triangle $ABD$ is isosceles with apex $A$, so $BD$ is perpendicular to the bisector of $\angle BAD$, which is the external bisector of $\angle BAC$; hence $BD\parallel\ell$ and $CE\perp\ell$. The reflection in $\ell$ maps $C$ to the point of ray $AB$ at distance $b$ from $A$, and the segment joining them is perpendicular to $\ell$; so $E=b$ on line $AB$. Therefore \[ CD=b+c,\quad BE=|b-c|,\quad M=\tfrac{b-c}2\ \text{on }AC,\quad N=\tfrac{b+c}2\ \text{on }AB . \]

Step 2: $N$ is the foot of $Q$ on $AB$, and $M$ the foot of $P$ on $AC$. $Q$ lies on $\ell$ and $QB=QC$. Its feet on $AB$ and $AC$ are symmetric in $\ell$, with the same coordinate $s$, and the right triangles they form with $B$, $C$ have equal legs (the distances from $Q$ to the two lines) and equal hypotenuses $QB=QC$; so $|s-c|=|s-b|$, and as $b\ne c$, $s=\tfrac{b+c}2$. The foot is $N$.

$P$ lies on the external bisector and $PB=PC$. The reflection in the external bisector maps ray $AB$ onto the ray opposite to $AC$, so if the foot of $P$ on $AB$ has coordinate $s$, its foot on $AC$ has coordinate $-s$. As before $|s-c|=|-s-b|=|s+b|$; the case $s-c=s+b$ is impossible, so $s-c=-(s+b)$, $s=\tfrac{c-b}2$, and the foot on $AC$ is $\tfrac{b-c}2$, which is $M$.

Thus $LN\perp AB$ and $LM\perp AC$.

Step 3: $LM^2-LN^2=bc$. The right angles at $M$ and $N$ give $LM^2=AL^2-AM^2$ and $LN^2=AL^2-AN^2$, so \[ LM^2-LN^2=AN^2-AM^2=\frac{(b+c)^2-(b-c)^2}{4}=bc . \]

Step 4: $KM^2-KN^2=bc$. $K$ lies on $BD$ and on $CE$, and these lines are perpendicular, so $\angle CKD=\angle BKE=90^\circ$ (or $K$ coincides with one of the points). Hence $K$ lies on the circles with diameters $CD$ and $BE$, whose centres are $M$ and $N$: \[ KM^2-KN^2=\frac{CD^2-BE^2}{4}=\frac{(b+c)^2-(b-c)^2}4=bc . \]

Step 5. For a fixed segment $MN$, the points $X$ with $XM^2-XN^2$ equal to a given constant form a line perpendicular to $MN$. By Steps 3 and 4, $K$ and $L$ lie on the same such line, so $KL\perp MN$. (The line $KL$ is defined because $K\ne L$; this fails only for $\angle BAC=90^\circ$, which the statement excludes: with $A$ as origin and $AB$, $AC$ as axes, $BD$ is $x-y=c$ and $CE$ is $x+y=b$, so $K=(\tfrac{b+c}2,\tfrac{b-c}2)$, and $ANLM$ is a rectangle, so $L=(\tfrac{b+c}2,\tfrac{b-c}2)=K$.)

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Find all polynomials $P$ with integer coefficients such that for all coprime positive integers $m$ and $n$, \[\gcd\big(n+P(m),\,mP(n)\big)=1.\]

Show the answer

$P(x)=x^k$ and $P(x)=-x^k$ with $k\ge1$

Show the solution

Solution (Primes of $P(m)$ divide $m$)

These polynomials work. Let $P=\pm x^k$, $k\ge1$, and let $q$ be a prime dividing both $n\pm m^k$ and $m\cdot(\pm n^k)$. Then $q\mid m$ or $q\mid n$; if $q\mid m$ then $q\mid n$, and if $q\mid n$ then $q\mid m^k$, so $q\mid m$. Either way $q$ divides the coprime numbers $m,n$, impossible. (The first number is $0$ only for the minus sign with $n=m^k$, which for coprime $m,n$ means $m=n=1$; there the gcd is $\gcd(0,-1)=1$ directly.)

Now let $P$ satisfy the condition. $P\ne0$, since $\gcd(n,0)=n$ fails for $n=2$, $m=1$.

Step 1: $P(0)=0$. Let $p$ be a prime with $p\nmid P(0)$. Choose $n\ge1$ with $n\equiv-P(0)\pmod p$; then $p\nmid n$, so $\gcd(p,n)=1$. Take $m=p$: then $n+P(p)\equiv n+P(0)\equiv0$ and $p\mid p\,P(n)$, so $p$ divides the gcd, a contradiction. Hence every prime divides $P(0)$, and $P(0)=0$.

So $P(x)=x^kQ(x)$ with $k\ge1$, $Q\in\mathbb Z[x]$, $Q(0)\ne0$.

Step 2: if a prime $p$ divides $P(m)$, then $p\mid m$. Suppose $p\mid P(m)$ and $p\nmid m$. Take $n=p$, coprime to $m$. Then $p\mid n+P(m)$, and $p\mid mP(p)$ because $p\mid P(p)$ (as $P(0)=0$). Contradiction.

Step 3: $Q=\pm1$. Take any $m\ge2$ coprime to $Q(0)$. Every prime factor of $Q(m)$ divides $P(m)$, hence divides $m$ (Step 2). But $Q(m)\equiv Q(0)\pmod m$, so $\gcd(Q(m),m)=\gcd(Q(0),m)=1$; in particular $Q(m)\ne0$, as $m\ge2$. Hence $Q(m)$ has no prime factor: $Q(m)=\pm1$. This holds for the infinitely many $m\ge2$ with $m\equiv1\pmod{Q(0)}$, so one of the values $1,-1$ is taken infinitely often and $Q$ is that constant.

Remark. Step 3 can also be done with Schur's theorem. Suppose $Q$ is not constant. By Schur's theorem infinitely many primes divide some value $Q(m)$, $m\ge1$; take such a prime $p\nmid Q(0)$ with $p\mid Q(m)$. Then $p\nmid m$ (otherwise $Q(m)\equiv Q(0)\not\equiv0 \pmod p$), but $p\mid P(m)$, contradicting Step 2. So $Q$ is a constant $c\ne0$; a prime $p\mid c$ would divide $P(1)=c$ but not $1$, again contradicting Step 2. Hence $c=\pm1$.

Therefore the solutions are $\boxed{P(x)=x^k\ \text{and}\ P(x)=-x^k\ \ (k\ge1)}$.
