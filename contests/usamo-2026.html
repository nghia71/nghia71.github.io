Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

Let $n$ be an integer greater than $1$. For which real numbers $x$ is \[\lfloor nx\rfloor-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lfloor kx\rfloor}{k}\] maximal, and what is the maximal value that this expression can take?

Note: $\lfloor z\rfloor$ denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to $z$.

Show the answer

The maximum is $\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n$; it is attained exactly when $x-\lfloor x\rfloor\ge 1-\frac1n$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Strong induction on a summation identity)

Write $E_n(x)=\lfloor nx\rfloor-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lfloor kx\rfloor}{k}$ and $H_n=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$. We prove that \[E_n(x)\le H_n-1=\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n\quad\text{for all real }x,\qquad\text{with equality if and only if } x-\lfloor x\rfloor\ge 1-\frac1n .\]

Reduction to $0\le x<1$. Replacing $x$ by $x+1$ adds $n$ to $\lfloor nx\rfloor$ and adds $\frac kk=1$ to each of the $n$ terms of the sum, so $E_n(x+1)=E_n(x)$. Hence we may assume $0\le x<1$.

Notation and two facts. Put $a_k=\lfloor kx\rfloor$ for $k\ge1$ and $S_m=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{a_k}{k}$, $S_0=0$.

  • For all $i,j\ge1$ we have $a_{i+j}\le a_i+a_j+1$, because $(i+j)x<(a_i+1)+(a_j+1)$.

  • For every $n\ge1$, \[nS_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}S_k.\tag{1}\] Indeed $kS_k=kS_{k-1}+a_k$, so $kS_k-(k-1)S_{k-1}=S_{k-1}+a_k$; adding these equalities for $k=1,\dots,n$ gives (1).

The inequality. We show by strong induction that $S_n\ge a_n-(H_n-1)$ for all $n\ge1$; this is the same as $E_n(x)\le H_n-1$. For $n=1$ both sides are $a_1$. Let $n\ge2$ and assume the claim for $1,\dots,n-1$. By (1) and the induction hypothesis, \[nS_n\ \ge\ a_n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_k+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(a_k-H_k+1\bigr)=a_n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(a_k+a_{n-k})-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(H_k-1).\tag{2}\] By the first fact, $a_k+a_{n-k}\ge a_n-1$ for each $k$, and $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}H_k=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-j}{j}=nH_{n-1}-(n-1)=nH_n-n$ (the term $\frac1j$ occurs in $H_j,\dots,H_{n-1}$). Therefore \[nS_n\ \ge\ a_n+(n-1)(a_n-1)-(nH_n-n)+(n-1)=n\,a_n-n(H_n-1),\] which is the claim for $n$.

Equality. If $1-\frac1n\le x<1$, then $k-1\le kx<k$ for every $1\le k\le n$ (because $x\ge1-\frac1k$), so $a_k=k-1$ and \[E_n(x)=(n-1)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl(1-\frac1k\Bigr)=H_n-1.\] Conversely, let $n\ge2$, $0\le x<1$ and $E_n(x)=H_n-1$. Then equality holds everywhere in (2) and in the step after it; in particular $S_{n-1}=a_{n-1}-(H_{n-1}-1)$ and $a_1+a_{n-1}=a_n-1$. We show by induction on $n$ that $x\ge1-\frac1n$. For $n=2$: $a_1=0$, so $a_2=2a_1+1=1$ and $x\ge\frac12$. For $n\ge3$: equality holds for $n-1$, so by the induction hypothesis $x\ge1-\frac1{n-1}$, hence $a_{n-1}=n-2$, and then $a_n=a_1+a_{n-1}+1=n-1$, i.e. $nx\ge n-1$.

Therefore the expression is maximal exactly for the real numbers $x$ whose fractional part is at least $1-\frac1n$ (that is, $x\in\bigl[m+1-\frac1n,\,m+1\bigr)$ for an integer $m$), and the maximal value is \[\boxed{\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n}\]

Solution 2 (Values at the breakpoints and sorted residues)

Write $E(x)=\lfloor nx\rfloor-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lfloor kx\rfloor}{k}$. We prove that $E(x)\le\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n$, with equality exactly when $x-\lfloor x\rfloor\ge1-\frac1n$.

Reduction to $0\le x<1$. Replacing $x$ by $x+1$ adds $n$ to $\lfloor nx\rfloor$ and adds $\frac kk=1$ to each of the $n$ terms of the sum, so $E(x+1)=E(x)$. Hence we may assume $0\le x<1$.

Reduction to the points $\frac jn$. Let $0\le j\le n-1$ and $\frac jn\le x<\frac{j+1}n$. On this interval $\lfloor nx\rfloor=j$ is constant, while every $\lfloor kx\rfloor$ is a nondecreasing function of $x$. Hence $E(x)\le E(\frac jn)$ on the interval.

The value at $\frac jn$. Let $r_k$ be the remainder of $kj$ upon division by $n$, so $\lfloor\frac{kj}n\rfloor=\frac{kj-r_k}{n}$ and $r_n=0$. Then \[E\Bigl(\frac jn\Bigr)=j-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl(\frac jn-\frac{r_k}{kn}\Bigr)=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{r_k}{k}.\]

The remainders. Let $d=\gcd(j,n)$ (so $d=n$ if $j=0$), $n=dn'$ and $j=dj'$. Then $r_k=d\cdot\rho_k$, where $\rho_k$ is the remainder of $kj'$ modulo $n'$. As $k$ runs over $0,1,\dots,n-1$, the residue of $k$ modulo $n'$ takes each value exactly $d$ times, and multiplication by $j'$ permutes the residues modulo $n'$ because $\gcd(j',n')=1$. So the numbers $r_0,r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}$ are $0,d,2d,\dots,n-d$, each taken $d$ times. Removing $r_0=0$, the $n-1$ numbers $r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}$ sorted in decreasing order are $t_1\ge t_2\ge\dots\ge t_{n-1}$, where the value $n-md$ occupies the places $k$ with $(m-1)d<k\le md$ ($1\le m\le n'$; the value $0$ occupies only $d-1$ places). For such $k$ we have $n-md\le n-k<n-(m-1)d$, so \[t_k=n-md=d\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-k}{d}\Bigr\rfloor\le n-k .\] If $d=1$ the list is $n-1,n-2,\dots,1$ and $t_k=n-k$ for all $k$; if $d>1$ then $t_1=n-d<n-1$.

Rearrangement. The weights $\frac1k$ decrease strictly. If $r_k<r_l$ for some $k<l$, exchanging $r_k$ and $r_l$ changes $\sum\frac{r_k}{k}$ by $(r_l-r_k)\bigl(\frac1k-\frac1l\bigr)>0$. Among the finitely many orderings of the numbers $r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}$, one with the largest weighted sum therefore has no such pair, i.e. it is the decreasing one. So \[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{r_k}{k}\ \le\ \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{t_k}{k}\ \le\ \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{k},\] with equality in the first step only if $r_1\ge r_2\ge\dots\ge r_{n-1}$ (otherwise some adjacent pair is in increasing order and exchanging it gives a strictly larger sum, still at most the maximum), and in the second step only if $d=1$. Dividing by $n$, \[E\Bigl(\frac jn\Bigr)\le\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(\frac1k-\frac1n\Bigr)=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1{n-1}-\frac{n-1}{n}=\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n .\]

Equality. Equality at $\frac jn$ forces $d=1$ and $r_1\ge\dots\ge r_{n-1}$; then the $r_k$ are $n-1,\dots,1$ in this order, so $r_1=j=n-1$. Conversely, for $j=n-1$ we get $r_k=n-k$ (as $(n-1)k\equiv-k\pmod n$), and equality holds. So for $0\le x<\frac{n-1}{n}$ we have $E(x)\le E(\frac jn)<\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$ with $j=\lfloor nx\rfloor\le n-2$. For $\frac{n-1}{n}\le x<1$ and $1\le k\le n$ we have $k-1\le k-\frac kn\le kx<k$, so every floor takes the same value as at $x=\frac{n-1}n$, and $E(x)=E(\frac{n-1}{n})$ is the maximum.

Therefore the expression is maximal exactly for the real numbers $x$ with $x-\lfloor x\rfloor\ge1-\frac1n$, and the maximal value is \[\boxed{\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n}\]

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence

Annie is playing a game where she starts with a row of positive integers, written on a blackboard, each of which is a power of $2$. On each turn, she can erase two adjacent numbers and replace them with a power of $2$ that is greater than either of the erased numbers. This shortens the row of numbers, and she continues to take turns until only one number remains. Annie wins the game if the final remaining number is less than $4$ times the sum of the original numbers. Is it always possible for Annie to win, regardless of the starting row of numbers?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Dyadic intervals and a binary tree)

Yes. Let the row be $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ with $a_i=2^{k_i}$, and let $S=a_1+\dots+a_n$. If $n=1$ no move is made and the final number is $S<4S$. Let $n\ge2$, and let $2^m$ be the smallest power of $2$ with $2^m\ge S$; then $2^{m-1}<S$, so $2^{m+1}<4S$. We show that Annie can finish with a number that is at most $2^{m+1}$.

Step 1: placing the numbers. Put $s_0=0$, $s_i=a_1+\dots+a_i$, and let $I_i=[s_{i-1},s_i)$; these are consecutive intervals of lengths $a_1,\dots,a_n$ inside $[0,2^m)$. Call an interval dyadic of length $2^t$ ($t$ an integer, possibly negative) if it has the form $[j2^t,(j+1)2^t)$ with $j$ an integer. The dyadic intervals of length $a_i/2$ tile the line, and $I_i$ has length $a_i$, twice as much; hence $I_i$ contains a whole dyadic interval $J_i$ of length $a_i/2$ (take the one whose left end is the first multiple of $a_i/2$ that is $\ge s_{i-1}$). The intervals $J_1,\dots,J_n$ are pairwise disjoint and lie in $[0,2^m)$ in this order from left to right.

Step 2: the tree. Consider the dyadic intervals contained in $[0,2^m)$ as a binary tree: the root is $[0,2^m)$, and each dyadic interval has two children, its left and right halves. Give every dyadic interval $J$ the value $v(J)=2\cdot(\text{length of }J)$. Thus $v$ halves from a node to its children, the root has value $2^{m+1}$, and $v(J_i)=a_i$.

For a dyadic interval $J\subseteq[0,2^m)$ let $R(J)$ be the set of indices $i$ with $J_i\subseteq J$. Two dyadic intervals are either nested or disjoint and the $J_i$ are in order, so $R(J)$ is a block of consecutive indices. We claim:

If $R(J)\neq\emptyset$, Annie can merge the numbers $a_i$, $i\in R(J)$ (they are adjacent in the row) into a single number that is at most $v(J)$.

We use induction on $|R(J)|$. If $|R(J)|=1$, say $R(J)=\{i\}$, there is nothing to merge: the number is $a_i=v(J_i)\le v(J)$. Let $|R(J)|\ge2$. Then $J$ is none of the $J_i$ (no $J_i$ contains another one), so each $J_i\subseteq J$ lies in the left half $J'$ or in the right half $J''$ of $J$, and $R(J)$ is the block $R(J')$ followed by the block $R(J'')$.

  • If one of the two blocks is empty, the other half has the same index set as $J$ and half its value. We replace $J$ by that half and repeat; this cannot go on for ever, because the lengths halve while $J$ must contain at least two of the $J_i$. So we arrive at an interval $J^{*}\subseteq J$ with $R(J^{*})=R(J)$, $v(J^{*})\le v(J)$, and both halves of $J^{*}$ containing some $J_i$. It is enough to prove the claim for $J^{*}$.

  • If both blocks are nonempty, each has fewer elements than $R(J)$. By the induction hypothesis Annie merges the block $R(J')$ into one number $u\le v(J')$ and the block $R(J'')$ into one number $w\le v(J'')$. These two numbers are now adjacent and both are at most $v(J')=v(J'')=\frac12v(J)$. Here $v(J)$ is a power of $2$ (an integer, since $J$ contains two disjoint intervals of length at least $\frac12$, so its length is at least $1$) and it is greater than $u$ and $w$. Annie replaces $u,w$ by $v(J)$.

This proves the claim.

Step 3. Apply the claim to the root $J=[0,2^m)$, for which $R(J)=\{1,\dots,n\}$: Annie reduces the whole row to one number that is at most $2^{m+1}<4S$. Hence Annie can always win.

Remark. The constant $4$ cannot be replaced by $2$: for the row $1,2,1$ we have $S=4$ and the smallest possible final number is $8=2S$. The construction is the one used for alphabetic prefix codes (Gilbert–Moore coding): a leaf of “weight” $a_i/2^{m}$ is placed at depth $m+1-k_i$.

Solution 2 (Induction with a weight that halves on each side)

Yes. For a row $a_1,\dots,a_n$ of powers of $2$ define its weight \[W=a_1\ \ (n=1),\qquad W=2a_1+4(a_2+\dots+a_{n-1})+2a_n\ \ (n\ge2).\] With $S=a_1+\dots+a_n$ we have $W=a_1<4S$ for $n=1$ and $W=4S-2a_1-2a_n<4S$ for $n\ge2$. So it suffices to prove:

Claim. Annie can reduce any row to a single number that is at most its weight $W$.

We use induction on $n$. For $n=1$ there is nothing to do. For $n=2$ Annie replaces $a_1,a_2$ by $2\max(a_1,a_2)$, a power of $2$ greater than both and at most $2a_1+2a_2=W$.

Let $n\ge3$ and assume the claim for all shorter rows. Note that \[\tfrac12W=2S-a_1-a_n .\] The idea is to cut the row into a left part $a_1,\dots,a_j$ and a right part $a_{j+1},\dots,a_n$ ($1\le j\le n-1$), each of weight at most $\frac12W$. Put $s_j=a_1+\dots+a_j$, $m_j=4s_j-2a_j$ for $1\le j\le n$, and $\theta=2S+a_1-a_n$.

The left part. If $j\ge2$, its weight is $2a_1+4(s_j-a_1-a_j)+2a_j=4s_j-2a_1-2a_j$, and this is at most $2S-a_1-a_n$ exactly when $4s_j-2a_j\le2S+a_1-a_n$, that is, when $m_j\le\theta$. If $j=1$, its weight is $a_1$, and $a_1\le\frac12W$ always holds: since $n\ge3$, $S>a_1+a_n$, so $2S-a_1-a_n>a_1$.

The right part. If $j\le n-2$, its weight is $2a_{j+1}+4(S-s_j-a_{j+1}-a_n)+2a_n=4(S-s_j)-2a_{j+1}-2a_n$, and this is at most $2S-a_1-a_n$ exactly when $2S+a_1-a_n\le4s_j+2a_{j+1}$, that is, when $\theta\le m_{j+1}$ (note $m_{j+1}=4s_{j+1}-2a_{j+1}=4s_j+2a_{j+1}$). If $j=n-1$, its weight is $a_n\le\frac12W$, as before.

Choosing $j$. So we need an index $j$, $1\le j\le n-1$, with $m_j\le\theta\le m_{j+1}$. Now $m_{j+1}-m_j=2a_j+2a_{j+1}>0$, so $m_1<m_2<\dots<m_n$. Moreover $m_1=2a_1\le\theta$ and $\theta\le4S-2a_n=m_n$; both inequalities say $a_1+a_n\le2S$, which is true. Hence $\theta$ lies in one of the intervals $[m_j,m_{j+1}]$ with $1\le j\le n-1$, and we take that $j$.

Finishing. Both parts are shorter than the row and have weight at most $\frac12W$. By the induction hypothesis Annie reduces the left part to one number $u\le\frac12W$ and, independently, the right part to one number $v\le\frac12W$ (moves inside one part do not affect the other part). Now $u$ and $v$ are adjacent; she replaces them by $2\max(u,v)$, a power of $2$ greater than both, and $2\max(u,v)\le W$. This proves the claim, and the final number is at most $W<4S$. So Annie can always win.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with no angle equal to $60^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. Let $\Delta_B$ be the equilateral triangle with three vertices on $\omega$, one of which is $B$. Let $\ell_B$ be the line through the two vertices of $\Delta_B$ other than $B$. Let $\Delta_C$ and $\ell_C$ be defined analogously. Let $Y$ be the intersection of $AC$ and $\ell_B$, and let $Z$ be the intersection of $AB$ and $\ell_C$.

Let $N$ be the midpoint of minor arc $BC$ on $\omega$. Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the triangle formed by $\ell_B$, $\ell_C$, and the tangent to $\omega$ through $N$. Prove that the circumcircle of $AYZ$ and the incircle of $\mathcal{R}$ are tangent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion in the circumcircle: both circles become circles with centers at distance 1)

Let $\omega$ have center $O$ and radius $1$, and let $A,B,C$ also denote the angles of the triangle. Let $\Omega$ be the incircle of $\mathcal R$. For a vertex $V$ of $ABC$ the line $\ell_V$ is defined as in the statement; in particular $\ell_A$ is the line through the two vertices other than $A$ of the equilateral triangle inscribed in $\omega$ with vertex $A$.

Step 1: the lines $\ell_B$, $\ell_C$ and the circle $\Omega$. The equilateral triangle $\Delta_B$ has center $O$, so the side opposite to $B$ is perpendicular to $OB$ at distance $\frac12$ from $O$, on the side of $O$ away from $B$. Thus, writing $\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf n$ for the unit vectors $\overrightarrow{OB},\overrightarrow{OC},\overrightarrow{ON}$, \[\ell_B=\{P:\ \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf b=-\tfrac12\},\qquad \ell_C=\{P:\ \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf c=-\tfrac12\},\qquad\text{tangent at }N=\{P:\ \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf n=1\}.\] Since the triangle is acute, $\angle BON=\angle NOC=A$, so $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c=\cos A$ and $\mathbf b+\mathbf c=2\cos A\,\mathbf n$ with $\cos A>0$. Hence the outward normals $-\mathbf b$, $-\mathbf c$, $\mathbf n$ of the three half-planes \[\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf b\ge-\tfrac12,\qquad \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf c\ge-\tfrac12,\qquad \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf n\le1\] do not lie in a half-plane ($\mathbf n$ is a positive multiple of $\mathbf b+\mathbf c$), so the intersection of the three half-planes is bounded: it is the triangle $\mathcal R$. Let $J$ be the point of the segment $ON$ with \[OJ=\rho:=\frac1{2(1+\cos A)},\qquad\text{and put}\quad r:=1-\rho=\frac{1+2\cos A}{2(1+\cos A)}.\] The distance from $J$ to the tangent at $N$ is $1-\rho=r$, and the distance from $J$ to $\ell_B$ (and to $\ell_C$) is $\rho\cos A+\frac12=r$; moreover $J$ satisfies the three inequalities, so it lies inside $\mathcal R$. Hence $\Omega$ is the circle with center $J$ and radius $r$.

Step 2: complex coordinates. Now let $\omega$ be the unit circle of the complex plane, with $O=0$; we identify a vector from $O$ with a complex number, so $\mathbf n$ is also the coordinate of $N$. Let $a,b,c$ be the coordinates of $A,B,C$; so $\bar a=\frac1a$, $\bar b=\frac1b$, $\bar c=\frac1c$. Put $\sigma_1=a+b+c$ and $\sigma_2=ab+bc+ca$. Then \[\overline{\sigma_1}=\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=\frac{\sigma_2}{abc},\qquad \overline{\sigma_2}=\frac{\sigma_1}{abc}.\] The point $\sigma_1$ is the orthocenter of $ABC$ (a standard fact), and it is not $O$ because the triangle is not equilateral. Hence $\sigma_1\ne0$, $\sigma_2=abc\,\overline{\sigma_1}\ne0$, and \[e:=\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad \bar e=\frac{\sigma_1/(abc)}{\sigma_2/(abc)}=\frac1e,\quad\text{that is,}\quad |e|=1 .\] Also $b^2\ne ac$, $c^2\ne ab$, $a^2\ne bc$: for instance $b^2=ac$ would mean that $B$ is a midpoint of an arc $AC$, so $BA=BC$; but the triangle is scalene.

Step 3: the point $Y$ and its inverse. A point $z$ lies on the line $AC$ if and only if $z+ac\,\bar z=a+c$. It lies on $\ell_B$ if and only if $\overrightarrow{Oz}\cdot\mathbf b=\frac12(z\bar b+\bar zb)=-\frac12$, that is, $\frac zb+b\bar z=-1$. Let \[y=\frac{b\,\sigma_2}{b^2-ac};\qquad\text{then}\qquad \bar y=\frac{\frac1b\cdot\frac{\sigma_1}{abc}}{\frac1{b^2}-\frac1{ac}}=\frac{\frac{\sigma_1}{ab^2c}}{\frac{ac-b^2}{ab^2c}}=\frac{-\sigma_1}{b^2-ac}.\] Using $b\sigma_2-ac\,\sigma_1=b^2(a+c)-ac(a+c)$ and $\sigma_2-b\sigma_1=ac-b^2$, \[y+ac\,\bar y=\frac{b\sigma_2-ac\,\sigma_1}{b^2-ac}=a+c,\qquad \frac yb+b\bar y=\frac{\sigma_2-b\sigma_1}{b^2-ac}=-1 .\] So $y$ is the coordinate of $Y$; in particular $Y\ne O$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion in $\omega$, $\iota(z)=\frac1{\bar z}$. Then \[\iota(Y)=\frac1{\bar y}=\frac{ac-b^2}{\sigma_1}=\frac{\sigma_2-b\sigma_1}{\sigma_1}=e-b .\] In the same way (exchange the roles of $b$ and $c$) $\iota(Z)=e-c$, and the point $X$ where the line $BC$ meets $\ell_A$ exists (its coordinate is $\frac{a\sigma_2}{a^2-bc}$) and satisfies $\iota(X)=e-a$.

Step 4: the image of the circle $AYZ$. The line $BC$ does not pass through $O$, so $\iota$ maps it to a circle through $O$, $B$, $C$: the circumcircle of triangle $OBC$. In the isosceles triangle $OBC$ we have $\angle BOC=2A<180^\circ$ and $BC=2\sin A$, so its circumradius is \[R':=\frac{BC}{2\sin2A}=\frac1{2\cos A},\] and its circumcenter lies on the perpendicular bisector $ON$ of $BC$, on the same side of $O$ as $N$: it is the point $V=R'\,\mathbf n$ (indeed $|V-b|^2=R'^2-2R'\cos A+1=R'^2$). Since $X$ lies on $BC$, the point $\iota(X)=e-a$ lies on this circle: $|e-a-V|=R'$. Let $\Gamma'$ be the circle with center $e-V$ and radius $R'$. Then, using $|z|=|-z|$, \[|a-(e-V)|=|e-a-V|=R',\qquad |(e-b)-(e-V)|=|V-b|=R',\qquad |(e-c)-(e-V)|=|V-c|=R' ,\] so $\Gamma'$ passes through $A=\iota(A)$, $\iota(Y)$ and $\iota(Z)$. These are three different points: $Y\ne A$, because $A\in\ell_B$ would mean $\overrightarrow{OA}\cdot\mathbf b=\cos2C=-\frac12$, i.e. $C=60^\circ$; similarly $Z\ne A$ because $B\ne60^\circ$; and $e-b\ne e-c$. Moreover $A,Y,Z$ are not collinear, since $Y\in AC$ and $Z\in AB$ both differ from $A$. The image of the circle $AYZ$ under $\iota$ is a circle or a line through these three points, and they lie on the circle $\Gamma'$; therefore $\iota(\text{circle }AYZ)=\Gamma'$.

Step 5: the image of $\Omega$. By Step 1, $\Omega$ has center $\rho\,\mathbf n$ and radius $1-\rho$. It is symmetric in the line $ON$ and meets this line at $N$ and at the point $(2\rho-1)\mathbf n=-\frac{\cos A}{1+\cos A}\,\mathbf n$. Since $\rho<\frac12$, the point $O$ lies inside $\Omega$, so $\iota(\Omega)$ is a circle, symmetric in $ON$, and its diameter on $ON$ joins the images \[\iota(N)=\mathbf n\qquad\text{and}\qquad \iota\Bigl(-\tfrac{\cos A}{1+\cos A}\mathbf n\Bigr)=-\tfrac{1+\cos A}{\cos A}\,\mathbf n .\] So the center of $\iota(\Omega)$ is $\frac12\bigl(1-\frac{1+\cos A}{\cos A}\bigr)\mathbf n=-\frac1{2\cos A}\mathbf n=-V$, and its radius is $\frac12\bigl(1+\frac{1+\cos A}{\cos A}\bigr)=1+\frac1{2\cos A}=1+R'$.

Step 6: tangency. The centers $e-V$ and $-V$ of $\Gamma'$ and $\iota(\Omega)$ are at distance $|e|=1$, which is the difference $(1+R')-R'$ of their radii. So $\Gamma'$ touches $\iota(\Omega)$ internally, at the point $T'=-V+(1+R')e$. Neither circle passes through $O$: if $O\in\Gamma'$, then $A$, $Y$, $Z$ would lie on a line, but $Y\in AC$ and $Z\in AB$ are different from $A$ and from each other; and $O$ lies inside $\Omega$, so not on $\iota(\Omega)$. The inversion $\iota$ maps $\Gamma'$ back to the circle $AYZ$ and $\iota(\Omega)$ back to $\Omega$; two curves with exactly one common point are mapped to two curves with exactly one common point. Hence the circle $AYZ$ and $\Omega$, the incircle of $\mathcal R$, have exactly one common point $T=\iota(T')$: they are tangent. $\blacksquare$

Remark. The point $e=\frac{ab+bc+ca}{a+b+c}$ is the Euler reflection point of $ABC$. Since $\iota(Y)=e-b$ and $\iota(Z)=e-c$ lie on the unit circle centered at $e$, which passes through $O$, the line $YZ$ (the inverse of that circle) is the perpendicular bisector of the segment from $O$ to $e$. A direct computation (inversion at $A$) reduces the problem to a trigonometric identity that is true but hard to verify by hand; the inversion at $O$ avoids it.

Solution 2 (Cartesian coordinates and a signed radius)

Let $\omega$ be the unit circle with center $O=(0,0)$, and put $c=\cos A$, $s=\sin A$, where $A=\angle BAC$; so $0<c<1$ and $s>0$. Since $\angle BOC=2A$ and $N$ is the midpoint of the minor arc $BC$, we may take \[N=(1,0),\qquad B=(c,s),\qquad C=(c,-s),\qquad A=(x,y),\quad x^2+y^2=1 .\] The triangle is acute, so $A$ lies on the arc $BC$ not containing $N$, and $x<c$. Put $p=c-x>0$. Points are identified with their position vectors.

The triangle $\mathcal R$ and its incircle. The two vertices of $\Delta_B$ other than $B$ are the points $P$ of $\omega$ with $\angle BOP=120^\circ$, i.e. with $P\cdot B=\cos120^\circ=-\frac12$. So $\ell_B$ is the line $cX+sY=-\frac12$, and likewise $\ell_C$ is $cX-sY=-\frac12$. They meet at $\bigl(-\frac1{2c},0\bigr)$, and the tangent at $N$ is $X=1$. The triangle $\mathcal R$ is symmetric in the $X$-axis, so its incenter is a point $I=(i,0)$ with $-\frac1{2c}<i<1$. Its distance to $X=1$ is $1-i$ and to $\ell_B$ it is $ci+\frac12$ (the normal $(c,s)$ is a unit vector). Hence $1-i=ci+\frac12$: \[i=\frac1{2(1+c)},\qquad r=1-i=\frac{1+2c}{2(1+c)},\] where $r$ is the inradius. The incircle $\Omega$ is the circle with center $I$ and radius $1-i$, so all its points $P$ have $|P|\le i+(1-i)=1$, with equality only at $N$.

The points $Y$ and $Z$. Put $b=AB^2$, $d=AC^2$, $e=BC^2=4s^2$. For unit vectors $|P-Q|^2=2-2P\cdot Q$, so $A\cdot B=1-\frac b2$, $A\cdot C=1-\frac d2$, $B\cdot C=1-\frac e2$, and \[b=2(1-cx-sy),\qquad d=2(1-cx+sy).\] Write $Y=A+\lambda(C-A)$. The condition $Y\cdot B=-\frac12$ reads $1-\frac b2+\lambda\cdot\frac{b-e}{2}=-\frac12$, so $\lambda=\frac{b-3}{b-e}$. In the same way $Z=A+\mu(B-A)$ with $\mu=\frac{d-3}{d-e}$. Here $b\ne e$ and $d\ne e$ because the triangle is scalene. Also $b\ne3$: by the law of sines $AB=2\sin C$, so $b=3$ would mean $\angle C=60^\circ$; likewise $d\ne3$. Thus $\lambda,\mu\ne0$, so $Y\ne A$ lies on line $AC$ and $Z\ne A$ lies on line $AB$; the points $A,Y,Z$ are not collinear.

Useful identities. From $x^2+y^2=1$ and $c^2+s^2=1$: \[b+d=4(1-cx),\qquad d-b=4sy,\qquad bd=4\bigl((1-cx)^2-s^2y^2\bigr)=4(c-x)^2=4p^2,\] \[e-b-d=-4cp,\qquad (b-e)(d-e)=bd-e(b+d)+e^2=4p^2-16s^2(c^2-cx)=4pD,\quad D=p-4cs^2 .\] Since $(b-e)(d-e)\ne0$, also $D\ne0$. (In terms of $x$ and $c$, $D=4c^3-3c-x$.)

The circle $AYZ$. Let its center be $A+(U,V)$. A point $Q$ is equidistant from $A$ and $A+\mathbf v$ iff $2(Q-A)\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf v|^2$. With $\mathbf v=\lambda(C-A)$, $C-A=(p,-s-y)$, and with $\mathbf v=\mu(B-A)$, $B-A=(p,s-y)$, after dividing by $\lambda$, resp. $\mu$: \[2pU-2(s+y)V=\lambda d,\qquad 2pU+2(s-y)V=\mu b .\tag{1}\] Subtracting, $4sV=\mu b-\lambda d$. Over the common denominator $(b-e)(d-e)=4pD$, the numerator of $\mu b-\lambda d$ is \[b(d-3)(b-e)-d(b-3)(d-e)=(b-d)\bigl(bd+3(e-b-d)\bigr)=-4sy\,(4p^2-12cp),\] so \[V=\frac{-16syp(p-3c)}{4s\cdot4pD}=\frac{y(3c-p)}{D}=\frac{y(x+2c)}{D}.\] Adding the equations (1), $4pU=\lambda d+\mu b+4yV$. The numerator of $\lambda d+\mu b$ over $4pD$ is \[d(b-3)(d-e)+b(d-3)(b-e)=bd(b+d-2e)-3(b^2+d^2)+3e(b+d).\] Insert $bd=4p^2$, $b+d=4q$, $b^2+d^2=16q^2-8p^2$, $e=4s^2$ with $q=1-cx=s^2+cp$; a direct expansion gives $8p\,G$ with $G=4c^3+2cx^2-5c-x$. Hence $\lambda d+\mu b=\frac{2G}{D}$ and, using $y^2=1-x^2$, \[U=\frac{G+2(1-x^2)(x+2c)}{2pD}=\frac{2(c-x)\bigl(x^2+2cx+2c^2-\frac12\bigr)}{2pD}=\frac{U_0}{D},\qquad U_0=x^2+2cx+2c^2-\tfrac12,\] because both $G+2(1-x^2)(x+2c)$ and $2(c-x)U_0$ expand to $4c^3-2cx^2-c-2x^3+x$.

Now put $W_0=2cx+2c^2+\frac12$, $V_0=x+2c$ and $W=\frac{W_0}{D}$. Then $W_0-U_0=1-x^2$ and $W_0+U_0=(x+2c)^2$, so \[U^2+V^2=\frac{U_0^2+(1-x^2)V_0^2}{D^2}=\frac{U_0^2+(W_0-U_0)(W_0+U_0)}{D^2}=W^2 .\] So the circle $AYZ$ has radius $|W|$; note $W\ne0$ as this radius is positive.

The distance of the centers. Let $L$ be the distance from $A+(U,V)$ to $I=(i,0)$. Using $x^2+y^2=1$ and $r^2=(1-i)^2$, \[L^2-(U^2+V^2)-r^2=(x+U-i)^2+(y+V)^2-U^2-V^2-(1-i)^2=2\bigl(i(1-x)+(x-i)U+yV\bigr).\] We claim that $i(1-x)+(x-i)U+yV=rW$. Multiply by $2(1+c)D$ and use $2(1+c)i=1$, $2(1+c)r=1+2c$, $y^2=1-x^2$; the claim becomes \[(1-x)D+\bigl(2(1+c)x-1\bigr)U_0+2(1+c)(1-x^2)V_0=(1+2c)W_0 .\] The three terms on the left expand to \[-4c^3x+4c^3+3cx-3c+x^2-x,\] \[4c^3x+4c^2x^2+4c^2x-2c^2+2cx^3+4cx^2-3cx+2x^3-x^2-x+\tfrac12,\] \[-4c^2x^2+4c^2-2cx^3-4cx^2+2cx+4c-2x^3+2x,\] whose sum is $4c^3+4c^2x+2c^2+2cx+c+\frac12=(1+2c)\bigl(2cx+2c^2+\frac12\bigr)$, the right side. Therefore \[L^2=W^2+2rW+r^2=(W+r)^2,\qquad L=|W+r| .\]

Conclusion. The radii are $|W|$ and $r$. If $W>0$, then $L=|W|+r$ and the circles touch externally. If $W<0$, then $L=\bigl|\,r-|W|\,\bigr|$, so they touch internally, unless they coincide. They do not coincide: the point $A\ne N$ of $\omega$ lies on the circle $AYZ$ but not on $\Omega$, since $N$ is the only point of $\Omega$ with $|P|=1$. Hence the circumcircle of $AYZ$ and the incircle of $\mathcal R$ are tangent. (Both signs of $W$ occur for acute triangles, so both kinds of tangency happen.)

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure

A positive integer $n$ is called solitary if, for any nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$ such that $a+b=n$, either $a$ or $b$ contains the digit “1”. Determine, with proof, the number of solitary integers less than $10^{2026}$.

Show the answer

$2^{2026}-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Following the carries)

Call a nonnegative integer good if its decimal representation has no digit $1$. So $n$ is solitary exactly when $n$ is not the sum of two good numbers. We determine all solitary numbers.

Column addition. Write $n$ with digits $d_0,d_1,d_2,\dots$ from the units upward ($d_i=0$ beyond the leading digit). A representation $n=a+b$ with good $a,b$ is the same as a choice, for every position $i$, of digits $x_i,y_i\in\{0,2,3,\dots,9\}$ (all $0$ from some position on) and carries $c_0=0,c_1,c_2,\dots\in\{0,1\}$ with \[x_i+y_i+c_i=d_i+10c_{i+1}\quad(i\ge0),\] and with $c_i=0$ for all large $i$ (when $x_i=y_i=d_i=0$, the equation gives $c_i=10c_{i+1}$, so $c_i=0$).

The possible values of $s=x_i+y_i$ are exactly $0,2,3,\dots,18$: everything from $0$ to $18$ except $1$. Hence, for a digit $d$ and carries $c$ (in) and $c'$ (out), a pair $(x_i,y_i)$ exists if and only if $s=d+10c'-c$ lies in $\{0,2,3,\dots,18\}$. This gives the table of allowed transitions $c\to c'$ at a position with digit $d$:

  • $0\to0$: $s=d$, allowed iff $d\ne1$;

  • $0\to1$: $s=d+10$, allowed iff $d\le8$;

  • $1\to0$: $s=d-1$, allowed iff $d\ge1$ and $d\ne2$;

  • $1\to1$: $s=d+9$, always allowed.

Reachable carries. For $i\ge0$ let $C_i\subseteq\{0,1\}$ be the set of carries $c_i$ that can occur after positions $0,\dots,i-1$ have been filled in a valid way. Then $C_0=\{0\}$, and $n$ is a sum of two good numbers iff $0\in C_L$, where $L$ is the number of digits of $n$ (beyond position $L$ all digits are $0$, and the carry must be $0$). From the table:

  • if $C_i=\{0\}$, then $C_{i+1}=\{0\}$ for $d_i=9$, $C_{i+1}=\{1\}$ for $d_i=1$, and $C_{i+1}=\{0,1\}$ for every other digit;

  • if $C_i=\{1\}$, then $C_{i+1}=\{1\}$ for $d_i\in\{0,2\}$ and $C_{i+1}=\{0,1\}$ for every other digit;

  • if $C_i=\{0,1\}$, then $C_{i+1}=\{0,1\}$ for every digit (for $d_i=9$ use $0\to0$ and $1\to1$; for $d_i=1$ use $1\to0$ and $1\to1$; otherwise $0\to0$ and $1\to1$).

So $C_i$ is never empty, and $n$ is solitary iff $C_L=\{1\}$. Starting from $\{0\}$, the only way to end in $\{1\}$ is: stay in $\{0\}$ (digits $9$), then move to $\{1\}$ (a digit $1$), then stay in $\{1\}$ (digits $0$ or $2$). Reading the digits from the units upward, this means:

$n$ is solitary if and only if its decimal representation consists of a (possibly empty) string of digits $0$ and $2$, then a single digit $1$, then a (possibly empty) string of digits $9$.

(The examples $1$, $19$, $21$, $199$, $201$, $219$, $221$ are the solitary numbers below $1000$.)

Counting. Write the numbers below $10^{2026}$ with exactly $2026$ digits, allowing leading zeros; leading zeros are digits $0$ in the first string, so the description does not change. If the digit $1$ stands at position $j$ from the right ($0\le j\le2025$), the $j$ digits to its right are all $9$ and each of the $2025-j$ digits to its left is $0$ or $2$: that is $2^{2025-j}$ numbers. Different strings are different numbers and all are positive. The number of solitary integers below $10^{2026}$ is \[\sum_{j=0}^{2025}2^{2025-j}=\boxed{2^{2026}-1}\]

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle. Points $D$, $E$, and $F$ lie on sides $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$, respectively, such that \[\angle AFE=\angle BDF=\angle CED.\] Let $O_A$, $O_B$, and $O_C$ be the circumcenters of triangles $AFE$, $BDF$, and $CED$, respectively. Let $M$, $N$, and $O$ be the circumcenters of triangles $ABC$, $DEF$, and $O_AO_BO_C$, respectively. Prove that $OM=ON$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Complex numbers and a weighted mean of two similar triangles)

Let $\theta$ be the common value of the three angles, and let $A,B,C$ be in counterclockwise order. We use complex coordinates (a point and its coordinate have the same name).

Step 1: triangle $EFD$ is similar to triangle $ABC$. In triangle $BDF$, $\angle BFD=180^\circ-\angle B-\theta$. Since $F$ lies on segment $AB$, \[\angle EFD=180^\circ-\angle AFE-\angle BFD=180^\circ-\theta-(180^\circ-\angle B-\theta)=\angle B .\] In the same way $\angle FDE=\angle C$ and $\angle DEF=\angle A$. So triangle $EFD$ is similar to triangle $ABC$ with $E,F,D$ corresponding to $A,B,C$; both are counterclockwise ($D,E,F$ lie on $BC,CA,AB$). Hence there are complex numbers $\alpha\neq0$ and $\beta$ with \[E=\alpha A+\beta,\qquad F=\alpha B+\beta,\qquad D=\alpha C+\beta .\tag{1}\] The map $z\mapsto\alpha z+\beta$ is a similarity, so it sends the circumcenter of $ABC$ to the circumcenter of $EFD$: $N=\alpha M+\beta$.

Step 2: the three circumcenters. Lemma. Let $X,Y,Z$ be points of a circle with center $W$, in clockwise order, and $\angle XZY=\theta$. Then \[W=uX+(1-u)Y,\qquad u=\tfrac12\,(1+i\cot\theta).\] Proof. $W$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$. Since $\angle XWY=2\theta$ (or $360^\circ-2\theta$ if $\theta>90^\circ$), its distance from the midpoint of $XY$ is $\frac{|XY|}{2}|\cot\theta|$, and it is on the same side of $XY$ as $Z$ exactly when $\theta<90^\circ$. The point $Z$ is on the right of the directed line $XY$, and the unit vector pointing to the right is $-i\frac{Y-X}{|Y-X|}$. Hence $W=\frac{X+Y}{2}-\frac{i\cot\theta}{2}(Y-X)=uX+(1-u)Y$. $\square$

The triples $(A,E,F)$, $(B,F,D)$, $(C,D,E)$ are clockwise, and $\angle AFE=\angle BDF=\angle CED=\theta$. By the lemma, \[O_A=uA+(1-u)E,\qquad O_B=uB+(1-u)F,\qquad O_C=uC+(1-u)D .\tag{2}\]

Step 3. Insert (1) into (2): with $\gamma=u+(1-u)\alpha$ and $\delta=(1-u)\beta$, \[O_A=\gamma A+\delta,\qquad O_B=\gamma B+\delta,\qquad O_C=\gamma C+\delta .\] The points $O_A,O_B,O_C$ form a triangle, so $\gamma\neq0$ and $z\mapsto\gamma z+\delta$ is a similarity taking $ABC$ to $O_AO_BO_C$. It takes circumcenter to circumcenter: \[O=\gamma M+\delta=uM+(1-u)(\alpha M+\beta)=uM+(1-u)N .\] Therefore \[O-\frac{M+N}{2}=\Bigl(u-\frac12\Bigr)(M-N)=\frac{i\cot\theta}{2}\,(M-N).\] The right-hand side is $M-N$ multiplied by a purely imaginary number, so it is perpendicular to $MN$ (or zero). Thus $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $MN$ (if $M=N$ then $O=M$), and $OM=ON$.

Remark. The formula shows more: $O$ is the apex of the isosceles triangle on the base $MN$ with $\angle MON=2\theta$ (or $360^\circ-2\theta$), exactly as $O_A$ stands over the chord $AE$.

Solution 2 (Three spiral similarities with a common center)

Let $\theta$ be the common value of the three angles; $D,E,F$ are interior points of the sides.

Triangle $EFD$ is similar to triangle $ABC$. In triangle $BDF$, $\angle BFD=180^\circ-\angle B-\theta$. Since $F$ lies on segment $AB$, \[\angle EFD=180^\circ-\angle AFE-\angle BFD=180^\circ-\theta-(180^\circ-\angle B-\theta)=\angle B,\] and similarly $\angle FDE=\angle C$ and $\angle DEF=\angle A$. So triangles $ABC$ and $EFD$ are similar ($A,B,C\leftrightarrow E,F,D$), and they have the same orientation because $D,E,F$ lie on $BC,CA,AB$. Let $\sigma$ be the similarity of the plane (a rotation combined with a scaling, possibly a translation) with $\sigma(A)=E$, $\sigma(B)=F$, $\sigma(C)=D$.

$\sigma$ is not a translation: if it were the translation by a vector $\mathbf v$, then $\mathbf v=\overrightarrow{AE}$ would be parallel to $CA$ (as $E\in CA$) and $\mathbf v=\overrightarrow{BF}$ would be parallel to $AB$ (as $F\in AB$); the lines $CA$, $AB$ are not parallel, so $\mathbf v=\mathbf 0$ and $E=A$, which is false. So $\sigma$ has a unique fixed point $S$, and $\sigma$ is a spiral similarity about $S$. Circumcenters correspond, so $\sigma(M)=N$. Note $A\neq S$, since $\sigma(A)=E\neq A$.

The triangles $AEO_A$, $BFO_B$, $CDO_C$. In the circle $AFE$ the chord $AE$ is seen from $F$ under the angle $\theta$; so $O_AA=O_AE$ and $O_A$ is determined by $A$, $E$ and $\theta$: it is the point on the perpendicular bisector of $AE$ at distance $\frac{AE}{2}|\cot\theta|$ from $AE$, on the side of $F$ if $\theta<90^\circ$ and on the other side if $\theta>90^\circ$. The same holds for $O_B$ (chord $BF$, seen from $D$) and for $O_C$ (chord $CD$, seen from $E$). Since $F$, $D$, $E$ lie on the same side (the right-hand side, if $ABC$ is counterclockwise) of the directed segments $AE$, $BF$, $CD$ respectively, the three centres are obtained from the directed segments $AE$, $BF$, $CD$ by one and the same rule: start at the midpoint and move along the perpendicular pointing to the right of the directed segment, a signed distance $\frac12\cot\theta$ times its length. A similarity that preserves orientation respects this rule; so if it maps $A,E$ to $B,F$, it maps the triangle $AEO_A$ to the triangle $BFO_B$ (for $\theta=90^\circ$ these "triangles" are degenerate, $O_A$ being the midpoint of $AE$, and the statement still holds).

A second and a third spiral similarity. First, $O_A\neq S$. Indeed, for $X\in\{B,C\}$ let $\rho_X$ be the spiral similarity about $S$ with $\rho_X(A)=X$ (it exists, as $A\neq S$ and $X\neq S$; $X=S$ would give $\sigma(X)=X$, but $\sigma(B)=F\neq B$ and $\sigma(C)=D\neq C$). We show below that $\rho_X$ maps $O_A$ to $O_X$; so $O_A=S$ would give $O_B=\rho_B(S)=S=O_A$, but $O_A,O_B,O_C$ form a triangle. Hence there is a spiral similarity $\tau$ about $S$ with $\tau(A)=O_A$. Spiral similarities about the same center commute (each is a rotation about $S$ followed by a scaling from $S$). For $X\in\{B,C\}$ we have $\rho_X(E)=\rho_X(\sigma(A))=\sigma(X)$, so $\rho_X$ maps the segment $AE$ to $BF$ (for $X=B$) and to $CD$ (for $X=C$); as it preserves shape and orientation, it maps the triangle $AEO_A$ to $BFO_B$, respectively $CDO_C$. Hence $\rho_B(O_A)=O_B$, $\rho_C(O_A)=O_C$, and \[O_B=\rho_B(O_A)=\rho_B(\tau(A))=\tau(\rho_B(A))=\tau(B),\qquad O_C=\tau(C).\] So $\tau$ maps triangle $ABC$ to triangle $O_AO_BO_C$, and therefore $\tau(M)=O$.

Conclusion. If $M=S$, then $N=\sigma(S)=S$ and $O=\tau(S)=S$, and $OM=ON=0$. Otherwise let $\psi$ be the spiral similarity about $S$ with $\psi(A)=M$. Then \[\psi(E)=\psi(\sigma(A))=\sigma(M)=N,\qquad \psi(O_A)=\psi(\tau(A))=\tau(M)=O .\] So $\psi$ maps the triangle $AEO_A$ to the triangle $MNO$. Since $O_AA=O_AE$, we get $OM=ON$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Let $a$ and $b$ be positive integers such that $\varphi(ab+1)$ divides $a^2+b^2+1$. Prove that $a$ and $b$ are Fibonacci numbers.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Parity, prime powers, and Vieta jumping)

Here $\varphi$ is Euler's function. Let $m=ab+1\ge2$ and $N=a^2+b^2+1$, and assume $\varphi(m)\mid N$. We show that $(a,b)$ is $(1,1)$, $(1,8)$, $(8,1)$, or a pair of consecutive odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers; here $F_1=F_2=1$, $F_{k+2}=F_{k+1}+F_k$, so $1,2,5,8,13,\dots$ are Fibonacci numbers.

Step 1: $m=2$ or $m=p^k$ with $p$ an odd prime.

  • If $m$ is even, then $a,b$ are odd and $N$ is odd, so $\varphi(m)$ is odd; as $\varphi(m)$ is even for $m\ge3$, we get $m=2$ and $a=b=1$.

  • If $m$ is odd, then $m\ge3$, $\varphi(m)$ is even, so $N$ is even; $ab$ is even, so exactly one of $a,b$ is even and $N\equiv 0+1+1=2\pmod 4$. Hence $4\nmid\varphi(m)$. If $m$ had two different odd prime factors $p,q$, then $(p-1)(q-1)\mid\varphi(m)$ would give $4\mid\varphi(m)$. So $m=p^k$ with $p$ an odd prime, $k\ge1$, and \[ab=p^k-1,\qquad p^{k-1}(p-1)\ \mid\ a^2+b^2+1.\tag{1}\]

In the second case $a\ne b$ (different parity); by symmetry let $a<b$.

Step 2: the case $k=1$. Then $ab\mid a^2+b^2+1$. Let $a^2+b^2+1=c\,ab$ with a positive integer $c$. We show $c=3$ and that $(a,b)$ is a pair of consecutive odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers.

Consider any pair of positive integers $x\le y$ with $x^2+y^2+1=cxy$. If $x=y$, then $x^2\mid 2x^2+1$, so $x=y=1$ and $c=3$. If $x<y$, the quadratic $t^2-cx\,t+(x^2+1)=0$ has the root $t=y$ and a second root $y'=cx-y=\frac{x^2+1}{y}$. So $y'$ is an integer, it is positive, and $y'\le x$ because $x^2+1\le x(x+1)\le xy$. Thus $(y',x)$ is again a solution with the same $c$, with $y'\le x$ and smaller sum $y'+x<x+y$. Repeating, the sum decreases as long as the two entries differ, so after finitely many steps we reach a solution with equal entries, i.e. $(1,1)$, and therefore $c=3$.

Reversing the steps: the step $(x,y)\mapsto(y',x)$ is undone by $(u,v)\mapsto(v,3v-u)$. Hence every solution with $x\le y$ is in the chain \[(1,1)\to(1,2)\to(2,5)\to(5,13)\to(13,34)\to\cdots,\qquad (u,v)\mapsto(v,3v-u).\] Since $F_{j+2}=3F_j-F_{j-2}$ (indeed $F_{j+2}=2F_j+F_{j-1}=3F_j-F_{j-2}$), this chain is $(F_1,F_1),(F_1,F_3),(F_3,F_5),(F_5,F_7),\dots$: the first step gives $(1,2)=(F_1,F_3)$ directly from $(1,1)$, and from then on each step is $(F_{j-2},F_j)\mapsto(F_j,3F_j-F_{j-2})=(F_j,F_{j+2})$ with $j\ge3$ odd. So $a$ and $b$ are Fibonacci numbers.

Step 3: the case $k\ge2$. We show that then $(a,b)=(1,8)$, which consists of Fibonacci numbers ($1^2+8^2+1=66$, $\varphi(9)=6$).

From $a^2+b^2+1=(b-a)^2+2(ab+1)-1=(b-a)^2-1+2p^k$ and (1), $p^{k-1}\mid(b-a-1)(b-a+1)$. The two factors differ by $2$ and $p$ is odd, so $p^{k-1}$ divides one of them: \[b-a=Tp^{k-1}+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\in\{1,-1\},\ T\ge0\ \text{an integer}.\tag{2}\]

$T=0$ is impossible. Then $b=a+1$ and $a^2+b^2+1=2(a^2+a+1)=2(ab+1)=2p^k$, so $(p-1)\mid 2p^k$; as $\gcd(p-1,p)=1$, $p-1\mid2$ and $p=3$. But $a^2+a+1$ is never divisible by $9$ (if $3\mid a^2+a+1$ then $a=3s+1$ and $a^2+a+1=9s^2+9s+3$), contradicting $a^2+a+1=3^k$, $k\ge2$.

So $T\ge1$. Then $b\ge a-1+p^{k-1}\ge p^{k-1}$ and $a=\frac{p^k-1}{b}<p$. Substituting (2) into $ab=p^k-1$: \[a^2+\varepsilon a+1=p^{k-1}\,(p-Ta).\tag{3}\] The left side is positive, so $p-Ta\ge1$ and $a^2+\varepsilon a+1\ge p^{k-1}$. But $a\le p-1$ gives $a^2+\varepsilon a+1\le(p-1)^2+p<p^2$. Hence $k=2$. Put $j=p-Ta\ge1$, so that \[a^2+\varepsilon a+1=jp,\qquad Ta=p-j.\tag{4}\] Moreover, using $b-a=Tp+\varepsilon$ and $ab=p^2-1$, and $p\equiv1\pmod{p-1}$, \[a^2+b^2+1=(Tp+\varepsilon)^2+2p^2-1\equiv(T+\varepsilon)^2+1\pmod{p-1},\] so (1) gives \[p-1\ \mid\ (T+\varepsilon)^2+1,\qquad\text{in particular}\quad (T+1)^2+1\ge p-1.\tag{5}\]

Case $j=1$. By (4), $p=a^2+\varepsilon a+1$ and $Ta=p-1=a(a+\varepsilon)$, so $T=a+\varepsilon$ and $p-1=a(a+\varepsilon)$. Then (5) says $a(a+\varepsilon)\mid(a+2\varepsilon)^2+1=a^2+4\varepsilon a+5$. Subtracting $a(a+\varepsilon)$, we get $a(a+\varepsilon)\mid 3\varepsilon a+5$, hence $a\mid5$. If $\varepsilon=1$: also $(a+1)\mid 3a+5-3(a+1)=2$, so $a=1$, $p=3$, $T=2$, $b=a+Tp+\varepsilon=8$. If $\varepsilon=-1$: $a\ge2$ (otherwise $p=1$), so $a=5$, and $20\mid -15+5=-10$ is false. So this case gives exactly $(a,b)=(1,8)$.

Case $j\ge2$. By (4), $a^2+a+1\ge 2p$ and $T=\frac{p-j}{a}\le\frac{p-2}{a}$. Let $q=\sqrt{p-2}$. By (5), $T\ge q-1$, hence $a\le\frac{p-2}{T}\le\frac{q^2}{q-1}=q+1+\frac1{q-1}$. If $p\ge 18$, then $q\ge4$, $a\le q+\frac43$ and \[a^2+a+1\le q^2+\tfrac{11}{3}q+\tfrac{37}{9}<2q^2+4=2p,\] because $q^2-\frac{11}3q-\frac19>0$ for $q\ge4$; this contradicts $a^2+a+1\ge2p$. So $p\le17$. By (4), $p\mid a^2+\varepsilon a+1$. If $p\ne3$, put $t=\varepsilon a$; then $t^2+t+1\equiv0\pmod p$, so $t^3-1=(t-1)(t^2+t+1)\equiv0$, while $t\not\equiv1$ (otherwise $t^2+t+1\equiv3\not\equiv0$). So $t$ has order $3$ modulo $p$, and as $t^{p-1}\equiv1$ by Fermat's theorem ($p\nmid t$, since $t^2+t+1\equiv0$), we get $3\mid p-1$, i.e. $p\equiv1\pmod 3$. Also $p\equiv3\pmod4$: otherwise $4\mid p-1\mid\varphi(m)$, contradicting Step 1. So $p\in\{3,7\}$ (the primes $13$, resp. $5,11,17$, are excluded). We check them directly, with $ab=p^2-1$, $a<b$, exactly one of $a,b$ even:

  • $p=3$: $ab=8$, so $(a,b)=(1,8)$ (which has $j=1$).

  • $p=7$: $ab=48$, so $(a,b)\in\{(1,48),(3,16)\}$; $a^2+b^2+1$ equals $2306$, $266$, and neither is divisible by $\varphi(49)=42$ ($2306=42\cdot54+38$, $266=42\cdot6+14$).

So no solution arises in the case $j\ge2$.

Conclusion. In every case $a$ and $b$ are Fibonacci numbers: $(1,1)$; consecutive odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers $F_{2i-1},F_{2i+1}$ (with $F_{2i-1}F_{2i+1}+1$ prime); or $(1,8)$.

Solution 2 (Cube roots of unity for the prime-power case)

Here $\varphi$ is Euler's function, and $F_1=F_2=1$, $F_{k+2}=F_{k+1}+F_k$, so the Fibonacci numbers are $1,1,2,3,5,8,13,\dots$ Let $N=ab+1\ge2$ and $S=a^2+b^2+1$, and assume $\varphi(N)\mid S$.

Step 1: $4\nmid\varphi(N)$. Squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$, so $S\equiv1,2$ or $3\pmod4$. Hence $4\nmid S$, and so $4\nmid\varphi(N)$.

Step 2: $N=2$, or $N=p^e$ with $p$ a prime, $p\equiv3\pmod4$. For $N\ge3$ the number $\varphi(N)$ is even: $r\mapsto N-r$ pairs the residues coprime to $N$, and $r=N-r$ would give $N=2r$ with $\gcd(r,N)=r=1$, i.e. $N=2$. If $N$ is even, then $ab$ is odd, so $a,b$ are odd and $S$ is odd; then $\varphi(N)$ is odd, so $N=2$ and $a=b=1$, which are Fibonacci numbers. If $N$ is odd, then $N\ge3$. If two different primes $p,q$ divided $N$, both odd, then $(p-1)(q-1)\mid\varphi(N)$ and $4\mid\varphi(N)$, contradicting Step 1. So $N=p^e$ with $p$ an odd prime and $e\ge1$; since $p-1\mid\varphi(N)=p^{e-1}(p-1)$, Step 1 gives $4\nmid p-1$, i.e. $p\equiv3\pmod4$.

Step 3: if $e\ge2$, then $\{a,b\}=\{1,8\}$. Now $p\mid\varphi(N)\mid S$. From $ab=p^e-1$ we get $ab\equiv-1\pmod p$, so $p\nmid a$, and \[a^2S=a^4+(ab)^2+a^2\equiv a^4+a^2+1\pmod p .\] Hence $p\mid a^4+a^2+1$. Suppose $p\neq3$ and let $t=a^2$. Then $t^3-1=(t-1)(t^2+t+1)\equiv0\pmod p$, while $t\not\equiv1$ (otherwise $t^2+t+1\equiv3\not\equiv0$). So $t$ has order $3$ modulo $p$, and since $t^{p-1}\equiv1$ by Fermat's theorem, $3\mid p-1$. Then $3\mid\varphi(N)\mid S$. But $p\equiv1\pmod3$ gives $ab=p^e-1\equiv0\pmod3$, so $3$ divides one of $a,b$, say $a$ (the other case is symmetric), and $S\equiv b^2+1\equiv1$ or $2\pmod3$, a contradiction. Therefore $p=3$.

If $e\ge3$, then $9\mid\varphi(3^e)=2\cdot3^{e-1}$, so $9\mid S$. Now $ab\equiv-1\pmod9$, so $a$ is prime to $3$ and, as above, $a^4+a^2+1\equiv a^2S\equiv0\pmod9$. But $a^2\equiv1,4$ or $7\pmod9$, and then $a^4+a^2+1\equiv3$, $21\equiv3$, $57\equiv3\pmod9$, a contradiction. So $e=2$ and $ab=8$. With $\varphi(9)=6$: the pairs $\{1,8\}$ give $S=66=6\cdot11$, and the pairs $\{2,4\}$ give $S=21$, not divisible by $6$. So $\{a,b\}=\{1,8\}$, and $1=F_1$, $8=F_6$ are Fibonacci numbers.

Step 4: if $e=1$, Vieta jumping. Now $N=p$ is prime and $\varphi(N)=p-1=ab$, so $ab\mid S$: $a^2+b^2+1=k\,ab$ for a positive integer $k$. Consider any positive integers $u\le v$ with $u^2+v^2+1=kuv$ (the same $k$).

  • If $u=v$, then $u^2\mid2u^2+1$, so $u^2\mid1$ and $(u,v)=(1,1)$.

  • If $u=1<v$, then $v\mid v^2+2$, so $v\mid2$ and $(u,v)=(1,2)$.

  • If $1<u<v$, then $v$ is a root of $t^2-ku\,t+(u^2+1)=0$, whose other root is $v'=ku-v=\frac{u^2+1}{v}$. So $v'$ is a positive integer, and $v'<u$ because $u^2+1<u(u+1)\le uv$. Thus $(v',u)$ is again a solution with the same $k$, its smaller entry first, and with a smaller sum.

Starting from $(\min(a,b),\max(a,b))$ and repeating the third case while it applies, the sum decreases, so we reach $(1,1)$ or $(1,2)$. Both give $k=3$ ($3=3\cdot1$, $6=3\cdot2$), so $k=3$. The step $(u,v)\mapsto(v',u)$ is undone by $(g,h)\mapsto(h,3h-g)$, because $v=3u-v'$. Since also $(1,1)\mapsto(1,2)$ under this map, the pair $(\min(a,b),\max(a,b))$ lies in the chain \[(1,1),\ (1,2),\ (2,5),\ (5,13),\ \dots,\qquad (g,h)\mapsto(h,3h-g).\] From $(1,2)=(F_1,F_3)$ on, the pairs are $(F_j,F_{j+2})$ with $j$ odd: indeed $F_{j+4}=F_{j+3}+F_{j+2}=2F_{j+2}+F_{j+1}=3F_{j+2}-F_j$ (as $F_{j+1}=F_{j+2}-F_j$), so $(F_j,F_{j+2})\mapsto(F_{j+2},F_{j+4})$. So $a$ and $b$ are Fibonacci numbers.

Conclusion. In every case $a$ and $b$ are Fibonacci numbers: $(a,b)$ is $(1,1)$, or $\{a,b\}=\{1,8\}$ (from $ab+1=9$), or $\{a,b\}=\{F_j,F_{j+2}\}$ with $j$ odd (when $ab+1$ is prime).
