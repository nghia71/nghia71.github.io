Problem 1

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

Initially, there are four positive integers $2,2,2,2$ on a board. Yani repeatedly does the following operation: Yani chooses two numbers $a$ and $b$ that satisfy $ab>1$, then changes them to $a-\frac1b$ and $b-\frac1a$ respectively.

After some operations, it is known that the numbers written on the board are $x,y,z,w$. If the product $xyzw$ is a positive integer, find all possible values of $xyzw$.

Show the answer

$1$, $4$, $9$ (and $16$ if no operation is made)

Show the solution

Solution (The product is always a square of a rational number)

Write the operation as \[a\mapsto a-\frac1b=a\Bigl(1-\frac1{ab}\Bigr),\qquad b\mapsto b-\frac1a=b\Bigl(1-\frac1{ab}\Bigr).\] All numbers stay positive and rational. Initially they are. If $a,b>0$ and $ab>1$, then $0<1-\frac1{ab}<1$, so the new numbers are again positive and rational. By induction, every number on the board is always a positive rational.

The product. One operation multiplies the product $\Pi$ of the four numbers by $\lambda^2$, where $\lambda=1-\frac1{ab}$ is a rational number with $0<\lambda<1$. Starting from $\Pi=16=4^2$, after any number of operations $\Pi=(4q)^2$ for a positive rational $q$, and after at least one operation $\Pi<16$.

If $\Pi=xyzw$ is a positive integer and also the square of a rational number, it is the square of an integer (if $(r/s)^2$ is an integer with $\gcd(r,s)=1$, then $s^2\mid r^2$, so $s=1$). So after at least one operation $\Pi\in\{1,4,9\}$.

All three values occur.

  • $(2,2,2,2)\to\bigl(\tfrac32,\tfrac32,2,2\bigr)$ (using $a=b=2$, $ab=4$): product $9$.

  • Then use $a=\tfrac32$, $b=2$ ($ab=3$): $\bigl(1,\tfrac32,\tfrac43,2\bigr)$, product $4$.

  • Then use $a=\tfrac32$, $b=\tfrac43$ ($ab=2$): $a-\frac1b=\frac34$, $b-\frac1a=\frac23$, giving $\bigl(1,\tfrac34,\tfrac23,2\bigr)$, product $1$.

Hence the possible values are $\boxed{1,\ 4,\ 9}$ (and $16$ if one also allows making no operation at all).

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral such that \[\angle ABC=140^\circ\quad\text{and}\quad\angle ACD=110^\circ.\] Let the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at $E$. It is known that the circumcenter of $CED$ lies on side $AD$ and the circumcenter of $BEC$ lies on the extension of side $AB$.

Determine $\angle BAD$.

Show the answer

$\angle BAD=50^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two circumcenters, then the arc midpoint)

We use a standard fact: in a triangle $XYZ$ with circumcenter $O$, the angle between the side $YX$ and the ray $YO$ is $|90^\circ-\angle Z|$; if $\angle Z$ is obtuse then $O$ and $Z$ lie on opposite sides of $XY$, and if $\angle Z$ is acute they lie on the same side. (The central angle $\angle XOY$ equals $2\angle Z$ or $360^\circ-2\angle Z$, and triangle $XOY$ is isosceles.)

Since $ABCD$ is convex, $E$ lies inside both diagonals, so $A$ and $C$ lie on opposite sides of $BD$, and $B$ and $D$ on opposite sides of $AC$.

Triangle $CED$. Its angle at $C$ is $\angle DCE=\angle DCA=110^\circ$, obtuse. So its circumcenter $O_1$ lies on the side of line $DE=BD$ opposite to $C$, i.e. on $A$'s side, and $\angle O_1DE=110^\circ-90^\circ=20^\circ$. As $O_1$ lies on side $AD$, ray $DO_1$ is ray $DA$, so $\angle ADB=20^\circ$.

Triangle $BEC$. Let $O_2$ be its circumcenter, on line $AB$ outside the segment $AB$. The angle between ray $BO_2$ and $BC$ is $|90^\circ-\angle BEC|<90^\circ$, so $O_2$ is not on ray $BA$ (that would give $140^\circ$); thus $O_2$ lies on the ray opposite to $BA$, and $\angle O_2BC=180^\circ-140^\circ=40^\circ$. If $\angle BEC$ were acute, $O_2$ would lie on the same side of $BC$ as $E$, and then $A$ (on the opposite ray) would lie on the other side; but $A$ and $E$ are on the same side of $BC$ since $E$ is on segment $AC$. Hence $\angle BEC$ is obtuse and $\angle BEC-90^\circ=40^\circ$, i.e. $\angle BEC=130^\circ$.

Triangle $ACD$. Now $\angle CED=180^\circ-130^\circ=50^\circ$, so $\angle CDE=180^\circ-110^\circ-50^\circ=20^\circ$. Thus $\angle ADC=40^\circ$, $DB$ bisects $\angle ADC$, and $\angle CAD=180^\circ-110^\circ-40^\circ=30^\circ$.

Locating $B$. Let $W$ be the second intersection of the bisector $DE$ with the circumcircle of $ACD$; it is the midpoint of arc $AC$ not containing $D$, so $WA=WC$, $W$ lies on ray $DE$ beyond $E$, and $\angle AWC=180^\circ-\angle ADC=140^\circ$. The point $B$ also lies on ray $DE$ beyond $E$ (on the other side of $AC$ from $D$) with $\angle ABC=140^\circ$. As a point $X$ moves along this ray away from $E$, the angle $\angle AXC$ decreases strictly from $180^\circ$ to $0^\circ$ (each later point lies outside the triangle formed by an earlier one with $A$ and $C$), so only one point of the ray sees $AC$ under $140^\circ$. Hence $B=W$, triangle $ABC$ is isosceles with $BA=BC$, and $\angle BAC=\frac{180^\circ-140^\circ}{2}=20^\circ$.

Therefore $\angle BAD=\angle BAC+\angle CAD=20^\circ+30^\circ=\boxed{50^\circ}$.

Solution 2 (Law of sines)

Since $ABCD$ is convex, $E$ is interior to both diagonals. The circumcenter conditions translate into angles as follows (in a triangle $XYZ$ with circumcenter $O$, the ray $YO$ makes the angle $|90^\circ-\angle Z|$ with side $YX$, and $O$ is on the far side of $XY$ from $Z$ exactly when $\angle Z$ is obtuse).

  • In triangle $CED$ the angle at $C$ is $110^\circ$, so the circumcenter is on $A$'s side of $BD$ and ray $DA$ makes $20^\circ$ with $DB$: $\angle ADB=20^\circ$.

  • In triangle $BEC$ the circumcenter $O_2$ lies on the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$ (on the other extension the ray $BO_2$ would make $140^\circ>90^\circ$ with $BC$), so ray $BO_2$ makes $180^\circ-140^\circ=40^\circ$ with $BC$. The points $A$ and $O_2$ lie on opposite sides of line $BC$, while $A$ and $E$ lie on the same side (as $E$ is on segment $AC$); so $O_2$ and $E$ are on opposite sides of $BC$, the angle $\angle BEC$ is obtuse, and $\angle BEC-90^\circ=40^\circ$, i.e. $\angle BEC=130^\circ$.

Thus $\angle CED=50^\circ$, $\angle CDE=20^\circ$, $\angle ADC=40^\circ$ and $\angle CAD=30^\circ$.

Put $CD=1$. In triangle $ACD$, $AC=\frac{\sin40^\circ}{\sin30^\circ}=2\sin40^\circ$; in triangle $CDE$, $CE=\frac{\sin20^\circ}{\sin50^\circ}$. Hence, using $2\sin40^\circ\sin50^\circ=2\sin40^\circ\cos40^\circ=\sin80^\circ$, \[\frac{AE}{CE}=\frac{AC}{CE}-1=\frac{\sin80^\circ-\sin20^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}=\frac{2\cos50^\circ\sin30^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}=\frac{\sin40^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}=2\cos20^\circ.\] Now let $\alpha=\angle BAC$, so $\angle BCA=40^\circ-\alpha$ with $0<\alpha<40^\circ$. In triangle $ABE$ the angles are $\alpha$, $\angle AEB=50^\circ$ and $130^\circ-\alpha$; in triangle $CBE$ they are $40^\circ-\alpha$, $130^\circ$ and $10^\circ+\alpha$. By the law of sines in both triangles (common side $BE$), \[\frac{AE}{CE}=\frac{\sin(130^\circ-\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}\cdot\frac{\sin(40^\circ-\alpha)}{\sin(10^\circ+\alpha)}=:g(\alpha).\] The first factor equals $\frac{\sin(50^\circ+\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}=\sin50^\circ\cot\alpha+\cos50^\circ$, positive and strictly decreasing on $(0^\circ,40^\circ)$; the second is positive with a decreasing numerator and an increasing denominator. So $g$ is strictly decreasing, and $g(\alpha)=2\cos20^\circ$ has at most one solution. At $\alpha=20^\circ$: $g(20^\circ)=\frac{\sin110^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}\cdot\frac{\sin20^\circ}{\sin30^\circ}=2\cos20^\circ$. Hence $\alpha=20^\circ$ and \[\angle BAD=\alpha+\angle CAD=20^\circ+30^\circ=\boxed{50^\circ}.\]

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure · uvw and Equal-Variable Reduction

Prove that for all positive real numbers $a,b,c$ with $abc=1$, the following inequality holds: \[\frac{a+3}{3b}+\frac{b+3}{3c}+\frac{c+3}{3a}\ge a+b+c+1.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reduction to p and q)

Let $p=a+b+c$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. Since $abc=1$, $\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=q$, so the left side equals \[\frac13\Bigl(\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca\Bigr)+q .\] By Cauchy–Schwarz (Engel form), $\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca=\frac{a^2}{ab}+\frac{b^2}{bc}+\frac{c^2}{ca}\ge\frac{p^2}{q}$. So it suffices to prove \[\frac{p^2}{3q}+q\ge p+1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad p^2-3pq+3q^2-3q\ge0 .\] Write the left side as $\bigl(p-\tfrac32q\bigr)^2+\tfrac34q(q-4)$. We use two facts: $q\ge3\sqrt[3]{(abc)^2}=3$ by AM–GM, and $q^2\ge3(ab\cdot bc+bc\cdot ca+ca\cdot ab)=3abc\,p=3p$.

Case $q\ge4$. Both terms are nonnegative.

Case $3\le q<4$. Then $p\le\frac{q^2}3<\frac32q$ (as $q<\frac92$), so $\frac32q-p\ge\frac32q-\frac{q^2}3\ge0$ and \[\Bigl(p-\tfrac32q\Bigr)^2+\tfrac34q(q-4)\ge\Bigl(\tfrac32q-\tfrac{q^2}3\Bigr)^2+\tfrac34q^2-3q=\frac{q^4}{9}-q^3+3q^2-3q=\frac q9\,(q-3)^3\ge0 .\] In both cases the inequality holds. Equality requires $q=3$ (in the case $q\ge4$ it would need $q=4$ and $p=\frac32q=6$, contradicting $q^2\ge3p$), hence $a=b=c=1$, where both sides equal $4$.

Solution 2 (The sign of (a-1)(b-1)(c-1))

Let $p=a+b+c$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. Since $abc=1$, we have $\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=q$, so the left side equals $\frac13\bigl(\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca\bigr)+q$. Also $(a-1)(b-1)(c-1)=abc-q+p-1=p-q$. Multiplying by $3$, the inequality is therefore equivalent to \[\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca-3\ \ge\ 3(a-1)(b-1)(c-1). \tag{1}\] By AM–GM, $\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca\ge3\sqrt[3]{\frac ab\cdot\frac bc\cdot\frac ca}=3$, so the left side of (1) is nonnegative, with equality only if $\frac ab=\frac bc=\frac ca$, that is $a=b=c=1$.

Case $(a-1)(b-1)(c-1)\le0$. Then (1) holds, and equality forces $a=b=c=1$.

Case $(a-1)(b-1)(c-1)>0$. Then either all three numbers exceed $1$, which is impossible as $abc=1$, or exactly two of them are less than $1$. Inequality (1) is unchanged by the cyclic shift $(a,b,c)\to(b,c,a)$, and any two of $a,b,c$ are cyclically consecutive, so we may assume $a<1$ and $b<1$. Write $a=\frac1u$, $b=\frac1v$ with $u,v>1$; then $c=uv$, and \[\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca=\frac vu+\frac1{uv^2}+u^2v,\qquad (a-1)(b-1)(c-1)=\frac{(u-1)(v-1)(uv-1)}{uv}.\] Multiplying the difference of the two sides of (1) by $uv^2>0$ gives \[G=v^3+1+u^3v^3-3uv^2-3v(u-1)(v-1)(uv-1).\] Put $u=1+x$, $v=1+y$ with $x,y>0$. Expanding, \[G=3y^2+2y^3+x\,(3y+3y^2)+x^2\,(3+6y+3y^2)+x^3\,(1+3y+3y^2+y^3),\] a polynomial in $x,y$ with nonnegative coefficients and a positive term $3x^2$. Hence $G>0$ and (1) holds strictly.

So the inequality holds for all positive $a,b,c$ with $abc=1$, with equality only for $a=b=c=1$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

Find all pairs of positive integers $(x,y)$ that satisfy the equation \[xy=x+y+2\bigl(\gcd(x,y)\bigr)^2.\]

Note: $\gcd(x,y)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of the positive integers $x$ and $y$.

Show the answer

$(x,y)\in\{(2,10),(10,2),(4,12),(12,4)\}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Divide out the gcd)

Let $g=\gcd(x,y)$, $x=gm$, $y=gn$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$. The equation becomes $g^2mn=g(m+n)+2g^2$, i.e. \[g\,(mn-2)=m+n .\] So $mn>2$ and $g=\frac{m+n}{mn-2}\ge1$ gives $mn-2\le m+n$, i.e. $(m-1)(n-1)\le3$. By symmetry let $m\le n$.

  • $m=1$: $g=\frac{n+1}{n-2}=1+\frac{3}{n-2}$ with $n\ge3$, so $n-2\in\{1,3\}$: $n=3$, $g=4$, giving $(x,y)=(4,12)$; or $n=5$, $g=2$, giving $(x,y)=(2,10)$.

  • $m=2$: $n-1\le3$ and $n\ge2$ coprime to $2$, so $n=3$, but $g=\frac54$ is not an integer.

  • $m\ge3$: $(m-1)(n-1)\ge4>3$, impossible.

Both pairs work: $4\cdot12=48=4+12+2\cdot16$ and $2\cdot10=20=2+10+2\cdot4$. With the symmetric ones, the solutions are $\boxed{(2,10),(10,2),(4,12),(12,4)}$.

Solution 2 (Shift by one)

Let $g=\gcd(x,y)$. The equation is equivalent to \[(x-1)(y-1)=2g^2+1 .\] By symmetry let $x\le y$ and write $x=gm$ with $m\ge1$. Then $(x-1)^2\le(x-1)(y-1)=2g^2+1$.

If $m\ge2$, then $(2g-1)^2\le(gm-1)^2\le2g^2+1$, i.e. $2g^2\le4g$, so $g\le2$. For $g=1$: $(x-1)(y-1)=3$ gives $x=2$, $y=4$, but then $g=2$, a contradiction. For $g=2$: $m=2$ and $(x-1)^2=9=2g^2+1$, so $x=y=4$, but then $g=4$, a contradiction.

So $m=1$, i.e. $x=g$, and $(g-1)(y-1)=2g^2+1$. Here $g\ge2$, and $g-1$ divides $2g^2+1=2(g-1)(g+1)+3$, so $g-1\in\{1,3\}$.

  • $g=2$: $y-1=9$, $y=10$, and indeed $\gcd(2,10)=2$.

  • $g=4$: $3(y-1)=33$, $y=12$, and indeed $\gcd(4,12)=4$.

The solutions are $\boxed{(2,10),(10,2),(4,12),(12,4)}$.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Equation of Loci and Curves

Let $m$ and $n$ be two positive integers. Let $\mathcal L:\ y=x^2+mx+n$ be a parabola in the Cartesian plane. Let $A$ and $B$ be two distinct lattice points on the parabola $\mathcal L$.

Prove that there exist infinitely many pairs of distinct lattice points $C$ and $D$ on the parabola such that $A,B,C,D$ lie on a circle.

Note: A lattice point in the Cartesian plane is a point of the form $(a,b)$, with $a$ and $b$ both integers.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The sum of the four abscissas)

Write $f(x)=x^2+mx+n$ and let $A=(a,f(a))$, $B=(b,f(b))$ with $a\ne b$ integers.

Claim. If $a,b,c,d$ are four distinct real numbers with $a+b+c+d=-2m$, then the points of $\mathcal L$ with these abscissas lie on a circle.

Indeed, three distinct points of a parabola are never collinear (a line meets $\mathcal L$ in at most two points), so there is a circle $x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F'=0$ through the points with abscissas $a,b,c$. A point $(x,f(x))$ of $\mathcal L$ lies on this circle exactly when \[Q(x)=x^2+f(x)^2+Dx+Ef(x)+F'=0 .\] Here $Q$ is a polynomial of degree $4$ with leading term $x^4$ and $x^3$-coefficient $2m$ (both come from $f(x)^2=x^4+2mx^3+\dots$). It has the real roots $a,b,c$, so its fourth root $r$ is real too, and by Vieta $a+b+c+r=-2m$. Hence $r=d$, and the point with abscissa $d$ lies on the circle.

Construction. For each integer $c>2|a|+2|b|+2m$ put $d=-2m-a-b-c$. Then $c>|a|,|b|$, so $c\ne a,b$. Also $d=a$, $d=b$ and $d=c$ would force $c=-2m-2a-b$, $c=-2m-a-2b$ and $2c=-2m-a-b$, and each right side is at most $2|a|+2|b|+2m$ in absolute value, which is less than $c$ (respectively $2c$); so $c,d,a,b$ are four distinct integers. The points $C=(c,f(c))$ and $D=(d,f(d))$ are lattice points because $m,n$ are integers, and by the claim $A,B,C,D$ are concyclic. Moreover $d<-2m-a-b-2|a|-2|b|-2m<0<c$, so different values of $c$ give different pairs $\{C,D\}$, and there are infinitely many such pairs.

Solution 2 (Two chords with opposite slopes)

Write $f(x)=x^2+mx+n$. The chord of $\mathcal L$ through the points with abscissas $s\ne t$ has slope $\frac{f(s)-f(t)}{s-t}=s+t+m$.

Choose integers $c\ne d$, both different from $a,b$, with $c+d=-2m-a-b$ (for example $c$ any large integer and $d=-2m-a-b-c$); there are infinitely many such pairs, and $C=(c,f(c))$, $D=(d,f(d))$ are lattice points. Then the slopes of $AB$ and $CD$ are $k=a+b+m$ and $c+d+m=-k$. Write these lines as $y=kx+u$ and $y=-kx+w$, and consider the curve \[\Phi(x,y)=(y-kx-u)(y+kx-w)+(1+k^2)\bigl(x^2+mx+n-y\bigr)=0 .\] Expanding, the $xy$-terms cancel ($kxy-kxy=0$), and the $x^2$- and $y^2$-coefficients are $-k^2+(1+k^2)=1$ and $1$. So $\Phi=0$ has the form $x^2+y^2+(\text{linear})=0$, which is a circle, a point, or empty. Each of $A,B,C,D$ lies on $\mathcal L$ and on line $AB$ or line $CD$, so both products vanish there and $\Phi=0$. Four distinct points lie on the curve, so it is a genuine circle through $A,B,C,D$.

Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Integer and Rational Root Structure

Find all pairs of integers $(a,b)$ such that the quadratic equation \[x^2+(an+b)x+n=0\] has two integer roots for infinitely many natural numbers $n$.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(1,1)$ and $(a,b)=(-1,-1)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A factorisation from Vieta)

Both pairs work: $x^2+(n+1)x+n=(x+1)(x+n)$ and $x^2-(n+1)x+n=(x-1)(x-n)$ have integer roots for every $n$.

Conversely, suppose the roots $r,s$ are integers for infinitely many $n$. By Vieta, $rs=n$ and $r+s=-(an+b)$, so \[a\,rs+r+s+b=0. \tag{1}\] If $a=0$, then $r+s=-b$ is fixed and $n=rs\le\frac{(r+s)^2}{4}=\frac{b^2}4$, so only finitely many $n$ occur. Hence $a\ne0$.

If $a\ne0$, multiply (1) by $a$ and add $1$: \[(ar+1)(as+1)=1-ab .\] If $1-ab\ne0$, the integer $1-ab$ has finitely many factorizations, so $ar+1$, $as+1$, hence $r,s$ and $n=rs$, take finitely many values, a contradiction. Thus $ab=1$, so $(a,b)=(1,1)$ or $(-1,-1)$, and these work as shown.

Solution 2 (One root is bounded)

As computed directly, $(a,b)=(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$ work: the roots are $-1,-n$ and $1,n$ respectively.

Suppose integer roots $r,s$ exist for infinitely many $n$; then $rs=n\ge1$ and $r+s=-(an+b)$. If $a=0$, then $|r|+|s|=|r+s|=|b|$ (the roots have equal signs), so $n=|rs|$ is bounded; impossible. So $|a|\ge1$, and \[|r|+|s|\ge|r+s|=|an+b|\ge n-|b|=|r|\,|s|-|b|,\] i.e. $(|r|-1)(|s|-1)\le|b|+1$. If both $|r|,|s|\ge|b|+3$, the left side would be at least $(|b|+2)^2>|b|+1$. So one root has absolute value at most $|b|+2$. Only finitely many such values exist, so some integer $r_0$ is a root for infinitely many $n$: \[r_0^2+(an+b)r_0+n=0\iff (ar_0+1)\,n=-(r_0^2+br_0).\] A linear function of $n$ vanishing for infinitely many $n$ is identically zero, so $ar_0+1=0$ and $r_0^2+br_0=0$. The first gives $ar_0=-1$, so $r_0=\pm1$ and $a=-r_0$; the second gives $b=-r_0$. Hence $(a,b)=(1,1)$ (with $r_0=-1$) or $(a,b)=(-1,-1)$ (with $r_0=1$).

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Hybrid Counting and Structure

A binary word consisting of 0s and 1s is called a palindrome if reading it from the front or from the back produces the same word. For example, 101 and 0110 are palindromes.

A binary word is called an $n$-palindrome if it can be cut into exactly $n$ disjoint palindromes. For example, the binary word 1100101101 is a 5-palindrome: $1\mid1001\mid0\mid1\mid101$, and is also a 3-palindrome: $11\mid00\mid101101$, but it is not a 1-palindrome.

Show that there exists a binary word that is not an $n$-palindrome for every $n=1,2,3,\dots,26$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A periodic word with only short palindromes)

It suffices to find a word $w$ that cannot be cut into fewer than $27$ palindromes: then it is not an $n$-palindrome for any $n\le26$.

Let $u=001011$ and $w=u^{18}$ ($u$ repeated $18$ times), of length $108$. We claim that every palindrome occurring in $w$ (as a block of consecutive letters) has length at most $4$.

A palindrome of length $\ell\ge5$ contains, as its middle part, a palindrome of length $5$ or $6$ (removing the first and last letters of a palindrome leaves a palindrome). So it suffices to check that no block of length $5$ or $6$ of $w$ is a palindrome. As $w$ is periodic with period $6$, these blocks are among the blocks of the six cyclic rotations of $u$: \[001011,\ 010110,\ 101100,\ 011001,\ 110010,\ 100101 .\] None of them is a palindrome (compare first and last letters, or, for $011001$ and $100101$, the second and fifth letters), and their length-$5$ prefixes $00101,01011,10110,01100,11001,10010$ are not palindromes either (again compare the first and last, or the second and fourth letters). This proves the claim.

Now, if $w$ is cut into $n$ palindromes, each has length at most $4$, so $108\le4n$ and $n\ge27$. Hence $w$ is not an $n$-palindrome for any $n=1,2,\dots,26$.

Solution 2 (Counting words that are n-palindromes)

Let $N=1000$. We count binary words of length $N$ that are $n$-palindromes for some $n\le26$, and show there are fewer than $2^N$ of them; then some word of length $N$ is not an $n$-palindrome for any $n\le26$.

An $n$-palindrome of length $N$ is determined by the lengths $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n$ of the pieces (a composition of $N$ into $n$ positive parts: $\binom{N-1}{n-1}$ choices) and by the first $\lceil\ell_i/2\rceil$ letters of each piece (the rest of a palindrome is the mirror image). Since $\sum\lceil\ell_i/2\rceil\le\sum\frac{\ell_i+1}2=\frac{N+n}2$, the number of $n$-palindromes of length $N$ is at most $\binom{N-1}{n-1}2^{(N+n)/2}$. Summing over $n\le26$, and using $\binom{N-1}{n-1}\le\binom{N-1}{25}\le N^{25}<2^{250}$ (as $N=1000<2^{10}$ and $n-1\le25<\frac{N-1}{2}$), the total is less than \[26\cdot2^{250}\cdot2^{(1000+26)/2}<2^5\cdot2^{250}\cdot2^{513}=2^{768}<2^{1000}.\] So some binary word of length $1000$ is not an $n$-palindrome for any $n=1,\dots,26$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Change of Variables and Strategic Reparameterization

Given a natural number $m\ge2$. Let $-1<a_1<a_2<\dots<a_m<1$ be rational numbers and let $T$ be the set of all numbers of the form \[\frac{a_i+a_j}{1-a_ia_j}\] with $1\le i\le j\le m$. Determine the minimum possible value of $n(T)$.

Remark. For any set $X$, we use $n(X)$ to denote the number of elements of $X$.

Show the answer

$2m-1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangent addition)

Write $a_i=\tan\theta_i$ with $-\frac\pi4<\theta_1<\dots<\theta_m<\frac\pi4$. Then \[\frac{a_i+a_j}{1-a_ia_j}=\tan(\theta_i+\theta_j),\] and $\theta_i+\theta_j\in(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)$, where $\tan$ is injective. So $n(T)$ equals the number of distinct sums $\theta_i+\theta_j$ ($i\le j$).

Lower bound. The chain \[\theta_1+\theta_1<\theta_1+\theta_2<\theta_2+\theta_2<\theta_2+\theta_3<\dots<\theta_{m-1}+\theta_m<\theta_m+\theta_m\] contains $2m-1$ distinct sums, so $n(T)\ge2m-1$.

Construction. Let $N=4m$ and $\varphi=\arctan\frac1N$, and put $a_k=\tan(k\varphi)$ for $k=1,\dots,m$. Since $\varphi<\frac1N$, we have $0<k\varphi\le2m\varphi<\frac12<\frac\pi4$ for $k\le2m$, so $0<a_1<\dots<a_m<1$. Each $\tan(k\varphi)$ is rational: by induction, $\tan((k+1)\varphi)=\frac{\tan k\varphi+\frac1N}{1-\frac1N\tan k\varphi}$ (the denominator is positive since both angles are below $\frac\pi4$). Now $\frac{a_i+a_j}{1-a_ia_j}=\tan((i+j)\varphi)$ takes only the $2m-1$ values $\tan(k\varphi)$, $2\le k\le2m$.

Hence the minimum of $n(T)$ is $\boxed{2m-1}$.

Solution 2 (Monotonicity and Gaussian rationals)

Let $g(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{1-xy}$ on $(-1,1)^2$, where $1-xy>0$.

Lower bound. $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\frac{(1-xy)+y(x+y)}{(1-xy)^2}=\frac{1+y^2}{(1-xy)^2}>0$, and by symmetry $g$ is also increasing in $y$. Hence \[g(a_1,a_1)<g(a_1,a_2)<g(a_2,a_2)<g(a_2,a_3)<\dots<g(a_m,a_m),\] $2m-1$ distinct elements of $T$.

Construction. The identity $(1+ix)(1+iy)=(1-xy)+i(x+y)$ shows: if $1+ix$ and $1+iy$ are positive multiples of complex numbers $z$ and $w$ with $\operatorname{Re}(zw)>0$, then $g(x,y)=\frac{\operatorname{Im}(zw)}{\operatorname{Re}(zw)}$. Let $z=1+\frac{i}{4m}$ and $z^k=X_k+iY_k$ ($X_k,Y_k$ rational). The argument of $z$ is $\psi=\arctan\frac1{4m}<\frac1{4m}$, so $z^k$ has argument $k\psi<\frac{\pi}{4}$ for $k\le2m$; thus $X_k>Y_k>0$. Put $a_k=\frac{Y_k}{X_k}$ for $k\le2m$: rational, in $(0,1)$, increasing in $k$, and $1+ia_k=\frac{z^k}{X_k}$. For $i\le j\le m$, the product $z^iz^j=z^{i+j}$ has positive real part, so \[g(a_i,a_j)=\frac{Y_{i+j}}{X_{i+j}}=a_{i+j}.\] So $T\subseteq\{a_2,\dots,a_{2m}\}$ has at most $2m-1$ elements, and the minimum is $\boxed{2m-1}$.

Problem 4

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Coordinate Optimization of Synthetic Figures

Given an acute non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ with orthocenter $H$ and circumcenter $O$. The angle bisector of $\angle BAC$ intersects side $BC$ at point $K$. The line passing through $H$ and perpendicular to $AK$ intersects the circumcircle of $ABC$ at points $D$ and $E$. Let the circumcircle of triangle $DEK$ intersect line $BC$ at points $F$ and $K$. Prove that line $AO$ is parallel to line $FH$.

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates on BC with the altitude as axis)

Let $\ell$ be the line $DE$ (through $H$, perpendicular to $AK$). Take the foot of the altitude from $A$ as the origin, $BC$ as the $x$-axis and the altitude as the $y$-axis: \[A=(0,u),\quad B=(b,0),\quad C=(c,0),\quad K=(k,0),\quad b<0<c,\ u>0 .\] For an acute triangle $H=(0,v)$ with $v=-\frac{bc}{u}>0$ (the line through $B$ perpendicular to $AC$ meets the $y$-axis there), so $bc=-uv$.

The bisector. Let $\theta_B,\theta_C$ be the signed angles of $AB$, $AC$ from the downward vertical: $\tan\theta_B=\frac bu$, $\tan\theta_C=\frac cu$. The bisector $AK$ has signed angle $\delta=\frac{\theta_B+\theta_C}{2}$ and $\tan\delta=\frac ku$. Here $\theta_B+\theta_C=\angle B-\angle C$ (as $\theta_C=90^\circ-\angle C$, $\theta_B=-(90^\circ-\angle B)$), which lies strictly between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ and is nonzero (the triangle is not isosceles), so $k\ne0$ and $|k|<u$. Computing $\tan2\delta$ in two ways, with $s=b+c$: \[\frac{2ku}{u^2-k^2}=\frac{\frac bu+\frac cu}{1-\frac{bc}{u^2}}=\frac{su}{u^2+uv}=\frac{s}{u+v},\qquad\text{so}\qquad s=\frac{2ku(u+v)}{u^2-k^2}. \tag{1}\]

The point $F$. The line $\ell$ consists of the points $X$ with $(X-H)\cdot(K-A)=0$; it is not parallel to $BC$ (else $AK\perp BC$), and it meets $BC$ at $P=(p,0)$ with $pk+uv=0$, i.e. $p=-\frac{uv}{k}$. Note $p$ and $k$ have opposite signs, so $P\ne K$. The power of $P$ with respect to the circumcircle and with respect to the circle $DEK$ (both contain $D$ and $E$, and $P$ lies on line $DE$) equals $\overline{PD}\cdot\overline{PE}$, hence (signed lengths along $BC$) \[(k-p)(f-p)=(b-p)(c-p)=bc-ps+p^2,\] where $F=(f,0)$ (if the circle $DEK$ is tangent to $BC$ at $K$, then $F=K$ and the same relation holds). With $bc=-uv$ and $p=-\frac{uv}k$: \[k-p=\frac{k^2+uv}{k},\qquad bc-ps+p^2=\frac{uv}{k^2}\bigl(ks+uv-k^2\bigr),\] so \[f=p+\frac{uv}{k}\cdot\frac{ks+uv-k^2}{k^2+uv}=\frac{uv}{k}\Bigl(\frac{ks+uv-k^2}{k^2+uv}-1\Bigr)=\frac{uv\,(s-2k)}{k^2+uv}.\] By (1), $s-2k=\frac{2k\left(u(u+v)-(u^2-k^2)\right)}{u^2-k^2}=\frac{2k(uv+k^2)}{u^2-k^2}$, hence \[f=\frac{2kuv}{u^2-k^2}.\]

Conclusion. The circumcenter is $O=\bigl(\frac s2,\frac{u^2+bc}{2u}\bigr)$, so $\overrightarrow{AO}=\bigl(\frac s2,\frac{bc-u^2}{2u}\bigr)=\frac12\bigl(s,-(u+v)\bigr)$, which by (1) is a positive multiple of $\bigl(2ku,-(u^2-k^2)\bigr)$. Also $\overrightarrow{FH}=(-f,v)=-\frac{v}{u^2-k^2}\bigl(2ku,-(u^2-k^2)\bigr)$. The two vectors are parallel, so $AO\parallel FH$.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Given a regular pentagon $ABCDE$. Let a circle $\Gamma$ be centered at a point inside the pentagon such that $\Gamma$ passes through $A$ and is tangent to $BC$ at $M$. Let points $A$ and $N$ be the intersections of $\Gamma$ with the side $AE$. Prove that $MN$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$ if and only if $\Gamma$ is tangent to the side $DE$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Both conditions fix the angle BAM)

Let $O$ be the centre and $r$ the radius of $\Gamma$. Each interior angle of the pentagon is $108^\circ$ and triangle $ABC$ is isosceles with $\angle BAC=36^\circ$. If $M=B$, then $O$ lies on the perpendicular to $BC$ at $B$ and on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, so $\angle ABO=108^\circ-90^\circ=18^\circ=\angle BAO$; then $O$ is not on the symmetry axis (which makes $54^\circ$ with $AB$) and $\angle MAE=108^\circ\ne90^\circ$, so (by Steps 2 and 3 below) both conditions fail. From now on $M\ne B$ and $x=\angle BAM$ satisfies $0<x\le36^\circ$. Each interior angle of the pentagon is $108^\circ$, and the axis of symmetry $s$ through $A$ (and the midpoint of $CD$) makes $54^\circ$ with $AB$ and with $AE$.

Step 1: $\angle BAO=18^\circ+2x$. In triangle $ABM$, $\angle ABM=108^\circ$, so $\angle AMB=72^\circ-x<90^\circ$. The radius $OM$ is perpendicular to $BC$, and $O$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $A$. So ray $MA$ lies strictly between rays $MB$ and $MO$, i.e. $B$ and $O$ lie on opposite sides of line $AM$, and $\angle AMO=90^\circ-(72^\circ-x)=18^\circ+x$. Triangle $OAM$ is isosceles ($OA=OM=r$), so $\angle OAM=18^\circ+x$ and \[\angle BAO=\angle BAM+\angle MAO=18^\circ+2x .\]

Step 2: $\Gamma$ is tangent to $DE$ iff $x=18^\circ$. The lines $BC$ and $DE$ are mirror images in $s$, and they meet at a point beyond $CD$; the pentagon lies inside the angle they form, whose bisector is $s$. A point inside the pentagon is equidistant from lines $BC$ and $DE$ iff it lies on $s$. If $O\in s$, the reflection in $s$ maps $\Gamma$ to itself and $BC$ to $DE$, so $\Gamma$ touches side $DE$ at the mirror image of $M$. Conversely, if $\Gamma$ touches $DE$, then $O$ is at distance $r$ from both lines, so $O\in s$. Thus $\Gamma$ is tangent to $DE$ iff $\angle BAO=54^\circ$, iff $18^\circ+2x=54^\circ$, iff $x=18^\circ$.

Step 3: $MN$ is a diameter iff $x=18^\circ$. Since $A$, $M$, $N$ lie on $\Gamma$ and $A\ne M$: if $N\ne A$, then $MN$ is a diameter iff $\angle MAN=90^\circ$ (Thales and its converse); if $N=A$ (line $AE$ tangent to $\Gamma$ at $A$), then $MN=MA$ is a diameter iff $AM$ passes through $O$, iff $AM\perp AE$. In both cases, $MN$ is a diameter iff $\angle MAE=90^\circ$, i.e. iff $108^\circ-x=90^\circ$, iff $x=18^\circ$.

Steps 2 and 3 together give: $MN$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$ if and only if $\Gamma$ is tangent to $DE$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Take side length $1$, $B=\bigl(-\frac12,0\bigr)$, $C=\bigl(\frac12,0\bigr)$, and the pentagon above the $x$-axis. Put $t=\cos36^\circ$, $s=\sin36^\circ$, $h=\sin72^\circ=2st$ and $H=h+s$. The exterior angle of a regular pentagon is $72^\circ$, so the side $BA$ leaves $B$ at $108^\circ$ to the positive $x$-axis and the side $AE$ leaves $A$ at $36^\circ$. We use $t=\cos36^\circ=\frac{1+\sqrt5}4$ and $\cos72^\circ=\frac{\sqrt5-1}4$; thus $\frac12+\cos72^\circ=t$, $\cos72^\circ=2t^2-1$, and $4t^2-2t-1=0$. Hence $A=B+(\cos108^\circ,\sin108^\circ)=(-t,h)$, $E=A+(t,s)=(0,H)$, and by symmetry in the $y$-axis $D=(t,h)$: \[A=(-t,\,h),\qquad E=(0,\,H),\qquad D=(t,\,h).\]

Let $O=(x,y)$ be the centre of $\Gamma$. Since $\Gamma$ touches $BC$ at $M$ and $O$ is inside the pentagon, $M=(x,0)$, the radius is $y>0$, and $-\frac12\le x\le\frac12$. Put $w=x+t$; then $w>0$ because $t>\frac12$. The condition $OA=y$ reads $w^2+(y-h)^2=y^2$, that is \[2hy=h^2+w^2. \tag{1}\]

The diameter condition. Let $M'=(x,2y)$ be the point of $\Gamma$ opposite $M$. Then $MN$ is a diameter if and only if $N=M'$. The line $AE$ is $Y=H+\frac st X$ (it passes through $E=(0,H)$ with slope $\tan36^\circ$). By (1), $M'$ lies on this line if and only if \[\frac{h^2+w^2}{h}=H+\frac st\,(w-t)\iff t(h^2+w^2)=htH+hs(w-t)\iff tw^2=hs\,w,\] where we used $htH-hst=ht(H-s)=th^2$. As $w>0$, this means $w=\frac{hs}{t}=2s^2$. If $MN$ is a diameter, then $M'=N$ lies on $AE$, so $w=2s^2$. Conversely, if $w=2s^2$, then $x=2s^2-t=\frac32-2t$ (since $2s^2=1-\cos72^\circ=\frac32-t$), and $-t<x<0$, so $M'$ lies on the side $AE$ (whose points have $-t\le X\le0$) and $M'\ne A$. Thus $M'$ is the second common point of $\Gamma$ and line $AE$, so $M'=N$. Hence \[MN\text{ is a diameter}\iff w=2s^2.\]

The tangency condition. The line $DE$ passes through $E$ with direction $(t,-s)$ (a unit vector, as $D-E=(t,-s)$), so its equation is $sX+tY=tH$, and the distance from the interior point $O$ to it is $tH-sx-ty$. If $\Gamma$ is tangent to side $DE$, this distance equals $y$. Multiplying $tH-s(w-t)-ty-y=0$ by $2h$ and using (1): \[2htH+2hst-2hsw-(t+1)(h^2+w^2)=0 .\] Since $tH+st=t(h+s)+st=th+h=(t+1)h$ (because $h=2st$), the constant terms combine to $(t+1)h^2$, and dividing by $-(t+1)$ we get, with $2hs=4s^2t=4t(1-t)(1+t)$, \[w^2+4t(1-t)\,w-4s^2t^2=0 .\] Now $4t(1-t)=2t^2-2s^2$: indeed $2t^2-2s^2=2\cos72^\circ=4t^2-2$, and $4t^2-2=4t-4t^2$ is $4t^2-2t-1=0$ again. So the equation factors as \[(w-2s^2)(w+2t^2)=0,\] and since $w>0$, tangency to $DE$ implies $w=2s^2$. Conversely, if $w=2s^2$, the same computation shows that the distance from $O$ to line $DE$ equals the radius $y$. The foot of the perpendicular from $O$ has coordinate $(O-E)\cdot(t,-s)=tx+s(H-y)$ along $DE$, measured from $E$; with $x=\frac32-2t$ and $2y=H+\frac st x$ (as $M'$ is on $AE$) this equals $2(1-t)=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx0.38$, which lies between $0$ and $1$. So the point of tangency lies on the side $DE$, and $\Gamma$ is tangent to side $DE$. Hence \[\Gamma\text{ is tangent to side }DE\iff w=2s^2.\]

Both conditions are equivalent to $w=2s^2$, so $MN$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$ if and only if $\Gamma$ is tangent to $DE$.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

Given positive real numbers $a,b,c$ and real numbers $x\le y\le z$ such that \[a+b+c=1\quad\text{and}\quad ax+by+cz=0.\] Prove that \[a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le\frac{z-x}{2}.\]

Show the 4 solutions

Solution 1 (Two cases by the sign of y)

Since $ax+by+cz=0$ is a weighted average (with positive weights summing to $1$) of $x\le y\le z$, we have $x\le0\le z$. Let $L=a|x|+b|y|+c|z|$.

Case $y\ge0$. Then $-ax=by+cz\ge0$ and $L=-ax+by+cz=2(-ax)=2(by+cz)$. Moreover $by+cz\le(b+c)z=(1-a)z$. Hence \[L^2=4\,(-ax)(by+cz)\le4\,a(1-a)\,(-x)z\le4\cdot\frac14\cdot\Bigl(\frac{z-x}{2}\Bigr)^2,\] using $a(1-a)\le\frac14$ and AM–GM for the nonnegative numbers $-x$ and $z$: $(-x)z\le\bigl(\frac{z+(-x)}2\bigr)^2$. So $L\le\frac{z-x}2$.

Case $y\le0$. Symmetrically $cz=-ax-by\ge0$, $L=2cz=2(-ax-by)$ and $-ax-by\le(a+b)(-x)=(1-c)(-x)$. Hence $L^2=4cz(-ax-by)\le4c(1-c)z(-x)\le\bigl(\frac{z-x}2\bigr)^2$.

In both cases $a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le\frac{z-x}{2}$. (Equality holds when $x=y=z=0$, and otherwise exactly when $z=-x>0$ and either $a=\frac12$, $y=z$, or $c=\frac12$, $y=x$; for example $a=\frac12$, $b=c=\frac14$, $(x,y,z)=(-1,1,1)$.)

Solution 2 (Extreme points of the weights)

Fix $x\le y\le z$. If $x=z$ then all are $0$ and both sides vanish; assume $x<z$, so $x<0<z$ (the weighted average is $0$). Consider the set $W$ of weight vectors $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ with $\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0$, $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$ and $\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z=0$. It is the intersection of the triangle (simplex) with a plane through the point $(a,b,c)$, so it is a segment (or a point) whose endpoints lie on the boundary of the triangle, i.e. have a zero coordinate. The function $L(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\alpha|x|+\beta|y|+\gamma|z|$ is linear, so on $W$ it is at most its value at an endpoint.

At an endpoint, at most two weights are positive, say $p$ at $s$ and $q$ at $t$ with $s<0<t$, $s,t\in\{x,y,z\}$ (a single positive weight would sit on a zero value, giving $L=0$). From $p+q=1$ and $ps+qt=0$: $p=\frac{t}{t-s}$, $q=\frac{-s}{t-s}$, and \[L=p(-s)+qt=\frac{2(-s)t}{t-s}\le\frac{2}{t-s}\Bigl(\frac{t-s}{2}\Bigr)^2=\frac{t-s}2\le\frac{z-x}{2},\] by AM–GM and $x\le s<t\le z$. Hence $a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le\frac{z-x}{2}$.

Solution 3 (A chord above the graph of the absolute value)

Since $0=ax+by+cz$ is a weighted average (positive weights with sum $1$) of $x\le y\le z$, we have $x\le0\le z$. If $x=z$, then $x=y=z=0$ and both sides are $0$. Let $x<z$.

For $x\le t\le z$ write $t=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)z$ with $\lambda=\frac{z-t}{z-x}\in[0,1]$. By the triangle inequality, \[|t|\le\lambda|x|+(1-\lambda)|z|=\lambda(-x)+(1-\lambda)z=:\ell(t),\] where $\ell$ is the linear function with $\ell(x)=-x$ and $\ell(z)=z$ (geometrically: the graph of $|t|$ lies below the chord joining $(x,-x)$ and $(z,z)$). Applying this to $x,y,z$ and using that $\ell$ is linear and $a+b+c=1$, \[a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le a\,\ell(x)+b\,\ell(y)+c\,\ell(z)=\ell(ax+by+cz)=\ell(0).\] For $t=0$ we have $\lambda=\frac{z}{z-x}$, so \[\ell(0)=\frac{z}{z-x}(-x)+\frac{-x}{z-x}\,z=\frac{-2xz}{z-x}\le\frac{z-x}2,\] because the last inequality is $-4xz\le(z-x)^2$, i.e. $0\le(z+x)^2$. Hence $a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le\frac{z-x}2$.

Solution 4 (Second moment and Cauchy–Schwarz)

As $0=ax+by+cz$ is a weighted average of $x\le y\le z$ with positive weights summing to $1$, we have $x\le0\le z$, so $-xz\ge0$.

For every $t$ with $x\le t\le z$ we have $(t-x)(z-t)\ge0$, that is $t^2\le(x+z)t-xz$. Applying this to $t=x,y,z$ with weights $a,b,c$: \[ax^2+by^2+cz^2\le(x+z)(ax+by+cz)-xz(a+b+c)=-xz .\] By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \[\bigl(a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\bigr)^2=\bigl(\sqrt a\cdot\sqrt a|x|+\sqrt b\cdot\sqrt b|y|+\sqrt c\cdot\sqrt c|z|\bigr)^2\le(a+b+c)(ax^2+by^2+cz^2)\le-xz .\] Finally, by AM–GM for the nonnegative numbers $-x$ and $z$, $-xz\le\bigl(\frac{z-x}2\bigr)^2$. Taking square roots, $a|x|+b|y|+c|z|\le\frac{z-x}2$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Given a positive integer $n$ and an $n\times n$ grid board consisting of $n^2$ unit squares. Alea places several $2\times2$ tiles on the board such that the following conditions hold:

  • Every side of a tile lies on the grid lines of the board.

  • The placed tiles cannot overlap, but may touch at their sides or vertices.

  • For every $2\times2$ subgrid of the board, there is a tile that covers at least one unit square of that subgrid.

Determine the minimum number of tiles that Alea can place.

Show the answer

$\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rceil^2$

Show the solution

Solution (Positions of subgrids and tiles)

For $n=1$ there is no $2\times2$ subgrid and no tile fits, so the answer is $0=\lceil0/3\rceil^2$. Let $n\ge2$ and $m=n-1$. Label a $2\times2$ subgrid, and also a tile, by the position $(i,j)$ of its top-left cell, $1\le i,j\le m$. A tile at $(i,j)$ covers a cell of the subgrid at $(p,q)$ iff $|p-i|\le1$ and $|q-j|\le1$; two tiles overlap iff their positions satisfy the same condition.

Lower bound. Let $t=\lceil m/3\rceil$ and consider the $t^2$ subgrids at positions $(3\alpha+1,3\beta+1)$, $0\le\alpha,\beta\le t-1$ (all $\le m$ since $3(t-1)+1\le m$). Two of them differ by at least $3$ in some coordinate, so no tile covers cells of two of them. Each needs its own tile, so at least $t^2$ tiles are needed.

Construction. Let $c_\alpha=\min(3\alpha+2,m)$ for $\alpha=0,\dots,t-1$, and place tiles at all positions $(c_\alpha,c_\beta)$. Consecutive values differ by $3$, except possibly the last step, where $c_{t-1}=m$ and $c_{t-2}=3t-4$; since $m\ge3t-2$, this difference is at least $2$. So no two tiles overlap. Every value $p\in\{1,\dots,m\}$ is within $1$ of some $c_\alpha$: for $\alpha\le t-2$ the value $3\alpha+2$ is within $1$ of $3\alpha+1,3\alpha+2,3\alpha+3$, which covers $1,\dots,3t-3$; and $3t-2\le m\le3t$, so the last value $c_{t-1}=\min(3t-1,m)$ is within $1$ of every integer from $3t-2$ to $m$ (if $m\ge3t-1$, it is $3t-1$; if $m=3t-2$, it is $m$ itself). Hence every subgrid $(p,q)$ meets the tile at $(c_\alpha,c_\beta)$ with $|p-c_\alpha|\le1$, $|q-c_\beta|\le1$.

So the minimum number of tiles is $\boxed{\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rceil^2}$.

Problem 8

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Function Equations on Integers

Let $A=\{n\in\mathbb N\mid n\ge2\}$. Determine all functions $f:A\to A$ such that $m^{f(n)}$ and $f(m)^n$ have the same number of positive divisors for all integers $m$ and $n$ in $A$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=n$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Everything is determined by f(2))

Write $d(\cdot)$ for the number of divisors. The identity function works, since then both numbers are $m^n$.

Let $c=f(2)=\prod_{j=1}^kp_j^{c_j}$ (distinct primes, $c_j\ge1$). Taking $m=2$: $d(2^{f(n)})=f(n)+1$ and $d(c^n)=\prod_j(nc_j+1)$, so \[f(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(nc_j+1)-1\qquad\text{for all }n\ge2. \tag{1}\] For $n=2$ this gives $c+1=\prod_j(2c_j+1)$, which is odd, so $c$ is even. Write $c=2^{e}R$ with $R$ odd and $e\ge1$, and $Q=\prod_{p_j\ \mathrm{odd}}(2c_j+1)$, so that \[2^eR+1=(2e+1)Q. \tag{2}\] For an odd prime power, $p^a\ge3^a\ge2a+1$, with equality only for $p^a=3$; hence $R\ge Q$, and $R=Q$ only if $R\in\{1,3\}$. Also, if a prime power $p^a$ with $p\ge5$ or $a\ge2$ divides $R$, then $\frac{p^a}{2a+1}\ge\frac53$ (for $p\ge5$: $p^a\ge5^a\ge\frac53(2a+1)$; for $p=3$, $a\ge2$: $\frac{3^a}{2a+1}\ge\frac95$), so $R\ge\frac53Q$.

  • If $e\ge3$, then $2^e\ge2e+2$, so $c+1=2^eR+1\ge(2e+2)Q+1>(2e+1)Q$, contradicting (2).

  • If $e=1$: (2) reads $2R+1=3Q$, so $\frac RQ=\frac{3R}{2R+1}<\frac32<\frac53$; thus $R\in\{1,3\}$. $R=3$ gives $7=9$, false; $R=1$ gives $c=2$.

  • If $e=2$: $4R+1=5Q$, so $\frac RQ=\frac{5R}{4R+1}<\frac54<\frac53$, again $R\in\{1,3\}$; $R=3$ gives $13=15$, false; $R=1$ gives $c=4$.

So $c\in\{2,4\}$. If $c=4$, (1) gives $f(n)=2n+1-1=2n$ for $n\ge2$ (and $f(2)=4$ fits). But then $m=3$, $n=2$ gives $d(3^{f(2)})=d(3^4)=5$ and $d(f(3)^2)=d(36)=9$, a contradiction. Hence $c=2$, and (1) gives $f(n)=(n+1)-1=n$ for all $n\ge2$.

The only solution is $\boxed{f(n)=n}$.

Solution 2 (Divisor polynomials modulo a prime)

For an integer $m\ge2$ with prime factorisation $m=\prod_i p_i^{e_i}$, let $D_m(X)=\prod_i(1+e_iX)$, a polynomial with integer coefficients, constant term $1$, degree equal to the number of distinct primes of $m$, and leading coefficient $\prod_ie_i$. The number of divisors of $m^k$ is $D_m(k)$. So the condition reads \[D_m\bigl(f(n)\bigr)=D_{f(m)}(n)\qquad\text{for all }m,n\ge2. \tag{1}\] The identity function works, since then both sides of (1) are $D_m(n)$.

Step 1: $f$ is a polynomial. With $m=2$, $D_2(X)=1+X$, so (1) gives $f(n)=F(n)$ for all $n\ge2$, where $F(X)=D_{f(2)}(X)-1$. The polynomial $F$ has integer coefficients, zero constant term, degree $d\ge1$ (the number of distinct primes of $f(2)$) and positive leading coefficient $A$.

Step 2: $d=1$. Let $k\ge2$ and $m=2^k$, so $D_m(X)=1+kX$. By (1), $1+kF(n)=D_{f(2^k)}(n)$ for all $n\ge2$; two polynomials that agree at infinitely many points are equal, so \[1+kF(X)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1+g_jX),\] where $g_1,\dots,g_r\ge1$ are the exponents in the factorisation of $f(2^k)$. Comparing degrees, $r=d$, and comparing leading coefficients, $kA=g_1g_2\cdots g_d$. Now choose $k$ to be a prime not dividing $A$, and reduce modulo $k$. The left side becomes the constant $1$, so in $\mathbb F_k[X]$ we have $\prod_j(1+\bar g_jX)=1$. Over a field the degree of a product is the sum of the degrees, so every factor $1+\bar g_jX$ has degree $0$, that is $k\mid g_j$ for every $j$. Then $k^d$ divides $g_1\cdots g_d=kA$, and since $k\nmid A$, this forces $d=1$. Thus $F(X)=AX$ and \[f(n)=An\qquad\text{for all }n\ge2.\]

Step 3: $A=1$. Let $p$ be a prime not dividing $A$, and take $m=p$ in (1): $D_p(An)=1+An$ and $D_{f(p)}(n)=D_{Ap}(n)$, so as before $1+AX=D_{Ap}(X)$ as polynomials. The left side has degree $1$, so $Ap$ has exactly one prime factor. As $p\mid Ap$ and $p\nmid A$, this forces $A=1$.

Hence $\boxed{f(n)=n}$ is the only solution.
