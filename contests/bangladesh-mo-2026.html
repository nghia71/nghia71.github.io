Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Ratio Interpretation · also Higher Secondary, Problem 1

If $\dfrac{b}{a}=5$ and $\dfrac{c}{b}=7$, find the value of $\dfrac{a+b}{b+c}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Notice which variable occurs in both given ratios. Mark where it appears in the numerator and the denominator of the requested fraction.
Hint 2: key idea
Use $b$ as the common reference quantity. One given ratio lets you express $a$ in terms of $b$, and the other does the same for $c$. (LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change)
Hint 3: approach
Rearrange both given equations to express the other variables in terms of $b$. Substitute those expressions into the target and factor out the common quantity from its numerator and denominator.
Common trap
Expressing $a$ in terms of $b$ requires reversing the first ratio.
Show the answer

$\dfrac{3}{20}$

Show solution 1 – Express everything through $b$

From $b/a=5$ we get $a=b/5$, and from $c/b=7$ we get $c=7b$ (note $a,b\ne0$, since we divide by them). Hence \[\frac{a+b}{b+c}=\frac{\frac b5+b}{b+7b}=\frac{\frac65 b}{8b}=\frac{6}{40}=\boxed{\dfrac{3}{20}}.\]

Show solution 2 – Divide by $b$

Since $b\neq 0$, divide the numerator and the denominator by $b$: \[\frac{a+b}{b+c}=\frac{\frac ab+1}{1+\frac cb}=\frac{\frac15+1}{1+7}=\frac{6/5}{8}=\boxed{\dfrac{3}{20}}.\]

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 41: Functions and Algebraic Graphs · Function as Rule · also Higher Secondary, Problem 2

Let $f$ be a continuous function satisfying $f(1+x)=f(1-x)$ for every real $x$. It is given that the equation $f(x)=0$ has $2026$ distinct roots. Find the sum of all the roots.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the inputs $1+x$ and $1-x$ on a number line for a sample value of $x$. Notice where their midpoint stays as $x$ changes.
Hint 2: key idea
For a root $r$, choose $x$ so that $1+x=r$. The equality gives another root at the corresponding reflected input, so examine when the two inputs coincide. (LPS VI, Chapter 41: Functions and Algebraic Graphs)
Hint 3: approach
Identify the only root that could be unpaired under this reflection and compare that possibility with the parity of the given root count. Determine how many pairs remain and express their total from the sum within one pair.
Common trap
A root at $x=1$ would be its own partner, so use the even number of roots to decide whether such a root can occur.
Show the answer

$2026$

Show solution 1 – Pairing roots symmetric about $1$

Put $x=r-1$ in $f(1+x)=f(1-x)$: we get $f(r)=f(2-r)$ for every real $r$. So if $r$ is a root, then $2-r$ is a root too.

The map $r\mapsto 2-r$ pairs the roots, and $r=2-r$ only for $r=1$. If $1$ were a root, the remaining roots would split into pairs $\{r,2-r\}$ with $r\neq 2-r$, and the total number of roots would be odd. But there are $2026$ roots, an even number, so $1$ is not a root and the $2026$ roots form $1013$ pairs $\{r,2-r\}$, each with sum $2$. The sum of all roots is $1013\cdot 2=\boxed{2026}$. (Continuity is not needed.)

Show solution 2 – Shift to an even function

Let $g(t)=f(1+t)$. The condition says $g(t)=g(-t)$, so $g$ is even, and $x$ is a root of $f$ exactly when $t=x-1$ is a root of $g$.

The roots of an even function are symmetric about $0$: with every root $t$ also $-t$ is a root. Hence the roots of $g$ have sum $0$ (the root $t=0$, if present, contributes $0$, and the others cancel in pairs $t,-t$). The $2026$ roots of $f$ are $x=t+1$ over the $2026$ roots $t$ of $g$, so their sum is $0+2026\cdot 1=\boxed{2026}$.

Problem 3

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition · also Higher Secondary, Problem 3

$ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral. $F$ is the midpoint of the arc $AB$ not containing $C$ or $D$. Let $CF\cap BD=P$ and $DF\cap AC=Q$. Prove that $PQ\parallel AB$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the equal arcs on either side of $F$ and the corresponding chords. At $C$ and $D$, identify which original chord lines contain the rays toward $P$ and $Q$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the equal chords at $F$ and inscribed angles on the original circle to compare $\angle QCP$ and $\angle QDP$ as directed angles. This suggests a second circle involving the intersection points. (LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing)
Hint 3: approach
Translate each of those angles at $C$ and $D$ into a base angle of triangle $FAB$, then use their equality to establish the auxiliary circle. On that circle, compare the angles subtended by $QD$ at $P$ and $C$. Use the original circle to express the resulting angle at $C$ in terms of lines through $B$.
Common trap
Directed angles keep the comparisons valid when a ray is replaced by another ray on the same line.
Show solution 1 – An auxiliary cyclic quadrilateral $CDPQ$

We use directed angles between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$, written $\measuredangle$; then "$W,X,Y,Z$ concyclic" is equivalent to $\measuredangle XWY=\measuredangle XZY$ (for four points, no three collinear), and two lines $\ell,m$ are parallel when $\measuredangle(\ell,n)=\measuredangle(m,n)$ for a third line $n$.

Since $F$ is the midpoint of arc $AB$, $FA=FB$, so in the isosceles triangle $FAB$ we have $\measuredangle FAB=\measuredangle ABF$.

Lines $CQ$ and $CA$ coincide, as do $CP$ and $CF$; lines $DQ$ and $DF$ coincide, as do $DP$ and $DB$. Using inscribed angles on the circle $ABCDF$: \[\measuredangle QCP=\measuredangle ACF=\measuredangle ABF,\qquad \measuredangle QDP=\measuredangle FDB=\measuredangle FAB=\measuredangle ABF.\] Hence $\measuredangle QCP=\measuredangle QDP$, so $C,D,P,Q$ are concyclic. (They are not collinear: $P$ and $Q$ lie inside the quadrilateral, because $F$ lies on the arc $AB$ that contains neither $C$ nor $D$, so chord $CF$ crosses chord $BD$ and chord $DF$ crosses chord $AC$.)

From this circle and then from the circle $ABCD$: \[\measuredangle QPD=\measuredangle QCD=\measuredangle ACD=\measuredangle ABD.\] So $\measuredangle(PQ,BD)=\measuredangle(BA,BD)$: the lines $PQ$ and $AB$ make the same angle with $BD$, hence $PQ\parallel AB$.

Show solution 2 – Pascal's theorem with a tangent

Let $t$ be the tangent to the circle at $F$. Because $F$ is the midpoint of arc $AB$, the radius to $F$ is perpendicular to chord $AB$ (it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$), and $t$ is perpendicular to that same radius. Therefore $t\parallel AB$.

Apply Pascal's theorem to the (degenerate) hexagon $C\,F\,F\,D\,B\,A$ inscribed in the circle; the "side" $FF$ is the tangent $t$. Pascal's theorem says that the three intersection points of opposite sides \[CF\cap DB=P,\qquad FF\cap BA=t\cap AB,\qquad FD\cap AC=Q\] are collinear. Since $t\parallel AB$, the middle point is the point at infinity of the direction $AB$. A line through $P$ and $Q$ that also passes through the point at infinity of $AB$ is a line parallel to $AB$. Hence $PQ\parallel AB$.

(Without points at infinity: Pascal's theorem also holds in the form “if one pair of opposite sides is parallel, then the line through the other two intersection points is parallel to them as well”; here the pair is $t\parallel AB$.)

Show solution 3 – A rational parametrisation of the circle

Choose coordinates with $F=(0,0)$ and the circle $x^2+y^2=y$ (centre $(0,\frac12)$, radius $\frac12$; this only fixes the unit of length and the direction of the axes). For a real number $u$ put \[T(u)=\Bigl(\frac{u}{1+u^2},\ \frac1{1+u^2}\Bigr).\] Since $\bigl(\frac{u}{1+u^2}\bigr)^2+\bigl(\frac1{1+u^2}\bigr)^2=\frac{1}{1+u^2}$, the point $T(u)$ lies on the circle. Conversely every point $(x,y)\ne F$ of the circle has $y=x^2+y^2>0$ and equals $T(u)$ for $u=\frac xy$ (substitute $x=uy$ into $x^2+y^2=y$ to get $y=\frac1{1+u^2}$). Note that $T(u)$ lies on the line $x=uy$ through $F$.

Chords. For $s\ne t$ the line through $T(s)$ and $T(t)$ is \[(s+t)\,x+(1-st)\,y=1,\] since at $T(s)$ the left side is $\frac{(s+t)s+1-st}{1+s^2}=1$, and similarly at $T(t)$.

The points. For a point $X$ of the circle, $FX^2=x^2+y^2=y$. As $FA=FB$, the points $A$ and $B$ have the same height, so $A=T(a)$ and $B=T(-a)$ for some $a\ne0$; in particular $AB$ is horizontal. Write $C=T(c)$ and $D=T(d)$ (possible since $C,D\ne F$).

The line $CF$ is $x=cy$ and the line $BD$ is $(d-a)x+(1+ad)y=1$, so \[y_P=\frac{1}{(d-a)c+1+ad}=\frac1{1+cd+a(d-c)} .\] The line $DF$ is $x=dy$ and the line $AC$ is $(a+c)x+(1-ac)y=1$, so \[y_Q=\frac{1}{(a+c)d+1-ac}=\frac1{1+cd+a(d-c)} .\] (The denominators are not zero, because the intersection points exist.) Hence $P$ and $Q$ have the same height. They are different points: the lines $CF$ and $DF$ meet only at $F$, which is not on $BD$. So $PQ$ is horizontal, that is, $PQ\parallel AB$.

Problem 4

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 26: Double Counting and Combinatorial Proof · Average from Total Contribution · also Higher Secondary, Problem 4

Given $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}$, Mr. Teham chooses a pair of elements of $S$ and writes the greater number of the pair. For example, he chooses $\{6,7\}$ and writes the number $7$. Find the sum of all the numbers he has written after choosing all possible pairs.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try the rule on the smaller set $\{1,2,3,4\}$. When a pair is chosen, note which of the two numbers contributes to the sum.
Hint 2: key idea
Group the contributions by the number written. For a fixed $k$, a pair writes $k$ precisely when it contains $k$ and a smaller element. (LPS VI, Chapter 26: Double Counting and Combinatorial Proof)
Hint 3: approach
For each $k$, count the available smaller partners and multiply by the value written for each partner. Set up the sum of these contributions over the given set.
Common trap
A pair is unordered, so swapping its two entries does not create another contribution.
Show the answer

$112$

Show solution 1 – Count how often each number is written

The number $k$ is written exactly when the chosen pair is $\{j,k\}$ with $j<k$, which happens for $k-1$ pairs. So the total is \[\sum_{k=1}^{7}k(k-1)=0+2+6+12+20+30+42=\boxed{112}.\]

Show solution 2 – Layer-cake counting

Write each written number $M$ as $M=\sum_{t=1}^{7}[M\ge t]$, where $[M\ge t]$ is $1$ if $M\ge t$ and $0$ otherwise. Then the total is $\sum_{t=1}^{7}N_t$, where $N_t$ is the number of pairs whose larger element is at least $t$. A pair has larger element less than $t$ exactly when both elements lie in $\{1,\dots,t-1\}$, so $N_t=\binom72-\binom{t-1}2=21-\binom{t-1}{2}$. Hence, by the hockey-stick identity $\sum_{t=1}^{7}\binom{t-1}{2}=\binom73=35$, \[\sum_{t=1}^{7}N_t=7\cdot 21-35=\boxed{112}.\]

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Power of a Point · also Higher Secondary, Problem 5

$\triangle ABC$ is isosceles with $\angle ACB=90^\circ$. $E$ and $F$ are two distinct points on the arc $AB$ (of the circumcircle of $ABC$) not containing $C$. $CE\cap AB=D$ and $CF\cap AB=G$.

  • 1) Prove that \[CE\cdot DG=k\cdot EF\cdot CG\] for a real number $k$.

  • 2) Find the value of $k$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the diameter $AB$ and mark the order of the points along each of the rays $CE$ and $CF$. Highlight the segments that connect the two rays.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare triangles $CAD$ and $CEA$, using the angle at $C$ and an inscribed angle standing on arc $CA$. Look for a product of distances that you can obtain by doing the same thing with $F$ and $G$. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Turn the two similarity relations into products and rearrange their equality into ratios of sides about the angle at $C$. Check whether triangles $CDG$ and $CFE$ have the required included angle, then match the sides needed in the requested relation.
Common trap
In the second similarity, match the vertices by their side ratios, since the two rays appear in opposite orders.
Show the answer

$k=1$

Show solution 1 – Two similar-triangle steps

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$; since $\angle ACB=90^\circ$, $AB$ is a diameter, and $CA=CB$. The points $E,F$ lie on the arc $AB$ opposite to $C$, so the segments $CE$ and $CF$ cross the diameter $AB$ at the points $D$ and $G$, which lie between $C$ and $E$, and between $C$ and $F$, respectively.

Step 1: $CD\cdot CE=CA^2=CG\cdot CF$. The inscribed angle $\angle CEA$ stands on the arc $CA$, which is a quarter of the circle, so $\angle CEA=45^\circ=\angle CAB=\angle CAD$. Triangles $CAD$ and $CEA$ also share the angle at $C$, so they are similar, giving $\dfrac{CA}{CE}=\dfrac{CD}{CA}$, that is, $CD\cdot CE=CA^2$. In the same way $CG\cdot CF=CA^2$.

Step 2. From $CD\cdot CE=CG\cdot CF$ we get $\dfrac{CD}{CF}=\dfrac{CG}{CE}$. Triangles $CDG$ and $CFE$ have the common angle $\angle DCG=\angle FCE$, and the sides around it are proportional, so they are similar (SAS). Therefore \[\frac{DG}{FE}=\frac{CG}{CE},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad CE\cdot DG=EF\cdot CG.\] Thus the relation holds with the constant $\boxed{k=1}$, for all positions of $E\ne F$ on the arc.

Show solution 2 – Inversion centred at $C$

Let $r=CA=CB$ and consider the inversion with centre $C$ and radius $r$. It fixes $A$ and $B$. The circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $ABC$ passes through the centre $C$, so its image is a line; this line contains the images of $A$ and $B$, i.e. $A$ and $B$ themselves. Hence $\Gamma$ is mapped to the line $AB$.

The image of $E\in\Gamma$ lies on line $AB$ and on ray $CE$, so it is $D$; likewise $F$ is mapped to $G$. In particular $CE\cdot CD=r^2$ and $CF\cdot CG=r^2$.

For an inversion with centre $C$ and radius $r$, the images $X',Y'$ of two points $X,Y$ satisfy $X'Y'=\dfrac{r^2\,XY}{CX\cdot CY}$ (this follows from the similar triangles $CXY$ and $CY'X'$). With $X=E$, $Y=F$: \[DG=\frac{r^2\,EF}{CE\cdot CF}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad CE\cdot DG=\frac{r^2}{CF}\,EF=CG\cdot EF.\] So $CE\cdot DG=k\cdot EF\cdot CG$ with $\boxed{k=1}$.

Problem 6

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 34: Distributions and Stars-and-Bars · Integer-Solution Interpretation

How many ordered quadruples $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ exist such that \[\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_i=26,\] where $x_i$ is an odd natural number for all $1\le i\le 4$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the first few allowed values of an entry and compare the gaps between them. Notice how much of the total is already used when all four entries take their least allowed value.
Hint 2: key idea
Write each odd entry as $2y_i+1$, with $y_i$ a nonnegative integer. This turns the problem into distributing a fixed number of identical units among four labelled positions. (LPS VI, Chapter 34: Distributions and Stars-and-Bars)
Hint 3: approach
Substitute and simplify to find the total of the $y_i$. Represent that total by stars with three bars separating the four entries, and work out the number of positions available for the bars.
Common trap
Empty groups of stars are allowed, because an original entry may be $1$.
Show the answer

$364$

Show solution 1 – Substitution and stars and bars

Write $x_i=2y_i+1$ with integers $y_i\ge 0$ (every odd natural number has this form, and conversely). The equation becomes $2(y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4)+4=26$, i.e. $y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4=11$. The number of solutions in non-negative integers is, by stars and bars, \[\binom{11+3}{3}=\binom{14}{3}=\boxed{364}.\]

Show solution 2 – Split into two pairs

For odd $x_1,x_2$ the sum $x_1+x_2$ is even; for $x_1+x_2=2j$ ($j\ge1$) the choices are $x_1\in\{1,3,\dots,2j-1\}$, so there are exactly $j$ of them. If $x_1+x_2=2j$, then $x_3+x_4=26-2j=2(13-j)$, which has $13-j$ choices; we need $1\le j\le 12$. Therefore the count is \[\sum_{j=1}^{12}j(13-j)=13\cdot\frac{12\cdot13}{2}-\frac{12\cdot 13\cdot 25}{6}=1014-650=\boxed{364}.\]

Problem 7

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Factoring to Solve · also Higher Secondary, Problem 7

Find all positive integers $n$ such that $n=p^k$, where $p$ is a prime number and $k$ is a positive integer, and the equation \[n=x^3-2x^2+2x-1\] holds for some integer $x$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Check the polynomial at a few small values of $x$, including $x=1$. Think about which of its values could qualify as a positive power of a prime.
Hint 2: key idea
Factor out $x-1$ and rewrite the other factor as a multiple of $x-1$ plus a constant. This lets you control the common divisors of the factors. (LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Complete the square in the quadratic factor to see what positivity requires of $x$. Use coprimality and the prime-power condition to decide which factor must be one, then check the resulting possibilities for $x$.
Common trap
The positivity condition matters because $x$ is allowed to be any integer.
Show the answer

$n=3$

Show solution 1 – Factor and use coprimality

Factor: $x^3-2x^2+2x-1=(x-1)(x^2-x+1)$. Since $x^2-x+1=(x-\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac34>0$ and $n>0$, we need $x-1\ge1$, i.e. $x\ge2$.

Any common divisor of $x-1$ and $x^2-x+1=x(x-1)+1$ divides $1$, so the two factors are coprime. Their product is $p^k$, so each factor is a power of $p$, and coprimality forces one of them to be $1$. Now $x^2-x+1=1$ means $x\in\{0,1\}$, excluded. So $x-1=1$, $x=2$, and $n=1\cdot 3=3=3^1$, which is indeed a prime power. The only value is $\boxed{n=3}$.

Show solution 2 – Work modulo $p$

Factor $n=x^3-2x^2+2x-1=(x-1)(x^2-x+1)$. Since $x^2-x+1=(x-\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac34>0$ and $n>0$, also $x-1>0$, i.e. $x\ge2$. Both factors are positive divisors of $p^k$, so $x-1=p^a$ and $x^2-x+1=p^b$ with $a,b\ge0$.

If $a\ge1$, then $x\equiv 1\pmod p$, so $x^2-x+1\equiv 1-1+1=1\pmod p$; then $p\nmid x^2-x+1$, so $b=0$ and $x^2-x+1=1$, which gives $x\in\{0,1\}$, contradicting $x\ge2$. Hence $a=0$, so $x=2$ and $n=3$. Answer: $\boxed{n=3}$.

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions · also Higher Secondary, Problem 8

Let $k$ be a prime number and $m,n$ be non-negative integers. Find all triples $(k,m,n)$ such that \[k^m-n^3-1=26.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Rearrange so that the prime power is alone on one side. Compare the constant on the other side with the term containing $n$.
Hint 2: key idea
Factor the sum of cubes and check that each factor is larger than one. Both must then be powers of the same prime, which you can identify by reducing the quadratic factor modulo the linear factor. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Write the linear factor as a power of the shared prime and express the quadratic factor using that power. For larger exponents, factor out the smallest power and check whether the leftover factor is greater than one and coprime to the prime. Check the small exponents separately.
Common trap
The variable $n$ is allowed to be zero.
Show the answer

$(k,m,n)=(3,3,0)$ and $(3,5,6)$

Show solution 1 – Sum of cubes and a gcd bound

The equation is $k^m=n^3+27=(n+3)(n^2-3n+9)$. Here $n+3\ge3$ and $n^2-3n+9=(n-\tfrac32)^2+\tfrac{27}4>6$, so both factors exceed $1$ and both are powers of the prime $k$ with positive exponent. Hence $k$ divides both, and so $k$ divides \[(n^2-3n+9)-(n+3)(n-6)=27.\] Therefore $k=3$. Write $n+3=3^a$ with $a\ge1$. Then \[n^2-3n+9=(n+3)^2-9(n+3)+27=3^{2a}-3^{a+2}+27.\]

  • $a=1$: $n=0$, and $n^2-3n+9=9$; then $3^m=3\cdot9=27$, $m=3$.

  • $a=2$: $n=6$, and $n^2-3n+9=27$; then $3^m=9\cdot27=243$, $m=5$.

  • $a\ge3$: $3^{2a}-3^{a+2}+27=27\,(3^{2a-3}-3^{a-1}+1)$, and the bracket is $\equiv1\pmod 3$ and greater than $1$ (as $2a-3>a-1$), so it is not a power of $3$. No solutions.

Check: $27-0-1=26$ and $243-216-1=26$. The solutions are $\boxed{(k,m,n)=(3,3,0),\ (3,5,6)}$.

Show solution 2 – Reduce modulo $k$

We have $k^m=n^3+27\ge27$, so $m\ge1$. Since $n+3$ divides $n^3+27=k^m$ and $n+3\ge3$, we get $n+3=k^a$ with $1\le a\le m$, and then $n^2-3n+9=k^{m-a}$.

The number $n^2-3n+9$ is never $1$ (its minimum over integers is $7$), so $m-a\ge1$ and $k\mid n^2-3n+9$. But $n\equiv-3\pmod k$, so $n^2-3n+9\equiv 9+9+9=27\pmod k$. Hence $k\mid 27$, i.e. $k=3$.

Now $n=3^a-3$ and $n^2-3n+9=(n+3)^2-9(n+3)+27=3^{2a}-3^{a+2}+27$ must equal $3^{m-a}$. Compare $3$-adic valuations of the three terms $3^{2a}$, $-3^{a+2}$, $27$: for $a\ge3$ the exponents satisfy $2a>a+2>3$ (as $a\ge3$), so the sum has $3$-adic valuation exactly $3$, i.e. it equals $27$ times a number not divisible by $3$; being a power of $3$, it would have to equal $27$. But $3^{2a}-3^{a+2}=3^{a+2}(3^{a-2}-1)>0$ for $a\ge3$, so the sum exceeds $27$. Hence $a\le2$.

So $a=1$ ($n=0$, $3^m=27$, $m=3$) or $a=2$ ($n=6$, $3^m=243$, $m=5$). The solutions are $\boxed{(3,3,0),\ (3,5,6)}$.

Problem 9

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Basic Congruence Manipulation · also Higher Secondary, Problem 9

Let $\{a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{1013}\}$ and $\{b_1,b_2,\dots,b_{1013}\}$ be two disjoint subsets of $\{1,2,3,\dots,2026\}$ such that $|a_i-b_i|\equiv 1\pmod 5$ for all $1\le i\le 1013$. Let \[S=\sum_{i=1}^{1013}|a_i-b_i|.\]

  • 1. Prove that $S\equiv 3\pmod 5$.

  • 2. Prove that $S\equiv 1\pmod 2$.

  • 3. Prove that, when written in decimal notation, the last digit of $S$ is $3$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a few of the summands and mark which original numbers they use. Use the sizes and disjointness of the two sets to check how many original numbers occur in the full list of pairs.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the stated remainder for the sum modulo $5$. For parity, compare $|a_i-b_i|$ with $a_i+b_i$ by using the fact that a sign change has no effect modulo $2$. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Add the remainders of all the terms modulo $5$. For the parity calculation, replace each difference by the pair's sum and apply the formula for the sum of consecutive integers. Write down the two restrictions any candidate last digit must meet.
Common trap
Reducing modulo $5$ alone generally leaves two possible decimal last digits.
Show solution 1 – Congruences mod $5$ and mod $2$

1. Each term satisfies $|a_i-b_i|\equiv1\pmod5$, so $S\equiv 1013\cdot1=1013\equiv 3\pmod5$.

2. The two sets are disjoint and have $1013+1013=2026$ elements in total, so together they are exactly $\{1,2,\dots,2026\}$. For every integer $t$ we have $|t|\equiv t\equiv -t\pmod 2$, hence $|a_i-b_i|\equiv a_i+b_i\pmod 2$ and \[S\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{1013}(a_i+b_i)=1+2+\dots+2026=\frac{2026\cdot2027}{2}=1013\cdot2027\pmod 2.\] This is a product of two odd numbers, so $S$ is odd.

3. By parts 1 and 2, $S-3$ is divisible by $5$ and is even, hence divisible by $10$. So the last digit of $S$ is $3$.

Show solution 2 – Counting odd differences

1. As each of the $1013$ terms is $\equiv 1\pmod 5$, $S\equiv1013\equiv3\pmod5$.

2. The $2026$ numbers $a_1,\dots,a_{1013},b_1,\dots,b_{1013}$ are distinct elements of $\{1,\dots,2026\}$, so they are all of $1,\dots,2026$, and they are matched in the $1013$ pairs $\{a_i,b_i\}$. The difference $|a_i-b_i|$ is odd exactly when the pair is "mixed" (one odd, one even number). Let $x$ be the number of pairs with two odd numbers and $y$ the number of mixed pairs. Counting the $1013$ odd numbers of $\{1,\dots,2026\}$ gives $2x+y=1013$, so $y$ is odd. Thus $S$ is a sum of $y$ odd terms (an odd number of them) and some even terms, so $S$ is odd.

3. The last digit of $S$ is odd (part 2) and is congruent to $S$ modulo $5$, i.e. to $3$ (part 1). Among the digits $1,3,5,7,9$ only $3$ is $\equiv 3\pmod 5$. So the last digit is $3$.

Problem 10

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Basic Graph Properties · also Higher Secondary, Problem 10

There are $n$ people in a math camp. Each person has exactly $8$ acquaintances in the camp. If two people are acquainted with each other, then exactly $4$ other people are acquainted with both of them. If two people are not acquainted with each other, then exactly $2$ other people are acquainted with both of them. Find all possible values of $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick one camper and separate the others into acquaintances and nonacquaintances of that camper. For someone in either group, distinguish their total acquaintances from those they share with the chosen camper.
Hint 2: key idea
Count acquaintance links crossing between the two groups from both sides. To test whether the resulting size is feasible, use the fact that an entry of the square of the adjacency matrix counts two-step walks. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Equate the two link counts to get a candidate size. Express the squared adjacency matrix using the identity, the adjacency matrix itself and the all-ones matrix, then restrict it to vectors perpendicular to the all-ones vector. Use the trace and the dimension to test whether the resulting eigenvalues could have integer multiplicities.
Common trap
The edge count supplies a necessary size, so existence at that size still needs to be checked.
Show the answer

No value of $n$ is possible: counting forces $n=21$, but a spectral argument shows that no camp with $21$ people exists (the intended answer is presumably $21$)

Show the solution – Double counting, then eigenvalues

Model the camp as a graph: people are vertices, acquaintances are edges. Every vertex has degree $8$; two adjacent vertices have exactly $4$ common neighbours; two non-adjacent vertices have exactly $2$ common neighbours.

Step 1: $n$ must be $21$. Fix a person $v$; let $N$ be the set of the $8$ acquaintances of $v$ and $R$ the set of the $n-9$ other people (not $v$, not in $N$). Count the edges between $N$ and $R$.

  • A person $u\in N$ knows $v$; the common acquaintances of $u$ and $v$ are exactly the acquaintances of $u$ inside $N$, so there are $4$ of them. Hence $u$ has $8-1-4=3$ acquaintances in $R$, giving $8\cdot3=24$ edges.

  • A person $x\in R$ does not know $v$, so $x$ and $v$ have exactly $2$ common acquaintances; these are the acquaintances of $x$ inside $N$. This gives $2(n-9)$ edges.

So $2(n-9)=24$ and $n=21$.

Step 2: no such group of $21$ people exists. Number the people $1,\dots,21$ and let $A$ be the $21\times21$ adjacency matrix ($A_{uv}=1$ if $u,v$ are acquainted, $0$ otherwise, $A_{uu}=0$). The entry $(A^2)_{uv}$ counts the common acquaintances of $u$ and $v$ (and $(A^2)_{uu}=8$). The conditions say exactly \[A^2=8I+4A+2(J-I-A)=6I+2A+2J,\] where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Also $AJ=8J$, i.e. the all-ones vector $\mathbf 1$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $8$.

$A$ is real symmetric, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and we may take $\mathbf1/\sqrt{21}$ as one of them; the other $20$ basis vectors $x$ are orthogonal to $\mathbf 1$, so $Jx=0$. For such an eigenvector $Ax=\theta x$ we get $\theta^2x=(6+2\theta)x$, so $\theta^2-2\theta-6=0$ and $\theta=1\pm\sqrt7$. Let these two values occur $f$ and $g$ times; then $f+g=20$. The trace of $A$ is $0$ (zero diagonal) and equals the sum of all eigenvalues: \[0=8+f(1+\sqrt7)+g(1-\sqrt7)=28+(f-g)\sqrt7.\] Thus $f-g=-28/\sqrt7=-4\sqrt7$, which is not an integer, a contradiction.

Conclusion. Step 1 shows that $n=21$ is the only candidate, and Step 2 shows that even $n=21$ is impossible. Hence no camp satisfies all the conditions, and the set of possible values of $n$ is empty: $\boxed{\text{no value of } n \text{ is possible}}$.

Remark on the statement. The setters most likely intended the answer $n=21$, which is what Step 1 alone gives. But Step 1 only proves “if such a camp exists, then $n=21$”; it does not produce a camp. Step 2 shows that the stated numbers ($8$ acquaintances, $4$ and $2$ common acquaintances) are inconsistent, so the problem as printed has no solution. Changing the data, for example to $6$ acquaintances, $1$ common acquaintance for acquainted pairs and $3$ for the others (the same count then gives $n=1+6+\frac{6\cdot4}{3}=15$), gives a camp that does exist: the $15$ pairs from a $6$-element set, two pairs being acquainted when they are disjoint.
