Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Let $n\ge 3$ be an integer. There are $n$ knights sitting around a round table. On the table there are $n$ candles, such that between each pair of adjacent knights there is a single candle. Some candles (possibly all, but possibly also none) are lit. For $i\in\{0,1,2\}$, let $m_i$ be the number of knights sitting next to exactly $i$ lit candles.

Find the smallest possible value of $m=\max\{m_0,m_1,m_2\}$ in terms of $n$.

Show the answer

$m_{\min}=\lceil n/3\rceil$ if $n\not\equiv 3 \pmod 6$, and $m_{\min}=n/3+1$ if $n\equiv 3\pmod 6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting runs of lit candles)

Number the knights $1,2,\dots,n$ around the table and let candle $i$ be the one between knights $i$ and $i+1$ (indices modulo $n$). Put $x_i=1$ if candle $i$ is lit and $x_i=0$ otherwise. Knight $i$ sits between candles $i-1$ and $i$, so he is next to $x_{i-1}+x_i$ lit candles. Thus $m_1$ is the number of indices $i$ with $x_{i-1}\ne x_i$, $m_2$ the number with $x_{i-1}=x_i=1$ and $m_0$ the number with $x_{i-1}=x_i=0$.

Lower bound. Since $m_0+m_1+m_2=n$, the largest of the three is at least $n/3$, so $m\ge\lceil n/3\rceil$. Walking once around the table, the value $x_i$ changes from $0$ to $1$ and from $1$ to $0$ alternately, and we end where we started; so the two kinds of changes are equally many and $m_1$ is even. Now let $n=6t+3$. If $m\le 2t+1$, then $m_0,m_1,m_2\le 2t+1$ and $m_0+m_1+m_2=6t+3$ force $m_0=m_1=m_2=2t+1$, which is odd, a contradiction. Hence $m\ge 2t+2=n/3+1$ in this case.

Constructions. Let $k\ge 1$ and $p,q\ge 0$ be integers with $2k+p+q=n$. Going around the table, light a block of $p+1$ consecutive candles, leave $q+1$ unlit, and then $k-1$ times light one candle and leave the next one unlit. This uses $(p+1)+(q+1)+2(k-1)=n$ candles and gives $k$ lit blocks and $k$ unlit blocks, alternating. A value change happens exactly at the $2k$ ends of blocks, so $m_1=2k$; a lit block of length $\ell$ contains $\ell-1$ knights between two lit candles, so $m_2=p$; similarly $m_0=q$. Choose

  • $n=6t$ ($t\ge1$): $k=t$, $p=q=2t$, giving $m=2t$;

  • $n=6t+1$ ($t\ge1$): $k=t$, $p=2t+1$, $q=2t$, giving $m=2t+1$;

  • $n=6t+2$ ($t\ge1$): $k=t$, $p=q=2t+1$, giving $m=2t+1$;

  • $n=6t+3$ ($t\ge0$): $k=t+1$, $p=2t+1$, $q=2t$, giving $m=2t+2$;

  • $n=6t+4$ ($t\ge0$): $k=t+1$, $p=q=2t+1$, giving $m=2t+2$;

  • $n=6t+5$ ($t\ge0$): $k=t+1$, $p=2t+2$, $q=2t+1$, giving $m=2t+2$.

(Since $n\ge 3$, the cases $n=1,2$ do not occur.) In every case $m$ equals the lower bound. Therefore \[ m_{\min}=\boxed{\begin{cases}\lceil n/3\rceil, & n\not\equiv 3\pmod 6,\\[2pt] n/3+1, & n\equiv 3\pmod 6.\end{cases}} \]

Solution 2 (Double counting and periodic patterns)

Write each lighting as a cyclic string of $n$ digits ($1$ for a lit candle, $0$ for an unlit one), where each knight sits between two neighbouring digits. Then $m_2$, $m_1$, $m_0$ count the neighbouring pairs of digits equal to $11$, to $10$ or $01$, and to $00$, respectively.

Lower bound. As $m_0+m_1+m_2=n$, we have $m\ge\lceil n/3\rceil$. Let $L$ be the number of lit candles and count pairs (knight, lit candle next to him). Each lit candle has two knights next to it, so the count is $2L$; counting by knights it is $m_1+2m_2$. Hence $m_1=2L-2m_2$ is even. If $n=6t+3$ and $m\le 2t+1$, then all three numbers would equal $2t+1$ (their sum is $6t+3$), so $m_1$ would be odd, which is impossible. So $m\ge 2t+2=n/3+1$ when $n\equiv 3\pmod 6$.

Constructions. The block $111000$ contributes, inside it and at its junction with a following block that starts with $1$, two pairs of each kind. Hence the string $(111000)^t$ (the block repeated $t$ times) has $m_0=m_1=m_2=2t$. Now let $n=6t+r$ and use:

  • $r=0$: $(111000)^t$, with $m=2t$;

  • $r=1$ ($t\ge1$): $1111000\,(111000)^{t-1}$; one more $11$ pair, so $(m_0,m_1,m_2)=(2t,2t,2t+1)$;

  • $r=2$ ($t\ge1$): $11110000\,(111000)^{t-1}$; one more $11$ and one more $00$ pair: $(2t+1,2t,2t+1)$;

  • $r=3$: $(111000)^t\,110$; the new runs $11$ and $0$ add two changes and one $11$ pair: $(2t,2t+2,2t+1)$;

  • $r=4$: $(111000)^t\,1100$: $(2t+1,2t+2,2t+1)$;

  • $r=5$: $(111000)^t\,11000$: $(2t+2,2t+2,2t+1)$.

(For $t=0$ the strings $110$, $1100$, $11000$ cover $n=3,4,5$.) In each case $m=\lceil n/3\rceil$, except for $r=3$ where $m=2t+2=n/3+1$; these match the lower bounds. So the answer is \[ \boxed{\begin{cases}\lceil n/3\rceil, & n\not\equiv 3\pmod 6,\\[2pt] n/3+1, & n\equiv 3\pmod 6.\end{cases}} \]

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Consider the system of equations: \[\begin{cases} x^2=y+1\\ xy=x+y\end{cases}\] Show that if $(x,y)=(x_0,y_0)$, where $x_0$ and $y_0$ are real numbers, is a solution to the above system of equations, then there is also a solution $(x,y)=(x_1,y_1)$, where $x_1$ and $y_1$ are real numbers and $y_1x_0=1$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An explicit partner solution)

First, $x_0\ne 0$: if $x_0=0$, the first equation gives $y_0=-1$, and the second gives $0=x_0y_0=x_0+y_0=-1$, which is false. Also $x_0\ne 1$: if $x_0=1$, then $y_0=0$ and the second equation reads $0=1$.

Substituting $y_0=x_0^2-1$ into $x_0y_0=x_0+y_0$ gives $x_0^3-x_0=x_0^2+x_0-1$, that is, \[x_0^3-x_0^2-2x_0+1=0.\tag{1}\] (One checks that $(1-x)^3f\bigl(\frac1{1-x}\bigr)=-f(x)$ for $f(x)=x^3-x^2-2x+1$, so the map $x\mapsto\frac1{1-x}$ permutes the roots of (1); this suggests the partner below.) Define \[x_1=\frac{1}{1-x_0},\qquad y_1=\frac1{x_0},\] which are real numbers with $y_1x_0=1$. We check both equations.

Second equation. $x_1y_1=\dfrac{1}{x_0(1-x_0)}$ and $x_1+y_1=\dfrac{x_0+(1-x_0)}{x_0(1-x_0)}=\dfrac{1}{x_0(1-x_0)}$, so they are equal.

First equation. \[x_1^2-1=\frac{1-(1-x_0)^2}{(1-x_0)^2}=\frac{x_0(2-x_0)}{(1-x_0)^2}.\] This equals $y_1=\frac1{x_0}$ if and only if $x_0^2(2-x_0)=(1-x_0)^2$, i.e. $-x_0^3+2x_0^2=1-2x_0+x_0^2$, i.e. $x_0^3-x_0^2-2x_0+1=0$, which is (1). Hence $(x_1,y_1)$ is a real solution with $y_1x_0=1$.

Solution 2 (A reciprocal polynomial)

Let $(x,y)$ be any real solution. Then $y\ne 1$, since $y=1$ in the second equation gives $x=x+1$. So the second equation can be solved for $x$: $x(y-1)=y$, i.e. $x=\frac{y}{y-1}$. Substituting into the first equation, $y^2=(y+1)(y-1)^2$, which expands to \[g(y)=y^3-2y^2-y+1=0 .\] Conversely, if $y$ is a real root of $g$, then $y\ne1$ (as $g(1)=-1$), and $x=\frac{y}{y-1}$ satisfies $xy=x+y$ by construction and $x^2=y+1$ because this is equivalent to $g(y)=0$. So the real solutions correspond exactly to the real roots $y$ of $g$.

Eliminating $y$ instead ($y=x^2-1$ in the second equation) shows that the first coordinate of every solution is a root of \[f(x)=x^3-x^2-2x+1 .\] Now $g$ is the reciprocal polynomial of $f$: \[x^3\,g\!\left(\tfrac1x\right)=1-2x-x^2+x^3=f(x).\] Since $f(0)=1\ne 0$, we have $x_0\ne 0$, and $g(1/x_0)=f(x_0)/x_0^3=0$. Thus $y_1=1/x_0$ is a real root of $g$, and with $x_1=\frac{y_1}{y_1-1}$ the pair $(x_1,y_1)$ is a real solution of the system with $y_1x_0=1$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral such that $AB=BC$. The internal angle bisectors of $\angle DBA$ and $\angle CBD$ intersect the perpendicular bisectors of $AD$ and $CD$ in $E$ and $F$, respectively. Prove that the circumcircles of $\triangle DAC$ and $\triangle DEF$ are tangent.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion at $D$ and Ptolemy)

For $E$ and $F$ to be defined we need $BA\ne BD$ (otherwise the bisector of $\angle DBA$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AD$); since $BA=BC$ this also gives $BC\ne BD$. We also use that $D,E,F$ are not collinear, as the circle $(DEF)$ exists.

Step 1: $E$ lies on the circle $(ABD)$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of the arc $AD$ of the circle $(ABD)$ not containing $B$. Equal arcs give $\angle ABM=\angle MBD$, so $M$ is on the bisector of $\angle DBA$; and $MA=MD$, so $M$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$. These two lines are different (if they coincided, $B$ would be on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, i.e. $BA=BD$), so they meet only at $M$, and $E=M$. In the same way $F$ is the midpoint of the arc $CD$ of the circle $(CBD)$ not containing $B$.

Step 2: Ptolemy. The points $A,E,D,B$ lie on a circle in this order, and $EA=ED$. Ptolemy's theorem gives $EA\cdot BD+ED\cdot AB=AD\cdot BE$, that is, \[ED\,(AB+BD)=AD\cdot BE.\tag{1}\] Likewise $FD\,(CB+BD)=CD\cdot BF$.

Step 3: inversion. Invert about $D$ with any radius $\rho$, and write $X^*$ for the image of $X$. Recall $DX^*=\rho^2/DX$ and $X^*Y^*=\rho^2\,XY/(DX\cdot DY)$. A circle through $D$ goes to a line not through $D$. The circle $(ABED)$ goes to a line through $B^*,A^*,E^*$; the arc $BAE$ that avoids $D$ goes to the segment $B^*E^*$, so $A^*$ lies between $B^*$ and $E^*$. Using (1), \[\frac{B^*E^*}{B^*A^*}=\frac{BE/(DB\cdot DE)}{BA/(DB\cdot DA)}=\frac{BE\cdot AD}{ED\cdot AB}=\frac{AB+BD}{AB}.\] In the same way $F^*$ lies on ray $B^*C^*$ and $\dfrac{B^*F^*}{B^*C^*}=\dfrac{CB+BD}{CB}$, which is the same number $k=\dfrac{AB+BD}{AB}>1$ because $AB=CB$. So the homothety with centre $B^*$ and ratio $k$ maps $A^*\mapsto E^*$ and $C^*\mapsto F^*$, and therefore maps the line $A^*C^*$ onto the line $E^*F^*$. Hence these lines are parallel.

Step 4: conclusion. The line $A^*C^*$ is the image of the circle $(DAC)$, and $E^*F^*$ is the image of the circle $(DEF)$. If $B$ is not on the circle $(DAC)$, then $B^*\notin A^*C^*$, so a homothety with ratio $k\ne1$ moves the line: $A^*C^*$ and $E^*F^*$ are distinct parallel lines with no common point. Therefore the circles $(DAC)$ and $(DEF)$ have no common point other than $D$, so they touch at $D$. (If $ABCD$ is cyclic, the same argument shows that the two circles coincide, a degenerate form of tangency.)

Solution 2 (A common foot on $BD$ and isogonal lines)

Put $\angle ABD=2\alpha$ and $\angle DBC=2\beta$. We need $BA\ne BD$ (otherwise the bisector of $\angle DBA$ coincides with the perpendicular bisector of $AD$ and $E$ is not defined); as $BA=BC$, also $BC\ne BD$. Let $O_1$ and $O_2$ be the centres of the circles $(DEF)$ and $(DAC)$. Two circles through $D$ are tangent at $D$ exactly when their centres are collinear with $D$ (then they share the tangent line at $D$, perpendicular to that line). So we prove that $D,O_1,O_2$ are collinear.

Step 1: $EF\perp BD$. Let $K$ and $H$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $E$ to the lines $BA$ and $BD$. Since $E$ lies on the bisector of $\angle ABD$, $EK=EH$, and $K$, $H$ are at the same signed distance $s$ from $B$, measured along the rays $BA$ and $BD$. By Pythagoras, \[EA^2=EK^2+(s-BA)^2,\qquad ED^2=EH^2+(s-BD)^2 .\] From $EA=ED$ we get $|s-BA|=|s-BD|$, and as $BA\ne BD$ this means $s=\frac{BA+BD}2$. The same argument for $F$ (on the bisector of $\angle DBC$, with $FC=FD$) shows that the foot of $F$ on $BD$ is at distance $\frac{BC+BD}2=\frac{BA+BD}2$ from $B$, i.e. it is the same point $H$. Hence $E$ and $F$ both project onto $BD$ at $H$, so $EF\perp BD$ at $H$, and $H\ne D$ because $BH=\frac{BA+BD}{2}\ne BD$. Thus the line $DB$ is the altitude from $D$ in triangle $DEF$.

Step 2: isogonal lines. We use the classical fact that in any triangle the altitude from a vertex and the line from that vertex to the circumcentre are symmetric with respect to the bisector of the angle at that vertex (both make the angle $|90^\circ-\gamma|$ with the two sides, where $\gamma$ is the angle at the opposite end). To compute with it, measure the direction $\theta(\ell)$ of every line $\ell$ as an angle modulo $180^\circ$, counterclockwise. A reflection in a line of direction $\varphi$ sends direction $\theta$ to $2\varphi-\theta$, and for the bisector of the lines $DX$, $DY$ we have $2\varphi\equiv\theta(DX)+\theta(DY)$ (both bisectors give the same value modulo $180^\circ$). Hence \[\theta(DO_1)\equiv\theta(DE)+\theta(DF)-\theta(DB),\qquad \theta(DO_2)\equiv\theta(DA)+\theta(DC)-\theta(h),\] where $h$ is the altitude from $D$ in triangle $DAC$. Since $BA=BC$, the bisector $BM$ of $\angle ABC$ is perpendicular to $AC$, so $h\parallel BM$ and $\theta(h)=\theta(BM)$.

Step 3: counting angles. Choose the orientation so that $\theta(BD)=0^\circ$ and ray $BA$ is obtained from ray $BD$ by a counterclockwise turn of $2\alpha$; then ray $BC$ is obtained by a clockwise turn of $2\beta$ (as $ABCD$ is convex, ray $BD$ lies inside $\angle ABC$), so $\theta(BA)=2\alpha$, $\theta(BC)=-2\beta$, $\theta(BM)=\alpha-\beta$, $\theta(BE)=\alpha$, $\theta(BF)=-\beta$. The points $A,B,D,E$ lie on one circle: $E$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$ and on the bisector of $\angle DBA$, and so is the midpoint of the arc $AD$ of $(ABD)$ not containing $B$; these two lines meet only once, since they are different ($BA\ne BD$). By the inscribed angle theorem for directed angles (on a circle through $A,E,B,D$, the chords $AE$ seen from $D$ and from $B$), \[\theta(DE)-\theta(DA)\equiv\theta(BE)-\theta(BA)=\alpha-2\alpha=-\alpha .\] Likewise $F$ lies on $(CBD)$ and $\theta(DF)-\theta(DC)\equiv\theta(BF)-\theta(BC)=-\beta+2\beta=\beta$. Therefore, modulo $180^\circ$, \[\theta(DO_1)-\theta(DO_2)\equiv\bigl(\theta(DE)-\theta(DA)\bigr)+\bigl(\theta(DF)-\theta(DC)\bigr)-\theta(DB)+\theta(BM)\equiv-\alpha+\beta-0+(\alpha-\beta)=0 .\] So the lines $DO_1$ and $DO_2$ have the same direction and both pass through $D$: they coincide, and $D,O_1,O_2$ are collinear. Hence the circles $(DAC)$ and $(DEF)$ have the same tangent at $D$ and are tangent there. (If $O_1=O_2$, which happens when $ABCD$ is cyclic, the circles coincide.)

Solution 3 (Coordinates centred at $D$)

For $E$ to be defined we need $BA\ne BD$: otherwise $B$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, and this bisector is the bisector of $\angle DBA$. Put $B$ at the origin with the positive $x$-axis along ray $BD$, and choose the unit of length so that $BA=BC=1$. Then $D=(d,0)$ with $d>0$ and $d\ne1$. Since $ABCD$ is convex, ray $BD$ lies inside $\angle ABC$, so $A$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of line $BD$; take $A$ above it. Let $\angle ABD=2\alpha$ and $\angle DBC=2\beta$, where $0<\alpha,\beta<90^\circ$, and put $u=\tan\alpha>0$, $v=\tan\beta>0$. By the half-angle formulas, \[A=\Bigl(\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},\ \frac{2u}{1+u^2}\Bigr),\qquad C=\Bigl(\frac{1-v^2}{1+v^2},\ -\frac{2v}{1+v^2}\Bigr).\]

The points $E$ and $F$. The bisector of $\angle DBA$ consists of the points $(t,tu)$ with $t>0$. For $P=(t,tu)$, \[\begin{aligned}PA^2-PD^2&=\bigl(t^2(1+u^2)-2t(A_x+uA_y)+1\bigr)-\bigl(t^2(1+u^2)-2td+d^2\bigr)\\&=2t(d-1)-(d^2-1),\end{aligned}\] because $A_x+uA_y=\frac{1-u^2+2u^2}{1+u^2}=1$. As $d\ne1$, this vanishes only for $t=\frac{d+1}2$. So $E=(e,eu)$ with $e=\frac{d+1}2$, and in the same way (replace $u$ by $-v$) $F=(e,-ev)$.

New origin at $D$. Use the coordinates $X=x-d$, $Y=y$ and put $g=e-d=\frac{1-d}2$. Then \[e+g=1,\qquad g\ne0,\qquad E=(g,\,eu),\qquad F=(g,\,-ev).\] Also $(1+u^2)X_A=(1-u^2)-d(1+u^2)=2g-2eu^2$ and $(1+u^2)Y_A=2u$, hence, using $1-2ge=g^2+e^2$ (which is $(e+g)^2=1$), \[(1+u^2)^2(X_A^2+Y_A^2)=4\bigl(g^2-2geu^2+e^2u^4+u^2\bigr)=4(1+u^2)(g^2+e^2u^2),\] that is, $X_A^2+Y_A^2=\dfrac{4(g^2+e^2u^2)}{1+u^2}$.

A circle through the new origin $D$ has an equation $X^2+Y^2+LX+MY=0$; its centre is $(-L/2,-M/2)$, so its tangent at $D$ is the line $LX+MY=0$. The points $D,E,F$ are not collinear ($E\ne F$ lie on the line $X=g$, which misses $D$). Substituting $E$ and $F$: \[g^2+e^2u^2+Lg+Meu=0,\qquad g^2+e^2v^2+Lg-Mev=0.\] Subtracting gives $e^2(u^2-v^2)+Me(u+v)=0$, so $M=e(v-u)$, and then $Lg=-(g^2+e^2uv)$. Thus the circle $(DEF)$ has \[L=-\frac{g^2+e^2uv}{g},\qquad M=e(v-u),\] and $L\ne0$ because $g^2+e^2uv>0$.

Key identity. Let $\lambda=\dfrac{euv-g}{2g}$. We claim \[LX_A+MY_A=\lambda\,(X_A^2+Y_A^2).\tag{1}\] Multiply the left side by $(1+u^2)g$: \[\begin{aligned}(1+u^2)g\,(LX_A+MY_A)&=-(g^2+e^2uv)(2g-2eu^2)+2geu(v-u)\\&=-2\bigl(g^3-g^2eu^2+ge^2uv-e^3u^3v-geuv+geu^2\bigr).\end{aligned}\] Since $e+g=1$, we have $ge^2uv-geuv=geuv(e-1)=-g^2euv$ and $geu^2-g^2eu^2=geu^2(1-g)=ge^2u^2$. So the bracket equals \[g^3-g^2euv+ge^2u^2-e^3u^3v=(g^2+e^2u^2)(g-euv),\] and the left side of (1), times $(1+u^2)g$, is $2(euv-g)(g^2+e^2u^2)$. The right side of (1), times $(1+u^2)g$, is $\frac{euv-g}{2}\cdot4(g^2+e^2u^2)$, the same. Dividing by $(1+u^2)g\ne0$ proves (1).

This computation used only algebra, valid for all real $u,v$. Replacing $(u,v)$ by $(-v,-u)$ turns $A$ into $C$ and leaves $L$, $M$, $\lambda$ unchanged (they depend on $u,v$ only through $uv$ and $v-u$). Hence also \[LX_C+MY_C=\lambda\,(X_C^2+Y_C^2).\tag{2}\]

Conclusion. If $\lambda=0$, then (1) and (2) say that $A$ and $C$ lie on the line $LX+MY=0$ through $D$, so $A,C,D$ would be collinear, which is impossible in a convex quadrilateral. So $\lambda\ne0$, and (1), (2) say that $A$ and $C$ lie on the circle \[X^2+Y^2-\frac L\lambda X-\frac M\lambda Y=0,\] which also passes through $D$. As $A,C,D$ are not collinear, this is the circle $(DAC)$. Its tangent at $D$ is $-\frac L\lambda X-\frac M\lambda Y=0$, the same line as the tangent $LX+MY=0$ of $(DEF)$ at $D$. Two circles through $D$ with the same tangent line at $D$ touch each other at $D$, so the circumcircles of $DAC$ and $DEF$ are tangent. (They coincide exactly when $\lambda=-1$, for example for $D=(2,0)$, $A=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$, $C=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$, where $ABCD$ is cyclic; this is the degenerate form of tangency.)

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

A pair of positive integers $(a,b)$ is good if all the fractions \[\frac{a}{b},\ \frac{a+1}{b+1},\ \dots,\ \frac{a+9}{b+9}\] are integers.

  • a) Prove that there are only finitely many good pairs $(a,b)$ with $b<a<b^9$.

  • b) Prove that there are infinitely many good pairs $(a,b)$ with $b<a<b^{10}$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A gcd bound for the lcm)

Reformulation. Since $\frac{a+i}{b+i}=1+\frac{a-b}{b+i}$, the pair $(a,b)$ with $a>b$ is good exactly when each of $b,b+1,\dots,b+9$ divides $a-b$, i.e. when $L(b)=\operatorname{lcm}(b,b+1,\dots,b+9)$ divides $a-b$. Then $a-b\ge L(b)$.

Lemma. For positive integers $x_1,\dots,x_m$, \[\operatorname{lcm}(x_1,\dots,x_m)\ \ge\ \frac{x_1x_2\cdots x_m}{\prod_{i<j}\gcd(x_i,x_j)} .\] Proof. Induction on $m$; $m=1$ is clear. Let $\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(x_1,\dots,x_{m-1})$ and $y=x_m$. Then $\operatorname{lcm}(\ell,y)=\ell y/\gcd(\ell,y)$. For each prime $p$ with exponents $e_i$ in $x_i$ and $f$ in $y$, the exponent of $p$ in $\gcd(\ell,y)$ is $\min(\max_i e_i,f)=\max_i\min(e_i,f)\le\sum_i\min(e_i,f)$, so $\gcd(\ell,y)\le\prod_{i<m}\gcd(x_i,y)$. Combining with the induction hypothesis for $\ell$ proves the lemma. $\square$

(a) For $0\le i<j\le 9$, $\gcd(b+i,b+j)$ divides $j-i$. By the lemma, \[L(b)\ge\frac{b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)}{K}>\frac{b^{10}}{K},\qquad K=\prod_{0\le i<j\le 9}(j-i),\] a fixed constant. If $(a,b)$ is good with $a<b^9$, then $b^9>a>a-b\ge L(b)>b^{10}/K$, so $b<K$. Then $a<b^9<K^9$ as well, so only finitely many pairs are possible.

(b) The lcm of several numbers divides the number obtained from their product by dividing out $\gcd$ of any two of them. Choose $i\in\{0,1\}$ with $b+i$ even; then $b+i$ and $b+i+2$ are both even, so $L(b)$ divides $\frac{1}{2}\,b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)$ and \[L(b)\le\tfrac12(b+9)^{10}=\tfrac12\,b^{10}\Bigl(1+\tfrac9b\Bigr)^{10}.\] Let $b\ge 900$, so $x=\frac9b\le\frac1{100}$. From $(1+x)^{10}(1-x)^{10}=(1-x^2)^{10}\le1$ and Bernoulli's inequality $(1-x)^{10}\ge1-10x$ we get $(1+x)^{10}\le\frac{1}{1-10x}\le\frac{1}{0.9}<1.12$. Hence $L(b)<0.56\,b^{10}$. Put $a=b+L(b)$. Then $a>b$, $a-b=L(b)$ so $(a,b)$ is good, and $a<b+0.56\,b^{10}<b^{10}$. This gives a good pair for every $b\ge 900$, hence infinitely many.

Solution 2 (Valuations and an explicit family)

A pair $(a,b)$ with $a>b$ is good if and only if $b+i\mid a-b$ for $i=0,\dots,9$ (because $\frac{a+i}{b+i}=1+\frac{a-b}{b+i}$), i.e. if and only if $L=\operatorname{lcm}(b,\dots,b+9)$ divides $a-b$.

(a) Lemma: $b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)$ divides $9!\cdot L$. Fix a prime $p$ and let $M=\max_i v_p(b+i)=v_p(L)$. For $e\ge1$ let $N_e$ be the number of $i$ with $p^e\mid b+i$; then $v_p\bigl(\prod_i(b+i)\bigr)=\sum_{e\ge1}N_e$. We have $N_e=0$ for $e>M$. If $N$ of ten consecutive integers are multiples of $p^e$, the largest and smallest of them differ by at least $(N-1)p^e$ and at most $9$, so $N_e\le\lfloor 9/p^e\rfloor+1$. By Legendre's formula $\sum_{e\ge1}\lfloor 9/p^e\rfloor=v_p(9!)$, hence \[v_p\Bigl(\prod_i(b+i)\Bigr)\le\sum_{e=1}^{M}\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\tfrac9{p^e}\Bigr\rfloor+1\Bigr)\le v_p(9!)+v_p(L).\] This holds for every prime, proving the lemma. So for a good pair, $a-b\ge L\ge\frac{b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)}{9!}>\frac{b^{10}}{9!}$. If moreover $a<b^9$, then $b^{10}/9!<b^9$, so $b<9!$ and $a<(9!)^9$: finitely many pairs.

(b) The family $b=2520t$. Let $t\ge1$ and $b=2520t$. Every $i\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ divides $2520=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,9)$, so $q_i=\frac{b+i}{i}=\frac bi+1$ is an integer. Let $N=b\,q_1q_2\cdots q_9$. Then $b\mid N$, and for $i\ge1$, $b\,q_i=\frac bi\,(b+i)$ is a multiple of $b+i$, so $b+i\mid N$. Hence $L\mid N$ and $L\le N$. Now $q_1=b+1$, $q_2=\frac b2+1$, and $q_i\le\frac b3+1\le\frac b2$ for $3\le i\le 9$. Therefore \[L\le N\le b(b+1)\Bigl(\frac{b+2}{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac b2\Bigr)^7=\frac{b^8(b+1)(b+2)}{256}\le\frac{b^{10}}{128},\] using $(b+1)(b+2)\le 2b^2$. Taking $a=b+L$ we get a good pair with $b<a\le b+\frac{b^{10}}{128}<b^{10}$, for every $t\ge1$.

Solution 3 (A partial-fraction identity)

Reformulation. Since $\frac{a+i}{b+i}=1+\frac{a-b}{b+i}$, a pair with $a>b$ is good exactly when each of $b,b+1,\dots,b+9$ divides $a-b$.

An identity. For every positive integer $b$, \[\sum_{i=0}^{9}\frac{(-1)^i\binom9i}{b+i}=\frac{9!}{b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)}.\tag{1}\] To prove it, multiply by $b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)$: the left side becomes $S(b)=\sum_{i=0}^9(-1)^i\binom9i\prod_{j\ne i}(b+j)$, a polynomial in $b$ of degree at most $9$. At $b=-k$ with $k\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}$, every term with $i\ne k$ contains the factor $b+k=0$, so \[S(-k)=(-1)^k\binom9k\prod_{j<k}(j-k)\prod_{j>k}(j-k)=(-1)^k\binom9k\,(-1)^k k!\,(9-k)!=9!.\] So $S(b)-9!$ has degree at most $9$ and ten roots; it is the zero polynomial, and $S(b)=9!$ for all $b$, which is (1).

(a) Let $(a,b)$ be good with $a>b$. Multiplying (1) by $a-b$ gives \[\sum_{i=0}^{9}(-1)^i\binom9i\,\frac{a-b}{b+i}=\frac{9!\,(a-b)}{b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)}.\] Every fraction on the left is an integer, so the right side is an integer; it is positive, so it is at least $1$. Hence \[a-b\ge\frac{b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)}{9!}>\frac{b^{10}}{9!}.\] If also $a<b^9$, then $\frac{b^{10}}{9!}<a-b<b^9$, so $b<9!$, and then $a<b^9<(9!)^9$. Only finitely many pairs remain.

(b) Let $b\ge200$, $P=b(b+1)\cdots(b+9)$ and $a=b+\frac P2$. Among ten consecutive integers five are even, so for each $i$ the product $\frac{P}{b+i}$ of the other nine factors still contains an even factor; hence $\frac{P}{2(b+i)}$ is an integer, i.e. $b+i\mid a-b$ for all $i$, and $(a,b)$ is good with $a>b$. For the upper bound, $P\le(b+9)^{10}$ and $\frac9b<\frac1{20}$, so \[\frac{a-b}{b^{10}}\le\frac12\Bigl(1+\frac1{20}\Bigr)^{10}=\frac12\sum_{j=0}^{10}\binom{10}{j}\frac1{20^j}.\] Using $\binom{10}{j}\le\frac{10^j}{j!}$ and $j!\ge2^{j-1}$ for $j\ge2$, the sum is at most \[1+\frac12+\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{(1/2)^j}{2^{j-1}}=\frac32+2\sum_{j\ge2}\frac1{4^j}=\frac32+\frac16=\frac53.\] So $a-b\le\frac56b^{10}$, and $a\le b+\frac56b^{10}<b^{10}$ because $b<\frac16b^{10}$. Every $b\ge200$ gives a good pair with $b<a<b^{10}$, so there are infinitely many.
