Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $n$ be a strictly positive integer, and let $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ and $(b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n)$ be strictly positive real numbers such that \[a_k(b_1+\dots+b_k)\le k\quad\text{and}\quad b_k(a_1+\dots+a_k)\le k\quad\text{for }k=1,2,\dots,n.\] Prove that \[(a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n)(b_1+b_2+\dots+b_n)\le n^2.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Induction through an exact identity)

Write $A_k=a_1+\dots+a_k$, $B_k=b_1+\dots+b_k$ and $P_k=A_kB_k$. The hypotheses say $a_kB_k\le k$ and $b_kA_k\le k$. We prove $P_k\le k^2$ for $k=1,\dots,n$ by induction; $k=n$ is the claim.

For $k=1$ the hypothesis reads $a_1b_1\le 1$, i.e. $P_1\le1$.

Let $k\ge2$, assume $P_{k-1}\le(k-1)^2$, and suppose for contradiction that $P_k>k^2$. Put $u=a_kB_k\le k$ and $v=b_kA_k\le k$. Since $\frac{a_k}{A_k}=\frac{a_kB_k}{A_kB_k}=\frac{u}{P_k}$, we have $A_{k-1}=A_k-a_k=A_k\bigl(1-\frac u{P_k}\bigr)$, and likewise $B_{k-1}=B_k\bigl(1-\frac v{P_k}\bigr)$. Multiplying, \[P_{k-1}=P_k\Bigl(1-\frac u{P_k}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac v{P_k}\Bigr).\] Because $u,v\le k<k^2<P_k$, both brackets are at least $1-\frac k{P_k}>0$, hence \[P_{k-1}\ge P_k\Bigl(1-\frac k{P_k}\Bigr)^2=P_k-2k+\frac{k^2}{P_k}=g(P_k),\qquad g(x)=x-2k+\frac{k^2}{x}.\] For $x>y\ge k$ we have $g(x)-g(y)=(x-y)\bigl(1-\frac{k^2}{xy}\bigr)>0$, so $g$ is strictly increasing on $[k,\infty)$. As $P_k>k^2\ge k$, \[P_{k-1}\ge g(P_k)>g(k^2)=k^2-2k+1=(k-1)^2,\] contradicting the induction hypothesis. Hence $P_k\le k^2$, and in particular $(a_1+\dots+a_n)(b_1+\dots+b_n)\le n^2$. Equality holds, for example, for $a_k=b_k=1$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Let $a_0$ and $N$ be positive integers. Ben and Lux take turns playing the following game: At the start of the game, the number $N$ is written on a board. At move $i\ge 1$ of the game, the player whose turn it is chooses a positive integer $a_i\le a_{i-1}$, and replaces the number $n$ on the board at that point with $n-a_i$. The first player to write down a number $n\le 0$ loses the game. Ben makes the first move. For each $N\ge 2$, determine the smallest possible value of $a_0$ for which Ben can win the game irrespectively of how Lux plays.

Show the answer

the largest power of $2$ dividing $N-1$, i.e. $a_0=2^{v_2(N-1)}$

Show the solution

Solution (Winning positions via the lowest binary digit)

For a positive integer $x$ let $v_2(x)$ be the exponent of $2$ in $x$, and put $v_2(0)=\infty$ (so $2^{v_2(0)}$ is larger than every number). A position is a pair $(n,m)$: the board shows $n\ge1$ and the player to move may subtract any positive integer $a\le m$.

A player facing $n=1$ must write a number $\le0$ and loses. A player facing $n\ge2$ can always subtract $1$ and stay positive, and subtracting $a\ge n$ loses at once, so the sensible moves are $1\le a\le\min(m,n-1)$.

Claim. The player to move at $(n,m)$ wins if and only if $2^{v_2(n-1)}\le m$.

Proof by strong induction on $n$. For $n=1$ the condition fails ($v_2(0)=\infty$) and indeed the mover loses. Let $n\ge2$ and $v=v_2(n-1)$ (finite), and assume the claim for smaller boards.

  • If $2^v\le m$: the mover subtracts $a=2^v\le n-1$. The new board $n'=n-2^v$ satisfies $n'\ge1$ and $n'-1=(n-1)-2^v$. As $(n-1)/2^v$ is odd, $(n'-1)/2^v$ is even, so $2^{v_2(n'-1)}\ge 2^{v+1}>2^v=a$ (also when $n'=1$). By induction the opponent, at $(n',a)$, loses.

  • If $2^v>m$: every sensible move $a\le m$ has $a<2^v\le n-1$ (so $n'=n-a\ge2$), hence $v_2(a)<v$ and therefore $v_2((n-1)-a)=v_2(a)$, i.e. $2^{v_2(n'-1)}=2^{v_2(a)}\le a$ for $n'=n-a$. By induction the opponent, at $(n',a)$, wins. (A move with $a\ge n$ loses immediately.)

This proves the claim. $\square$

At the start Ben faces $(N,a_0)$, so he can force a win exactly when $a_0\ge 2^{v_2(N-1)}$. The smallest such $a_0$ is \[a_0=\boxed{2^{v_2(N-1)}},\] the largest power of $2$ dividing $N-1$ (for instance $1$ when $N$ is even, $2$ when $N\equiv3\pmod4$). Ben's winning strategy is: always subtract the largest power of $2$ dividing (current number $-1$).

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios

Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram such that $|AC|=|BC|$, and let $A'$ be the reflection of $A$ in $B$. Lines $BD$ and $A'D$ meet the circumcircle of triangle $ACD$ again at $E$ and $F$, respectively. Lines $AE$ and $A'C$ intersect at $G$. Show that the lines $AC$, $EF$, $BG$ are concurrent or pairwise parallel.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Since $CA=CB$, the point $C$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$. Choose axes and unit so that \[A=(-1,0),\quad B=(1,0),\quad C=(0,h)\ (h>0);\qquad\text{then}\quad D=A+C-B=(-2,h),\quad A'=2B-A=(3,0).\] The circle. The circle through $A,C,D$ is $\omega:\ x^2+y^2+2x-\frac{h^2+1}{h}\,y+1=0$ (substitute the three points to check).

The point $E$. Points of line $DB$ are $D+t(B-D)=(-2+3t,\ h-ht)$. Substituting into $\omega$, everything simplifies to $(h^2+9)t^2-(h^2+5)t=0$. The root $t=0$ is $D$, so $t=\frac{h^2+5}{h^2+9}$ gives \[E=\Bigl(\frac{h^2-3}{h^2+9},\ \frac{4h}{h^2+9}\Bigr).\] The point $F$. Points of line $DA'$ are $(-2+5t,\ h-ht)$; substituting gives $(h^2+25)t^2-(h^2+9)t=0$, so $t=\frac{h^2+9}{h^2+25}$ and \[F=\Bigl(\frac{3h^2-5}{h^2+25},\ \frac{16h}{h^2+25}\Bigr).\] The point $G$. $E-A=\frac{2}{h^2+9}(h^2+3,\,2h)$, so line $AE$ is $(-1+s(h^2+3),\ 2sh)$. Line $A'C$ is $\frac x3+\frac yh=1$; substituting, $-1+s(h^2+3)+6s=3$, so $s=\frac4{h^2+9}$ and \[G=\Bigl(\frac{3(h^2+1)}{h^2+9},\ \frac{8h}{h^2+9}\Bigr).\]

Case $h^2\ne 7$. Let $P=\Bigl(\frac{h^2+1}{7-h^2},\ \frac{8h}{7-h^2}\Bigr)$. We show $P$ lies on all three lines.

  • $P-A=\frac{8}{7-h^2}(1,\,h)$, parallel to $C-A=(1,h)$: $P\in AC$.

  • $P-B=\frac{2}{7-h^2}(h^2-3,\,4h)$ and $G-B=\frac{2}{h^2+9}(h^2-3,\,4h)$: $P\in BG$.

  • A short computation gives \[F-E=\frac{2\,(h^4+15,\ 6h^3+22h)}{(h^2+9)(h^2+25)},\qquad P-E=\frac{2\,(h^4+15,\ 6h^3+22h)}{(h^2+9)(7-h^2)},\] which are parallel: $P\in EF$.

So $AC$, $EF$, $BG$ are concurrent at $P$.

Case $h^2=7$. The directions are $C-A=(1,h)$, $G-B\parallel(h^2-3,4h)=4(1,h)$ and $F-E\parallel(h^4+15,6h^3+22h)=(64,64h)=64(1,h)$. So the three lines are pairwise parallel. This completes the proof.

Solution 2 (Pascal and Ceva)

We work in the projective plane: parallel lines meet at a point at infinity, so we must show that $AC$, $EF$, $BG$ pass through one (possibly infinite) point. Let $\omega$ be the circle $(ACD)$.

Step 1: $AB$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. In the parallelogram, $AD=BC=AC$, so $A$ is the midpoint of the arc $CD$ and the tangent at $A$ is parallel to $CD$, hence to $AB$. As it passes through $A$, the tangent at $A$ is the line $AB$, which also contains $A'$. In particular $E\ne A$ (the tangent meets $\omega$ only at $A$), so the line $AE$ is not $AB$.

Step 2: Pascal. Apply Pascal's theorem to the hexagon $AACDFE$ inscribed in $\omega$, where the side $AA$ means the tangent at $A$. Its three pairs of opposite sides meet at \[AA\cap DF=A',\qquad AC\cap FE=:P,\qquad CD\cap EA=:Q,\] because $A'$ lies on the tangent $AB$ and on line $DF$ (by the definition of $F$). Pascal's theorem says that $A',P,Q$ are collinear.

Step 3: Ceva in triangle $AA'G$. The points $A,A',G$ form a triangle ($G$ is on line $AE\ne AB$ and $G\ne A$). On its sides lie $B$ (on $AA'$, the midpoint), $C$ (on $A'G$, by the definition of $G$) and $Q$ (on $GA$). Write $C=tA'+(1-t)G$ with a real $t$. Since $CD\parallel AB$, the line $CQ$ is parallel to $AA'$, so by the intercept theorem $Q=tA+(1-t)G$ with the same $t$. We use homogeneous barycentric coordinates with respect to triangle $AA'G$: $A=(1:0:0)$, $A'=(0:1:0)$, $G=(0:0:1)$, $B=(1:1:0)$, $C=(0:t:1-t)$, $Q=(t:0:1-t)$. The points $(\lambda:t:1-t)$ form the line $AC$ and the points $(t:\mu:1-t)$ form the line $A'Q$, so both contain $X=(t:t:1-t)$; and $X$ lies on line $GB$, the points whose first two coordinates are equal. (Not all of $t,t,1-t$ vanish; if $t+t+(1-t)=0$, $X$ is a point at infinity.) So the lines $AC$, $GB$, $A'Q$ pass through one point $X$, possibly at infinity.

Step 4: conclusion. The lines $AC$ and $A'Q$ are different (the line $A'Q$ contains $A'$, which is not on $AC$). By Step 2, $P$ lies on both $AC$ and $A'Q$, so $P=X$. Hence $P=AC\cap EF$ lies on $BG$: the lines $AC$, $EF$, $BG$ are concurrent, or (when $X$ is at infinity) pairwise parallel.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Find all pairs $(a,b)$ of positive integers for which there exists a positive integer $c$ such that $a^2+b^2+c^2$ divides $(a+b+c)^2$.

Show the answer

exactly the pairs for which $ab$ is a perfect square, i.e. $(a,b)=(dx^2,dy^2)$ with $d,x,y$ positive integers

Show the solution

Solution (The quotient is 2 or 3)

Let $S=a^2+b^2+c^2$ and $T=a+b+c$, and suppose $S\mid T^2$, say $T^2=kS$ with $k$ a positive integer.

Bounding $k$. $T^2=S+2(ab+bc+ca)>S$, so $k\ge2$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz (or QM–AM) inequality $T^2\le 3S$, with equality only for $a=b=c$. So $k\in\{2,3\}$.

$k=3$. Then $a=b=c$, and $ab=a^2$ is a perfect square.

$k=2$. The identity \[2S-T^2=a^2+b^2+c^2-2ab-2bc-2ca=(a+b-c)^2-4ab\] shows that $T^2=2S$ means $4ab=(a+b-c)^2$. Then $4ab$ is a perfect square, so $ab$ is a perfect square ($\sqrt{ab}=\frac{|a+b-c|}{2}$ is rational, and a rational square root of an integer is an integer).

So in every case $ab$ is a perfect square.

Conversely, let $ab=s^2$ with $s$ a positive integer and put $c=a+b+2s$. Then $(a+b-c)^2=4s^2=4ab$, so by the identity $T^2=2S$, and $S\mid T^2$.

Hence the answer is \[\boxed{\text{all pairs }(a,b)\text{ with }ab\text{ a perfect square, i.e. }(a,b)=(dx^2,dy^2),\ d,x,y\in\mathbb Z_{>0}}.\] (The two descriptions agree: if $ab$ is a square, let $d=\gcd(a,b)$; then $a/d$ and $b/d$ are coprime with square product, so both are squares. For example $c=d(x+y)^2$ works for $(dx^2,dy^2)$.)
