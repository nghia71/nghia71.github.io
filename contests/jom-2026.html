Problem 1

HNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Strategic State Tracking

Let $p$ be a prime number. Tintinn and Tantann play a game. Two numbers $a,b$ are written on a blackboard, with $a=b=1$ in the beginning. With Tintinn going first, they take turns. In each turn, the player picks a number $a$ or $b$ and multiplies it by $5$. If $a+b$ is divisible by $p$ after one of a player's turns, then they win and the game ends. If the game continues forever, no one wins.

  • (a) Find all $p$ such that Tintinn can guarantee a win.

  • (b) Find all $p$ such that Tantann can guarantee a win.

Show the answer

(a) $p\in\{2,3\}$; (b) $p\in\{5,13\}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Walking on the cycle of powers of 5)

At every moment $a=5^x$ and $b=5^y$ for some non-negative integers $x,y$, and after $t$ turns $x+y=t$.

Small primes. If $p=2$, then $a+b$ is always even, so Tintinn wins with his first move. If $p=3$, Tintinn's first move gives $a+b=6$, so again Tintinn wins. If $p=5$, then $5\mid 5^x+5^y$ exactly when $x\ge1$ and $y\ge1$. After Tintinn's first move one exponent is $1$ and the other is $0$ (sum $6$); Tantann then multiplies the other number by $5$ and gets $5+5=10$. So Tantann wins.

From now on $p\ne2,3,5$. Then $5$ is invertible modulo $p$, and \[p\mid 5^x+5^y\iff 5^{x-y}\equiv-1\pmod p .\] Let $d$ be the order of $5$ modulo $p$ (the least $d\ge1$ with $5^d\equiv1$). Then $5^u\equiv5^v$ exactly when $u\equiv v\pmod d$, and $d\ge2$ because $5\not\equiv1\pmod p$.

  • If $d$ is odd, $-1$ is not a power of $5$: from $5^u\equiv-1$ we would get $5^{2u}\equiv1$, so $d\mid 2u$, so $d\mid u$ and $-1\equiv5^u\equiv1$, impossible for $p\ne2$. Then nobody ever wins.

  • If $d$ is even, $\bigl(5^{d/2}\bigr)^2\equiv1$ and $5^{d/2}\not\equiv1$, so $5^{d/2}\equiv-1$ (as $p$ is prime). Hence $p\mid a+b$ exactly when $x-y\equiv d/2\pmod d$.

In the second case put $s=x-y \bmod d$. Think of the residues $0,1,\dots,d-1$ as $d$ points on a circle; $s$ starts at $0$, every turn moves $s$ one step clockwise or anticlockwise (multiplying $a$ or $b$ by $5$), and the player who moves $s$ onto the target point $d/2$ wins. The start is at distance $d/2$ from the target (the largest possible distance), and a move changes the distance by exactly $1$, except that from distance $d/2$ both moves lead to distance $d/2-1$. A player who moves to distance $1$ loses at once, since the opponent then steps onto the target.

  • $d=2$: the first move reaches the target, so Tintinn wins. But $d=2$ means $p\mid 5^2-1=24$, i.e. $p\in\{2,3\}$, already treated.

  • $d=4$: Tintinn must move from distance $2$ to distance $1$, and Tantann wins. Here $p\mid 5^4-1=624=2^4\cdot3\cdot13$ and $p\nmid 24$, so $p=13$ (indeed $5^2=25\equiv-1\pmod{13}$; Tantann wins with $a=25$, $b=1$).

  • $d\ge6$: the target is at distance $d/2\ge3$ from the start. Each player can make sure never to move to distance $1$: from distance $r$ with $2\le r<d/2$ he moves to $r+1$; from distance $d/2$ he moves to $d/2-1\ge2$; and if the opponent ever moves to distance $1$, he simply wins. With this strategy a player never lets the opponent win, so neither player can guarantee a win.

Answer. (a) Tintinn can guarantee a win exactly for $\boxed{p=2,3}$. (b) Tantann can guarantee a win exactly for $\boxed{p=5,13}$. For all other primes nobody can force a win.

Solution 2 (Two blocking strategies)

At every moment $a=5^x$ and $b=5^y$ for non-negative integers $x,y$, and each turn raises $x$ or $y$ by $1$.

Small primes. Tintinn's first move gives the numbers $1,5$ with sum $6$. So for $p=2$ and $p=3$ Tintinn wins at once. For $p=5$ and $p=13$ the sum $6$ is not divisible by $p$, and Tantann wins on her first move: for $p=5$ she multiplies the $1$ and gets $5+5=10$; for $p=13$ she multiplies the $5$ and gets $1+25=26$. Hence the answers include $p=2,3$ in (a) and $p=5,13$ in (b), and for these four primes the other player cannot guarantee a win.

Every other prime: nobody can force a win. Let $p\notin\{2,3,5,13\}$. Then $p$ divides none of the numbers $6\cdot5^k$, $2\cdot5^k$, $26\cdot5^k$ (their prime factors are $2,3,5,13$). We show that each player can stop the other from ever winning.

  • Tantann stops Tintinn. She makes sure that at the start of each of Tintinn's turns the exponents are equal, $x=y=k$; this holds at the start. Tintinn then makes them $k$ and $k+1$, with sum $5^k+5^{k+1}=6\cdot5^k$, so he does not win. Tantann multiplies the smaller number by $5$: the exponents become $k+1,k+1$, with sum $2\cdot5^{k+1}$, and the condition holds again. So Tintinn never wins.

  • Tintinn stops Tantann. He makes sure that after each of his turns the exponents differ by exactly $1$, say they are $k$ and $k+1$; this holds after his first move. Tantann then makes them $k+1,k+1$ (sum $2\cdot5^{k+1}$) or $k,k+2$ (sum $5^k+5^{k+2}=26\cdot5^k$), so she does not win. Tintinn answers by raising one exponent of an equal pair, or the smaller exponent of the pair $k,k+2$; in both cases the exponents again differ by $1$. So Tantann never wins.

Therefore Tintinn can guarantee a win exactly for $\boxed{p=2,3}$, and Tantann exactly for $\boxed{p=5,13}$.

Problem 2

SGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 36: Power of a Point and Circle Measurement · Intersecting Chords

Suppose acute $\triangle ABC$ has circumcircle $\Gamma$. Points $B'$ and $C'$ lie on minor arc $\widehat{BC}$ of $\Gamma$ such that $BB'\parallel AC$ and $CC'\parallel AB$. Let $E$ and $F$ be the feet of altitudes from $B$ to $AC$ and $C$ to $AB$. Suppose line $EF$ intersects $AC'$ at $X$ and $AB'$ at $Y$.

Prove that the line passing through the two intersections of the circumcircles of $\triangle AEX$ and $\triangle AFY$ also passes through the midpoint of $BC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion at A)

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and let $A'$ be the point for which $ABA'C$ is a parallelogram. Since $CA'\parallel AB$ and $BA'\parallel AC$, the point $A'$ lies on line $CC'$ and on line $BB'$; these two lines are parallel to $AB$ and $AC$, so they are not parallel and meet only at $A'$. The diagonals of the parallelogram bisect each other, so line $AA'$ passes through $M$.

Since $\angle BEC=\angle BFC=90^\circ$, the points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$; as the triangle is acute, $E$ and $F$ lie inside the segments $AC$ and $AB$, and the power of $A$ gives \[k=AE\cdot AC=AF\cdot AB>0 .\] Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\sqrt k$. It swaps $E\leftrightarrow C$ and $F\leftrightarrow B$. The line $EF$ does not pass through $A$, so $\Phi$ maps it to the circle through $A$, $C$, $B$, namely $\Gamma$.

The point $X$ lies on line $EF$ and on line $AC'$, so $\Phi(X)$ lies on $\Gamma$ and on line $AC'$ and is different from $A$; hence $\Phi(X)=C'$. In the same way $\Phi(Y)=B'$.

The circle $(AEX)$ passes through the centre $A$, so $\Phi$ maps it (without $A$) onto the line through $\Phi(E)=C$ and $\Phi(X)=C'$, i.e. line $CC'$. Likewise $(AFY)$ goes to line $BB'$. A common point $Z\ne A$ of the two circles goes to a common point of the two lines, and conversely; the lines have exactly one common point $A'$, so the circles meet at $A$ and at exactly one more point $Z=\Phi(A')$, which lies on ray $AA'$.

So the line through the two intersection points is line $AZ=AA'$, and it passes through the midpoint $M$ of $BC$.

Solution 2 (Power of a point and similar triangles)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$, and let $A'$ be the point with $ABA'C$ a parallelogram; then $A'$ lies on lines $CC'$ (as $CA'\parallel AB$) and $BB'$ (as $BA'\parallel AC$), and line $AA'$ passes through $M$.

Step 1: $E,C,C',X$ are concyclic, and so are $F,B,B',Y$. The points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, and $A,B,C,C'$ lie on $\Gamma$. Hence \[\measuredangle(EX,EC)=\measuredangle(EF,EC)=\measuredangle(BF,BC)=\measuredangle(BA,BC)=\measuredangle(C'A,C'C)=\measuredangle(C'X,C'C),\] so $E,C',X,C$ are concyclic. In the same way $\measuredangle(FY,FB)=\measuredangle(CE,CB)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\measuredangle(B'A,B'B)=\measuredangle(B'Y,B'B)$, so $F,B',Y,B$ are concyclic.

Step 2: one constant. As the triangle is acute, $E$ and $F$ lie inside the sides $AC$, $AB$, and $B,C,E,F$ are concyclic, so $k=AE\cdot AC=AF\cdot AB>0$. The power of $A$ with respect to the circles of Step 1 gives $AX\cdot AC'=AE\cdot AC=k$ and $AY\cdot AB'=AF\cdot AB=k$ (with $X$ on ray $AC'$ and $Y$ on ray $AB'$, as the products are positive). Let $Z$ be the point of ray $AA'$ with $AZ\cdot AA'=k$.

Step 3: $Z$ lies on both circles. From $AX\cdot AC'=AE\cdot AC$ we get $AX:AC=AE:AC'$, and the angle at $A$ is common, so triangles $AXE$ and $ACC'$ are similar, with opposite orientation ($X\leftrightarrow C$, $E\leftrightarrow C'$). Likewise $AZ\cdot AA'=AE\cdot AC$ makes triangles $AZE$ and $ACA'$ oppositely similar ($Z\leftrightarrow C$, $E\leftrightarrow A'$). Opposite similarity reverses directed angles, and lines $CC'$ and $CA'$ coincide, so \[\measuredangle(XA,XE)=-\measuredangle(CA,CC')=-\measuredangle(CA,CA')=\measuredangle(ZA,ZE).\] Hence $A,E,X,Z$ are concyclic. The same argument with $AY\cdot AB'=AZ\cdot AA'=AF\cdot AB$ gives $\measuredangle(YA,YF)=-\measuredangle(BA,BB')=-\measuredangle(BA,BA')=\measuredangle(ZA,ZF)$, so $A,F,Y,Z$ are concyclic.

Conclusion. The two circles are different (the circle through $A,E,F$ meets line $EF$ only at $E$ and $F$, while $X$ is neither), and $Z\ne A$ is on both. So their two common points are $A$ and $Z$, and the line through them is line $AA'$, which passes through the midpoint $M$ of $BC$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Series and Summation Patterns

Prove that for any set of distinct positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$, \[\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\frac{a_ia_j}{(a_i^2-a_j^2)^2}<\frac n2 .\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (AM-GM and counting by gaps)

For $n=1$ the sum is empty and $0<\tfrac12$. Let $n\ge2$ and order the numbers so that $a_1<a_2<\dots<a_n$. For $i<j$, \[(a_i^2-a_j^2)^2=(a_j-a_i)^2(a_i+a_j)^2>(a_j-a_i)^2\cdot4a_ia_j,\] because $(a_i+a_j)^2-4a_ia_j=(a_j-a_i)^2>0$. So each term is less than $\dfrac{1}{4(a_j-a_i)^2}$. Since the $a$'s are increasing integers, $a_j-a_i\ge j-i$, hence \[\sum_{i<j}\frac{a_ia_j}{(a_i^2-a_j^2)^2}<\frac14\sum_{i<j}\frac1{(j-i)^2}=\frac14\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-d}{d^2}<\frac n4\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\frac1{d^2}.\] Finally \[\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\frac1{d^2}\le1+\sum_{d=2}^{n-1}\frac1{d(d-1)}=1+\sum_{d=2}^{n-1}\Bigl(\frac1{d-1}-\frac1d\Bigr)=2-\frac1{n-1}<2,\] so the sum is less than $\dfrac n4\cdot2=\dfrac n2$.

Solution 2 (A difference of squares and distinct gaps)

For $n=1$ the sum is empty. For distinct positive $x,y$ we have the identity \[\frac{1}{(x-y)^2}-\frac1{(x+y)^2}=\frac{(x+y)^2-(x-y)^2}{(x^2-y^2)^2}=\frac{4xy}{(x^2-y^2)^2},\] so \[\frac{a_ia_j}{(a_i^2-a_j^2)^2}=\frac14\Bigl(\frac1{(a_i-a_j)^2}-\frac1{(a_i+a_j)^2}\Bigr)<\frac1{4(a_i-a_j)^2}.\] Order the numbers so that $a_1<\dots<a_n$ and fix $i$. The differences $a_j-a_i$ for $j>i$ are distinct positive integers, so \[\sum_{j>i}\frac1{(a_j-a_i)^2}\le\sum_{d=1}^{n-i}\frac1{d^2}<2,\] using $\frac1{d^2}<\frac1{d-1}-\frac1d$ for $d\ge2$ (the sum telescopes to less than $1+1$). Summing over $i=1,\dots,n$, \[\sum_{i<j}\frac{a_ia_j}{(a_i^2-a_j^2)^2}<\frac14\sum_{i=1}^{n}2=\frac n2 .\]

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 26: Double Counting and Combinatorial Proof · Average from Total Contribution

Find all infinite sequences of integers $1=a_1\le a_2\le\cdots$ satisfying $a_k\le 1+a_1+\cdots+a_{k-1}$ for all $k$, such that there exists a sequence of non-negative integers $b_1,b_2,\dots$ that satisfies \[\max\{b_i,b_{i+1},\dots,b_{i+a_k-1}\}\ge k-1\] for any $i\ge 0$, and $b_1+b_2+\cdots+b_k<k$ for all $k$.

Show the answer

only $a_k=2^{k-1}$ for all $k$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting large terms)

We read the window condition for every $k\ge1$ and every $i\ge1$ (the sequence $b$ starts at $b_1$). The answer is $\boxed{a_k=2^{k-1}\text{ for all }k}$.

Step 1: $a_k\le2^{k-1}$. By strong induction: $a_1=1$, and if $a_j\le2^{j-1}$ for $j<k$, then $a_k\le1+1+2+\dots+2^{k-2}=2^{k-1}$.

Step 2: $a_k=2^{k-1}$ works. It is non-decreasing, starts with $1$, and $1+1+2+\dots+2^{k-2}=2^{k-1}$, so $a_k\le1+a_1+\dots+a_{k-1}$ holds with equality. Let $b_j$ be the exponent of $2$ in $j$. Any $2^{k-1}$ consecutive positive integers contain a multiple of $2^{k-1}$, whose $b$-value is at least $k-1$; this is the window condition. And by Legendre's formula \[b_1+\dots+b_m=\sum_{t\ge1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac m{2^t}\Bigr\rfloor\le\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac m{2^t}=m\bigl(1-2^{-T}\bigr)<m,\] where $2^T\le m<2^{T+1}$.

Step 3: nothing else works. Suppose $b$ satisfies the conditions but $a_m<2^{m-1}$ for some $m$ (then $m\ge2$). Put $\delta=\frac1{a_m}-\frac1{2^{m-1}}>0$ and choose $K\ge m$ with $2^{-(K-1)}<\delta/2$. By Step 1, \[S=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\frac1{a_k}\ge\sum_{k=2}^{K}\frac1{2^{k-1}}+\delta=1-2^{-(K-1)}+\delta>1+\frac\delta2 .\] Take a large integer $N$ and fix $k\in\{2,\dots,K\}$. Cut $b_1,\dots,b_N$ into $\lfloor N/a_k\rfloor$ disjoint blocks of $a_k$ consecutive terms starting at $b_1$ (and a leftover piece). Each block contains a term $\ge k-1$, so \[c_k=\#\{j\le N:\ b_j\ge k-1\}\ge\Bigl\lfloor\frac N{a_k}\Bigr\rfloor>\frac N{a_k}-1 .\] Now count: for each $j$, the number of $k\in\{2,\dots,K\}$ with $b_j\ge k-1$ is $\min(b_j,K-1)\le b_j$. Adding over $j\le N$, \[b_1+\dots+b_N\ge\sum_{k=2}^{K}c_k>N\cdot S-(K-1)>N\Bigl(1+\frac\delta2\Bigr)-K .\] For $N>2K/\delta$ the right side is larger than $N$, contradicting $b_1+\dots+b_N<N$. Hence $a_k=2^{k-1}$ for every $k$.

Problem 5

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Spiral Similarity Centers

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with orthocenter $H$ and circumcenter $O$. Let $M$ and $N$ denote the midpoints of $BC$ and $AH$ respectively. Lines $MN$ and $AC$ meet at $L$. Suppose $M_c$ and $M_b$ are the midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$.

Prove that there exist a point $P$ such that $OP\parallel BC$ and $\triangle PM_cB\sim\triangle PM_bL$ in that order.

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates, power of a point and two circles)

Let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$. Take coordinates with $D=(0,0)$, $B=(-x,0)$, $C=(y,0)$, $A=(0,h)$. As the triangle is acute, $x,y,h>0$; as $\angle A$ is acute, $\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}=h^2-xy>0$; as it is scalene, $AB\ne AC$, i.e. $x\ne y$.

The points. The circumcentre is $O=\bigl(\tfrac{y-x}2,\tfrac{h^2-xy}{2h}\bigr)$ (it is on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, and $OA^2-OB^2=0$ is checked directly). The orthocentre is $H=\bigl(0,\tfrac{xy}h\bigr)$ (it is on $AD$, and $\overrightarrow{BH}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}=xy-xy=0$). So $N=\bigl(0,\tfrac{h^2+xy}{2h}\bigr)$, $M=\bigl(\tfrac{y-x}2,0\bigr)$, $M_c=\bigl(-\tfrac x2,\tfrac h2\bigr)$, $M_b=\bigl(\tfrac y2,\tfrac h2\bigr)$.

Define $P=\bigl(0,\tfrac{h^2-xy}{2h}\bigr)$, the foot of the perpendicular from $O$ to $AD$. Then $OP\parallel BC$ (equal $y$-coordinates, and $P\ne O$ since $x\ne y$), and $0<\tfrac{h^2-xy}{2h}<h$, so $P$ lies strictly between $A$ and $D$. We show $\triangle PM_cB\sim\triangle PM_bL$.

The point $L$. We have $\overrightarrow{MN}=\bigl(\tfrac{x-y}2,\tfrac{h^2+xy}{2h}\bigr)=\overrightarrow{OA}$, so $MN\parallel AO$. Write $L=C+\lambda(A-C)$. Then $L-M=\bigl(\tfrac{x+y}2-\lambda y,\ \lambda h\bigr)$ is parallel to $\bigl(\tfrac{x-y}2,\tfrac{h^2+xy}{2h}\bigr)$, which gives \[\Bigl(\tfrac{x+y}2-\lambda y\Bigr)\frac{h^2+xy}{2h}=\lambda h\,\frac{x-y}2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda=\frac{(x+y)(h^2+xy)}{2x(h^2+y^2)}>0 .\] Since $CA^2=h^2+y^2$, the point $L$ lies on ray $CA$ with $CL\cdot CA=\lambda\,CA^2=\dfrac{(x+y)(h^2+xy)}{2x}$. Moreover $L\ne A$ and $L\ne M_b$, because \[(x+y)(h^2+xy)-2x(h^2+y^2)=(y-x)(h^2-xy)\ne0,\qquad (x+y)(h^2+xy)-x(h^2+y^2)=y(h^2+x^2)>0 .\]

Step 1: $A,B,L,P$ are concyclic. Let $\omega$ be the circle through $A,B,P$ (they are not collinear), and let it meet line $BC$ again at $B_2$. The point $D$ lies on line $AP$ outside the segment $AP$, so its power is $DA\cdot DP=\tfrac{h^2-xy}2>0$; thus $B_2$ is on ray $DB$ with $DB_2=\tfrac{h^2-xy}{2x}$, and $CB_2=y+\tfrac{h^2-xy}{2x}=\tfrac{h^2+xy}{2x}$. Hence the power of $C$ with respect to $\omega$ is \[CB\cdot CB_2=\frac{(x+y)(h^2+xy)}{2x}>0 .\] Line $CA$ meets $\omega$ at $A$ and at a second point $L'$ on ray $CA$ with $CL'\cdot CA$ equal to this power, i.e. $CL'=CL$. So $L'=L$ and $L\in\omega$.

Step 2: $A,M_c,M_b,P$ are concyclic. $\angle APO=90^\circ$ (as $OP\parallel BC\perp AD$), and $\angle AM_cO=\angle AM_bO=90^\circ$ ($O$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $AC$). So all four points lie on the circle with diameter $AO$.

Step 3: the similarity. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ modulo $180^\circ$. Since $M_c,A,B$ are collinear, $M_b,A,L$ are collinear, and by Steps 1 and 2, \[\measuredangle(M_cP,M_cB)=\measuredangle(M_cP,M_cA)=\measuredangle(M_bP,M_bA)=\measuredangle(M_bP,M_bL),\] \[\measuredangle(BP,BM_c)=\measuredangle(BP,BA)=\measuredangle(LP,LA)=\measuredangle(LP,LM_b).\] Both triangles are non-degenerate ($P$ is on $AD$ and $P\ne A$, so $P$ is on neither $AB$ nor $AC$; $L\ne M_b$). Two pairs of equal directed angles give $\triangle PM_cB\sim\triangle PM_bL$, with the vertices in this order. Together with $OP\parallel BC$, this proves the claim.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Partial Totals and Cyclic Sums

Find, with proof, all sequences $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{2026}$ of positive real numbers such that \[\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}\ge\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}{2}\] for each positive integer $i$. Note that $a_{2026+n}=a_n$.

Show the answer

the constant sequences $a_1=a_2=\dots=a_{2026}=c$ ($c>0$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sum of the inequalities)

All indices are taken modulo $2026$. By AM-GM, $\frac{a_i+a_{i+5}}2\ge\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}$, so the condition gives \[\frac{a_i+a_{i+5}}2-\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}2=\Bigl(\frac{a_i+a_{i+5}}2-\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}\Bigr)+\Bigl(\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}-\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}2\Bigr)\ge0 .\] Add this over $i=1,\dots,2026$. Each of $\sum a_i$, $\sum a_{i+5}$, $\sum a_{i+1}$, $\sum a_{i+4}$ is the sum $T$ of all terms, so the total is $\frac{2T}2-\frac{2T}2=0$. A sum of non-negative numbers equal to $0$ has all terms $0$, so both brackets vanish for every $i$. In particular $\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}=\frac{a_i+a_{i+5}}2$, which forces $a_i=a_{i+5}$ for every $i$.

Hence $a_1=a_6=a_{11}=\cdots$, i.e. $a_j=a_1$ for all $j\equiv1+5t\pmod{2026}$. Since $\gcd(5,2026)=1$, the numbers $1+5t$ run through all residues modulo $2026$, so the sequence is constant. Conversely, a constant sequence satisfies the condition with equality. The answer: $\boxed{\text{all constant sequences}}$.

Solution 2 (Product of the inequalities)

Indices are modulo $2026$. Multiply the $2026$ inequalities (all sides positive): \[\prod_{i}\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}\ \ge\ \prod_i\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}2\ \ge\ \prod_i\sqrt{a_{i+1}a_{i+4}},\] the second step by AM-GM in every factor. The left product equals $\sqrt{\prod a_i\cdot\prod a_i}=\prod a_i$, and so does the right product. So all three products are equal. If some inequality $\sqrt{a_ia_{i+5}}\ge\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}2$ or some AM-GM step were strict, the corresponding product inequality would be strict (all factors are positive). Hence in every factor $\frac{a_{i+1}+a_{i+4}}2=\sqrt{a_{i+1}a_{i+4}}$, i.e. $a_{i+1}=a_{i+4}$, so $a_j=a_{j+3}$ for all $j$.

Then $a_1=a_4=a_7=\cdots$, and since $\gcd(3,2026)=1$ the indices $1+3t$ cover every residue modulo $2026$; the sequence is constant. Constant sequences work (equality everywhere). Answer: $\boxed{\text{all constant sequences}}$.

Problem 7

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Classification Buckets

Let $S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$, where $n$ is an integer greater than $1$. For each $n$, determine the maximum integer $k$ such that there exists a set of $k$ non-empty subsets of $S$ with the following property:

For any two subsets $A\ne B$ that share at least one element, \[\min(A\cap B)\ne\max(A\cup B),\] where $A\cap B$ refers to the set of elements shared by both sets $A$ and $B$, and $A\cup B$ refers to the set of all elements in $A$ and $B$ combined.

Show the answer

$k=2^{n-1}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pairing sets with the same maximum)

When is a pair bad? Let $A\ne B$ share an element. Every element of $A\cap B$ is at most $\max A$ and at most $\max B$, and both are at most $\max(A\cup B)$. So $\min(A\cap B)=\max(A\cup B)=m$ happens exactly when $\max A=\max B=m$ and $A\cap B=\{m\}$. Call such a pair bad; the family must contain no bad pair.

Upper bound. Fix $m\ge2$. To a set $A$ with $\max A=m$ attach \[A^*=\{m\}\cup\bigl(\{1,\dots,m-1\}\setminus A\bigr).\] Then $\max A^*=m$, $A^*\ne A$ (exactly one of them contains $1$), $A^{**}=A$, and $A\cap A^*=\{m\}$, so $\{A,A^*\}$ is a bad pair. Thus the $2^{m-1}$ subsets with maximum $m$ split into $2^{m-2}$ bad pairs, and the family contains at most one set from each pair, i.e. at most $2^{m-2}$ sets with maximum $m$. With maximum $1$ there is only $\{1\}$. Hence \[k\le1+\sum_{m=2}^{n}2^{m-2}=1+(2^{n-1}-1)=2^{n-1}.\]

Construction. Take all $2^{n-1}$ subsets containing $1$. Any two distinct ones share $1$, so $\min(A\cap B)=1$; and $A\cup B\ne\{1\}$ (they are distinct), so $\max(A\cup B)\ge2$. The condition holds. Hence the maximum is $\boxed{k=2^{n-1}}$.

Solution 2 (Induction on n)

Let $f(n)$ be the largest size of a family of non-empty subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with the property (also for $n=1$, where $f(1)=1$).

Reformulation. For distinct $A,B$ with a common element, all elements of $A\cap B$ are at most $\max A,\max B\le\max(A\cup B)$; so $\min(A\cap B)=\max(A\cup B)$ exactly when $A,B$ have the same maximum $m$ and $A\cap B=\{m\}$.

Recursion. Let $\mathcal F$ be a good family on $\{1,\dots,n\}$, $n\ge2$. The sets of $\mathcal F$ not containing $n$ form a good family on $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, so there are at most $f(n-1)$ of them. The sets containing $n$ all have maximum $n$, so any two of them must share an element other than $n$.

  • If $\{n\}\in\mathcal F$, it is the only set containing $n$ (it meets every other such set exactly in $\{n\}$).

  • Otherwise the sets $A\setminus\{n\}$ are distinct, non-empty, pairwise intersecting subsets of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$. Such a family never contains a set together with its complement in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, and the $2^{n-1}$ subsets form $2^{n-2}$ complementary pairs, so there are at most $2^{n-2}$ of them.

Either way at most $2^{n-2}$ sets contain $n$, so $f(n)\le f(n-1)+2^{n-2}$, and by induction $f(n)\le1+1+2+\dots+2^{n-2}=2^{n-1}$.

Equality. Build $\mathcal G_1=\{\{1\}\}$ and $\mathcal G_n=\mathcal G_{n-1}\cup\{A\cup\{n\}:\ A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n-1\},\ 1\in A\}$. The new sets have maximum $n$ and pairwise share $1\ne n$; a new set and an old set have different maxima. By induction $\mathcal G_n$ is good, with $|\mathcal G_n|=|\mathcal G_{n-1}|+2^{n-2}=2^{n-1}$. So $f(n)=\boxed{2^{n-1}}$.

Problem 8

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 36: Power of a Point and Circle Measurement · Secant-Tangent Power

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle and let $D$, $E$, $F$ be the midpoints of sides $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ respectively. Point $K$ lies on $BC$ such that line $KE$ is tangent to the circumcircle of $\triangle AEF$. Suppose $BE$ intersects the circumcircles of $\triangle AEF$ and $\triangle CEK$ at $P$, $Q$ respectively such that $B,P,Q,E$ lie on segment $BE$ in that order. Let $R$ be the midpoint of segment $AQ$.

Prove that $AP=RD$.

Show the solution

Solution (Power of a point gives BQ = 2PE)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$.

Step 1: $A,B,K,E$ are concyclic. The homothety with centre $A$ and ratio $2$ maps $E\mapsto C$, $F\mapsto B$, hence the circle $(AEF)$ to the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $ABC$, and the tangent at $E$ to the tangent $t$ at $C$. So $EK\parallel t$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and the tangent-chord angle, \[\measuredangle(EK,EA)=\measuredangle(t,CA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(BK,BA),\] so $A,B,K,E$ are concyclic.

Step 2: lengths. The power of $C$ with respect to circle $ABKE$ gives $CK\cdot CB=CE\cdot CA=\tfrac{b^2}2$; this is positive, so $K$ is on ray $CB$ with $CK=\tfrac{b^2}{2a}$, and, measuring along ray $BC$, $BK\cdot BC=a^2-\tfrac{b^2}2$.

The power of $B$ with respect to circle $(CEK)$, using the lines $BC$ and $BE$, gives \[BQ\cdot BE=BK\cdot BC=\frac{2a^2-b^2}2 ,\] and the power of $B$ with respect to circle $(AEF)$, using the lines $BA$ and $BE$, gives $BP\cdot BE=BF\cdot BA=\tfrac{c^2}2$. (By the given order, $P$ and $Q$ lie on segment $BE$, so these are ordinary lengths.) The median length formula gives $BE^2=\tfrac{2a^2+2c^2-b^2}4$. Therefore \[PE=BE-BP=\frac{BE^2-\frac{c^2}{2}}{BE}=\frac{2a^2-b^2}{4\,BE}=\frac{BQ}2 .\]

Step 3: conclusion with vectors. The vectors $\overrightarrow{BQ}$ and $\overrightarrow{PE}$ both point from $B$ towards $E$, and $BQ=2PE$, so $Q-B=2(E-P)$. Using $2E=A+C$, \[Q=B+A+C-2P,\qquad R=\frac{A+Q}2=A+\frac{B+C}2-P=A+D-P .\] Hence $R-D=A-P$, so $RD=AP$ (indeed $ARDP$ is a parallelogram).

Problem 9

HNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 35: Factorials and Prime Counting · Prime Exponents in Factorials

Find all integers $n>2026$ such that $\tau((n-1)!)$ divides $\tau(n!)$, where $\tau(k)$ denotes the number of positive divisors of $k$.

Show the answer

all primes $n>2026$

Show the solution

Solution (Bounding the ratio between 1 and 2)

For a prime $q$ let $e_q$ be the exponent of $q$ in $(n-1)!$ and $v_q$ its exponent in $n$. Then $n!=(n-1)!\cdot n$ and \[\frac{\tau(n!)}{\tau((n-1)!)}=\prod_{q\mid n}\frac{e_q+v_q+1}{e_q+1}=\prod_{q\mid n}\Bigl(1+\frac{v_q}{e_q+1}\Bigr).\] The divisibility holds exactly when this ratio is an integer.

Primes work. If $n$ is prime, then $n\nmid(n-1)!$, so $e_n=0$, $v_n=1$, and the ratio is $2$.

Composite $n>2026$ fail. The ratio is greater than $1$; we show it is less than $2$.

(i) A bound for each factor. If $q\mid n$, the multiples $q,2q,\dots,n-q$ of $q$ are all in $(n-1)!$, so $e_q\ge\frac nq-1$ and \[1+\frac{v_q}{e_q+1}\le1+\frac{v_qq}{n}=1+x_q,\quad\text{say}.\] (ii) The sum $s=\sum x_q$ is small. $\sum_{q\mid n}v_qq$ is the sum of the prime factors of $n$ counted with multiplicity. For integers $u,w\ge2$ we have $uw\ge u+w$, so a product of numbers $\ge2$ is at least their sum; hence for every integer $m\ge2$ the sum of the prime factors of $m$ (with multiplicity) is at most $m$. Write $n=uw$ with $2\le u\le w$. Then the sum of prime factors of $n$ is at most $u+w\le\frac{uw}2+2$, because $\frac{uw}2+2-u-w=\frac{(u-2)(w-2)}2\ge0$. So $s\le\frac12+\frac2n<0.51$.

(iii) The product is less than $2$. Let there be $r$ prime factors. By AM-GM and the binomial theorem, \[\prod_q(1+x_q)\le\Bigl(1+\frac sr\Bigr)^r=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom rj\frac{s^j}{r^j}\le\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{s^j}{j!}\le1+s+\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{s^j}{2^{j-1}}=1+s+\frac{s^2}{2-s},\] using $\binom rj\le\frac{r^j}{j!}$ and $j!\ge2^{j-1}$. For $s<0.51$ this is less than $1+0.51+\frac{0.27}{1.49}<1.7<2$.

So for composite $n>2026$ the ratio lies strictly between $1$ and $2$ and is not an integer. The answer is: $\boxed{\text{all primes }n>2026}$.

Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ such that for all positive reals $x,y$, \[xf(x)+\frac1y=xf(x+f(y))+f(xy+1)+f(y).\]

Show the answer

$f(x)=\dfrac1x$

Show the solution

Solution (Telescoping along steps of length f(y))

Check. For $f(x)=\frac1x$ the left side is $1+\frac1y$ and the right side is \[\frac{x}{x+\frac1y}+\frac1{xy+1}+\frac1y=\frac{xy+1}{xy+1}+\frac1y=1+\frac1y .\]

Uniqueness. Let $h(y)=f(y)-\frac1y$. Rearranged, the equation says that for all $x,y>0$ \[f(x)-f\bigl(x+f(y)\bigr)=\frac{f(xy+1)+h(y)}{x}.\tag{$*$}\] Fix $y$, put $a=f(y)>0$ and apply $(*)$ at the points $x,\ x+a,\ \dots,\ x+(n-1)a$. Adding (the left sides telescope): \[f(x)-f(x+na)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{f\bigl((x+ja)y+1\bigr)+h(y)}{x+ja}.\tag{$**$}\]

Step 1: $h\le0$. Suppose $h(y)>0$. All values of $f$ are positive, so $(**)$ gives \[f(x)>f(x)-f(x+na)>h(y)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac1{x+ja}\ge\frac{h(y)}{c}\Bigl(1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n\Bigr),\qquad c=\max(x,a),\] since $x+ja\le(j+1)c$. The harmonic sums are unbounded, so this fails for large $n$. Hence $f(t)\le\frac1t$ for all $t>0$.

Step 2: $h\ge0$. Suppose $h(y)=-\delta<0$. By Step 1, $f\bigl((x+ja)y+1\bigr)<\frac1{(x+ja)y}$, which is less than $\frac\delta2$ for all $j\ge J$, for some $J$. For those $j$ the summand in $(**)$ is less than $-\frac{\delta}{2(x+ja)}$. Again by the divergence of the harmonic series, the right side of $(**)$ tends to $-\infty$ as $n\to\infty$, so $f(x+na)\to+\infty$. But $f(x+na)\le\frac1{x+na}<\frac1x$ by Step 1, a contradiction.

So $h\equiv0$, i.e. $\boxed{f(x)=\tfrac1x}$, which is indeed a solution.
