Problem 1

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Euler Totient and Related Functions

Let $p_1<p_2<\dots<p_k$ be prime numbers, and let $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_k$ be integers. Prove that there exist positive integers $b$ and $c$ that are not divisible by any prime greater than $p_k$ and satisfy \[p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}=\frac{\varphi\left(b^2\right)}{\varphi\left(c^2\right)},\] where $\varphi$ is Euler's totient function.

Show the solution – Induction over the primes up to $p_k$

Recall that $\varphi$ is multiplicative and $\varphi(q^{2e})=q^{2e-1}(q-1)$ for a prime $q$ and $e\ge1$. Call a positive integer $x$-smooth if it has no prime factor greater than $x$.

Let $q_1<q_2<\dots<q_r$ be all primes not exceeding $p_k$ (so $q_r=p_k$, and every $p_i$ is among them). We prove by induction on $j$ the stronger statement:

$(S_j)$: every rational number $\rho=q_1^{\beta_1}\cdots q_j^{\beta_j}$ with $\beta_i\in\mathbb Z$ can be written as $\rho=\varphi(b^2)/\varphi(c^2)$ with $b,c$ being $q_j$-smooth.

The problem is $(S_r)$ applied to $\rho=p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}$.

$j=1$ ($q_1=2$, $\rho=2^\beta$). For $e\ge1$ we have $\varphi(2^{2e})=2^{2e-1}$. If $\beta=2t\ge0$ take $b=2^{t+1},c=2$; if $\beta=-2t<0$ take $b=2,c=2^{t+1}$; if $\beta=2t-1>0$ take $b=2^t,c=1$; if $\beta=1-2t<0$ take $b=1,c=2^t$. In each case $\varphi(b^2)/\varphi(c^2)=2^\beta$.

Step $j-1\to j$. Write $q=q_j$ and $\rho=q^{\beta}\rho'$, where $\rho'$ involves only $q_1,\dots,q_{j-1}$. Note that $q-1$ is $q_{j-1}$-smooth.

  • $\beta$ even: choose $e,f\ge1$ with $2(e-f)=\beta$. By $(S_{j-1})$ write $\rho'=\varphi(b'^2)/\varphi(c'^2)$ and put $b=q^eb'$, $c=q^fc'$. Then \[\frac{\varphi(b^2)}{\varphi(c^2)}=\frac{q^{2e-1}(q-1)}{q^{2f-1}(q-1)}\cdot\frac{\varphi(b'^2)}{\varphi(c'^2)}=q^\beta\rho'=\rho.\]

  • $\beta$ odd and positive: let $e=\frac{\beta+1}{2}$. By $(S_{j-1})$ write $\rho'/(q-1)=\varphi(b'^2)/\varphi(c'^2)$ and put $b=q^eb'$, $c=c'$. Then $\varphi(b^2)/\varphi(c^2)=q^{\beta}(q-1)\cdot\rho'/(q-1)=\rho$.

  • $\beta$ odd and negative: let $f=\frac{1-\beta}{2}$. By $(S_{j-1})$ write $\rho'(q-1)=\varphi(b'^2)/\varphi(c'^2)$ and put $b=b'$, $c=q^fc'$. Then $\varphi(b^2)/\varphi(c^2)=\rho'(q-1)/(q^{-\beta}(q-1))=\rho$.

Here we used $\varphi(q^{2e}x^2)=\varphi(q^{2e})\varphi(x^2)$, valid because $b',c'$ are $q_{j-1}$-smooth and hence coprime to $q$. The new $b,c$ are $q_j$-smooth, which completes the induction.

Applying $(S_r)$ gives positive integers $b,c$ with no prime factor greater than $p_k$ and $p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}=\varphi(b^2)/\varphi(c^2)$.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

For each positive integer $n$, find the greatest possible value of \[\frac{a_1a_2\cdots a_k\,(a_1+1)(a_2+1)\cdots(a_k+1)}{2^k},\] where $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$ are positive integers with sum $n$. (The number $k$ of the integers $a_i$ is not fixed.)

Show the answer

$1$ for $n=1$; $3$ for $n=2$; $6^{n/3}$ if $3\mid n$; $10\cdot 6^{(n-4)/3}$ if $n\equiv1\pmod 3$, $n\ge4$; $3\cdot6^{(n-2)/3}$ if $n\equiv2\pmod3$

Show solution 1 – Local improvements

The expression equals $\prod_i T(a_i)$, where $T(a)=\frac{a(a+1)}2$ is the $a$-th triangular number: $T(1)=1$, $T(2)=3$, $T(3)=6$, $T(4)=10$. Since there are finitely many choices, a maximum exists; take a maximising choice with the largest possible number of parts. We show that its parts can be assumed to be in $\{2,3,4\}$ with very few $2$'s and $4$'s.

  • No part $a\ge5$: replacing $a$ by $2$ and $a-2$ changes $T(a)=\frac{a^2+a}{2}$ into $3T(a-2)=\frac{3(a^2-3a+2)}2$, and $3(a^2-3a+2)-(a^2+a)=2a^2-10a+6>0$ for $a\ge5$. This would increase the product.

  • No part $1$ if $n\ge2$: a part $1$ contributes the factor $1$; merging it with another part $a$ replaces $T(a)$ by $T(a+1)>T(a)$.

  • Among $2,4$: two parts $4,4$ can be replaced by $3,3,2$ ($100<108$); parts $4,2$ by $3,3$ ($30<36$); parts $2,2$ by $4$ ($9<10$).

So for $n\ge2$ an optimal choice consists of $3$'s together with at most one extra part, which is a $2$ or a $4$; which case occurs is forced by $n\bmod 3$: \[n=3m:\ 6^m,\qquad n=3m+1\ (m\ge1):\ 10\cdot6^{m-1},\qquad n=3m+2:\ 3\cdot6^m,\] and the value $1$ for $n=1$. These values are attained, so they are the maxima. (For $n=2$ the formula $3\cdot6^0=3$ applies.)

Show solution 2 – A superadditive bound

Write the expression as $\prod T(a_i)$ with $T(a)=\frac{a(a+1)}2$, and let $F(1)=1$ and, for $n\ge2$, \[F(n)=6^{n/3}\ (3\mid n),\qquad F(n)=10\cdot 6^{(n-4)/3}\ (n\equiv1),\qquad F(n)=3\cdot6^{(n-2)/3}\ (n\equiv 2 \pmod 3).\] So $F(1),\dots,F(8)=1,3,6,10,18,36,60,108$ and $F(n+3)=6F(n)$ for $n\ge2$.

(i) $T(a)\le F(a)$. This holds with equality for $a=1,2,3,4$. For $a\ge2$, $\frac{T(a+3)}{T(a)}=\frac{(a+3)(a+4)}{a(a+1)}\le 6$ because $5a^2-a-12\ge0$; together with $F(a+3)=6F(a)$ this gives $T(a)\le F(a)$ for all $a$ by induction.

(ii) $F(m)F(l)\le F(m+l)$. If $m=1$ this is $F(l)\le F(l+1)$, true since $F(l+1)/F(l)\in\{3,2,\frac53,\frac95\}$. If $m,l\ge2$, replacing $m$ by $m-3$ (when $m-3\ge2$) divides both sides by $6$, so we may assume $m,l\in\{2,3,4\}$; then \[(m,l)=(2,2),(2,3),(2,4),(3,3),(3,4),(4,4):\quad 9\le10,\ 18\le18,\ 30\le36,\ 36\le36,\ 60\le60,\ 100\le108.\]

Conclusion. By (i) and repeated use of (ii), \[\prod_i T(a_i)\le\prod_i F(a_i)\le F(a_1+\dots+a_k)=F(n).\] Equality is attained by the parts $3,3,\dots,3$ together with one $4$ (if $n\equiv1$, $n\ge4$) or one $2$ (if $n\equiv2$), and by $a_1=1$ for $n=1$. Hence the maximum is $F(n)$.

Problem 3

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation · Inversion with Power and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$, orthocenter $H$, and circumcircle $\omega$ with center $O$. Let $D$, $E$, and $F$ be the feet of the altitudes from $A$, $B$, and $C$. Let $X$ be the midpoint of $OH$, and let $Y$ be the circumcenter of triangle $BOC$. Let $S$ be the midpoint of the arc $\widehat{BAC}$ of $\omega$. The lines $AS$ and $XY$ intersect at $W$. The circumcircles of triangles $AEF$ and $ABC$ intersect again at $Q$, and the circumcircles of triangles $BOC$ and $DEF$ intersect at $U$ and $V$. Prove that the points $A,Q,U,V,W$ lie on a circle.

Show the solution – A radical centre and an inversion at A

Let $N$ denote the nine-point circle (the circle $DEF$, centre $X$) and $\gamma$ the circle $BOC$ (centre $Y$).

Step 1: $A,Q,U,V$ are concyclic. Since $AB\ne AC$, the line $EF$ is not parallel to $BC$; let $T=EF\cap BC$. Three circles meet pairwise along $AQ$, $EF$, $BC$: $\omega$ and the circle $AEF$ (whose diameter is $AH$) meet at $A,Q$; the circle $AEF$ and the circle with diameter $BC$ (through $B,C,E,F$) meet at $E,F$; that circle and $\omega$ meet at $B,C$. The three radical axes concur, so $T$ lies on $AQ$ and \[TA\cdot TQ=TB\cdot TC .\] Since $AD,BE,CF$ concur at $H$ and $T=EF\cap BC$, the points $T,D$ divide $BC$ harmonically. With $M$ the midpoint of $BC$ this gives $MB^2=MD\cdot MT$, hence $TB\cdot TC=TM^2-MB^2=TM^2-MD\cdot MT=TM\cdot TD$. But $M,D\in N$ and $B,C\in\gamma$, so $T$ has equal powers $TM\cdot TD=TB\cdot TC$ with respect to $N$ and $\gamma$: $T$ lies on their radical axis $UV$, and $TU\cdot TV=TB\cdot TC=TA\cdot TQ$. By the converse of the power of a point, $A,Q,U,V$ lie on a circle $\Gamma$.

Step 2: $AU$ and $AV$ are isogonal in $\angle BAC$. Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and power $\frac12AB\cdot AC$, followed by the reflection in the bisector of $\angle BAC$. $\Phi$ is an involution that maps circles not through $A$ to circles. It maps $B$ to the point of ray $AC$ at distance $\frac{AB\cdot AC}{2AB}=\frac{AC}2$, i.e. to the midpoint of $AC$, and similarly $C$ to the midpoint of $AB$. The isogonal of line $AO$ is the altitude $AD$, and $AO\cdot AD=R\cdot\frac{AB\cdot AC}{2R}=\frac12AB\cdot AC$ (using $AD=\frac{AB\cdot AC}{2R}$), so $\Phi(O)=D$. Hence $\Phi(\gamma)$ is the circle through the two midpoints and $D$, which is $N$; as $\Phi$ is an involution, $\Phi$ swaps $\gamma$ and $N$ and maps $\{U,V\}=\gamma\cap N$ onto itself. If $\Phi(U)=U$ and $\Phi(V)=V$, both points would lie on the bisector at distance $\sqrt{AB\cdot AC/2}$ from $A$ on the same ray, so $U=V$, which is false. Hence $\Phi(U)=V$: the rays $AU$, $AV$ are symmetric in the bisector of $\angle A$.

Step 3: $W\in\Gamma$. By Step 2 the internal and external bisectors of $\angle BAC$ are also the bisectors of $\angle UAV$. The line $AS$ is the external bisector of $\angle BAC$ ($S$ is the midpoint of arc $BAC$), so its second intersection with $\Gamma$ is a midpoint of an arc $UV$ of $\Gamma$, hence lies on the perpendicular bisector of $UV$. That perpendicular bisector is the line of centres $XY$ of the two circles $N,\gamma$ through $U,V$. Therefore the second intersection of $AS$ with $\Gamma$ is $AS\cap XY=W$.

So $A,Q,U,V,W$ all lie on $\Gamma$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Each vertex of a regular polygon $A_1A_2\dots A_{3n}$ is colored white, green, or red so that each color occurs at $n$ vertices and the number of indices $1\le i\le 3n$ for which $A_i$ and $A_{i+1}$ have different colors is even. (We set $A_{3n+1}=A_1$.) Prove that there exists a line that intersects the interior of the polygon, passes through none of its vertices, and has the following property: in each of the two half-planes determined by the line, the number of white vertices equals the number of green vertices and the number of red vertices.

Show the solution – A walk on the triangular lattice

Reformulation. A line that meets the interior of the convex polygon and avoids its vertices splits the vertices into two non-empty arcs of consecutive vertices; conversely, for any arc $A_{i+1},\dots,A_j$ of consecutive vertices that is neither empty nor everything, the line through two interior points of the sides $A_iA_{i+1}$ and $A_jA_{j+1}$ separates exactly that arc from the rest. If one arc contains equally many vertices of each colour, so does the complementary arc. Hence it suffices to find a balanced arc (equally many white, green, red vertices) that is non-empty and proper.

A walk. Let $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and assign the steps $1,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^2$ to white, green, red. Let $z_0=0$ and $z_t=z_{t-1}+(\text{step of }A_t)$, $t=1,\dots,3n$. Since $1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2=0$ and each colour occurs $n$ times, $z_{3n}=0$. Moreover $u+v\varepsilon+w\varepsilon^2=0$ with integers $u,v,w$ holds only for $u=v=w$, so the arc $A_{s+1},\dots,A_t$ ($0\le s<t\le 3n$) is balanced if and only if $z_s=z_t$.

Suppose no proper non-empty arc is balanced. Then $z_0,z_1,\dots,z_{3n-1}$ are pairwise distinct, and the closed walk $z_0\to z_1\to\dots\to z_{3n}=z_0$ is a closed polygon whose edges are unit edges of the triangular lattice in the three directions $0^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$. Two distinct edges of the triangular lattice can meet only at lattice points, and the vertices are distinct, so this polygon is simple.

At the vertex $z_t$ the direction changes exactly when $A_t$ and $A_{t+1}$ have different colours (indices mod $3n$), and since only the three directions $0^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$ occur, every change of direction is a turn by $+120^\circ$ or $-120^\circ$. For a simple closed polygon the sum of the signed exterior angles equals $\pm360^\circ$. If there are $p$ left turns and $q$ right turns, then $120^\circ(p-q)=\pm360^\circ$, so $p-q=\pm3$ and $p+q$ is odd. But $p+q$ is the number of indices $i$ with $A_i,A_{i+1}$ of different colours, which is even by hypothesis — a contradiction.

Hence a proper non-empty balanced arc exists, and the line described above has the required property.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Strategy Stealing and Symmetry

Let $a$, $b$, and $n$ be positive integers. Alexandra and Boris play a game on a rectangular $a\times b$ board divided into unit squares. They take turns, with Alexandra moving first. On a turn, the player chooses a polyomino consisting of several unit squares, at least one and at most $n$, joined along their sides, whose boundary is a connected polygonal line, and places it on the cells of the rectangle so that it neither overlaps a previously placed polyomino nor extends beyond the board. A player who cannot make a move loses. Who has a winning strategy (depending on $a$, $b$, and $n$)?

Show the answer

Boris wins if $n=1$ and $ab$ is even, or if $a,b$ are both even and $n\in\{2,3\}$; in all other cases Alexandra wins

Show the solution – Central symmetry with a large central piece

Let $Z$ be the centre of the board and $\rho$ the half-turn about $Z$; $\rho$ maps the board onto itself. “Connected” means connected through common sides. A polyomino whose boundary is one closed polygonal line is a connected set of cells without holes; we only use that pieces are connected and that a piece and its image under $\rho$ are both legal pieces.

Boris's wins. If $n=1$ every move covers one cell, the game lasts exactly $ab$ moves, and if $ab$ is even Boris makes the last move. If $a,b$ are even and $n\in\{2,3\}$, Boris keeps the occupied set $\rho$-symmetric. When Alexandra places $P$ with $P\cap\rho(P)=\emptyset$ he places $\rho(P)$, which is free because the occupied set was symmetric. Otherwise $P$ contains two cells $c\ne\rho(c)$; with coordinates in which the four central cells are $(i,j)$, $i,j\in\{-1,0\}$, and $\rho(i,j)=(-1-i,-1-j)$, the two cells are at distance $|2i+1|+|2j+1|\ge2$ (steps between side-adjacent cells), with equality only when both are central. A connected piece of at most $3$ cells containing them must be three of the four central cells; Boris then places the fourth central cell (free, since it is the image of the middle cell of $P$). Either way Boris can always answer, so he wins.

Key lemma. Let $K$ be a connected, $\rho$-symmetric set of cells whose union contains $Z$, with bounding box of $w$ columns and $h$ rows. If a connected set $P$ of cells is disjoint from $K$ and $P\cap\rho(P)\ne\emptyset$, then $|P|\ge w+h+3$.

Proof. Take $c\in P$ with $\rho(c)\in P$ and a path $\gamma$ of distinct cells of $P$ from $c$ to $\rho(c)$, consecutive cells sharing a side. Join the centres of consecutive cells of $\gamma$ and then of $\rho(\gamma)$: this is a closed lattice polygon $\Pi$ with $2(|\gamma|-1)$ unit steps. Each step lies inside two adjacent cells of $P\cup\rho(P)$, so $\Pi$ avoids the closed cells of $K$. Along $\rho(\gamma)$ the direction seen from $Z$ turns by the same angle as along $\gamma$, and along $\gamma$ it turns by an odd multiple of $\pi$ (from $c$ to the opposite point $\rho(c)$); so $\Pi$ winds an odd number of times around $Z$, hence (as $K$ is connected and disjoint from $\Pi$) around every point of $K$. Therefore every ray from a point of $K$ meets $\Pi$; using the rays to the left and to the right from the leftmost and rightmost cells of $K$, $\Pi$ visits a column left of the box and a column right of it, so it makes at least $2(w+1)$ horizontal steps, and similarly at least $2(h+1)$ vertical steps. Thus $2(|\gamma|-1)\ge 2(w+h+2)$ and $|P|\ge|\gamma|\ge w+h+3$. $\square$

Alexandra's strategy. In all remaining cases she first places a piece $K$ (connected, $\rho$-symmetric, containing $Z$, $|K|\le n$) such that either (I) $K$ cuts the board into two parts exchanged by $\rho$, or (II) $w+h+3>n$. Afterwards she answers every move $P$ of Boris by $\rho(P)$. This is legal: the occupied set stays symmetric, so $\rho(P)$ avoids it, and $\rho(P)\cap P=\emptyset$ — in case (I) because $P$ lies in one part and $\rho(P)$ in the other, in case (II) by the lemma since $|P|\le n<w+h+3$. So she always has a move and wins. The choice of $K$:

  • $a,b$ odd (any $n$), or exactly one of $a,b$ odd and $n\ge2$: say the number of rows $a$ is odd. Take in the middle row the segment of $m$ cells centred at $Z$, where $m$ is the largest integer $\le n$ with $m\equiv b\pmod 2$ (so $m\ge n-1\ge1$), or the whole middle row if $m\ge b$. The whole row gives (I); otherwise $w+h+3=m+4>n$ gives (II).

  • $a,b$ even and $n\ge4$: with the coordinates above put $K_t=\{(i,0):-1\le i\le t-1\}\cup\{(i,-1):-t\le i\le 0\}$, a symmetric connected “S” of $2t+2$ cells, $w=2t$, $h=2$. Let $t=\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\ge1$, so $n-1\le|K_t|\le n$ and $w+h+3=|K_t|+3>n$ (II), if $2t\le b$. If $2t>b$ use $K_{b/2}$, which has $b+2\le 2t\le n$ cells and spans the whole width; it cuts the board into the part above it (with the cells $(i,0)$, $i\le-2$) and the part below it (with the cells $(i,-1)$, $i\ge1$), which $\rho$ exchanges (I).

This covers every case not won by Boris, which proves the answer.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

For each odd positive integer $n\ge 3$, find the smallest positive integer $S$ for which there exist $n$ distinct positive integers whose sum is at least $S$ and such that any $\frac{n-1}{2}$ of them have sum at most $\frac{S-1}{2}$.

Show the answer

$S=\left(\frac{n+1}2\right)^3+\left(\frac{n-1}2\right)^3=\frac{n^3+3n}4$

Show the solution – Compare the top half with the bottom half

Let $n=2m+1$ and let the numbers be $x_1<x_2<\dots<x_n$. The largest sum of $m$ of them is $A=x_{m+2}+\dots+x_{2m+1}$; let $B=x_1+\dots+x_{m+1}$. The conditions are $A+B\ge S$ and $A\le\frac{S-1}2$, i.e. \[2A+1\le S\le A+B .\] Lower bound. From the display, $B\ge A+1$. Put $t=x_{m+1}$. Since the numbers are distinct integers, $x_{m+1-j}\le t-j$ and $x_{m+1+j}\ge t+j$, so \[B\le (m+1)t-\frac{m(m+1)}2,\qquad A\ge mt+\frac{m(m+1)}2 .\] Then $B\ge A+1$ gives $t\ge m(m+1)+1$, and \[S\ge 2A+1\ge 2mt+m(m+1)+1\ge 2m(m^2+m+1)+m(m+1)+1=(m+1)^3+m^3 .\] Example. Take the $2m+1$ consecutive integers $t-m,\dots,t+m$ with $t=m^2+m+1$, and $S=(m+1)^3+m^3$. Here $A=mt+\frac{m(m+1)}2$ and $B=(m+1)t-\frac{m(m+1)}2=A+t-m(m+1)=A+1$, so $S=2A+1=A+B$: the total is $S$ and every $m$ of the numbers have sum at most $A=\frac{S-1}2$.

Hence the smallest $S$ is $\boxed{(m+1)^3+m^3=\frac{n^3+3n}{4}}$ (for example $9$ for $n=3$ and $35$ for $n=5$).

Problem 7

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation · Tangency and Inversion Cleanup

The incircle of a triangle $ABC$ with $AB<AC$ touches the sides $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$ at $D$, $E$, and $F$, respectively. The bisector of $\angle BAC$ intersects the lines $DE$ and $DF$ at $K$ and $L$, respectively. The circle with diameter $KL$ intersects the side $BC$ at $S$ and $T$. Prove that the circumcircle of triangle $AST$ is tangent to the circumcircle of $ABC$, the incircle of $ABC$, and the $A$-excircle of $ABC$.

Show the solution – Inversion at A with power s(s-a)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths, $s$ the semiperimeter, $I$ the incentre, $\ell$ the line $AI$, and let $BC'$ denote the reflection of line $BC$ in $\ell$ (we have $BC'\ne BC$ since $AB\ne AC$).

1. $K$ and $L$ are the feet of the perpendiculars from $B$ and $C$ to $\ell$. Let $K'$ be the foot from $B$ to $\ell$. We use directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$, and orient the triangle counterclockwise, so that $\measuredangle(BC,BA)=B$, $\measuredangle(CA,CB)=C$, $\measuredangle(AB,AC)=A$, where $A,B,C$ also denote the angles of the triangle. Since $\angle BDI=\angle BK'I=90^\circ$, the points $B,D,I,K'$ lie on the circle with diameter $BI$, so \[\measuredangle(DK',\ell)=\measuredangle(K'D,K'I)=\measuredangle(BD,BI)=\measuredangle(BC,BI)=\tfrac B2 .\] On the other hand $DE\perp CI$ (as $CD=CE$ and $CI$ bisects $\angle DCE$), and \[\measuredangle(CI,\ell)=\measuredangle(CI,CA)+\measuredangle(CA,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,AI)=-\tfrac C2-A+\tfrac A2=-\tfrac{A+C}2,\] so $\measuredangle(DE,\ell)=90^\circ-\frac{A+C}2=\frac B2$. The lines $DK'$ and $DE$ pass through $D$ and make the same directed angle with $\ell$, so they coincide. Hence $K=K'$, and similarly $L$ is the foot from $C$. Consequently $AK=c\cos\frac A2$, $AL=b\cos\frac A2$ and \[AK\cdot AL=bc\cos^2\tfrac A2=s(s-a).\] 2. The circle $\kappa$ with diameter $KL$. Let $D_1=\ell\cap BC$. The right triangles $BKD_1$ and $CLD_1$ are similar, so $\frac{D_1K}{D_1L}=\frac{BD_1}{CD_1}=\frac cb=\frac{AK}{AL}$; thus $(K,L;D_1,A)$ is harmonic, and $\kappa$ is the Apollonius circle of the segment $AD_1$: every point $P\in\kappa$ satisfies $\frac{PD_1}{PA}=\lambda$ for a fixed $\lambda$. For the chord $ST$ of $\kappa$ through $D_1$ this gives $\frac{SD_1}{TD_1}=\frac{SA}{TA}$, so $AD_1=\ell$ bisects $\angle SAT$. Hence the line $AS$ is the reflection of the line $AT$ in $\ell$.

3. The inversion. Let $\Psi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and power $s(s-a)=AK\cdot AL$. As $s(s-a)$ is also the product of the tangent lengths from $A$ to the incircle and to the $A$-excircle ($AE=s-a$ and $s$), $\Psi$ swaps these two circles. The power of $A$ with respect to $\kappa$ is $AK\cdot AL$, so $\Psi$ maps $S$ to the second intersection $S^*$ of line $AS$ with $\kappa$. By Step 2 and the symmetry of $\kappa$ in $\ell$, line $AS$ meets $\kappa$ at the reflections $T^r,T''^r$ of the two points $T,T''$ where line $AT$ meets $\kappa$. Since $S\ne T^r$ (otherwise $T^r=S\in BC$ and $T\in BC\cap BC'=\{D_1\}$, forcing $S=T=D_1$), we get $S=T''^r$ and $S^*=T^r\in BC'$. Similarly $\Psi(T)\in BC'$.

Hence $\alpha\ge\frac{2k}{k+1}$.

Admissible vectors. Call $(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ admissible if $\sum s_i=0$ and for every set $I$ of $m$ indices \[\sum_{i\in I}s_i\le c\,m(n-m).\tag{1}\] Every row is admissible: the sum over $I$ is at most $m$ (entries $\le1$) and at most $n-m$ (it is minus the sum over the complement, whose entries are $\ge-1$); and since $\max(m,n-m)\ge k+1$, we have $\min(m,n-m)\le c\,m(n-m)$.

Transfers. An antisymmetric array $(f_{ij})$ ($f_{ji}=-f_{ij}$) with $|f_{ij}|\le c$ and $s_i=\sum_jf_{ij}$ makes $s$ admissible: summing over $i\in I$, the terms with $i,j\in I$ cancel, leaving $m(n-m)$ terms of size at most $c$; the same cancellation gives $\sum s_i=0$.

For sorted $s$, condition (1) is equivalent to \[S_j=s_1+\dots+s_j\ge W_j\quad(0\le j\le n),\qquad S_n=0,\tag{2}\] since the largest sum of $m$ entries is the sum of the last $m$, which is $-S_{n-m}$. We show its vertices are the vectors obtained by cutting $1,\dots,n$ into consecutive blocks and replacing the $w_i$ of each block by their average.

Let $s\in\mathcal P$ and call $j$ tight if $S_j=W_j$. For tight $0<j<n$, (2) at $j\pm1$ gives $s_j\le w_j<w_{j+1}\le s_{j+1}$. Cut at all tight indices. If $s$ is constant on every block, then, as both ends of each block are tight, the constant is the average of the $w_i$ over the block. Otherwise some block contains two different maximal runs of equal entries; raising one run and lowering the other by amounts keeping the block sum fixed preserves all tight equalities and all equalities between neighbouring entries, while every other inequality is strict at $s$. So $s$ is the midpoint of a segment in $\mathcal P$ and is not a vertex. Hence every vertex is a block-average vector, and every $s\in\mathcal P$ is a convex combination of them.

For a block-average vector put $f_{ij}=c$ if $i$ lies in a later block than $j$, $f_{ij}=-c$ if earlier, and $f_{ij}=0$ in the same block. For $i$ in the block $\ell,\dots,r$ the net outflow is $c\bigl((\ell-1)-(n-r)\bigr)=c(\ell+r-n-1)$, the average of $w_\ell,\dots,w_r$. Taking the same convex combination of these arrays proves the lemma. $\square$

Upper bound. Place the rows one by one, starting from column sums $0$. Before each row, number the columns so that the current sums $s_1\le\dots\le s_n$ increase, and place the new row so that its entries $t_1\ge\dots\ge t_n$ decrease. By induction $s$ is admissible; the lemma gives an array for $s$ with $f_{ij}\in[0,c]$ for $i>j$. The row $t$ is admissible and increasing in the reversed order, so the lemma (applied in that order) gives an array for $t$ with $g_{ij}\in[-c,0]$ for $i>j$. Then $f+g$ is antisymmetric with entries in $[-c,c]$ and represents $s+t$, which is therefore admissible.

At the end, (1) for $I=\{i\}$ and for its complement gives $|s_i|\le c(n-1)=\frac{2k}{k+1}$ for every column. Hence $\alpha=\frac{2k}{k+1}$.

Suppose that $P(c)=P(a)-2^n$ with integers $a,n\ge0$. As $P'=Q'/D>0$ on $[x_0,\infty)$, we have $P(x)\le P(c)$ for all $x\in[0,c]$; since $P(a)>P(c)$, this gives $a>c$. Put $h=a-c>0$. Then $Q(c+h)-Q(c)=D\cdot2^n$, that is, $h\,G(c,h)=2^{\delta+n}D'$. Hence $h=e\cdot2^u$ with $e$ a positive divisor of $D'$ and $u\ge0$, and $G(c,h)=\frac{D'}{e}2^{\delta+n-u}$. If $u\ge\mu+1$, every term $q_j(c)h^{j-1}$ with $j\ge2$ is divisible by $2^{\mu+1}$, so $G(c,h)\equiv Q'(c)\pmod{2^{\mu+1}}$ is divisible by $2^\mu$ but not by $2^{\mu+1}$. Then $\delta+n-u=\mu$ and $G(c,h)=\frac{D'}{e}2^{\mu}\le D'2^\mu<Q'(c)$, contradicting (1). Therefore $u\le\mu$, so $h\in H$ and \[Q(c+h)-Q(c)=D\cdot2^n\qquad\text{for some }h\in H.\tag{2}\]

Lemma. If $R$ is a nonconstant polynomial with integer coefficients and $S$ is a finite set of primes, then some prime outside $S$ divides a value $R(r)$, $r\in\mathbb Z$. Indeed, if $R(0)=0$, every prime $p\notin S$ divides $R(p)$. Otherwise let $b=R(0)\ne0$; then $R(bx)=b\bigl(1+xT(x)\bigr)$ with $T$ a nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. Let $s$ be the product of the primes in $S$ ($s=1$ if $S$ is empty). The nonconstant polynomial $1+stT(st)$ in $t$ takes each of the values $\pm1$ only finitely often, so for some integer $t$ the number $1+stT(st)$ has a prime factor $p$. This number is $\equiv1$ modulo every prime of $S$, so $p\notin S$, and $p\mid R(bst)$.

Blocking (2). For $h\in H$ the polynomial $R_h(x)=Q(x+h)-Q(x)$ has degree $d-1\ge1$ (its leading coefficient is $dh$ times that of $Q$). Applying the lemma to the members of $H$ one after another, each time with $S$ consisting of $2$, the prime factors of $D$ and the primes already chosen, we obtain distinct odd primes $p_h\nmid D$ ($h\in H$) and integers $r_h$ with $p_h\mid R_h(r_h)$. By the Chinese remainder theorem the integers $c$ with \[c\equiv c^*\pmod{2^{\mu+1}},\qquad c\equiv r_h\pmod{p_h}\ \ (h\in H)\] form an arithmetic progression, and all of its large terms satisfy the conditions of the key step. For such $c$ and every $h\in H$, $p_h$ divides $R_h(c)=Q(c+h)-Q(c)$ but not $D\cdot2^n$, so (2) is impossible. Hence $P(c)$ is not good. These numbers $P(c)$ are positive integers ($P$ is faithful and $P(c)\to\infty$) and pairwise distinct ($P$ is increasing on $[x_0,\infty)$), so infinitely many positive integers are not good.

Putting the cases together: there are infinitely many positive integers not of the form $P(a)-2^n$ for every faithful $P$ except $P(x)=x+c$ and $P(x)=2x+c$ with $c\in\mathbb Z$.

A labeling is called $c$-tiger if, for every point with coordinates $(x,y)$, the number written there does not exceed $x^c+y^c$. Find all positive real numbers $c$ for which every $c$-tiger labeling contains $1000$ distinct points with the same number written at them.

Show the answer

exactly the $c$ with $0<c<2$

Show solution 1 – Pigeonhole for $c<2$, an injective labelling for $c\ge2$

$c<2$. Take $M$ large and look at the $M^2$ points with $1\le x,y\le M$. Their labels are positive integers at most $2M^c$, so some label occurs at least $\frac{M^2}{2M^c}=\frac{M^{2-c}}2$ times. For $M$ large this exceeds $999$, so every $c$-tiger labelling has $1000$ points with equal labels.

$c\ge2$. We label all points with distinct numbers. List the points in order of increasing $x^2+y^2$ (ties in any order) and give the $k$-th point the label $k$. If the $k$-th point is $(x,y)$ with $x^2+y^2=v$, then all of the first $k$ points satisfy $x'^2+y'^2\le v$. The unit squares $[x'-1,x']\times[y'-1,y']$ of these points are disjoint and lie in the quarter disc of radius $\sqrt v$, so $k\le\frac{\pi v}{4}<v$. Hence the label $k$ is at most $x^2+y^2\le x^c+y^c$ (as $x,y\ge1$), the labelling is $c$-tiger, and no label repeats.

Show solution 2 – Diagonal enumeration

$c<2$. Count the points with $x+y\le M$: there are $\frac{M(M-1)}2$ of them and their labels are at most $2M^c$ (since $x,y<M$). So some label is used at least $\frac{M(M-1)}{4M^c}$ times, which tends to infinity for $c<2$; hence $1000$ equal labels always occur.

$c\ge2$. Enumerate the points diagonal by diagonal: first the point with $x+y=2$, then the two with $x+y=3$, and so on, and label each point by its position in this list. A point with $x+y=s$ gets a label at most $1+2+\dots+(s-1)=\frac{s(s-1)}2<\frac{s^2}{2}\le x^2+y^2\le x^c+y^c$ (using $(x+y)^2\le 2(x^2+y^2)$ and $x,y\ge1$). All labels are distinct, so for $c\ge2$ there is a $c$-tiger labelling without even two equal labels.

Therefore the required $c$ are exactly those with $\boxed{0<c<2}$.

Problem 2

SAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Hybrid Olympiad Algebra

Find all nonnegative integers $n$ with the following property: for arbitrary real numbers $a_1,\dots,a_{2026}$ and $b_1,\dots,b_{2026}$, if the set of numbers $y$ for which the equation in $x$ \[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2026}a_i\,|x-b_i|\right|=y\] has exactly $n+1$ real roots is finite, then the set of numbers $y$ for which the same equation has at least $n$ real roots is also finite, when:

  • (b) $y$ is a real number.

Show the answer

(a) all $n$ except $n=0$ and $n=2$; (b) $n=4051$ and all $n\ge4053$

Show the solution – Counting crossings of a piecewise linear function

Let $G(x)=\sum a_i|x-b_i|$, $F=|G|$, and let $N(y)\in\{0,1,2,\dots,\infty\}$ be the number of roots of $F(x)=y$. Put $s=\sum a_i$ and $\beta=\sum a_ib_i$. Then $G$ is continuous and piecewise linear with at most $2026$ breakpoints; $G(x)=sx-\beta$ to the right of all $b_i$ and $G(x)=-sx+\beta$ to the left.

Exceptional values. Let $E$ be the finite set consisting of $0$, $|\beta|$, the values of $F$ at the breakpoints, and the values of $F$ on its constant pieces. Only $y\in E$ can have $N(y)=\infty$ or odd; finitely many $y$ never decide whether a set is finite, so only $y\notin E$ matter.

(F1) For $y\notin E$, $y>0$, $N(y)$ is even. The roots are the crossings of the levels $y$ and $-y$ by $G$. At both ends $G$ tends to $+\infty$ or both to $-\infty$ (if $s\ne0$), or $G$ is constant there with values $-\beta$ and $\beta$ (if $s=0$); in each case $F-y$ has the same sign at both ends, so it changes sign an even number of times. For $y<0$, $N(y)=0$.

(F2) For $y\notin E$, $N(y)\le4052$. Let $r\le2026$ be the number of breakpoints. If $s\ne0$, the values of $G$ at $-\infty$, at the breakpoints and at $+\infty$ form a list of $r+2$ terms starting and ending with the same infinite value, so each level is crossed an even number of times, at most $r+1\le2027$ times, hence at most $2026$ times; in total $N(y)\le4052$. If $s=0$ the two end pieces are constant and the crossings happen on the $r-1\le2025$ bounded pieces: $N(y)\le2\cdot2025=4050$.

(F3) Examples.

  • Zigzag. Take $b_i=i$ and let $H$ be the piecewise linear function with $H(1)=-10$, $H(i)=10(-1)^i$ for $2\le i\le2025$, $H(2026)=30$, slope $-\sigma$ left of $1$ and $\sigma$ right of $2026$, where $\sigma=\frac{-10+30}{2025}>0$. Then $H-\sum a_i|x-i|$ (with $2a_i$ the slope jump of $H$ at $i$) has slope $0$ everywhere, and this constant is $0$ because for $\sum a_i|x-i|$ the two end lines satisfy $G(1)+G(2026)=s\,(2026-1)$, which $H$ was built to satisfy. So $H$ is of the required form. For $0<y<10$ each of the $2025$ pieces between consecutive breakpoints crosses both $y$ and $-y$, and the left ray (from $+\infty$ down to $-10$) crosses both; the right ray crosses neither: $N(y)=4052$. For $y>10$ (outside $E$) $N(y)=2$.

  • $|x|$ ($a_1=1$, $b_1=0$, other $a_i=0$): $N(y)=2$ for all $y>0$, $N(0)=1$.

  • Two tents: $G=|x+1|-2|x|+2|x-1|-2|x-2|+|x-3|$ is $0$ outside $[-1,3]$ and zigzags $0,2,0,2,0$ at $-1,0,1,2,3$: $N(y)=4$ for $0<y<2$, $N(2)=2$, $N(y)=0$ for $y>2$.

(b) real $y$. Generic counts are even and at most $4052$.

  • $n$ even, $n\ge4054$, or $n=4053$: $N(y)\ge n$ forces $y\in E$, so the set is always finite: the property holds.

  • $n=4051$: if $\{N\ge4051\}$ is infinite it contains some $y\notin E$, where $N(y)=4052=n+1$; and $\{N=4052\}$ is a union of open intervals minus a finite set, hence infinite. So the hypothesis fails whenever the conclusion fails: the property holds.

  • $n\le4052$ even (including $0$), or $n$ odd with $3\le n\le4049$: the zigzag has $N\ge n$ on $(0,10)$, while $N=n+1\in[1,4053]\setminus\{2,4052\}$ happens only on $E$. The property fails.

  • $n=1$: the two tents have $N(y)=2$ only for $y=2$ but $N(y)\ge1$ on $(0,2]$. The property fails.

So in (b) the answer is $n=4051$ and all $n\ge4053$.

(a) integer $y$. A set of integers is finite iff it is bounded. If $s\ne0$, then $N(y)=2$ for every $y$ above all values of $F$ at the breakpoints; if $s=0$, $F$ is bounded and $N(y)=0$ for large $y$.

  • $n\ge3$: $\{y\in\mathbb Z:N(y)\ge3\}$ is bounded in both cases, so the property holds.

  • $n=1$: if $s\ne0$, $N(y)=2$ for all large integers, so the hypothesis fails; if $s=0$, $\{N\ge1\}$ is bounded. The property holds.

  • $n=0$ and $n=2$: for $|x|$, $N=1$ only at $0$ and $N=3$ never, while $N(y)\ge0$ for all integers and $N(y)\ge2$ for all positive integers. The property fails.

So in (a) the answer is every $n$ except $0$ and $2$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Let $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{Q}$ be convex polygons with areas $S_{\mathcal{P}}$ and $S_{\mathcal{Q}}$, respectively, such that $\mathcal{Q}$ lies entirely in the interior of $\mathcal{P}$. Prove that there exists a polygon $\mathcal{R}$ with area $S_{\mathcal{R}}$, similar to $\mathcal{P}$ and with sides parallel to those of $\mathcal{P}$, that lies entirely in the interior of $\mathcal{Q}$ and satisfies \[S_{\mathcal{Q}}^2\le 1000\cdot S_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot S_{\mathcal{R}}.\]

Show the solution – A rectangle around P and an incircle inside Q

All statements are invariant under affine maps of the plane: they preserve convexity, inclusions, ratios of areas, and map a positive homothetic copy of $\mathcal P$ (a polygon similar to $\mathcal P$ with corresponding sides parallel) to a positive homothetic copy of the image. We construct $\mathcal R$ as a positive homothetic copy of $\mathcal P$.

1. A rectangle around $\mathcal P$. Let $A,B\in\mathcal P$ be at maximal distance $d$. Then $\mathcal P$ lies between the lines through $A$ and $B$ perpendicular to $AB$. Let $C,D\in\mathcal P$ be the points farthest from line $AB$ on its two sides, at distances $h_1,h_2$. Then $\mathcal P$ lies in a rectangle $\Pi$ of area $d(h_1+h_2)$ and contains the quadrilateral $ACBD$ of area $\frac12d(h_1+h_2)$. So $S_\Pi\le 2S_{\mathcal P}$. Apply an affine map taking $\Pi$ to the unit square; now $S_{\mathcal P}\ge\frac12$.

The lines through its vertices parallel to the opposite sides bound a triangle of area $4S_\Delta$ that contains $\mathcal Q$ (a point of $\mathcal Q$ beyond one of these lines would give a larger triangle). So $S_\Delta\ge\frac14 S_{\mathcal Q}$.

The homothety $h$ with ratio $\mu$ taking the centre of $\Pi$ to $I$ maps $\Pi$ onto a square of circumradius $\frac{\mu}{\sqrt2}=0.9r<r$, which lies in the open incircle, hence in the interior of $\Delta\subseteq\mathcal Q$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AC$, and let $N$ be a point on the circumcircle of triangle $PXY$ such that $MN$ is tangent to it. Prove that $M$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment $AN$.

The parallelogram is symmetric about $M$, which maps $A,B,C$ to $C,D,A$; hence $\omega_2$ is the reflection of $\omega_1$ in $M$. The right side is $2\operatorname{pow}_k(Z)$.

Put $p_1=\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_1}(P)=\overrightarrow{PB}\cdot\overrightarrow{PX}$ and $p_2=\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_2}(P)=\overrightarrow{PD}\cdot\overrightarrow{PY}$ (signed products along the lines). Use vectors with origin $P$: $\vec X=\frac{p_1}{|\vec B|^2}\vec B$ and $\vec Y=\frac{p_2}{|\vec D|^2}\vec D$. The inversion with centre $P$ and power $1$ (written $\vec Z\mapsto\vec Z/|\vec Z|^2$) maps $X,Y$ to $\vec X^*=\vec B/p_1$, $\vec Y^*=\vec D/p_2$, and maps $\Gamma$ to the line $X^*Y^*$.

Problem 5

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $k$ be a positive integer. Some cells of a $(2k+1)\times(2k+1)$ board are colored blue so that a chess king can reach any blue cell from any other blue cell while moving only through blue cells. In one move, the king may move to a cell sharing a side or a vertex with its current cell. In terms of $k$, find the largest positive integer $d$ for which it is possible to have two blue cells such that the king cannot get from one to the other in fewer than $d$ moves while moving through blue cells.

Show the answer

$d=2k(k+1)$

Show the solution – Counting path edges in blocks

Reduction. Take two blue cells at blue distance $d$ and a shortest blue path between them; keeping only its cells blue does not decrease distances. A shortest path is induced: two of its cells are king-adjacent only if they are consecutive. Conversely, colouring exactly the cells of an induced path makes its endpoints $d$ apart, $d$ its number of edges. So $d$ is the largest number of edges of an induced path in the king graph of the board.

Construction. Number rows and columns $0,\dots,2k$. For $0\le j<k$ put a connector in row $2j+1$, in column $2k$ for even $j$ and column $0$ for odd $j$. In each even row use every cell except those directly above or below a connector. Each even row is then one horizontal interval; each connector touches exactly one used cell in each neighbouring even row (diagonally, at the end of the interval); cells in different even rows, or in different connector rows, are never adjacent. So the cells form an induced path with $(k+1)(2k+1)-2k+k=2k(k+1)+1$ cells, i.e. $2k(k+1)$ edges.

What to count. Call a cell special if both coordinates are odd, and an edge (pair of king-adjacent cells) regular if neither end is special. Two special cells are never adjacent, so if the path $P$ contains $S$ special cells and $R$ regular edges, its length is at most $2S+R$ (each special cell is on at most two path edges). We cover the board by blocks; each block designates some special cells and some regular edges, and its weight is the number of designated special cells on $P$ plus the number of designated regular edges on $P$.

Small block. A $2\times2$ square with an even-even corner $p$ and the opposite corner special; it designates the special cell and the three edges among the other three cells. Any three cells of a $2\times2$ square are pairwise adjacent, so $P$ has at most two cells there and the weight is at most $1$.

Large block. A $3\times3$ square with rows $(a,p,b)$, $(s,q,t)$, $(c,r,e)$, where $p,r$ are even-even and $s,t$ special (or a rotation of this). It designates $s,t$, the six edges from $q$ to $a,p,b,c,r,e$, and the edges $pa,pb$. If $q\in P$: an edge $pa$ or $pb$ of $P$ would form a triangle with $q$, and a special cell $s$ or $t$ on $P$ must be joined to $q$ by a path edge; so the weight is at most the degree of $q$ in $P$, i.e. at most $2$. If $q\notin P$: only $pa,pb$ can count among the edges; $pa\in P$ excludes $s$ (triangle $p,a,s$) and $pb\in P$ excludes $t$; so again the weight is at most $2$.

Placement. At each even-even cell $p=(x,y)$ compare its distances $|x-k|$, $|y-k|$ to the centre. If they differ, place a large block with $p$ as the end of its axis $p,q,r$, the axis pointing two steps toward the centre along the coordinate with the larger distance. If they are equal and positive, place a small block at $p$ pointing toward the centre in both coordinates. At the centre (only when $k$ is even) place nothing. All blocks lie inside the board: the axis has room for two inward steps because the board ends at even coordinates, and in the perpendicular direction $p$ is not on the boundary (its distance there is less than $k$).

Coverage. Horizontal and vertical regular edges. By symmetry consider the edge from an even-even $p$ to $p+(1,0)$. A large block at $p$ pointing up or down designates both horizontal edges $pa,pb$ at $p$; one pointing right designates $pq$, this edge; a small block pointing right designates it too. Otherwise $p$ is the centre or points left, so $x\ge k$ and $x-k\ge|y-k|$. Then $p+(2,0)$ is on the board, its horizontal distance strictly exceeds its vertical one, and its large block points left with $q=p+(1,0)$, $r=p$: it designates the edge $qr$.

Special cells and diagonal regular edges. Every special cell is the centre of a square with even-even corners at coordinate distance $2$, and every regular diagonal edge joins the midpoints of two neighbouring sides of such a square. Rotate and reflect so that this square is no farther from the top side of the board than from the other sides; then its upper-left corner $(x,y)$ satisfies $y\le x\le 2k-2-y$ and $y\le k-1$. If $x>y$, the block at the upper-left corner is large and points down: it designates the centre of the square and both diagonals on the left side of the square. If $x=y$, its small block points down and right, designating the centre and the diagonal near the upper-left corner, and the diagonal near the lower-left corner is designated by the block at $(y,y+2)$: a large block pointing right if $y<k-1$, a small block pointing up and right if $y=k-1$. Reflecting left-to-right handles the right side and designates the centre once more, at the upper-right corner. So every special cell is designated at least twice and every regular edge at least once.

Count. Hence $2S+R$ is at most the total weight bound: $1$ per small block and $2$ per large block. There are $(k+1)^2$ even-even cells, $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$ of them at the centre ($\varepsilon=1$ iff $k$ is even), and $4\lceil k/2\rceil$ small blocks (the diagonal corners of the layers at inset $0,2,4,\dots<k$). The bound is \[2\bigl((k+1)^2-\varepsilon\bigr)-4\lceil k/2\rceil=2(k+1)^2-2(k+1)=2k(k+1),\] using $2\lceil k/2\rceil+\varepsilon=k+1$. Together with the construction, $d=2k(k+1)$.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Let $m$ and $n$ be positive integers. There are $m+n$ points in general position in the plane, meaning that no three of them are collinear. Of these, $n$ are red and the remaining $m$ are blue. Prove that the points can be joined by segments so that each segment joins a red point to a blue point, no two segments cross, and there is a path consisting of such segments between every two points.

Show solution 1 – Split along a bichromatic line

We prove by induction on $m+n$ that the points can be joined by non-crossing red–blue segments forming a connected graph (a "good drawing").

Base: $m=1$ or $n=1$. Join the single point of one colour to all points of the other colour. These segments share an endpoint and no three points are collinear, so they do not cross.

Step: $m,n\ge2$. Claim: there are a red point $R$ and a blue point $B$ such that both open half-planes bounded by line $RB$ contain points of the set. If some point is not a vertex of the convex hull, every line through it has points on both sides; pair it with any point of the other colour. Otherwise all points are in convex position, and a line through two hull vertices has points on both sides unless the two vertices are adjacent on the hull. If every red–blue pair were adjacent, then a red point, having only two hull neighbours, would see at most $2$ blue points, so $m\le2$, and similarly $n\le2$; with $m=n=2$ in convex position the colours alternate around the hull, and we can simply take three consecutive hull sides as the drawing. Otherwise the claim holds.

Let $H_1,H_2$ be the two closed half-planes of line $RB$. The sets $S_i=(\text{points in }H_i)$ each contain $R$, $B$ and at least one more point, and each has fewer points than the whole set and both colours. By induction each $S_i$ has a good drawing $\mathcal D_i$; all its segments lie in $H_i$. Take the union $\mathcal D_1\cup\mathcal D_2$. A segment of $\mathcal D_1$ and one of $\mathcal D_2$ can meet only on the line $RB$; the only points of the set on that line are $R$ and $B$, and a segment meets the line only at an endpoint of it lying on the line (or is the segment $RB$ itself, which may appear in both drawings and then is one segment). So segments meet only at common endpoints. Both drawings are connected and share $R$, so the union is connected. This completes the induction.

Show solution 2 – A fan from a vertex of the convex hull

The statement is symmetric in the two colours, so we may rename them. Let $R$ be a vertex of the convex hull of all $m+n$ points and choose the names so that $R$ is red. Because $R$ is a vertex of the hull, all other points lie in a closed angle $W$ with vertex $R$ and opening less than $180^\circ$ (bounded by the rays from $R$ through its two neighbours on the hull).

The segments. Join $R$ to each of the $m\ge1$ blue points; call these segments spokes. No two blue points lie on one ray from $R$ (no three points are collinear), so the rays from $R$ through the blue points are distinct and cut $W$ into closed convex angular regions with vertex $R$, the sectors; each sector is bounded by two rays, at least one of which passes through a blue point. No red point $Q\ne R$ lies on a ray through a blue point. In every sector that contains red points other than $R$, choose one blue point $b$ on a bounding ray of that sector and join every red point of the sector to $b$.

No two segments cross. Let $Qb$ be one of the new segments, with $Q$ in the sector $S$. Since $S$ is convex with opening less than $180^\circ$, and $Q$ and $b$ lie on the boundary of $S$ only if they are on different bounding rays, every point of $Qb$ other than $Q$ and $b$ lies in the interior of $S$. The spokes lie on the bounding rays of the sectors.

  • Two new segments in different sectors have their inner points in disjoint open regions, so they can meet only at endpoints.

  • Two new segments in the same sector share the endpoint $b$ and are not collinear, so they meet only at $b$.

  • A new segment $Qb$ and a spoke $Rb'$: the spoke lies on a bounding ray, which $Qb$ meets at most at $b$ (and at $Q$ if $Q$ lies on the boundary of $W$, where there is no spoke). If $b'=b$ this is a common endpoint; if $b'\ne b$, then $b$ is not on the segment $Rb'$, which lies on a different ray.

  • Two spokes meet only at $R$.

Connectedness. Every blue point is joined to $R$, and every red point other than $R$ is joined to a blue point. So any two points are connected through $R$. Every segment joins a red point to a blue point, which completes the proof (the $m+n-1$ segments even form a tree).

Problem 7

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

Let $n>1$ be a positive integer, and let $q$ be its largest prime divisor. Sashka writes on a board all possible sums of some of the divisors of $n$, possibly just one divisor. It turns out that at least $q+1$ of the numbers not exceeding $q+2$ are written on the board, and none of these numbers is repeated. Prove that no two numbers on the board are equal.

Show the solution – The hypothesis forces $n=2^a$ or $n=2^aq^b$ with $q=2^{a+1}+1$

Sums are of distinct divisors. Only divisors $\le q+2$ matter for the numbers $1,\dots,q+2$, and by hypothesis at most one of these $q+2$ numbers is missing from the board, and none is written twice.

$n$ is even. Suppose $n$ is odd; then $q\ge3$ and every divisor is odd. The number $2$ is not a sum of distinct odd divisors, so it is the missing number and $3,4,\dots,q+2$ are all written. $3$ can only be the divisor $3$, so $3\mid n$. If $q=3$, then $n=3^b$ and $5$ is not written either — two missing numbers. So $q\ge5$; then $5$ is written, and since $1+3=4$ and any sum of three distinct odd divisors is at least $1+3+5$, $5$ must be a divisor. Likewise $7\le q+2$ must be written, and a sum of distinct odd divisors equal to $7$ must be the divisor $7$ itself ($1+3+5=9$). But then $8=1+7=3+5$ is written twice, with $8\le q+2$ unless $q=5$; and for $q=5$ the number $7$ is missing as well as $2$. Contradiction.

Write $n=2^am$ with $a\ge1$, $m$ odd. If $q=2$, $n=2^a$; its divisors are distinct powers of $2$, so all subset sums are distinct (binary representation), as claimed. Let $q\ge3$. The divisors $1,2,\dots,2^a$ give every number from $1$ to $B=2^{a+1}-1$ exactly once. An odd divisor $d>1$ with $d\le B$ would be written twice; also $q$ itself is written as a divisor, so $q>B$, i.e. $2^{a+1}\le q-1$. Hence all odd divisors greater than $1$ exceed $B$, and all other divisors that are not powers of $2$ are at least twice an odd divisor. Consequently the number $2^{a+1}\le q+2$ is not written: it is the missing number, and everything else up to $q+2$ is written.

The prime $r=2^{a+1}+1$. The number $2^{a+1}+1\le q+2$ is written. A sum equal to it exceeds $B$, so it uses a divisor that is not a power of $2$; that divisor is odd and at least $2^{a+1}+1$, so it equals $2^{a+1}+1$. Thus $r=2^{a+1}+1$ divides $n$, and $r$ is the least odd divisor greater than $1$, hence a prime.

$q=r$. The divisors $1,2,\dots,2^a,r$ give exactly the sums in $[1,r-2]\cup[r,2r-2]$. Suppose $q>r$.

  • If $q\le 2r-2$, then $q=r+(q-r)$ with $1\le q-r\le r-2=B$, so $q$ is written twice.

  • If $q=2r-1$, then $q+1=2r$ is a divisor and also equals $q+1$: written twice.

  • If $q\ge2r$, the number $2r-1\le q$ must be written; it exceeds $2r-2$, so it needs another divisor $d\le 2r-1$. Even divisors other than powers of $2$ are at least $2r$; an odd divisor $d$ with $r<d\le 2r-2$ would be written twice as $r+(d-r)$; a composite odd divisor is at least $r^2>2r-1$. So $2r-1$ is a prime divisor, and then $2r=(2r-1)+1$ is written twice ($2r$ is a divisor and $2r\le q$).

Hence $q=r$ and $n=2^aq^b$ with $q=2^{a+1}+1>\sigma(2^a)$.

All sums are distinct. List the divisors $2^iq^j$ in increasing order. For each divisor $2^iq^j$, the divisors smaller than it are the $2^{i'}q^{j'}$ with $j'<j$, and the $2^{i'}q^{j}$ with $i'<i$; their sum is \[\sigma(2^a)\frac{q^j-1}{q-1}+(2^i-1)q^j\le (q^j-1)+(2^i-1)q^j<2^iq^j .\] So every divisor exceeds the sum of all smaller divisors. Then two different sets of divisors have different sums (compare the largest divisor in which they differ), i.e. no two numbers on the board are equal.

Show the solution – Two cases for f(0)

$c\in\{0,1\}$. $E(0,0)$ gives $f(c^2)=0$; then $E(c^2,y)$ gives $f(0)=c^4$, so $c=c^4$ and $c\in\{0,1\}$.

Case $c=1$. Then $f(1)=f(c^2)=0$ and $E(0,y)$ gives $f(f(y))=y$: $f$ is a bijective involution. For any $x$ choose $y=f(-x)$ in $E(x,y)$: since $f(y)=-x$, we get $1=f(0)=x^2+f(-x)f(x)$, i.e. \[f(x)f(-x)=1-x^2 .\tag{1}\] $E(x,0)$ reads $f\bigl(f(x)(x+1)\bigr)=x^2$; applying $f$ gives $f(x)(x+1)=f(x^2)$, and with $-x$ in place of $x$, $f(-x)(1-x)=f(x^2)$. Hence $f(-x)=f(x)\frac{1+x}{1-x}$ for $x\ne1$, and (1) gives $f(x)^2=(1-x)^2$ for $x\ne\pm1$. So $f(x)=\pm(1-x)$. If $f(x)=x-1$ for some $x\notin\{0,1,2,-1\}$, then $f(x-1)=x$ (involution) while $f(x-1)=\pm(2-x)$, which forces $x=1$: impossible. The remaining values: $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=0$; $f(2)\ne1$ because $f(1)=0\ne2$, so $f(2)=-1$; and $f(-1)=f(f(2))=2$. Hence $f(x)=1-x$.

Case $c=0$. If $f(t)=0$ then $E(t,0)$ gives $0=f(0)=t^2$, so $f(t)=0$ only for $t=0$. For $x\ne0$ the right side of $E(x,y)$ takes every real value as $y$ varies, so $f$ is surjective; and $f(y_1)=f(y_2)$ gives $y_1f(x)=y_2f(x)$, so $f$ is injective. $E(x,0)$ gives $f(xf(x))=x^2=f(-xf(-x))$, so $f(-x)=-f(x)$. If $f$ is a solution, so is $-f$ (substitute and use oddness). With $a=f(1)$, $E(1,0)$ gives $f(a)=1$ and $E(a,0)$ gives $f(a)=a^2$, so $a=\pm1$; replacing $f$ by $-f$ if necessary, we may assume $f(1)=1$. Let $g=f^{-1}$.

  • $E(1,y)$: $f(1+f(y))=1+y$, i.e. $f(1+t)=1+g(t)$ for all $t$; equivalently $g(1+t)=1+f(t)$.

  • $E(x,y)$ with $f(y)=-x$ (so $y=g(-x)=-g(x)$): $0=x^2-g(x)f(x)$, so $f(t)g(t)=t^2$ for all $t$.

Applying the second item at $1+t$ and using the first: $(1+g(t))(1+f(t))=(1+t)^2$. Subtracting $f(t)g(t)=t^2$ gives $f(t)+g(t)=2t$. So $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ are the roots of $z^2-2tz+t^2=(z-t)^2$, and $f(t)=t$. Undoing the normalisation, $f(x)=x$ or $f(x)=-x$.

Boris asks $S_1,S_2,\dots$ until some $|L_j|\ge46$, which gives an increasing subsequence, or until he has asked $45$ questions. In the latter case $|L_1|+\dots+|L_{45}|\le45\cdot45=2025$, so some position $x_{46}\in S_{46}$ remains. For $j=45,44,\dots,1$ let $x_j$ be the last position of $L_j$ before $x_{j+1}$. This exists and $p_{x_j}>p_{x_{j+1}}$: as $x_{j+1}\in S_{j+1}\subseteq S_j$ is not leading in $S_j$, the largest value at positions of $S_j$ before $x_{j+1}$ exceeds $p_{x_{j+1}}$, and that maximum sits at the last leading position before $x_{j+1}$. Also $x_j\in L_j\subseteq S_j=S_{j-1}\setminus L_{j-1}$, so the next step works. Thus $x_1<\dots<x_{46}$ with $p_{x_1}>\dots>p_{x_{46}}$, all determined by the answers.

44 questions do not suffice. Let the arrangement be decreasing. Then every answer is “only the first position of $S$ is leading”. These answers are also produced by every arrangement in which, for each asked $S$, the value at the first position of $S$ is the largest in $S$: such arrangements are exactly the linear extensions of the partial order generated by the relations “the first position of $S$ beats every other position of $S$”. Boris's output must be correct for all of them.

  • An increasing pair $i<j$ is never forced: every generated relation says that an earlier position has a larger value, and in the decreasing arrangement $p_i>p_j$.

  • A decreasing $46$-term subsequence needs $45$ forced relations, which chain together into a path $i_{46}<\dots<i_1$ (in values) in the generated order. Each step of a shortest such path is “an element of $S$ is below the first position of $S$” for an asked $S$, and the same $S$ cannot be used twice (the path would pass through the first position of $S$ twice). So at least $45$ questions are needed.

  • “No such positions exist” is false.

Hence the smallest $q$ is $\boxed{45}$.

Problem 7

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Multi-Method Diophantine Problems

Find all positive integers $k$ for which there exists a strictly increasing arithmetic progression of four positive integers $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ such that the product $abcd$ is a perfect $k$th power of a positive integer.

Show the answer

all odd $k$

Show the solution – Scaling for odd $k$; Euler's theorem for even $k$

Odd $k$ work. Start from $1,2,3,4$ with product $24$. For an integer $t\ge1$ the progression $t,2t,3t,4t$ is strictly increasing and has product $24t^4$. Since $k$ is odd, $\gcd(4,k)=1$, so there is $j\ge0$ with $4j+1\equiv0\pmod k$. With $t=24^j$ the product is $24^{4j+1}$, a perfect $k$-th power.

Even $k$ fail. If $abcd$ is a $k$-th power with $k$ even, it is a perfect square. But a classical theorem of Euler states that the product of four consecutive terms of a non-constant arithmetic progression of positive integers is never a perfect square; see L. E. Dickson, History of the Theory of Numbers, Vol. II (Diophantine Analysis), Chelsea, New York, 1966, pp. 440 and 635, and, for a modern treatment with generalisations, N. Hirata-Kohno, S. Laishram, T. N. Shorey and R. Tijdeman, An extension of a theorem of Euler, Acta Arithmetica 129 (2007). Hence no even $k$ works, and the answer is: all odd $k$.

Remark (structure of Euler's proof, not needed above). Dividing by $\gcd(a,r)^4$, where $r$ is the common difference, one may assume $\gcd(a,r)=1$. Then $abcd=X(X+2r^2)$ with $X=a(a+3r)$ and $\gcd(X,X+2r^2)\mid2$, so both factors are squares or both are twice squares; each case is reduced, by infinite descent, to quartic equations such as $x^4-x^2y^2+y^4=z^2$ that have only trivial solutions.

Problem 8

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Fermat and Euler Type Results

Prove that, for every positive integer $a$, there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ for which $a\cdot n!+1$ is composite.

Show the solution – Wilson backwards

A Wilson identity. For a prime $p$ and $1\le k<p$, $(p-1)!=(p-k)!\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(p-j)\equiv(p-k)!\,(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\pmod p$, and $(p-1)!\equiv-1$, so \[(p-k)!\equiv\frac{(-1)^k}{(k-1)!}\pmod p\qquad(p>k).\] Hence if $p>k$ is a prime with $(k-1)!\equiv(-1)^{k+1}a\pmod p$, then $a\,(p-k)!\equiv-1$, i.e. $p\mid a\,(p-k)!+1$.

Choice of $k$ and $p$. Let $k\ge2a+2$ be composite and not of the form $a\cdot m!+1-m$ ($m\ge1$); infinitely many such $k$ exist, since the numbers $a\cdot m!+1-m$ grow fast. Then $(k-1)!$ is divisible by $a$, and $T=\frac{(k-1)!}{a}+(-1)^k$ is divisible by no prime $\le k-1$: for a prime $\ell\le k-1$ not dividing $a$ this is clear as $\ell\mid\frac{(k-1)!}a$, and for $\ell\mid a$ we have $v_\ell((k-1)!)\ge v_\ell((2a)!)\ge v_\ell(a)+1$. As $T\ge2$, it has a prime factor $p\ge k$, and $p\ne k$ because $k$ is composite. From $p\mid T$ we get $p\mid (k-1)!+(-1)^ka$, i.e. $(k-1)!\equiv(-1)^{k+1}a\pmod p$. Put $n=p-k\ge1$; then $p\mid a\,n!+1$.

Composite. If $a\,n!+1=p$, then $k=p-n=a\,n!+1-n$, which we excluded. So $a\,n!+1$ is a multiple of $p$ larger than $p$, hence composite.

Infinitely many $n$. For a fixed $n$, the prime $p$ divides the fixed number $a\,n!+1$, so only finitely many pairs $(k,p)$ give this $n$. Infinitely many admissible $k$ therefore give infinitely many different $n$.

Problem 1

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Injectivity and Surjectivity

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that \[f\bigl(x^2+f(y)\bigr)=f\bigl(f(y)-x^2\bigr)+f(xy)\] for all real numbers $x$ and $y$.

Show the answer

$f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=\frac{x^2}4$

Show the solution – Injectivity on the positive reals

The two solutions. $f\equiv0$ works. For $f(x)=\frac{x^2}4$, \[f\bigl(x^2+f(y)\bigr)-f\bigl(f(y)-x^2\bigr)=\frac{(x^2+\frac{y^2}4)^2-(\frac{y^2}4-x^2)^2}4=\frac{x^2y^2}4=f(xy).\] Now let $f$ be any solution.

First facts. With $x=0$ we get $f(0)=0$. With $y=0$ we get $f(x^2)=f(-x^2)$, so $f$ is even. Put $t=x^2\ge0$ and $a=f(y)$; since $f(xy)=f(|x|y)$ by evenness, the equation reads \[f(a+t)-f(a-t)=f\bigl(y\sqrt t\bigr)\qquad\text{for all real }y\text{ and }t\ge0,\ a=f(y).\tag{1}\]

Step 1: zeros. If $f(y_0)=0$ for some $y_0\ne0$, then (1) with $y=y_0$ has left side $f(t)-f(-t)=0$, so $f(y_0\sqrt t)=0$ for all $t\ge0$; with evenness, $f\equiv0$. From now on assume $f\not\equiv0$, so $f(y)\ne0$ for every $y\ne0$.

Step 2: $f$ is injective on $(0,\infty)$. Suppose $f(u)=f(v)$ with $0<u<v$. Then (1) for $y=u$ and for $y=v$ has the same left side, so $f(u\sqrt t)=f(v\sqrt t)$ for all $t\ge0$. With $\lambda=\frac vu>1$ this says $f(\lambda w)=f(w)$ for all $w\ge0$. Let $a=f(1)\ne0$ and $t=|a|\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda+1}$, so that $0<t<|a|$ and $|a|+t=\lambda\,(|a|-t)$. By evenness, the two numbers $f(a+t),f(a-t)$ are $f(|a|+t)$ and $f(|a|-t)$ in some order, and these are equal because $f(\lambda w)=f(w)$ for $w=|a|-t$. So the left side of (1) with $y=1$ is $0$, and $f(\sqrt t)=0$ with $\sqrt t\ne0$, contradicting Step 1.

Step 3: $f(y)>0$ for $y\ne0$. Suppose $a=f(y)<0$. Then (1) with $t=-a$ gives $f(0)-f(2a)=f(y\sqrt{-a})$, so by evenness $f(u)=-f(v)$ with $u=2|a|>0$ and $v=|y|\sqrt{|a|}>0$. By Step 1, $f(u)\ne0$, so $u\ne v$. Now compare (1) for $y=u$ and for $y=v$: writing $b=f(u)$, so that $f(v)=-b$, evenness gives \[f(-b+t)-f(-b-t)=f(b-t)-f(b+t)=-\bigl(f(b+t)-f(b-t)\bigr),\] so $f(v\sqrt t)=-f(u\sqrt t)$ for all $t\ge0$. With $\lambda=\frac uv\ne1$ this reads $f(\lambda w)=-f(w)$ for all $w\ge0$ (put $\sqrt t=\frac wv$). Applying it twice, $f(\lambda^2w)=f(w)$ for $w>0$, where $\lambda^2w\ne w$: this contradicts Step 2. Hence $f(y)>0$ for all $y\ne0$.

Step 4: the formula. Let $y\ne0$ and $a=f(y)>0$. Then (1) with $t=a$ gives $f(2a)=f(0)+f(y\sqrt a)=f\bigl(|y|\sqrt a\bigr)$. Both arguments are positive, so Step 2 gives $2a=|y|\sqrt a$, that is, $\sqrt a=\frac{|y|}2$ and $f(y)=\frac{y^2}4$. Together with $f(0)=0$, $f(x)=\frac{x^2}4$ for all $x$.

Therefore the solutions are exactly $f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=\frac{x^2}4$.

Problem 2

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Hybrid Congruence and Factorization Arguments

For each positive integer greater than $1$ with prime factorization $p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_s^{\alpha_s}$, where $p_1,p_2,\dots,p_s$ are distinct primes, its sequence of exponents consists of the numbers $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_s$ arranged in ascending order. For example, for $3000=2^3\cdot 3\cdot 5^3$, this sequence is $1,3,3$. Positive integers $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are such that, for every positive integer $n$, the numbers $An+B$ and $Cn+D$ have the same sequence of exponents. Prove that there exists a rational number $k$ such that $An+B=k(Cn+D)$ for every $n$.

Show the solution – A large prime power on one side, a sieve on the other

It suffices to show $AD=BC$: then $An+B=\frac AC(Cn+D)$ for all $n$. Suppose $\Delta=AD-BC\ne0$.

Setup. Let $g=\gcd(A,B)$, $A=gA'$, $B=gB'$, and fix an integer $e\ge2+v_q(g)$ for every prime $q$. Choose a prime $p$ larger than $A,B,C,D,|\Delta|$ and $g$. Pick $n_0$ with $Cn_0+D\equiv p^e\pmod{p^{e+1}}$ (possible as $p\nmid C$). For every $n\equiv n_0\pmod{p^{e+1}}$ we have $v_p(Cn+D)=e$, so $e$ occurs in the exponent sequence of $Cn+D$; and $p\nmid An+B$, since otherwise $p$ would divide $A(Cn+D)-C(An+B)=\Delta$.

Goal. Find such an $n$ for which every exponent of $An+B$ is less than $e$; then the two exponent sequences differ, a contradiction. For a prime $q\ne p$: $v_q(An+B)=v_q(g)+v_q(A'n+B')$. If $q\mid A'$ then $q\nmid B'$ and $v_q(An+B)=v_q(g)<e$. If $q\nmid A'$, then $v_q(An+B)\ge e$ means $q^{f}\mid A'n+B'$ with $f=e-v_q(g)\ge2$, which (together with $n\equiv n_0$) is one residue class modulo $p^{e+1}q^{f}$.

Counting. Among $n\le X$ with $n\equiv n_0\pmod{p^{e+1}}$ there are at least $\frac X{p^{e+1}}-1$ numbers. A prime $q$ can only spoil some $n\le X$ if $q^2\le q^f\le A'X+B'$, and then spoils at most $\frac{X}{p^{e+1}q^2}+1$ of them. So at most \[\frac{X}{p^{e+1}}\sum_q\frac1{q^2}+\sqrt{A'X+B'}<\frac{X}{2p^{e+1}}+\sqrt{A'X+B'}\] numbers are spoiled, where we used $\sum_q\frac1{q^2}<\frac14+\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1{(2j+1)^2}<\frac14+\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1{4j(j+1)}=\frac12$. For large $X$ this is less than $\frac X{p^{e+1}}-1$, so a good $n$ exists. This contradiction proves $AD=BC$, and $k=\frac AC$ is the required rational number.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

The plane is divided into unit squares colored in $n$ different colors. A polygon $P$ consists of $n$ unit squares joined along their sides, and its boundary is a connected polygonal line. It is known that every polygon obtained from $P$ by a translation covers $n$ squares of different colors. Prove that the plane can be covered with copies of $P$ so that each cell is covered exactly once.

Show the solution – Rainbow translates of the reflected shape

Identify cells with points of $\mathbb Z^2$ and let $P=\{p_1,\dots,p_n\}$. For a vector $v$, the translate $P+v$ covers the cells $p_j+v$.

Key fact. If two cells $u\ne w$ satisfy $u-w=p_i-p_j$ for some $i,j$, they have different colours: they are the cells $p_i+v$ and $p_j+v$ of the translate with $v=w-p_j$, which is rainbow.

The reflected shape is rainbow too. For any cell $z$ consider the $n$ cells $z-p_1,\dots,z-p_n$. The difference of two of them is $p_j-p_i$, so by the key fact they have $n$ different colours, i.e. all $n$ colours, each exactly once.

The tiling. Fix colour $1$ and use all translates $P+v$ for which the cell $p_1+v$ has colour $1$. A cell $z$ is covered by $P+v$ iff $z=p_j+v$ for some $j$, i.e. $v=z-p_j$, and this translate is used iff the cell $z-p_j+p_1$ has colour $1$. The cells $z+p_1-p_j$ ($j=1,\dots,n$) are $z'-p_j$ with $z'=z+p_1$, which by the previous paragraph contain colour $1$ exactly once. So every cell is covered by exactly one of the chosen translates.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Sequences with Arithmetic Constraints

A finite sequence of positive integers is formed as follows: its last number is $a$, and each number after the first is the preceding number minus the sum of its digits. Prove that, for every positive integer $n$, there is a positive integer $a$ for which such a sequence can be formed with $n$ terms, including $a$ itself as the last term, but not with $n+1$ terms.

Show the solution – Longest chains, and a gap below each power of 10

Let $S(x)$ be the digit sum of $x$ and $f(x)=x-S(x)$. A sequence as in the problem is $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_N=a$ with $x_{j+1}=f(x_j)$, all positive. Let $L(a)\in\{1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$ be the supremum of the numbers $N$ of terms of such sequences. Dropping initial terms of a sequence gives shorter ones, so we must find, for each $n$, a number $b$ with $L(b)=n$. For $n=1$ take $b=1$: every value $f(x)$ is a multiple of $9$ (as $x\equiv S(x)\pmod 9$), so nothing precedes $1$. Let $n\ge2$.

Three facts about $f$.

  • (a) $f$ is non-decreasing: $f(x+1)-f(x)=1-\bigl(S(x+1)-S(x)\bigr)=9t\ge0$, where $t$ is the number of trailing nines of $x$.

  • (b) Each number has finitely many preimages: $f(x)=a$ gives $x=a+S(x)\le a+9\cdot(\text{number of digits of }x)$, which bounds $x$.

  • (c) A gap. For $m\ge1$ let $g_m=f(10^{m+1}-1)=10^{m+1}-10-9m$. By (a), $f(x)\le g_m$ for $x\le10^{m+1}-1$ and $f(x)\ge f(10^{m+1})=10^{m+1}-1$ for $x\ge10^{m+1}$. So no number strictly between $g_m$ and $10^{m+1}-1$ is a value of $f$.

Step 1: inside a longest sequence the values of $L$ are exact. Let $L(a)=M<\infty$ and let $y_1>\dots>y_M=a$ be a sequence with $M$ terms. Then $L(y_j)=j$ for every $j$: the terms $y_1,\dots,y_j$ show $L(y_j)\ge j$, and any sequence ending at $y_j$ can be continued by $y_{j+1},\dots,y_M$, so $L(y_j)+(M-j)\le M$. Hence it suffices to find $a$ with $n\le L(a)<\infty$; then $b=y_n$ has $L(b)=n$.

Step 2: when $L(a)$ is infinite. Suppose $L(a)=\infty$. The sequences ending at $a$, read backwards, form a tree with root $a$ in which the children of a number are its preimages; by (b) every vertex has finitely many children, and there are arbitrarily long paths from the root. By Kőnig's lemma there is an infinite path $a=u_0<u_1<u_2<\cdots$ with $f(u_{i+1})=u_i$, so every $u_i$ is a value of $f$.

Claim: if $a<g_m$, then $g_m=u_i$ for some $i$, i.e. $a=f^{i}(g_m)$. The $u_i$ are increasing integers, so some $u_i\ge10^{m+1}-1$; take the least such $i$ ($i\ge1$). Then $u_{i-1}<10^{m+1}-1$ is a value of $f$, so $u_{i-1}\le g_m$ by (c). If $u_i\ge10^{m+1}$, then $u_{i-1}=f(u_i)\ge10^{m+1}-1$ by (c), which is false. Hence $u_i=10^{m+1}-1$ and $u_{i-1}=f(u_i)=g_m$.

So: if $a<g_m$ and $a$ is not of the form $f^{i}(g_m)$, then $L(a)<\infty$.

Step 3: numbers just below $10^{m+1}$. Let $C=9(m+1)$. For $0\le z<10^{m+1}$ the number $10^{m+1}-1-z$ is obtained from $99\dots9$ ($m+1$ nines) by subtracting $z$ digit by digit, without borrowing, so its digit sum is $C-S(z)$ and \[f\bigl(10^{m+1}-1-z\bigr)=10^{m+1}-1-\Phi(z),\qquad \Phi(z)=z+C-S(z).\] By (a), $\Phi$ is non-decreasing. The number $10^{m+1}-1$ corresponds to $z=0$, $g_m$ to $\Phi(0)=C$, and $f^{i}(g_m)$ to $\Phi^{i+1}(0)$.

Fix $n\ge2$, let $d$ be the number of digits of $nC$, and choose $m$ so large that $\frac C2-1>9d(n-1)$ and $nC<10^{m+1}$ (possible, since $C$ grows linearly in $m$ and $d$ only logarithmically). Put $z_0=\lfloor C/2\rfloor$, $x_1=10^{m+1}-1-z_0$ and $x_{j+1}=f(x_j)$; then $x_j$ corresponds to $\Phi^{j-1}(z_0)$. All numbers $\Phi^{i}(0),\Phi^i(z_0),\Phi^i(C)$ with $i\le n-1$ lie between $0$ and $nC$ (as $\Phi(z)\le z+C$), so their digit sums are at most $9d$, and $\Phi(z)>z$ for them. Since every application of $\Phi$ adds $C$ and subtracts a digit sum, \[\Phi^{n-1}(z_0)-\Phi^{n-1}(0)\ge z_0-9d(n-1)>0,\qquad \Phi^{n}(0)-\Phi^{n-1}(z_0)=\Phi^{n-1}(C)-\Phi^{n-1}(z_0)\ge (C-z_0)-9d(n-1)>0 .\] The numbers $\Phi^{i}(0)$ increase with $i$, and $\Phi^{n-1}(z_0)$ lies strictly between two consecutive ones, so it is none of them. Translating back: $a=x_n$ is not of the form $f^{i}(g_m)$, and $a<g_m$ because $\Phi^{n-1}(z_0)>\Phi^{n-1}(0)\ge C$. By Step 2, $L(a)<\infty$, while $x_1>\dots>x_n=a$ shows $L(a)\ge n$. By Step 1 the $n$-th term $b$ of a longest sequence ending at $a$ satisfies $L(b)=n$: a sequence ending at $b$ can be formed with $n$ terms but not with $n+1$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n\ge 2$ be a positive integer. In an $n\times n$ grid, the cells on the main diagonal from top left to bottom right are black, and the remaining $n^2-n$ cells are white. The white region is partitioned into rectangles so that each white cell belongs to exactly one rectangle and no black cell belongs to a rectangle. Find the minimum possible sum of the perimeters of the rectangles.

Show the answer

$4\bigl(nL-2^{L}+n\bigr)$ with $L=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ (e.g. $8,20,32,48,64$ for $n=2,3,4,5,6$)

Show the solution – Hooks and a Kraft inequality

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,n$; write a rectangle as $I\times J$ (row interval $I$, column interval $J$). A rectangle avoiding the diagonal has $I\cap J=\emptyset$ (otherwise it contains a cell $(t,t)$), so it lies entirely above the diagonal ($\max I<\min J$) or entirely below it. The cells above the diagonal form the staircase $\Sigma_n=\{(i,j):1\le i<j\le n\}$, and those below form its transpose. The perimeter of $I\times J$ is $2(|I|+|J|)$, so the answer is $4h(n)$, where $h(n)$ is the minimum of $\sum(|I|+|J|)$ over all partitions of $\Sigma_n$ into rectangles. Let $L=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ and $T(n)=nL-2^L+n$. We show $h(n)=T(n)$.

Hooks. The hook $H_i$ of an index $i$ consists of the cells $(i,j)$, $j>i$, and $(j,i)$, $j<i$. For a partition of $\Sigma_n$, let $\ell_i$ be the number of its rectangles meeting $H_i$. A rectangle $I\times J$ meets $H_i$ exactly when $i\in I\cup J$, and $I\cap J=\emptyset$, so \[\sum_{\text{rectangles}}\bigl(|I|+|J|\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i .\] The same definitions apply to the staircase $\Sigma_{[p,q]}=\{(i,j):p\le i<j\le q\}$ on any index interval $[p,q]$. Intersecting the rectangles of a partition of $\Sigma_{[p,q]}$ with the square $[p',q']\times[p',q']$, for $[p',q']\subseteq[p,q]$, gives a partition of $\Sigma_{[p',q']}$ (the induced partition); its rectangles meeting the hook of $i$ are pieces of different rectangles of the original partition that meet the hook of $i$.

Lemma 1 (Kraft inequality). For every partition of $\Sigma_{[p,q]}$ there are numbers $\pi_i\ge0$ ($p\le i\le q$) with $\sum\pi_i=1$ and $\pi_i\ge2^{-\ell_i}$ for all $i$. In particular $\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}\le1$.

Proof by induction on $q-p$. If $p=q$ the staircase is empty, $\ell_p=0$, and $\pi_p=1$ works. Let $p<q$ and let $Q=[p,r]\times[c,q]$ ($r<c$) be the rectangle containing the corner cell $(p,q)$; its rows start at the first row $p$ and its columns end at the last column $q$. The intervals $[p,c-1]$ and $[r+1,q]$ are shorter than $[p,q]$; apply the induction hypothesis to the induced partitions of their staircases, with hook counts $\ell'_i$, $\ell''_i$ and numbers $\pi'_i$, $\pi''_i$, and put \[\pi_i=\tfrac12\pi'_i\,[i\le c-1]+\tfrac12\pi''_i\,[i\ge r+1],\qquad\text{so that }\textstyle\sum\pi_i=\frac12+\frac12=1 .\]

  • $p\le i\le r$: only the first term occurs. The rectangle $Q$ meets $H_i$ (at the cell $(i,q)$) but does not meet $\Sigma_{[p,c-1]}$ (its columns are $\ge c$), so $\ell_i\ge\ell'_i+1$ and $\pi_i=\frac12\pi'_i\ge2^{-\ell'_i-1}\ge2^{-\ell_i}$.

  • $c\le i\le q$: in the same way (with the cell $(p,i)$ and $\Sigma_{[r+1,q]}$), $\pi_i=\frac12\pi''_i\ge2^{-\ell_i}$.

  • $r<i<c$: here $\ell'_i\le\ell_i$ and $\ell''_i\le\ell_i$, so by the AM–GM inequality $\pi_i=\frac12(\pi'_i+\pi''_i)\ge\frac12\bigl(2^{-\ell'_i}+2^{-\ell''_i}\bigr)\ge2^{-(\ell'_i+\ell''_i)/2}\ge2^{-\ell_i}$. $\square$

Lemma 2. If $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n$ are integers with $\sum2^{-\ell_i}\le1$, then $\sum\ell_i\ge T(n)$.

Proof. The function $t\mapsto2^{-t}$ is convex, so at every integer $t$ (none lies strictly between $L-1$ and $L$) it is at least the linear function through its values at $L-1$ and $L$: $2^{-t}\ge2^{-L}(L+1-t)$. Summing, $1\ge2^{-L}\bigl(n(L+1)-\sum\ell_i\bigr)$, i.e. $\sum\ell_i\ge nL+n-2^L$. $\square$

Lower bound. By Lemmas 1 and 2 every partition of $\Sigma_n$ has $\sum(|I|+|J|)=\sum\ell_i\ge T(n)$, so $h(n)\ge T(n)$.

Construction. Split $\Sigma_n$ into the rectangle $[1,m]\times[m+1,n]$, $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ (with $|I|+|J|=n$), and the staircases on $[1,m]$ and $[m+1,n]$, and continue recursively. The resulting sum $g(n)$ satisfies $g(1)=0$, $g(n)=n+g(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)+g(\lceil n/2\rceil)$. We check $g(n)=T(n)$ by induction. Write $T_\lambda(k)=k\lambda-2^\lambda+k$; then $T(k)=T_{\lceil\log_2k\rceil}(k)$, and also $T(k)=T_{\lambda}(k)$ for $\lambda=\lceil\log_2k\rceil+1$ when $k=2^{\lambda-1}$ (both equal $k(\lambda-1)$). For $n\ge2$ we have $2^{L-1}<n\le2^L$, so both halves $k\in\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lceil n/2\rceil\}$ satisfy $2^{L-2}\le k\le2^{L-1}$, hence $T(k)=T_{L-1}(k)$, and \[g(n)=n+T_{L-1}\bigl(\lfloor\tfrac n2\rfloor\bigr)+T_{L-1}\bigl(\lceil\tfrac n2\rceil\bigr)=n+n(L-1)-2^{L}+n=T(n).\] Doing the same below the diagonal gives total perimeter $4T(n)$, so the minimum is \[\boxed{4\bigl(n\lceil\log_2n\rceil-2^{\lceil\log_2n\rceil}+n\bigr)},\] for example $8,20,32,48,64,80,96,116,136$ for $n=2,\dots,10$.

Problem 6

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry · Circle and Line Structure via Complex Equations

A circle $\omega$ with center $O$ and a point $H$ inside it are fixed. Let $O_9$ be the midpoint of $OH$. An acute triangle $ABC$ is inscribed in $\omega$ and has orthocenter $H$. The tangents to $\omega$ at $B$ and $C$, together with the line through $H$ perpendicular to $AO_9$, determine a triangle with circumcircle $\omega_A$; define $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$ analogously. Assume that the circles $\omega_A$, $\omega_B$, and $\omega_C$ do not have a common radical axis. Let $W$ be the radical center of $\omega_A$, $\omega_B$, and $\omega_C$. Prove that, as $ABC$ varies, the point $W$ is fixed and has constant power with respect to $\omega_A$, $\omega_B$, and $\omega_C$.

Show the answer

proof; $\overrightarrow{OW}=\frac{4R^2}{3R^2+OH^2}\,\overrightarrow{OH}$, with power $-\frac{12R^4(R^2-OH^2)}{(3R^2+OH^2)^2}$

Show the solution – Complex coordinates

Take $\omega$ as the unit circle (so $R=1$), lower-case letters for complex coordinates, and $h=a+b+c$, $o_9=\frac h2$. For a point $z$ on the unit circle $\bar z=\frac1z$.

The triangle defining $\omega_A$. The tangents at $b,c$ are $z+b^2\bar z=2b$ and $z+c^2\bar z=2c$; they meet at $t=\frac{2bc}{b+c}$. The line $\ell_A$ through $h$ perpendicular to $a-o_9$ meets the tangent at $b$ at \[u=\frac{2ab}{a-c}:\] indeed $\bar u=\frac{2c}{b(c-a)}$, so $u+b^2\bar u=\frac{2ab-2bc}{a-c}=2b$; and $u-h=\frac{(a+c)(b+c-a)}{a-c}$, $a-o_9=\frac{a-b-c}2$, so $\frac{u-h}{a-o_9}=-\frac{2(a+c)}{a-c}$, which is purely imaginary (its conjugate is its negative). Similarly $\ell_A$ meets the tangent at $c$ at $v=\frac{2ac}{a-b}$. (In the figure, $t,u,v$ are the points $T,U,V$.)

The circle $\omega_A$. A direct check shows that $o_A=\frac{2a^2bc}{(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)}$ is equidistant from $t,u,v$, so it is the centre of $\omega_A$; $\bar o_A=\frac{2bc}{(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)}$, and the power of $O$ is \[\pi_A:=|o_A|^2-r_A^2=\frac{4abc}{(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)} .\] So the power of a point $z$ with respect to $\omega_A$ is $|z|^2-(\bar o_Az+o_A\bar z)+\pi_A$.

The point $W$. Let $\Pi=(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)$, $D=\Pi+4abc$, and $z_0=\frac{4h}{3+h\bar h}$. Note $h\bar h=\frac{(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)}{abc}=\frac{\Pi+abc}{abc}$, so $3+h\bar h=\frac{D}{abc}$ and $z_0=\frac{4abc\,h}{D}$, $|z_0|^2=\frac{16abc(\Pi+abc)}{D^2}$. A computation gives \[\bar o_Az_0+o_A\bar z_0=\frac{8abc\,(\Pi-a(b^2+c^2))}{(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)\,D},\] and, since $4abc-\Pi+2a(b^2+c^2)=-(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)$, \[\pi_A-(\bar o_Az_0+o_A\bar z_0)=\frac{4abc\,(D-2\Pi+2a(b^2+c^2))}{(a-b)(a-c)(b+c)D}=-\frac{4abc}{D}.\] Hence the power of $z_0$ with respect to $\omega_A$ is \[\frac{16abc(\Pi+abc)}{D^2}-\frac{4abc}{D}=\frac{12abc\,\Pi}{D^2}=\frac{12(h\bar h-1)}{(h\bar h+3)^2}=-\frac{12(1-OH^2)}{(3+OH^2)^2},\] using $\frac{\Pi}{abc}=h\bar h-1$ and $\frac D{abc}=h\bar h+3$. This expression is symmetric in $a,b,c$, so $z_0$ has the same power with respect to $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$.

Conclusion. Since $\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C$ do not share a radical axis, their radical centre is unique, so $W=z_0=\frac{4}{3+OH^2}\,h$. It depends only on $O,H$ (and $R$), and its power $-\frac{12(1-OH^2)}{(3+OH^2)^2}$ (with $R=1$) is the same for all three circles and all triangles. In general units, $\overrightarrow{OW}=\frac{4R^2}{3R^2+OH^2}\overrightarrow{OH}$.

Problem 7

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

Let $n=p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_s^{\alpha_s}$, where $p_1<p_2<\dots<p_s$ are prime numbers and $\alpha_i$, $i=1,2,\dots,s$, are positive integers. Prove that the number of positive integers $k$ for which the sum of the positive divisors of $k$ equals $\frac{n}{k}$ is at most $\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_s$.

Show the solution – An injective record of the exponents

The condition is $\sigma(k)=\frac nk$, that is, $k\,\sigma(k)=n$. In particular $k\mid n$, so $k=p_1^{\beta_1}\cdots p_s^{\beta_s}$ with $0\le\beta_i\le\alpha_i$. We use that $\sigma$ is multiplicative, that $\sigma(2^\beta)=2^{\beta+1}-1$ is odd, and that for an odd prime $p$ the number $\sigma(p^\beta)=1+p+\dots+p^\beta$ is a sum of $\beta+1$ odd numbers, hence odd exactly when $\beta$ is even; also $\sigma(p)=p+1$.

The record. To a solution $k$ assign the vector $r(k)=(r_1,\dots,r_s)$ with \[r_i=\max(\beta_i,1)\ \text{ if }p_i\text{ is odd},\qquad r_i=\min(\beta_i,\alpha_i-1)\ \text{ if }p_i=2.\] For odd $p_i$ the entry $r_i$ takes one of the $\alpha_i$ values $1,\dots,\alpha_i$ (the exponents $0$ and $1$ are merged); for $p_i=2$ it takes one of the $\alpha_i$ values $0,\dots,\alpha_i-1$ (the exponents $\alpha_i-1$ and $\alpha_i$ are merged). So there are at most $\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_s$ possible records, and it suffices to show that two different solutions $k\ne k'$ cannot have the same record. Assume $r(k)=r(k')$. Then for each odd $p_i$ the exponents of $p_i$ in $k,k'$ are equal or are $0$ and $1$; the exponents of $2$ are equal or are $\alpha-1$ and $\alpha$, where $\alpha=v_2(n)$.

Case 1: $n$ odd. Then $k$ is odd and $\sigma(k)=\frac nk$ is odd, so every exponent $\beta_i$ of $k$ is even. An even $\beta_i$ is determined by $\max(\beta_i,1)$ (the value $1$ means $\beta_i=0$). The same holds for $k'$, so $k=k'$, a contradiction.

Case 2: $n$ even and $v_2(k)=v_2(k')$. Let $m=\gcd(k,k')$ and write $k=mA$, $k'=mB$. Here $A$ is the product of the odd primes with exponent $1$ in $k$ and $0$ in $k'$, and $B$ the product of those with exponent $1$ in $k'$ and $0$ in $k$. So $A$ and $B$ are squarefree, $\gcd(A,B)=\gcd(m,AB)=1$, and $A\ne B$ since $k\ne k'$. By multiplicativity, $m\sigma(m)A\sigma(A)=n=m\sigma(m)B\sigma(B)$, so \[\prod_{p\mid A}p(p+1)=\prod_{q\mid B}q(q+1).\] Let $p$ be the largest prime dividing $AB$ (it exists since $AB>1$); by symmetry $p\mid A$, so $p$ divides the left side. Every prime $q\mid B$ is odd and smaller than $p$, so $q\le p-2$ and $q+1<p$; thus $p$ divides none of the factors $q,q+1$ on the right (an empty product if $B=1$). This is a contradiction.

Case 3: $n$ even and $v_2(k)\ne v_2(k')$. Then the exponents of $2$ are $\alpha$ and $\alpha-1$; say $v_2(k)=\alpha$ and $v_2(k')=\alpha-1$. Since $v_2(k)+v_2(\sigma(k))=\alpha$, the number $\sigma(k)$ is odd, so every odd prime exponent of $k$ is even. For an odd $p_i$ with $r_i\ge2$ both $k,k'$ have exponent $r_i$, which is even; for an odd $p_i$ with $r_i=1$, $k$ has exponent $0$ (the even choice) and $k'$ has $0$ or $1$. So $k=2^\alpha m$ and $k'=2^{\alpha-1}mA$, where $m$ is odd with all exponents even and $A$ is the product of the odd primes with exponent $1$ in $k'$, $\gcd(A,2m)=1$. In $k'$ we have $v_2(\sigma(k'))=\alpha-(\alpha-1)=1$, while $\sigma(2^{\alpha-1})$ and $\sigma(m)$ are odd, so $\sum_{p\mid A}v_2(p+1)=1$. Each $p+1$ is even, hence $A=p$ is a single odd prime. Now $k\sigma(k)=k'\sigma(k')$ gives \[2^\alpha(2^{\alpha+1}-1)\,m\sigma(m)=2^{\alpha-1}(2^\alpha-1)\,m\sigma(m)\,p(p+1),\] so \[\frac{p(p+1)}2=\frac{2^{\alpha+1}-1}{2^\alpha-1}=2+\frac1{2^\alpha-1}\le3.\] But $p\ge3$ gives $\frac{p(p+1)}2\ge6$, a contradiction.

So different solutions have different records, and the number of positive integers $k$ with $\sigma(k)=\frac nk$ is at most $\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_s$.

Problem 8

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Geometric Reflection and Isometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle. A point $X$ is said to be attached to $ABC$ if it lies in its interior or on its boundary. Prove that, for every point $P$ in the plane of $ABC$, there exists a composition of reflections, each across one of the lines $AB$, $AC$, or $BC$, such that the image of $P$ under this composition is a point attached to $ABC$. (It is not necessary to use all three reflections. A reflection may be used more than once.)

Show solution 1 – A minimising sequence and folding at a vertex

Let $I$ be the incentre and $r$ the inradius of $ABC$, and let $\mathcal O$ be the set of all images of $P$ under compositions of the three reflections.

Two lemmas. (i) If $X$ and $I$ lie on opposite sides of a side line $\ell$, at distances $u$ and $r$, and $X'$ is the reflection of $X$ in $\ell$, then $|X'I|^2=|XI|^2-4ur$ (in coordinates with $\ell$ the $x$-axis, $I=(0,r)$, $X=(x,-u)$: $x^2+(r+u)^2-\bigl(x^2+(r-u)^2\bigr)=4ru$). (ii) Folding at a vertex. Any point $Y$ can be moved by reflections in $AB$ and $AC$ into the closed angular region $\angle BAC$ without changing $|YA|$. Indeed, $s_{AC}\circ s_{AB}$ is the rotation about $A$ by $2\alpha$ ($\alpha=\angle BAC$), so all rotations about $A$ by multiples of $2\alpha$ are available; measuring polar angles from ray $AB$ towards $AC$, rotate $Y$ to an angle in $[-\alpha,\alpha)$ and, if the angle is negative, reflect in $AB$.

A minimising sequence. Let $\delta=\inf\{|XI|:X\in\mathcal O\}$ and choose $X_k\in\mathcal O$ with $|X_kI|\to\delta$. The $X_k$ are bounded, so a subsequence converges to some point $X^*$.

$X^*$ is attached to $ABC$. Otherwise $X^*$ lies strictly beyond some side line, say beyond $BC$ at distance $h>0$. For large $k$, $X_k$ lies beyond $BC$ at distance at least $\frac h2$, and by (i) its reflection $X_k'\in\mathcal O$ satisfies $|X_k'I|^2\le|X_kI|^2-2hr<\delta^2$ for large $k$, contradicting the definition of $\delta$.

Conclusion. If $X^*$ is an interior point, then $X_k$ is interior for large $k$. If $X^*$ is on side $BC$ but not a vertex, then for large $k$ the point $X_k$ is close to $X^*$, hence strictly inside the half-planes of $AB$ and $AC$ containing the triangle; either it is also on $A$'s side of $BC$ (attached), or its reflection in $BC$ is (and it stays close to $X^*$). If $X^*$ is a vertex, say $A$, choose $k$ with $|X_kA|<\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is the distance from $A$ to $BC$; by (ii) some image of $X_k$ lies in the angular region at $A$ within distance $\varepsilon$ of $A$, hence in the triangle. In all cases some point of $\mathcal O$ is attached to $ABC$.

Show solution 2 – Reflect in the most violated side

Say that a point $X$ violates a side line $\ell$ of the triangle if $X$ lies strictly on the side of $\ell$ not containing the triangle, and call the distance from $X$ to $\ell$ the violation. A point is attached exactly when it violates none of the three lines. Fix a point $C_0$ inside the triangle and let $r>0$ be the smallest of its distances to the three side lines.

The procedure. Put $X_0=P$. If $X_j$ is attached, stop. Otherwise reflect $X_j$ in a side line that it violates with the largest violation $d_j$, and call the image $X_{j+1}$. It suffices to show that the procedure stops.

A decreasing quantity. If $X$ violates $\ell$ with violation $d$ and $X'$ is its reflection in $\ell$, then $|X'C_0|^2=|XC_0|^2-4dd_0$, where $d_0\ge r$ is the distance from $C_0$ to $\ell$. Indeed, take $\ell$ as the $x$-axis with the triangle above it: $C_0=(p,d_0)$, $X=(q,-d)$, $X'=(q,d)$, and $(d_0+d)^2-(d_0-d)^2=4dd_0$.

If the procedure never stopped. Then $4r\sum_j d_j\le|PC_0|^2$, so $\sum_jd_j$ is finite. As $|X_{j+1}X_j|=2d_j$, the points $X_j$ form a Cauchy sequence and converge to a point $L$, and $d_j\to0$. Since $d_j$ is the largest violation of $X_j$ and the violation of a fixed line depends continuously on the point, $L$ violates no line: $L$ is attached. There are three cases.

  • $L$ is inside the triangle. Then $X_j$ is inside for large $j$, and the procedure stops.

  • $L$ lies on a side line $\ell$ but is not a vertex. Take $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than the distances from $L$ to the other two side lines. For large $j$, $|X_jL|<\varepsilon$, so $X_j$ can violate only $\ell$ and $X_{j+1}$ is its reflection in $\ell$. As $L\in\ell$, also $|X_{j+1}L|<\varepsilon$, so $X_{j+1}$ violates neither of the other lines; it does not violate $\ell$ either. Thus $X_{j+1}$ is attached, and the procedure stops.

  • $L$ is a vertex, say $A$. Take $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than the distance from $A$ to $BC$. For $j\ge J$ we have $|X_jA|<\varepsilon$, so only the lines $AB$ and $AC$ can be used, and reflections in lines through $A$ keep the distance to $A$ unchanged. So $|X_jA|=|X_JA|$ for all $j\ge J$, and since $X_j\to A$, this distance is $0$: $X_J=A$ is attached, and the procedure stops.

Each case contradicts the assumption, so the procedure stops after finitely many reflections. The composition of these reflections takes $P$ to an attached point.
