Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $N$ be a positive integer. We have coins of denominations the distinct positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N$, so that there are exactly $i$ coins of denomination $a_i$ for every $i=1,2,\dots,N$. Determine, in terms of $N$, the largest integer $S$, for which there exist distinct integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N$, so that every sum from $1$ to $S$ can be obtained as a sum of one or more of the coins.

Show the answer

$S=(N+1)!-1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting selections and the factorial number system)

Upper bound. A sum of coins is determined by how many coins of each denomination it uses: $c_i$ coins of value $a_i$, where $0\le c_i\le i$. There are $2\cdot 3\cdots (N+1)=(N+1)!$ choices of $(c_1,\dots,c_N)$, and one of them uses no coin at all. Hence at most $(N+1)!-1$ different positive sums can be formed, and if every integer from $1$ to $S$ is a sum, then $S\le (N+1)!-1$.

Construction. Take $a_i=i!$ (the values $1!,2!,\dots,N!$ are distinct). We show by induction on $N$ that every integer $s$ with $0\le s\le (N+1)!-1$ can be written as \[ s=c_1\cdot 1!+c_2\cdot 2!+\dots+c_N\cdot N!,\qquad 0\le c_i\le i . \] For $N=1$ we need $s\in\{0,1\}$, and $s=c_1\cdot 1$. For the step, divide $s$ by $N!$: $s=c_N\cdot N!+r$ with $0\le r\le N!-1$. Then $c_N\le s/N!<(N+1)!/N!=N+1$, so $0\le c_N\le N$, and $r$ is written with $1!,\dots,(N-1)!$ by the induction hypothesis.

So with $c_i$ coins of value $i!$ ($c_i\le i$ are available) we obtain every sum from $1$ to $(N+1)!-1$, and the answer is \[ \boxed{S=(N+1)!-1}. \]

Solution 2 (Growing an interval of sums, coin type by coin type)

A growth lemma. Suppose a collection of coins gives every sum $0,1,\dots,T$ (the sum $0$ means using no coin). If we add $k$ coins of value $T+1$, we obtain every sum $0,1,\dots,(k+1)(T+1)-1$. Indeed, such a number can be written as $q(T+1)+r$ with $0\le q\le k$ and $0\le r\le T$; take $q$ of the new coins and old coins with sum $r$.

Construction. Now give the denominations in the order $i=N,N-1,\dots,1$, each time choosing the value one more than the largest sum reached so far. Start with the $N$ coins of type $N$ and value $a_N=1$: they give $0,1,\dots,N$. By the lemma, after the types $N,N-1,\dots,j$ have been added, all sums $0,1,\dots,T_j$ are reached, where \[ T_N=N=(N+1)-1,\qquad T_{j}=(j+1)(T_{j+1}+1)-1 , \] so $T_j+1=(N+1)N\cdots(j+1)$ and the value used for type $j$ is $a_j=T_{j+1}+1=(N+1)N\cdots(j+2)$. These values strictly increase as $j$ decreases, so they are distinct. At the end $T_1+1=(N+1)!$, so every sum from $1$ to $(N+1)!-1$ is obtained. (For $N=3$: $a_3=1$, $a_2=4$, $a_1=12$, reaching $1,\dots,23$.)

Upper bound (the same count as in Solution 1). A sum is determined by the numbers $c_i$ of coins of value $a_i$ used, $0\le c_i\le i$, that is, by a point of $\{0,1\}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\dots\times\{0,1,\dots,N\}$. There are $(N+1)!$ such vectors $(c_1,\dots,c_N)$, one of them zero, so at most $(N+1)!-1$ positive sums occur and $S\le(N+1)!-1$. With the construction above, \[ \boxed{S=(N+1)!-1}. \]

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer. The real numbers $a$ and $b$ are such that $a(b-1)^n+b(a-1)^n=0$. Determine the maximum possible value of $ab$.

Show the answer

$1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Signs, then a monotone function)

For $a=b=1$ the equation holds and $ab=1$. We show that always $ab\le 1$; then the maximum is $1$.

If $ab\le 0$ there is nothing to prove, so let $ab>0$.

$a,b$ cannot both be negative. Then $a-1<0$ and $b-1<0$. For even $n$ both terms $a(b-1)^n$ and $b(a-1)^n$ are negative; for odd $n$ both are positive. Their sum is not $0$.

So $a,b>0$. If $n$ is even, both terms are $\ge 0$ and their sum is $0$, so $(a-1)^n=(b-1)^n=0$, $a=b=1$ and $ab=1$.

Let $n\ge 3$ be odd. If $a=1$ the equation becomes $(b-1)^n=0$, so $b=1$; similarly $b=1$ forces $a=1$. Otherwise the two terms are nonzero with opposite signs, so $a-1$ and $b-1$ have opposite signs; by symmetry $a>1>b>0$. The equation reads $a(1-b)^n=b(a-1)^n$, that is \[ \varphi(b)=\frac{(a-1)^n}{a},\qquad\text{where } \varphi(t)=\frac{(1-t)^n}{t}\quad(0<t<1). \] On $(0,1)$ the numerator of $\varphi$ is positive and decreasing and the denominator is positive and increasing, so $\varphi$ is strictly decreasing. Now $1/a\in(0,1)$ and \[ \varphi\!\left(\tfrac1a\right)=\Bigl(1-\tfrac1a\Bigr)^{n}a=\frac{(a-1)^n}{a^{\,n-1}}<\frac{(a-1)^n}{a}=\varphi(b), \] because $a>1$ and $n-1\ge 2>1$. Since $\varphi$ is decreasing, $b<1/a$, i.e. $ab<1$.

Hence $ab\le 1$ in all cases, with equality only for $a=b=1$. The maximum of $ab$ is $\boxed{1}$.

Solution 2 (A variant: comparing $n$-th roots)

This is the same comparison $b<1/a$ as in Solution 1, done with $n$-th roots instead of monotonicity.

The pair $a=b=1$ gives $ab=1$. Suppose some solution has $ab>1$; we derive a contradiction.

Then $a$ and $b$ have the same sign. If both were negative, then $a-1$, $b-1$ are negative and the two terms $a(b-1)^n$, $b(a-1)^n$ have the same sign (both negative for even $n$, both positive for odd $n$), so their sum is not zero. Hence $a,b>0$. Neither equals $1$: $a=1$ would give $(b-1)^n=0$, $b=1$, $ab=1$. So both terms are nonzero, and they must have opposite signs; this is impossible for even $n$, where both are positive. So $n\ge3$ is odd and $a-1$, $b-1$ have opposite signs; by symmetry $a>1>b>0$, and \[ a(1-b)^n=b(a-1)^n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1-b}{a-1}=\Bigl(\frac ba\Bigr)^{1/n} \] (both sides positive). Now use $b>1/a$: \[ \frac{1-b}{a-1}<\frac{1-1/a}{a-1}=\frac1a,\qquad \Bigl(\frac ba\Bigr)^{1/n}>\Bigl(\frac1{a^2}\Bigr)^{1/n}=a^{-2/n}. \] Hence $a^{-1}>a^{-2/n}$. But $a>1$ and $-1<-2/n$ (as $n\ge 3$), so $a^{-1}<a^{-2/n}$, a contradiction.

Therefore $ab\le 1$ for every solution, and the maximum is $\boxed{1}$, attained at $a=b=1$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool

Let $a>1$ be an integer and $p>2$ be a prime. Determine the remainder of $(a-1)(a^2-1)\cdots(a^{p-1}-1)$ when divided by $1+a+\dots+a^{p-1}$.

Show the answer

$p$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A polynomial identity via roots of unity)

Let $\Phi(x)=1+x+\dots+x^{p-1}$ and $M=\Phi(a)$. Since $a\ge 2$, $M\ge 1+2+\dots+2^{p-1}=2^p-1>p$.

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}$. The roots of $\Phi$ are $\omega,\omega^2,\dots,\omega^{p-1}$, so $\Phi(x)=\prod_{m=1}^{p-1}(x-\omega^m)$, and at $x=1$: $\prod_{m=1}^{p-1}(1-\omega^m)=\Phi(1)=p$. Consider \[ F(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(x^k-1)\;-\;p . \] For $1\le j\le p-1$ the numbers $jk$ ($k=1,\dots,p-1$) run over all nonzero residues modulo the prime $p$, so \[ F(\omega^j)=\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(\omega^{jk}-1)-p=\prod_{m=1}^{p-1}(\omega^{m}-1)-p=(-1)^{p-1}\prod_{m=1}^{p-1}(1-\omega^m)-p=p-p=0, \] using that $p-1$ is even. Thus $F$ vanishes at the $p-1$ distinct roots of $\Phi$, so $\Phi$ divides $F$. Since $\Phi$ is monic with integer coefficients, long division of $F$ by $\Phi$ stays in $\mathbb Z[x]$; the remainder is $0$, so $F=\Phi\cdot G$ with $G\in\mathbb Z[x]$. Putting $x=a$, \[ (a-1)(a^2-1)\cdots(a^{p-1}-1)=p+M\cdot G(a),\qquad G(a)\in\mathbb Z . \] As $0\le p<M$, the remainder is $\boxed{p}$.

Solution 2 (Newton identities in $\mathbb Z[x]/(\Phi)$)

Let $\Phi(x)=1+x+\dots+x^{p-1}$ and $M=\Phi(a)>p$ (as $M\ge 2^p-1$). We prove, without complex numbers, that $\Phi(x)$ divides $\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(x^k-1)-p$ in $\mathbb Z[x]$; then $x=a$ gives the remainder $p$ as in Solution 1.

Work in the ring $R=\mathbb Z[x]/(\Phi(x))$. Because $\Phi$ is monic of degree $p-1$, every element of $R$ is uniquely an integer combination of $1,x,\dots,x^{p-2}$; in particular, if $j\,r=0$ in $R$ for a positive integer $j$, then $r=0$. In $R$ we have $x^p=1$, since $x^p-1=(x-1)\Phi(x)$.

Let $e_1,\dots,e_p$ be the elementary symmetric functions and $s_m=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}x^{km}$ the power sums of the $p$ elements $1,x,\dots,x^{p-1}$ of $R$. For $1\le m\le p-1$ the exponents $km$ ($0\le k\le p-1$) run over all residues mod $p$, and $x^p=1$, so $s_m=1+x+\dots+x^{p-1}=0$ in $R$. Newton's identities, valid in every commutative ring, \[ j\,e_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{i-1}e_{j-i}\,s_i\qquad(1\le j\le p), \] give $j\,e_j=0$, hence $e_j=0$, for $1\le j\le p-1$. Also $e_p=x^{0+1+\dots+(p-1)}=(x^p)^{(p-1)/2}=1$. Therefore, in $R[t]$, \[ \prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(t-x^k)=t^p+(-1)^pe_p=t^p-1=(t-1)(1+t+\dots+t^{p-1}). \] The left side is $(t-1)\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(t-x^k)$. Cancelling the monic factor $t-1$ (if $(t-1)h(t)=0$ with $h\ne0$, the leading coefficient of $(t-1)h$ equals that of $h$, which is nonzero), we get $\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(t-x^k)=1+t+\dots+t^{p-1}$ in $R[t]$. Setting $t=1$ and using that $p-1$ is even, \[ \prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(x^k-1)=\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(1-x^k)=p\quad\text{in } R, \] which means that $\Phi(x)$ divides $\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(x^k-1)-p$ in $\mathbb Z[x]$. With $x=a$: the product is $\equiv p\pmod M$ and $0\le p<M$, so the remainder is $\boxed{p}$.

Day 2

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$. A circle $\Omega$ through $B$ and $C$ intersects the sides $AB$ and $AC$ at $D$ and $E$, respectively. Let $F$ be a point on the smaller arc $\widehat{DE}$ of $\Omega$. Denote by $\omega$ the circle, passing through $A$ and $F$, and tangent to $\Omega$. Prove that $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ if and only if the line $AF$ bisects $\angle DFE$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion centred at $A$)

Conventions. The “smaller arc” $DE$ is the arc of $\Omega$ between $D$ and $E$ that does not contain $B$ and $C$; it lies on the same side of $DE$ as $A$. This arc is indeed the smaller one: its measure is $2\angle DBE=2\angle DCE$ (inscribed angles from $B$ and $C$, which lie on the other arc), where $\angle DBE=\angle ABE<\angle ABC$ and $\angle DCE=\angle ACD<\angle ACB$; at least one of $\angle ABC,\angle ACB$ is acute, so the arc is less than $180^\circ$. Since $F\in\Omega$, the circle $\omega$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $F$; since $A\in\Gamma$, the circle $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ exactly when it touches $\Gamma$ at $A$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of the arc $DE$ of $\Omega$ that contains $B$ and $C$, and let $F'$ be the second intersection of line $AF$ with $\Omega$.

Step 1: where $F'$ lies. $A$ is outside $\Omega$ (its power $AD\cdot AB$ is positive). Every line through $A$ that cuts $\Omega$ in two points meets it first at a point of the arc of $\Omega$ facing $A$ (between the two tangency points of the tangents from $A$), and then at a point of the complementary arc. $D$ and $E$ are first points (on rays $AB$, $AC$, before $B$ and $C$), while $B$ and $C$ are second points. The facing arc is connected, contains $D$, $E$ and not $B$, $C$, so it contains the whole arc $DE$ without $B,C$. Hence $F$ is a first point and $F'$, the second point on line $AF$, lies on the arc $DE$ containing $B$ and $C$ (and $F'\ne D,E$).

Step 2: the bisector. $F$ and $M$ lie on different arcs $DE$, and the arcs $DM$ and $ME$ not containing $F$ are equal, so $\angle DFM=\angle MFE$: line $FM$ is the bisector of $\angle DFE$. Thus $AF$ bisects $\angle DFE$ $\iff$ $M\in AF$ $\iff$ $F'=M$. Moreover, the tangent to $\Omega$ at a point $X$ is parallel to $DE$ iff the radius to $X$ is perpendicular to $DE$, i.e. iff $X$ is the midpoint of one of the two arcs $DE$. As $F'$ is on the arc containing $B,C$: \[ AF \text{ bisects } \angle DFE\iff F'=M\iff \text{the tangent to }\Omega\text{ at }F'\text{ is parallel to }DE. \tag{1} \]

Step 3: inversion. Let $k=AD\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC=AF\cdot AF'>0$ be the power of $A$ with respect to $\Omega$, and let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\sqrt k$. It swaps $D\leftrightarrow B$, $E\leftrightarrow C$, $F\leftrightarrow F'$ and maps $\Omega$ to itself. The circle $\Gamma$ passes through $A,B,C$, so $\iota(\Gamma)$ is the line through $\iota(B)=D$ and $\iota(C)=E$, the line $DE$. The circle $\omega$ passes through $A$ and $F$, so $\iota(\omega)$ is a line through $F'$; as $\omega$ touches $\Omega$ at $F$, this line touches $\iota(\Omega)=\Omega$ at $F'$: it is the tangent to $\Omega$ at $F'$.

The circles $\omega$ and $\Gamma$ are different ($F\in\omega$, but $\Gamma\cap\Omega=\{B,C\}$). Two different circles through $A$ are tangent iff $A$ is their only common point, iff their images under $\iota$ (two lines) have no common point, iff these lines are parallel. So $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ iff the tangent to $\Omega$ at $F'$ is parallel to $DE$, and by (1) this happens iff $AF$ bisects $\angle DFE$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (A homothety at $F$ and the antiparallel $DE$)

Setup (as in Solution 1). The smaller arc $DE$ is the one not containing $B,C$: its measure is $2\angle DBE=2\angle DCE$, with $\angle DBE<\angle ABC$, $\angle DCE<\angle ACB$, and one of these two angles of the triangle is acute. Let $F'$ be the second intersection of line $AF$ with $\Omega$. The point $A$ is outside $\Omega$, and the arc of $\Omega$ facing $A$ contains $D,E$ (first points on rays $AB,AC$) but not $B,C$ (second points); so it contains the arc $DE$ through $F$, and $F'$ lies on the arc $DE$ containing $B,C$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of that arc. Then $FM$ bisects $\angle DFE$ (equal arcs $DM$, $ME$), so $AF$ bisects $\angle DFE$ iff $F'=M$. Finally, the tangent to $\Omega$ at a point $X$ is parallel to $DE$ iff the radius to $X$ is perpendicular to $DE$, i.e. iff $X$ is the midpoint of an arc $DE$. Hence \[ AF \text{ bisects } \angle DFE\iff F'=M\iff \text{the tangent to }\Omega\text{ at }F'\text{ is parallel to }DE. \tag{1} \]

The tangent to $\Gamma$ at $A$ is parallel to $DE$. Let $t_A$ be this tangent. By the tangent–chord angle, $t_A$ makes with the chord $AB$ the angle $\angle ACB$, on the side of $AB$ not containing $C$. Since $BCED$ is cyclic, the exterior angle at $D$ gives $\angle ADE=\angle BCE=\angle ACB$, with $E$ on the same side of $AB$ as $C$. These are alternate angles at the transversal $AB$, so $t_A\parallel DE$.

Reduction. $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ iff they touch at $A$, iff the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ is $t_A$, iff (as both lines pass through $A$) the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ is parallel to $DE$.

Homothety. The circles $\omega$ and $\Omega$ are tangent at $F$, so a homothety $h$ with centre $F$ maps $\omega$ onto $\Omega$. It maps $A\in\omega$, $A\ne F$, to a point of $\Omega$ on line $FA$ different from $F$, namely $F'$. A homothety maps tangent lines to tangent lines and every line to a parallel line, so the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ is parallel to the tangent to $\Omega$ at $F'$.

Combining: $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ $\iff$ the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ is parallel to $DE$ $\iff$ the tangent to $\Omega$ at $F'$ is parallel to $DE$ $\iff$ (by (1)) $AF$ bisects $\angle DFE$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ be a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers. Suppose there exists an integer $m$ such that $a_{i+1}-a_i<m$ for all $i\ge 1$. Prove that there exist pairwise distinct indices $p,q,r,s$ such that $a_pa_q=a_ra_s$.

Show the solution

Solution (Dilations of the sequence and double counting)

Let $S$ be the set of terms. As the sequence is strictly increasing, distinct terms have distinct indices, so it suffices to find four distinct elements $y_1,x_1,y_2,x_2$ of $S$ with $x_1y_2=x_2y_1$.

Density. For every $X>0$, $|S\cap[1,X]|\ge (X-a_1)/m$. This is clear if $X<a_1$. Otherwise let $a_j\le X<a_{j+1}$; then $X-a_1<a_{j+1}-a_1=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(a_{i+1}-a_i)<jm$, so $j>(X-a_1)/m$.

Double counting. Since the harmonic series diverges, fix $K$ with $H_K=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1K\ge 2m$ (the gaps are at least $1$, so $m\ge2$ and $K\ge2$). Let $N$ be a large integer. For $1\le k\le K$ let $kS=\{ks: s\in S,\ ks\le N\}$; then $|kS|=|S\cap[1,N/k]|\ge \frac{N}{km}-\frac{a_1}{m}$. For $1\le n\le N$ let $f(n)$ be the number of $k\le K$ with $n\in kS$. Then \[ \sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|kS|\ge \frac{N H_K}{m}-\frac{Ka_1}{m}\ge 2N-\frac{Ka_1}{m}. \] For every integer $f\ge 0$ we have $\binom f2-(f-1)=\frac{(f-1)(f-2)}2\ge 0$. Hence the number of triples $(n,k_1,k_2)$ with $k_1<k_2$ and $n\in k_1S\cap k_2S$ is \[ \sum_{n=1}^N\binom{f(n)}{2}\ge\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(f(n)-1\bigr)\ge N-\frac{Ka_1}{m}. \] There are fewer than $K^2$ pairs $(k_1,k_2)$, so some pair occurs in at least $(N-Ka_1/m)/K^2$ triples; for $N$ large this is at least $3$. So there are $n_1<n_2<n_3$ such that $y_i=n_i/k_2$ and $x_i=n_i/k_1$ are in $S$ ($i=1,2,3$). With $\rho=k_2/k_1>1$ we have $x_i=\rho y_i$, $y_1<y_2<y_3$, and for any $i,j$: \[ x_i\,y_j=\rho\,y_iy_j=x_j\,y_i . \]

Distinctness. We need $i\ne j$ with $\{y_i,\rho y_i\}\cap\{y_j,\rho y_j\}=\varnothing$; for $i<j$ the only possible coincidence is $y_j=\rho y_i$ (as $\rho y_j>y_j>y_i$ and $\rho y_i\ne\rho y_j$). If $y_3\ne\rho y_1$, take $(i,j)=(1,3)$. If $y_3=\rho y_1$, take $(i,j)=(1,2)$: then $y_2<y_3=\rho y_1$. In both cases $y_i,x_i,y_j,x_j$ are four distinct terms with $x_iy_j=x_jy_i$, i.e. there are pairwise distinct indices $p,q,r,s$ with $a_pa_q=a_ra_s$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

In the country of Baron Munchausen there are $2^n$ cities, numbered with the integers from $0$ to $2^n-1$. Two cities, numbered as $a$ and $b$, are friendly if in their binary representations the numbers $a$ and $b$ differ in exactly one digit. (For example, for $n=3$, the cities numbered $0$ and $4$ are friendly, as well as those numbered $5$ and $7$).

Between every two friendly cities there are two one-way roads – one in each direction. Each road has a category, which is an integer from $1$ to $n$. While traveling through the country, Baron Munchausen observed the following:

  • (1) For every city, the categories of the outgoing roads from it are all distinct.

  • (2) For every city, all incoming roads to it have the same category.

Find all $n$ for which this is possible.

Show the answer

exactly the powers of two, $n=2^k$ ($k\ge 0$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting colour classes; a linear colouring)

The answer is: exactly when $n$ is a power of $2$.

Reformulation. Suppose the categories are as described. By (2), every city $v$ has a colour $c(v)\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, the common category of the roads entering $v$; so the road from $u$ to $v$ has category $c(v)$. By (1), the $n$ friendly neighbours of every city $u$ have pairwise different colours, i.e. each of the $n$ colours appears exactly once among the neighbours of $u$. Conversely, any colouring $c$ with this property gives a valid choice of categories (give the road $u\to v$ the category $c(v)$).

Necessity. Fix a colour $i$ and let $C_i$ be the set of cities of colour $i$. Count the pairs $(u,v)$ of friendly cities with $v\in C_i$. Every city $u$ has exactly one neighbour of colour $i$, so there are $2^n$ pairs. Every $v\in C_i$ has $n$ neighbours, so there are $n|C_i|$ pairs. Hence $n|C_i|=2^n$, so $n$ divides $2^n$ and $n$ is a power of $2$.

Construction for $n=2^k$. Number the binary digits of a city $0,1,\dots,n-1$ and attach to digit $j$ the vector $v_j\in\mathbb F_2^{\,k}$ formed by the $k$ binary digits of $j$; the $v_j$ are all $2^k=n$ vectors of $\mathbb F_2^{\,k}$. Give the city $x$ the colour \[ c(x)=\sum_{j:\ \text{digit } j \text{ of } x \text{ is } 1} v_j\ \in\mathbb F_2^{\,k} \] (sum modulo $2$, coordinatewise), and identify $\mathbb F_2^{\,k}$ with $\{1,\dots,n\}$. The neighbour of $x$ obtained by changing digit $j$ has colour $c(x)+v_j$. As $j$ runs over all digits, $v_j$ runs over all of $\mathbb F_2^{\,k}$, so these $n$ colours are all different. Hence the condition holds and the arrangement is possible. (For $n=1$ both cities get colour $1$.)

So the possible $n$ are exactly $\boxed{n=2^k,\ k\ge 0}$.

Solution 2 (Counting colour classes; a doubling construction)

Reformulation and necessity. The category of every road entering a city $v$ is the same, call it the colour $c(v)$; a road $u\to v$ has category $c(v)$, so condition (1) says that the $n$ neighbours of each city carry all $n$ colours, each once. For a fixed colour $i$, counting adjacent pairs $(u,v)$ with $c(v)=i$ in two ways gives $2^n=n\cdot\#\{v: c(v)=i\}$; so $n\mid 2^n$ and $n$ is a power of $2$. Conversely, a colouring of the cities in which every city sees all $n$ colours among its neighbours yields valid categories.

Construction by doubling. For $n=1$ colour both cities $1$. Suppose $f$ is a good colouring for $n$ (colours $1,\dots,n$). For $2n$ digits write a city as a pair $(x,y)$, $x$ the lower $n$ digits and $y$ the upper $n$ digits, and put \[ g(x,y)=f(x\oplus y)+n\cdot\pi(x), \] where $\oplus$ is digitwise addition mod $2$ and $\pi(x)\in\{0,1\}$ is the parity of the number of ones in $x$. The colours are $1,\dots,2n$. Let $e$ run over the $n$ strings with a single one.

  • The neighbours $(x\oplus e,y)$ have colours $f(x\oplus y\oplus e)+n(1-\pi(x))$; since $f$ is good at the city $x\oplus y$, these are the $n$ colours of the block $\{1,\dots,n\}+n(1-\pi(x))$, each once.

  • The neighbours $(x,y\oplus e)$ have colours $f(x\oplus y\oplus e)+n\pi(x)$: each colour of the other block once.

So every city sees all $2n$ colours, and $g$ is good. By induction good colourings exist for $n=1,2,4,8,\dots$, i.e. for all powers of two, and by the necessity part for no other $n$. So the possible $n$ are exactly $\boxed{n=2^k,\ k\ge 0}$.
