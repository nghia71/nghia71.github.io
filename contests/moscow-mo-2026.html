Grade 8

Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Comparative Constraints · also Grade 9, Problem 1

Alice wrote down positive numbers $a,b,c,d,e$ (not necessarily integers), and Marusya wrote down the numbers $\frac1a,\frac1b,\frac1c,\frac1d,\frac1e$. It turned out that the sum of Alice's numbers is greater than the sum of Marusya's numbers. Could the product of Alice's numbers be less than the product of Marusya's numbers?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write Marusya's product in terms of Alice's product and simplify the comparison between them. Check what happens to both sums when one number becomes large.
Hint 2: key idea
Try one large number and four equal numbers moderately below $1$. The large number raises one sum strongly, while the four smaller factors can counteract its effect on the product. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Fix the four equal numbers first, then find the range of the remaining number allowed by the product comparison. Within that range, test a convenient value against the sum comparison.
Common trap
Marusya's product is $\frac{1}{abcde}$, so the product condition is simply $abcde<1$; do not compare the products factor by factor.
Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – One large number and four small ones

Yes. The product of Marusya's numbers is $\frac{1}{abcde}$, so we need $abcde<\frac1{abcde}$, that is, $abcde<1$, together with \[a+b+c+d+e>\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c+\frac1d+\frac1e .\] A large number contributes a lot to Alice's sum and almost nothing to Marusya's sum, while it can be balanced in the product by several numbers that are only moderately small. So take \[a=10,\qquad b=c=d=e=\tfrac12 .\] Then Alice's sum is $10+4\cdot\frac12=12$ and Marusya's sum is $\frac1{10}+4\cdot 2=8.1<12$, while Alice's product is $10\cdot\frac1{16}=\frac58$ and Marusya's product is $\frac1{10}\cdot 16=\frac85>\frac58$. So the product of Alice's numbers can indeed be less than the product of Marusya's numbers.

Remark. With only two numbers this is impossible: if $xy<1$ then $x+y-\frac1x-\frac1y=(x+y)\bigl(1-\frac1{xy}\bigr)<0$. This is why several small numbers are needed to balance one large number.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry · Classical Congruence Criteria

In an acute triangle $ABC$, a point $E$ is chosen on the altitude $BD$, inside the triangle. Point $K$ is the midpoint of segment $AE$, and point $L$ is the midpoint of segment $BC$. It turned out that $K$, $D$, $L$ are consecutive vertices of a square. Prove that the fourth vertex of this square lies on the line $AB$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the right triangles $ADE$ and $BDC$, and mark their hypotenuse midpoints. Add the fourth square vertex, calling it $M$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the hypotenuse-midpoint property and the equal square sides to compare $AE$ and $BC$. The perpendicular square sides also let you compare an acute angle of $ADE$ with one of $BDC$. (LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Prove the two right triangles congruent and use their equal legs to identify two isosceles right triangles. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AB$ and $T$ the midpoint of $EC$, then express the vector from $N$ to $M$ using $D,T$. Compare its direction with $AB$ using the isosceles right triangles.
Common trap
The vertices $K,D,L$ are consecutive, so $DK$ and $DL$ are sides of the square rather than its diagonals.
Show solution 1 – Two congruent right triangles

Since the triangle is acute, the foot $D$ of the altitude lies inside the side $AC$, and $\angle ADE=\angle BDC=90^\circ$ (the point $E$ lies on $BD$).

Step 1: $AE=BC$. In the right triangle $ADE$ the midpoint $K$ of the hypotenuse is its circumcentre, so $KA=KD=KE=\frac12AE$. Likewise $LB=LD=LC=\frac12BC$ in the right triangle $BDC$. Since $K,D,L$ are consecutive vertices of a square, $DK=DL$ and $\angle KDL=90^\circ$. Hence $AE=BC$.

Step 2: $\angle DAE=\angle DBC$. The point $K$ (midpoint of $AE$) lies on the same side of line $BD$ as $A$, and $L$ (midpoint of $BC$) on the same side as $C$; both lie on the same side of $AC$ as $B$. So the rays $DK$ and $DL$ lie inside the right angles $ADB$ and $BDC$, and \[\angle ADK+\angle LDC=180^\circ-\angle KDL=90^\circ .\] The triangles $KAD$ and $LDC$ are isosceles, so $\angle ADK=\angle DAK=\angle DAE$ and $\angle LDC=\angle LCD=\angle BCD$. Therefore $\angle DAE=90^\circ-\angle BCD=\angle DBC$ (right triangle $BDC$).

Step 3: congruence. The right triangles $ADE$ and $BDC$ have equal hypotenuses $AE=BC$ and equal acute angles at $A$ and $B$, so they are congruent: $AD=BD$ and $DE=DC$. Thus $ADB$ and $EDC$ are isosceles right triangles.

Step 4: the fourth vertex. The fourth vertex $M$ of the square $KDLM$ satisfies $\overrightarrow{DM}=\overrightarrow{DK}+\overrightarrow{DL}$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AB$ and $T$ the midpoint of $EC$. Writing every point as a vector, \[M-N=\Bigl(\frac{A+E}{2}+\frac{B+C}{2}-D\Bigr)-\frac{A+B}{2}=\frac{E+C}{2}-D=T-D,\] so $\overrightarrow{NM}=\overrightarrow{DT}$. In the isosceles right triangle $EDC$ the median $DT$ bisects the right angle $EDC$, so $\angle TDC=45^\circ$. In the isosceles right triangle $ADB$ we have $\angle BAD=45^\circ$, and $\angle BAC=\angle BAD$ because $D$ lies on the segment $AC$. So the lines $DT$ and $AB$ make equal corresponding angles $\angle TDC=\angle BAC=45^\circ$ with the line $AC$ (the rays $DC$ and $AC$ point the same way), and the points $T$ and $B$ lie on the same side of $AC$. Hence $DT\parallel AB$. Since $N$ lies on $AB$ and $\overrightarrow{NM}\parallel AB$, the point $M$ lies on the line $AB$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put $D$ at the origin, the line $AC$ on the $x$-axis and the altitude $BD$ on the $y$-axis: \[A=(-a,0),\quad C=(c,0),\quad B=(0,b),\quad E=(0,e),\qquad a,b,c>0,\ 0<e<b\] ($D$ lies between $A$ and $C$ because the triangle is acute). Then \[K=\Bigl(-\frac a2,\frac e2\Bigr),\qquad L=\Bigl(\frac c2,\frac b2\Bigr).\] The points $K,D,L$ are consecutive vertices of a square exactly when $DK\perp DL$ and $DK=DL$: \[-\frac{ac}{4}+\frac{eb}{4}=0,\qquad \frac{a^2+e^2}{4}=\frac{c^2+b^2}{4}.\] From the first equation $e=\frac{ac}{b}$; substituting into the second, \[a^2+\frac{a^2c^2}{b^2}=b^2+c^2\iff \frac{a^2(b^2+c^2)}{b^2}=b^2+c^2\iff a^2=b^2,\] so $b=a$ and then $e=c$.

The fourth vertex of the square is $M=K+L-D=\Bigl(\dfrac{c-a}{2},\dfrac{a+c}{2}\Bigr)$. The line $AB$ passes through $(-a,0)$ and $(0,a)$, so its equation is $y=x+a$, and indeed \[\frac{c-a}{2}+a=\frac{a+c}{2}.\] Hence $M$ lies on the line $AB$.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 11: Divisors and Arithmetic Functions of a Number · Sum and Product of Divisors

Polina wrote down a natural number ending in the digits $2026$. Stepa computed the sum of all natural divisors of this number (including $1$ and the number itself). Could he have obtained the number $1\,000\,000\,001$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the original number as $10000q+2026$. Determine how many factors of $2$ divide it.
Hint 2: key idea
Separate its divisors into odd ones and even ones. Try matching an odd divisor with twice that divisor and check that this accounts for every divisor exactly once. (LPS VI, Chapter 11: Divisors and Arithmetic Functions of a Number)
Hint 3: approach
Express the sum of each matched pair as a fixed multiple of its odd member. Compare the divisibility forced on the full divisor sum with the digit sum of the proposed result.
Common trap
An even divisor has this paired form because the original number is not divisible by $4$.
Show the answer

No

Show solution 1 – Pairing the divisors d and 2d

No. Let $N$ be Polina's number. It ends in $2026$, so $N=10000q+2026$ for some integer $q\ge 0$. Then $N=2m$ with $m=5000q+1013$ odd. Thus $N$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$.

Every divisor of $N$ is either odd, and then it divides $m$, or even, and then it has the form $2d$ where $d$ divides $m$ (because $4\nmid N$). Conversely, for every divisor $d$ of $m$ both $d$ and $2d$ divide $N$. So the divisors of $N$ split into the pairs $\{d,2d\}$, $d\mid m$, and \[\sigma(N)=\sum_{d\mid m}(d+2d)=3\sum_{d\mid m}d ,\] which is divisible by $3$. But the digit sum of $1\,000\,000\,001$ is $2$, so this number is not divisible by $3$. Hence Stepa could not have obtained $1\,000\,000\,001$.

Show solution 2 – The divisor sum is even

No. Let $N$ be Polina's number and $\sigma(N)$ the sum of its divisors. Since $N$ ends in $2026$, we have $N=10000q+2026=2m$ with $m=5000q+1013$ odd, so $2\mid N$ but $4\nmid N$; also $N\equiv1\pmod 5$, because its last digit is $6$. We show that $\sigma(N)$ is even, while $1\,000\,000\,001$ is odd.

Reduction to $m$. Since $4\nmid N$, every divisor of $N$ is either a divisor $d$ of $m$ or twice such a divisor, and conversely all these numbers divide $N$. Hence $\sigma(N)=\sum_{d\mid m}(d+2d)=3\sigma(m)$, and $\sigma(N)$ is odd only if $\sigma(m)$ is odd.

When is $\sigma(m)$ odd? All divisors of the odd number $m$ are odd, so $\sigma(m)$ has the same parity as the number of divisors of $m$. The divisors split into pairs $\{d,\,m/d\}$ with $d\ne m/d$, except for the divisor $d=\sqrt m$ when $m$ is a perfect square. So $\sigma(m)$ is odd exactly when $m$ is a perfect square.

Conclusion. If $\sigma(N)$ were odd, then $m=s^2$ for an integer $s$ and $N=2s^2$, so $2s^2\equiv1\pmod5$; multiplying by $3$ gives $s^2\equiv3\pmod 5$. But the squares modulo $5$ are $0,1,4$ (from $s\equiv0,\pm1,\pm2$). Hence $\sigma(N)$ is even for every number ending in $2026$, and Stepa could not have obtained the odd number $1\,000\,000\,001$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

The king decided to test his court sage. He laid out $144$ gold coins that look identical in the form of a $12\times 12$ square and announced that among them there are exactly $12$ counterfeit coins, and that these lie in one line of the square (one whole row or one whole column). All genuine coins weigh the same; the counterfeit coins may have different weights, but each of them is lighter than a genuine coin. The king asks the sage to find $110$ genuine coins using two weighings on a balance scale without weights. Can the sage act so as to be sure to accomplish the king's task?

A balance scale shows which of its two pans carries the heavier load; if the loads weigh the same, it shows equality. Any number of coins may be put on each pan.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Treat each possible counterfeit row or column as a separate possibility. Each weighing gives three possible outcomes, so record possibilities by pairs of outcomes.
Hint 2: key idea
Give each row and column a two-coordinate label from $\{1,2,3\}^2$. In each weighing, put coins on a pan only where both their row and column have that coordinate equal to the pan's label, leaving coordinate $3$ unweighed. (LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles)
Hint 3: approach
Use every label once for the rows and add an extra copy of each diagonal label; for columns add extra copies of $(1,2),(2,3),(3,1)$ instead. Check equal pan sizes and show how the counterfeit line's label determines both outcomes. Count the union of the possible rows and columns sharing one observed label before selecting genuine coins.
Common trap
The counterfeit weights may differ, so comparisons that put counterfeit coins on both pans need more justification than a count.
Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – Labelling every row and column by the pair of outcomes

Yes. Call the $24$ rows and columns lines. Each weighing has three outcomes: $1$ = the left pan is lighter, $2$ = the right pan is lighter, $3$ = balance. Give every line a label $(x,y)$ with $x,y\in\{1,2,3\}$, which will be the pair of outcomes of the two weighings if the counterfeit coins form this line:

  • rows: the labels $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, $(3,3)$ are given to two rows each, and each of the other six labels to one row ($3\cdot2+6=12$ rows);

  • columns: the labels $(1,2)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,1)$ are given to two columns each, and each of the other six labels to one column ($12$ columns).

Weighing $k$ ($k=1,2$): put on the left pan every coin whose row and column both have $k$-th label coordinate $1$, and on the right pan every coin whose row and column both have $k$-th label coordinate $2$. Exactly $4$ rows and $4$ columns have first coordinate $1$ (rows: $(1,1),(1,1),(1,2),(1,3)$; columns: $(1,1),(1,2),(1,2),(1,3)$), so the left pan gets $16$ coins; in the same way each pan gets $16$ coins in both weighings.

The outcome is determined. Let the counterfeit coins form a line $\ell$ with label $(x,y)$, and look at the first weighing.

  • If $x=1$: a coin of $\ell$ on the right pan would need both its row and its column to have first coordinate $2$, which is false for the line $\ell$ itself; so no counterfeit coin is on the right pan. On the other hand $\ell$ crosses the $4$ lines of the other direction with first coordinate $1$, so at least one counterfeit coin is on the left pan. All other coins on the scale are genuine and the pans hold equally many coins, so the left pan is lighter: outcome $1$.

  • If $x=2$: in the same way the right pan is lighter: outcome $2$.

  • If $x=3$: no coin of $\ell$ is on the scale, and the pans balance: outcome $3$.

This does not depend on the weights of the counterfeit coins. The same holds for the second weighing and $y$. So after the two weighings the sage knows the label $(x,y)$ of the counterfeit line.

Finding $110$ genuine coins. The lines with a given label are: two rows and one column, or one row and two columns, or one row and one column. In each case they cover at most $12+12+12-2=34$ coins. All the other coins, at least $144-34=110$ of them, are certainly genuine, and the sage hands them to the king.

Show solution 2 – Choosing the second weighing after the first

Yes. Call the $12$ rows and $12$ columns lines. Split the rows into three groups $\mathcal R_0,\mathcal R_1,\mathcal R_2$ of four rows each, and the columns into three groups $\mathcal C_0,\mathcal C_1,\mathcal C_2$ of four columns each. For $k=1,2$ let $B_k$ be the $4\times4$ block of coins lying in a row of $\mathcal R_k$ and a column of $\mathcal C_k$.

A general remark. Suppose both pans hold equally many coins and the counterfeit line meets at most one pan. If it meets the left pan, the left pan holds some lighter coins and the right pan only genuine ones, so the left pan is lighter; similarly for the right pan; if it meets neither pan, the pans balance. This holds whatever the individual weights of the counterfeit coins are.

First weighing: $B_1$ (16 coins) on the left pan, $B_2$ (16 coins) on the right pan. A row of $\mathcal R_1$ or a column of $\mathcal C_1$ meets $B_1$ and misses $B_2$; a row of $\mathcal R_2$ or a column of $\mathcal C_2$ meets $B_2$ and misses $B_1$; a line of $\mathcal R_0$ or $\mathcal C_0$ misses both. By the remark, the outcome (left lighter, right lighter, balance) tells the sage an index $g\in\{1,2,0\}$ such that the counterfeit line is one of the four rows $r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4$ of $\mathcal R_g$ or one of the four columns $c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4$ of $\mathcal C_g$.

Second weighing: the coins $(r_1,c_1)$ and $(r_2,c_1)$ on the left pan, the coins $(r_3,c_2)$ and $(r_3,c_3)$ on the right pan (here $(r,c)$ is the coin in row $r$ and column $c$). The candidate lines $r_1,r_2,c_1$ meet the left pan and miss the right one; $r_3,c_2,c_3$ meet the right pan and miss the left one; $r_4,c_4$ miss both. By the remark:

  • left lighter: the counterfeit line is $r_1$, $r_2$ or $c_1$; these lines cover $12+12+12-2=34$ coins;

  • right lighter: it is $r_3$, $c_2$ or $c_3$; these cover $12+12+12-2=34$ coins;

  • balance: it is $r_4$ or $c_4$; these cover $12+12-1=23$ coins.

Every coin outside the candidate lines is certainly genuine, so in all cases the sage can point to at least $144-34=110$ genuine coins.

Problem 5

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry · Auxiliary Lines for Congruence

Inside an isosceles triangle $ABC$ ($AB=AC$) a point $D$ is chosen such that $AD=BC$ and $\angle DAB=\angle DBC$. A point $K$ on the segment $BD$ is such that $\angle AKD=60^\circ$, and the points $K$ and $B$ are different. Prove that $\angle BAK=2\angle KAD$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Put $\alpha=\angle BAC$ and express $\angle ADB$ using the equality of angles in the statement. Use $D$ being interior to compare $AD$ with the equal legs of $ABC$.
Hint 2: key idea
On $AC$, seek an auxiliary point $E$ for which $\angle EBC=\alpha$. Compare the sides and angles of $EBC$, then try matching triangle $ADB$ with triangle $BEA$. (LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the auxiliary triangle to get the needed angle at $B$ in $ADB$, checking why the obtuse opposite angle makes the congruence unambiguous. Express the angles at $A$ in triangle $AKD$ and compare them with the full angle $BAD$.
Common trap
Two sides and an arbitrary equal angle do not establish congruence, so use the fact that the matching angle is obtuse.
Show solution 1 – A congruent copy of triangle ADB

Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$; then $\angle ABC=\angle ACB=90^\circ-\frac{\alpha}{2}$.

Step 1: $\angle ADB=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$. Since $D$ is inside the triangle, the ray $BD$ lies inside the angle $ABC$, so $\angle ABD+\angle DBC=\angle ABC$. Using $\angle DAB=\angle DBC$, \[\angle ADB=180^\circ-\angle DAB-\angle ABD=180^\circ-\angle DBC-\angle ABD=180^\circ-\angle ABC=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2 .\] Step 2: $\alpha<60^\circ$. All three vertices of the triangle lie in the closed disc with centre $A$ and radius $AB=AC$, so the whole triangle does, and the only points of the triangle on the boundary circle are $B$ and $C$. Hence $AD<AB$ for the interior point $D$, that is, $BC=AD<AB$. In the isosceles triangle $ABC$ the base is shorter than the legs exactly when the apex angle is the smallest angle: $\alpha<90^\circ-\frac\alpha2$, i.e. $\alpha<60^\circ$.

Step 3: an auxiliary point. Since $\alpha<90^\circ-\frac\alpha2=\angle ABC$, the ray from $B$ inside the angle $ABC$ that makes the angle $\alpha$ with $BC$ meets the side $AC$ at an interior point $E$. In the triangle $EBC$, \[\angle BEC=180^\circ-\alpha-\Bigl(90^\circ-\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=90^\circ-\frac\alpha2=\angle BCE,\] so $BE=BC=AD$. Moreover $\angle AEB=180^\circ-\angle BEC=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$, $\angle BAE=\alpha$ and $\angle ABE=\angle ABC-\alpha=90^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}{2}$.

Step 4: congruence. The triangles $ADB$ and $BEA$ have the common side $AB$, the equal sides $AD=BE$, and equal obtuse angles $\angle ADB=\angle BEA=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$ opposite the common side. Two triangles with two pairs of equal sides and equal obtuse angles opposite one of these pairs are congruent (the obtuse angle lies opposite the largest side, and for the angle opposite the larger side the “side-side-angle” test is valid). With the correspondence $A\leftrightarrow B$, $D\leftrightarrow E$, $B\leftrightarrow A$ we get \[\angle ABD=\angle BAE=\alpha,\qquad \angle BAD=\angle ABE=90^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}{2}.\] Step 5: the point $K$. $K$ lies on the segment $BD$ and differs from $B$ and from $D$, so $\angle ADK=\angle ADB=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$ and, in the triangle $AKD$, \[\angle KAD=180^\circ-60^\circ-\Bigl(90^\circ+\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=30^\circ-\frac\alpha2 .\] Since $K$ lies on the segment $BD$, the ray $AK$ lies inside the angle $BAD$, and \[\angle BAK=\angle BAD-\angle KAD=\Bigl(90^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}2\Bigr)-\Bigl(30^\circ-\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=60^\circ-\alpha=2\angle KAD .\]

Show solution 2 – Law of sines

Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$, so $\angle ABC=\angle ACB=90^\circ-\frac\alpha2$ and, by the law of sines in $ABC$, $BC=2AB\sin\frac\alpha2$.

Since $D$ is inside the triangle, $\angle ABD+\angle DBC=\angle ABC$, and with $\angle DAB=\angle DBC$ the triangle $ABD$ has \[\angle ADB=180^\circ-\angle DBC-\angle ABD=180^\circ-\angle ABC=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2 .\] The law of sines in $ABD$ gives \[\sin\angle ABD=\frac{AD\cdot\sin\angle ADB}{AB}=\frac{BC\cos\frac\alpha2}{AB}=2\sin\frac\alpha2\cos\frac\alpha2=\sin\alpha .\] Hence $\angle ABD=\alpha$ or $\angle ABD=180^\circ-\alpha$. The second value is impossible, because $\angle ABD<\angle ABC=90^\circ-\frac\alpha2<180^\circ-\alpha$. So $\angle ABD=\alpha$ and \[\angle BAD=180^\circ-\alpha-\Bigl(90^\circ+\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=90^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}{2}.\] The point $K\ne B$ lies on the segment $BD$ (and $K\ne D$, since the angle $AKD$ exists), so $\angle ADK=\angle ADB=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$ and in the triangle $AKD$ \[\angle KAD=180^\circ-60^\circ-\Bigl(90^\circ+\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=30^\circ-\frac\alpha2 .\] As $K$ is on the segment $BD$, the ray $AK$ lies inside the angle $BAD$, so \[\angle BAK=\angle BAD-\angle KAD=60^\circ-\alpha=2\Bigl(30^\circ-\frac\alpha2\Bigr)=2\angle KAD .\]

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

A council consists of $n\ge 5$ elves, each of whom trusts one or more of the other elves (trust need not be mutual). They want one of the elves to take the Ring of Omnipotence, and then the current owner of the Ring to pass it to one of the elves he trusts, and so on. It is known that in this way the Ring can get from any elf (not necessarily directly) to any other elf. The elves want to act so that after $K$ passes of the Ring it has visited every elf at least once. For what smallest $K$ (depending on $n$) can they certainly do this, regardless of who trusts whom?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw an arrow whenever one elf trusts another. Try following a path with distinct vertices, then decide how to continue if some elves remain unvisited.
Hint 2: key idea
Start with a longest path of distinct vertices and subsequently use a shortest route to an unvisited vertex. The longest-path size bounds the length of each such route. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Count the initial passes and the number of further stages in terms of the longest-path size, then bound the resulting product. For sharpness, try one chain of compulsory transfers leading to several branch vertices, each of which returns to the chain's start. Compare the time between successive first visits to different branch vertices.
Common trap
The elves may choose the initial holder, so the lower-bound example must work regardless of where they start.
Show the answer

$K=\left\lfloor \dfrac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor$

Show solution 1 – A longest chain of trust, and a council with one long cycle

Draw an arrow from each elf to every elf he trusts. A pass of the Ring goes along an arrow, and by hypothesis from every elf one can reach every other elf along arrows. Call a chain a sequence of distinct elves $v_1,\dots,v_m$ in which each trusts the next. The answer is $K=\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil$.

$\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ passes always suffice. Let $v_1,\dots,v_m$ be a chain with the largest possible number $m$ of elves; $m\ge2$, since every elf trusts somebody. Let $v_1$ take the Ring and pass it along the chain: after $m-1$ passes the elves $v_1,\dots,v_m$ have held it. Now repeat: while some elf has not held the Ring yet, pass it from the current owner along a shortest route to an elf who has not held it. Such a route exists, and it consists of distinct elves (if an elf occurred twice, cutting out the part between the two occurrences would give a shorter route). So the route is a chain, it has at most $m$ elves and at most $m-1$ passes, and it brings the Ring to at least one new elf. At most $n-m$ such stages are needed, so the total number of passes is at most \[(m-1)+(n-m)(m-1)=(m-1)(n-m+1).\] The two factors are positive integers with sum $n$, and $xy=\frac{(x+y)^2-(x-y)^2}{4}$ is largest when $|x-y|$ is smallest, i.e. $|x-y|\le1$; so $(m-1)(n-m+1)\le\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor\lceil\frac n2\rceil=\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\rfloor$. If the Ring has visited everybody earlier, the elves just keep passing it (everybody trusts somebody), so exactly $\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ passes can always be made with every elf visited.

Fewer passes are not always enough. Let $q=\lceil\frac n2\rceil-1$ and $p=n-q=\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor+1$; for $n\ge5$ we have $q\ge2$. Take elves $v_1,\dots,v_q$ and $x_1,\dots,x_p$; let $v_i$ trust only $v_{i+1}$ ($i<q$), let $v_q$ trust all of $x_1,\dots,x_p$, and let each $x_j$ trust only $v_1$. From any elf one can reach any other along $x_j\to v_1\to\dots\to v_q\to x_{j'}$, so the condition holds. The Ring can leave an elf $x_j$ only to $v_1$, then must go along $v_2,\dots,v_q$, and only $v_q$ can pass it to an elf $x_{j'}$. So between two moments at which two different elves $x_j$, $x_{j'}$ hold the Ring there are at least $q+1$ passes. Order the elves $x_1,\dots,x_p$ by the moment they first receive the Ring; between consecutive first visits there are at least $q+1$ passes, so in total at least \[(p-1)(q+1)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac n2\Bigr\rfloor\Bigl\lceil\frac n2\Bigr\rceil=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\Bigr\rfloor\] passes are needed. Hence the smallest $K$ that always works is $\boxed{\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor}$.

Show solution 2 – A shortest covering route, and a council with one long cycle

Draw an arrow from each elf to every elf he trusts; a pass of the Ring goes along an arrow, and from every elf one can reach every other elf along arrows. A route of length $L$ is a sequence of elves $w_0,w_1,\dots,w_L$ in which each trusts the next; it is covering if every elf occurs in it. The answer is $K=\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil$.

$\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ passes always suffice. Covering routes exist (go from one elf to the next one along arrows), so choose a covering route $W=(w_0,\dots,w_L)$ of the smallest possible length. Call an elf single if he occurs exactly once in $W$, and let $k$ be the number of single elves.

(a) $w_0$ and $w_L$ are single, and different. If $w_0$ occurred again, the route $w_1,\dots,w_L$ would still be covering and shorter; similarly for $w_L$. As $L\ge1$, they occupy different positions, and a single elf has only one position, so $w_0\ne w_L$ and $k\ge2$.

(b) If $w_i=w_j$ with $i<j$, then a single elf occurs at some position strictly between $i$ and $j$. The sequence $w_0,\dots,w_i,w_{j+1},\dots,w_L$ is a route (as $w_i=w_j$ trusts $w_{j+1}$) and is shorter than $W$, so it misses some elf $x$. All occurrences of $x$ in $W$ are at positions $i+1,\dots,j$, and not at $j$ (because $w_j=w_i$ is kept), so they lie strictly between $i$ and $j$. If $x$ is single we are done. Otherwise take two occurrences $i'<j'$ of $x$, with $i<i'<j'<j$, and repeat the argument for them. The interval shrinks each time, so after finitely many steps we find a single elf strictly between $i$ and $j$.

(c) An elf occurring $t\ge2$ times satisfies $t\le k-1$. Let his positions be $p_1<\dots<p_t$. By (b), each of the $t-1$ open gaps $(p_s,p_{s+1})$ contains a position of a single elf; the gaps are disjoint and a single elf has only one position, so these are $t-1$ different single elves. None of them is $w_0$ or $w_L$, because the gaps lie strictly between $p_1\ge 0$ and $p_t\le L$. Hence $t-1\le k-2$.

Counting the $L+1$ positions of $W$: the $k$ single elves take $k$ positions and each of the other $n-k$ elves at most $k-1$, so \[L+1\le k+(n-k)(k-1),\qquad L\le (k-1)(n-k+1).\] The factors $k-1$ and $n-k+1$ are positive integers with sum $n$; since $xy=\frac{(x+y)^2-(x-y)^2}{4}$ is largest when $|x-y|\le1$, we get $L\le\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor\lceil\frac n2\rceil=\lfloor\frac{n^2}4\rfloor$. So the elves give the Ring to $w_0$ and pass it along $W$; every elf has held it after $L\le\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ passes, and afterwards they keep passing it (everybody trusts somebody) until exactly $\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ passes have been made.

Fewer passes are not always enough. Let $r=\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ and $q=n-r-1$; for $n\ge5$ we have $q\ge2$. Take elves $v_1,\dots,v_q$ and $x_1,\dots,x_{r+1}$; let $v_i$ trust only $v_{i+1}$ ($i<q$), let $v_q$ trust all of $x_1,\dots,x_{r+1}$, and let each $x_j$ trust only $v_1$. From any elf one can reach any other along $x_j\to v_1\to\dots\to v_q\to x_{j'}$, so the condition holds. From an elf $x_j$ the Ring can only go to $v_1$, then along $v_2,\dots,v_q$, and only $v_q$ passes it to an elf $x_{j'}$; so between the moments when two different elves $x_j$, $x_{j'}$ hold the Ring there are at least $q+1=n-r$ passes. Ordering $x_1,\dots,x_{r+1}$ by the moment they first get the Ring, we need at least \[r(n-r)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac n2\Bigr\rfloor\Bigl\lceil\frac n2\Bigr\rceil=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n^2}4\Bigr\rfloor\] passes. Hence the smallest $K$ that always works is $\boxed{\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor}$.

Since $XB=XD$ and the ray $BX$ is the ray $BC$, \[\angle XDB=\angle XBD=\angle CBD=90^\circ-\beta .\] Because $\beta+\gamma=180^\circ-\alpha>90^\circ$, we have $90^\circ-\beta<\gamma=\angle BDA$. So the ray $DX$ lies inside the angle $BDA$ and \[\angle XDA=\angle BDA-\angle BDX=\gamma-(90^\circ-\beta)=90^\circ-\alpha .\] In the same way, $\angle YDC=\angle YCD=\angle BCD=\angle BAD=90^\circ-\gamma<\beta=\angle ADC$, so the ray $DY$ lies inside the angle $ADC$ and $\angle ADY=\beta-(90^\circ-\gamma)=90^\circ-\alpha$.

Thus the rays $DX$ and $DY$ lie on different sides of the line $DA$ and make equal angles with the ray $DA$: $DA$ is the bisector of the angle $XDY$.

Show solution 2 – Reflection in the line OX

Let $O$ be the circumcentre; it is the midpoint of the diameter $AD$, so the ray $DO$ is the ray $DA$. Since the triangle is acute, $O$ lies inside it, and the central angle $BOC$ equals $2\alpha$, so the isosceles triangle $BOC$ has base angles $\angle OBC=\angle OCB=90^\circ-\alpha$.

Show solution 3 – Coordinates and the sum of two unit vectors

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the base angles of the isosceles triangle $ABC$ and compare $\angle XAK$ with $\angle YCK$. Look for a side ratio that would make triangles $XAK,YCK$ similar.
Hint 2: key idea

Hint 3: approach
Locate the new intersections on $KC$ and $KA$, then use power at $C$ and $A$ to express $CY$ and $AX$. Divide the two expressions and use $AB=BC$ to compare the sides around the equal angles.

Use the interior locations of $X,Y$ to locate the additional intersections before subtracting segment lengths.
Show solution 1 – Power of a point and similar triangles

Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $ABD$ and $\omega_2$ the circle $BCD$. We shall prove that the triangles $XAK$ and $YCK$ are similar.

The angles at $A$ and $C$. Since $X$ lies on the side $AB$ and $Y$ on the side $BC$, the rays $AX$, $CY$ are the rays $AB$, $CB$, so $\angle XAK=\angle BAC=\angle BCA=\angle YCK$ (the triangle $ABC$ is isosceles).

The ratio $AX:CY$. The point $K$ lies inside the chord $BD$ of $\omega_1$, hence inside $\omega_1$. The point $Y\ne B$ of $\omega_1$ lies on the side $BC$, so the chord $BY$ of $\omega_1$ is part of the segment $BC$ and $C$ lies outside $\omega_1$. Therefore the segment $KC$ meets $\omega_1$ at exactly one point $A'$, and the line $AC$ meets $\omega_1$ at $A$ and $A'$, with $K$ between them. By the power of the point $K$, \[KA\cdot KA'=KB\cdot KD,\qquad\text{so}\qquad CA'=CK-KA'=\frac{KA\cdot KC-KB\cdot KD}{KA}>0 .\] By the power of the point $C$ with respect to $\omega_1$ (secants $CYB$ and $CA'A$), \[CY\cdot CB=CA'\cdot CA,\qquad CY=\frac{CA\,(KA\cdot KC-KB\cdot KD)}{CB\cdot KA}.\] In exactly the same way, with $\omega_2$, the second intersection $C'$ of the line $AC$ with $\omega_2$ lies on the segment $AK$, $KC\cdot KC'=KB\cdot KD$, and \[AX\cdot AB=AC'\cdot AC,\qquad AX=\frac{AC\,(KA\cdot KC-KB\cdot KD)}{AB\cdot KC}.\] Since $AB=CB$, dividing gives \[\frac{AX}{CY}=\frac{KA}{KC},\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{AX}{AK}=\frac{CY}{CK}.\] Conclusion. The triangles $XAK$ and $YCK$ have equal angles at $A$ and $C$ and proportional sides around these angles, so they are similar, and the corresponding angles at $K$ are equal: $\angle AKX=\angle CKY$.

Show solution 2 – Similar triangles at D and a ratio of areas

Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $ABD$ and $\omega_2$ the circle $BCD$. If $Y=C$, then $C$ lies on $\omega_1$, so $ABCD$ is cyclic, $X=A$ and the angle $AKX$ is not defined; so $Y\ne C$, and likewise $X\ne A$.

The rays $AX$, $AK$ are the rays $AB$, $AC$ and the rays $CY$, $CK$ are the rays $CB$, $CA$, so $\angle XAK=\angle BAC=\angle BCA=\angle YCK$ (the triangle $ABC$ is isosceles). Show solution 3 – Coordinates centred at B

Scale so that $AB=BC=1$, and take $B$ as the origin and the bisector of the angle $ABC$ as the $x$-axis: \[B=(0,0),\qquad A=(u,v),\qquad C=(u,-v),\qquad u,v>0,\ u^2+v^2=1 .\] The diagonal $AC$ lies on the line $x=u$. Let $D=(p,q)$. As $ABCD$ is convex, the diagonals meet at a point $K$ inside both; $K$ is the point of $BD$ with $x=u$, so $p>u$ and \[K=(u,w),\qquad w=\frac{uq}{p},\qquad -v<w<v .\] The circles. A circle through the origin has an equation $x^2+y^2+rx+sy=0$. Let the circle $ABD$ be $x^2+y^2+rx+sy=0$ and the circle $BCD$ be $x^2+y^2+r'x+s'y=0$. Substituting $A$, $C$ and $D$ (and $u^2+v^2=1$): \[ru+sv=-1,\qquad r'u-s'v=-1,\qquad p^2+q^2+rp+sq=0=p^2+q^2+r'p+s'q .\] The points $X$ and $Y$. The point $\lambda C=(\lambda u,-\lambda v)$ lies on the circle $ABD$ when $\lambda^2+\lambda(ru-sv)=0$; the root $\lambda\ne0$ is $\lambda=sv-ru=1+2sv$, so $Y=\lambda C$ with $\lambda=1+2sv$. In the same way $X=\mu A$ with $\mu=-(r'u+s'v)=1-2s'v$.

A relation between $\lambda$ and $\mu$. Subtracting the two equations for $D$ gives $(r-r')p+(s-s')q=0$. From the equations for $A$ and $C$, $u(r-r')=(-1-sv)-(-1+s'v)=-v(s+s')$. Hence $-\frac{vp}{u}(s+s')+(s-s')q=0$, and multiplying by $\frac up$, \[s(w-v)=s'(w+v).\] Therefore \[(v+w)(\mu-1)=-2vs'(v+w)=-2vs(w-v)=(v-w)(\lambda-1). \tag{1}\] A reflection. We have \[\overrightarrow{KX}=\bigl(u(\mu-1),\ v\mu-w\bigr),\qquad \overrightarrow{KY}=\bigl(u(\lambda-1),\ -v\lambda-w\bigr).\] Multiply them by the positive numbers $v+w$ and $v-w$. By (1) the first coordinates become equal. The second coordinates are opposite: by (1), $(v+w)\mu-(v-w)\lambda=(v+w)-(v-w)=2w$, so \[(v+w)(v\mu-w)+(v-w)(-v\lambda-w)=v\bigl((v+w)\mu-(v-w)\lambda\bigr)-2vw=2vw-2vw=0 .\] So the ray $KY$ is the mirror image of the ray $KX$ in the line $m$ through $K$ parallel to the $x$-axis. Yes. Let $S=\{4^k:\ k=0,1,2,\dots\}$; every $4^k=(2^k)^2$ is a square of a natural number.

Take two distinct elements $x=4^i$ and $y=4^j$ with $i<j$, and put $d=j-i\ge1$. Choose \[z=4^{\,2j-i-1}\in S\qquad(\text{note } 2j-i-1\ge j\ge 1).\] Then \[x+y+z=4^i\bigl(1+4^{d}+4^{2d-1}\bigr)=4^i\bigl(1+2\cdot2^{2d-1}+(2^{2d-1})^2\bigr)=\bigl(2^i(2^{2d-1}+1)\bigr)^2 ,\] a square of a natural number. Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction · also Grade 11 Day 1, Problem 4

A hotel has $a>1$ floors, and there are $b$ single rooms on each floor. Exactly $ab$ mathematicians came to a mathematical congress. It turned out that each mathematician likes exactly one room on each floor. Prove that the number of ways to accommodate all the mathematicians in the hotel (one in each room) so that everyone likes his or her room is even.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Make a zero-one table with mathematicians as rows and rooms as columns. Describe a valid accommodation by the entries it selects in that table.
Hint 2: key idea
Count accommodations by the sum of products selecting one entry in every row and column. Modulo $2$, compare that sum with the determinant, since changing the sign of a product has no effect. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Add the columns for all rooms on one floor and inspect the resulting column vector. Compare that vector for two different floors to find a relation among the columns. Use the relation in the determinant comparison.
Common trap
The number of valid accommodations can be zero, which is even.
Show solution 1 – The determinant modulo 2

Let $N=ab$, number the mathematicians $1,\dots,N$ and the rooms $1,\dots,N$, and let $M$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with $m_{ij}=1$ if mathematician $i$ likes room $j$ and $m_{ij}=0$ otherwise. An admissible accommodation is a permutation $\sigma$ with $m_{1\sigma(1)}=\dots=m_{N\sigma(N)}=1$, so the number of accommodations is \[\operatorname{per}M=\sum_{\sigma}m_{1\sigma(1)}m_{2\sigma(2)}\cdots m_{N\sigma(N)} .\] The determinant $\det M$ is the same sum with signs $\pm1$, so $\operatorname{per}M\equiv\det M\pmod 2$.

Each mathematician likes exactly one room on the first floor, so in every row of $M$ exactly one of the $b$ columns of first-floor rooms contains a $1$: the sum of these $b$ columns is the column consisting of $N$ ones. The same is true for the $b$ columns of the second floor (there is a second floor since $a>1$). Hence \[\bigl(\text{sum of the first-floor columns}\bigr)-\bigl(\text{sum of the second-floor columns}\bigr)=0,\] a nontrivial linear relation between the columns of $M$. Therefore $\det M=0$, and $\operatorname{per}M\equiv0\pmod2$: the number of accommodations is even.

Show solution 2 – Linearity of the permanent

Let $N=ab$ and let $M$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with $m_{ij}=1$ if mathematician $i$ likes room $j$, else $0$. The number of accommodations is the permanent $\operatorname{per}M=\sum_\sigma\prod_i m_{i\sigma(i)}$ over all permutations $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,N\}$. We use two properties of the permanent.

  • It is linear in each column (every product contains exactly one entry of each column).

  • A matrix with two equal columns $r\ne s$ has an even permanent: composing a permutation with the transposition of $r$ and $s$ (exchanging which rows are assigned to columns $r$ and $s$) does not change the product; doing it twice gives back the original permutation, and it never gives the same permutation, so it splits all permutations into pairs with equal products.

Fix a room $r$ on the first floor, and for every room $s$ on the first or second floor let $M_s$ be the matrix $M$ with column $r$ replaced by column $s$; so $M_r=M$. In each row exactly one first-floor entry and exactly one second-floor entry is $1$, hence the sum of all these $2b$ columns is the column of $2$'s. By linearity in column $r$, \[\sum_{s}\operatorname{per}M_s=\operatorname{per}\bigl(M\text{ with column } r \text{ replaced by } (2,\dots,2)^T\bigr)=2\operatorname{per}M',\] where $M'$ is $M$ with column $r$ replaced by ones. For $s\ne r$ the matrix $M_s$ has two equal columns ($r$ and $s$), so $\operatorname{per}M_s$ is even. Therefore \[\operatorname{per}M=2\operatorname{per}M'-\sum_{s\ne r}\operatorname{per}M_s\] is even.

Show solution 3 – Reversing a cycle of preferences

Call an accommodation good if everybody likes his or her room. We pair up the good accommodations: we construct a rule that sends every good accommodation $\sigma$ to a different good accommodation $\sigma'$ and sends $\sigma'$ back to $\sigma$. Then the good accommodations split into pairs $\{\sigma,\sigma'\}$, and their number is even.

Since $a>1$, there are floors $1$ and $2$; fix an order of their $2b$ rooms.

The arrows. Let $\sigma$ be good. For each room $R$ on floor $1$ or $2$, draw an arrow from $R$ to the room $f(R)$ on the other of these two floors that the occupant of $R$ likes. Every one of the $2b$ rooms has exactly one outgoing arrow. Starting from any room and following arrows we must eventually repeat a room, so the arrows contain cycles. A cycle alternates between the two floors, so it has at least two rooms. Let $R_1$ be the first room (in the fixed order) that lies on a cycle, and let \[R_1\to R_2\to\dots\to R_k\to R_1\qquad(k\ge2,\ R_1,\dots,R_k\text{ distinct})\] be its cycle.

The new accommodation. Let $\sigma'$ be obtained by moving the occupant of $R_i$ to $R_{i+1}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$ (with $R_{k+1}=R_1$); everybody else stays. Each room $R_i$ receives exactly one person, and the occupant of $R_i$ likes $R_{i+1}=f(R_i)$, so $\sigma'$ is good. It differs from $\sigma$, because the occupant of $R_1$ has moved to $R_2\ne R_1$.

The arrows of $\sigma'$. The new occupant of $R_{i+1}$ lived in $R_i$ under $\sigma$, so he likes $R_i$; this room lies on the floor (among $1,2$) other than that of $R_{i+1}$, and he likes exactly one room there, so the new arrow from $R_{i+1}$ goes to $R_i$. Rooms off the chosen cycle keep their occupants and their arrows. So the arrows of $\sigma'$ are those of $\sigma$ with the chosen cycle reversed.

Reversing it does not change which rooms lie on cycles. Indeed, take a room $R$ off the chosen cycle. If the path of arrows from $R$ reaches the chosen cycle, it then stays on that cycle forever (before and after the reversal), so it never returns to $R$. If it does not reach the chosen cycle, it uses only unchanged arrows, so it is the same path for $\sigma$ and for $\sigma'$. Hence $R_1$ is again the first room on a cycle, and its cycle for $\sigma'$ is $R_1\to R_k\to\dots\to R_2\to R_1$. Applying the rule to $\sigma'$ moves the occupant of $R_{i+1}$ back to $R_i$ for every $i$, which gives $\sigma$ again.

So the rule pairs every good accommodation with a different one, and the number of good accommodations is even.

(LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy)

Show solution 1 – Induction on the number of boxes

Yes. Call a box bad if its two balls have the same colour; the player gets the prize at the first moment when there is no bad box. We prove, by induction on $m\ge2$, the following statement for a game with $m$ colours and $m$ boxes numbered $1,\dots,m$:

The sequence of moves $S_2=(1,2)$, $S_m=S_{m-1},\,(1,m),\,S_{m-1}$ has the property that for every initial arrangement and every behaviour of the magician, at some moment (possibly the initial one) there is no bad box.

Two observations. (a) If the player names a bad box $Z=\{c,c\}$ and another box $\{f,g\}$, then after the swap $Z$ contains $c$ and $f$ or $g$; neither equals $c$, because both balls of colour $c$ were in $Z$. So $Z$ is no longer bad. (b) Moves that do not involve a box do not change its contents.

Base $m=2$. Either both boxes are two-coloured at the start, or they are $\{a,a\}$, $\{b,b\}$; then the move $(1,2)$ produces $\{a,b\}$, $\{a,b\}$.

Step. Let $m\ge3$ and let $Z$ be box $m$; the sequence $S_{m-1}$ never touches $Z$.

Claim. If $Z$ is not bad at the start of a run of $S_{m-1}$, then during this run there is a moment with no bad box. Indeed, let $Z=\{c,d\}$ with $c\ne d$; the other ball of colour $c$ and the other ball of colour $d$ lie in boxes among $1,\dots,m-1$. We get an arrangement of $m-1$ colours (two balls each) in the boxes $1,\dots,m-1$, two balls per box, and the real swaps during $S_{m-1}$ are swaps of this imaginary game (the same physical balls move). By the induction hypothesis, at some moment of the run no box among $1,\dots,m-1$ is bad in the imaginary game. Repainting only merges colours, so a box that is bad in the real game is bad in the imaginary one; hence at that moment no box among $1,\dots,m-1$ is bad in the real game, and $Z=\{c,d\}$ is not bad either.

Now consider $S_m$. If $Z$ is not bad at the start, the claim applies to the first run of $S_{m-1}$. Call a set of $k$ consecutive natural numbers good if one can choose a prime divisor of each of these numbers so that different numbers get different chosen primes. Otherwise call the set bad. Is it true that for every natural $k$ the number of bad sets of $k$ consecutive natural numbers is finite?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Two different numbers in a block of $k$ consecutive integers differ by less than $k$. What does that tell you about a common divisor of those two numbers?
Hint 2: key idea
Factor each sufficiently large number into its exact prime-power factors. If every one of these factors is smaller than $k$, bound both their sizes and how many distinct primes can occur. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use this observation to give a threshold beyond which each number has an exact prime-power factor at least $k$, then choose the prime below that factor. If the same prime is chosen twice, compare the smaller of its two exact powers with the difference of the two numbers. Treat $k=1$ directly.
Common trap
A large prime-power factor can come from a small prime, so keep track of the exponent as well.
Show the answer

Yes, for every $k$ there are only finitely many bad sets

Show solution 1 – Choosing the prime of the largest prime power

Yes. For $k=1$ the only bad set is $\{1\}$ (every number greater than $1$ has a prime divisor). Let $k\ge2$.

Lemma. Every natural number $x\ge k^k$ has a prime power divisor $p^e$ with $p^e\ge k$ and $p^{e+1}\nmid x$.

Indeed, otherwise every prime power $p^e$ that divides $x$ exactly is less than $k$; then every prime divisor of $x$ is less than $k$, so $x$ has fewer than $k$ distinct prime divisors, and $x$ is a product of fewer than $k$ factors, each less than $k$. Hence $x<k^{k}$, a contradiction.

Every set of $k$ consecutive numbers all $\ge k^k$ is good. For each number $x$ of the set choose, by the lemma, a prime $p(x)$ with $p(x)^{e}\ge k$, where $p(x)^e$ is the exact power of $p(x)$ dividing $x$. Suppose two different numbers $x$, $y$ of the set got the same prime $p$, with exact powers $p^e$ in $x$ and $p^f$ in $y$, both at least $k$. Then $p^{\min(e,f)}\ge k$ divides $x-y$, while $0<|x-y|\le k-1$, which is impossible. So the chosen primes are distinct and the set is good.

Consequently a bad set must contain a number smaller than $k^k$, i.e. its least element is less than $k^k$. There are fewer than $k^k$ such sets, so the bad sets are finitely many.

Show solution 2 – Hall's marriage theorem and small primes

Yes. For $k=1$ the only bad set is $\{1\}$, so let $k\ge2$, and let $\{n+1,\dots,n+k\}$ be a bad set with $n\ge1$ (only one set, $\{1,\dots,k\}$, contains $1$). Form the bipartite graph joining each number of the set to its prime divisors. Choosing distinct prime divisors means finding a matching that covers all the numbers, so by Hall's marriage theorem there is a subset $X$ of $m$ numbers of the set that together have fewer than $m$ prime divisors.

Call a prime big if it is at least $k$ and small otherwise. A big prime divides at most one number of the set, since two of its multiples differ by at least $k$. Let $X_1$ be the numbers of $X$ having a big prime divisor and $X_0=X\setminus X_1$, $s=|X_0|$. Assigning to each $x\in X_1$ one of its big primes gives distinct primes, so $X$ has at least $|X_1|$ big prime divisors, and the numbers of $X_0$, which have only small prime divisors, use at most \[t\le (m-1)-|X_1|=s-1\] primes in total. As $t\ge0$, this forces $s\ge1$; every number of $X_0$ exceeds $1$ and so has a prime divisor, hence $t\ge1$ and $s\ge t+1\ge2$.

Each $x\in X_0$ is a product of at most $t$ prime powers, so its largest prime power factor $q(x)=p^e$ (exact power) satisfies $p^e\ge x^{1/t}$. There are $s\ge t+1$ numbers in $X_0$ but only $t$ primes, so two different numbers $x,y\in X_0$ have their largest prime power factors $p^e$, $p^f$ with the same prime $p$. Then $p^{\min(e,f)}$ divides $x-y\ne0$, hence $p^{\min(e,f)}\le k-1$, and \[\min(x,y)\le \bigl(p^{\min(e,f)}\bigr)^{t}\le (k-1)^{t}\le (k-1)^{k-1}.\] So every bad set contains a number at most $(k-1)^{k-1}$, and there are finitely many bad sets.

A natural number $n\ge 2$ is given. A symmetric polynomial $P(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with real coefficients is such that the equation $P(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0$ has real solutions, and all these solutions are obtained by permuting the numbers of one tuple $a_1,\dots,a_n$. For example, the polynomial $P(x,y)=x^3y^3+xy^4+x^4y$ has degree $6$ (since $3+3=6$, and this is more than $4+1=5$).

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try prescribing a tuple with only two possible coordinate values. How could several nonnegative polynomial terms force every coordinate to have one of those values?

Hint 3: approach

Symmetry supplies different zeros when the prescribed tuple has two different entries.
Show the answer

$4$ for every $n\ge 2$

Show solution 1 – A sum of squares, and why degrees up to 3 fail

The answer is $\boxed{4}$ for every $n\ge2$.

Degree $4$ is possible. Let \[P(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2(x_i-1)^2+\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i-1\Bigr)^2 .\] It is symmetric and has degree $4$. A sum of squares of real numbers vanishes only if every square vanishes, so $P=0$ exactly when every $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ and $x_1+\dots+x_n=1$, i.e. when $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is a permutation of $(1,0,\dots,0)$, a tuple with two different numbers.

Degree at most $3$ is impossible. Let $P$ satisfy the conditions. Its zero set $Z$ is the set of permutations of $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$: it is finite and has at least two points (exchange two different entries). $P$ is not the zero polynomial and not a nonzero constant (then $Z$ would be everything or empty).

Odd degree $d$ ($d=1$ or $3$). Let $H$ be the sum of the terms of degree $d$; $H$ is a nonzero polynomial, so $H(v)\ne0$ for some vector $v$. For any point $w$, the function $t\mapsto P(w+tv)$ is a polynomial in $t$ of degree $d$ with leading coefficient $H(v)\ne0$; having odd degree, it has a real root. The lines $\{w+tv\}$ for $w=w_0+\lambda u$, with $u$ not parallel to $v$ (possible as $n\ge2$) and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, are pairwise disjoint, so $Z$ is infinite, a contradiction.

Degree $2$. Take two different zeros $p\ne q$ and consider $g(t)=P(p+t(q-p))$, a polynomial of degree at most $2$ with $g(0)=g(1)=0$. If $g\equiv0$, the whole line $pq$ lies in $Z$, a contradiction. Otherwise $g(t)=c\,t(t-1)$ with $c\ne0$, so $g(\tfrac12)$ and $g(2)$ have opposite signs: $P$ takes a positive value at some point $u$ and a negative value at some point $u'$. Choose a vector $e$ not parallel to $u'-u$ and, for $\lambda>0$, the broken line from $u$ to $w_\lambda=\frac{u+u'}{2}+\lambda e$ and on to $u'$. Different $\lambda$ give broken lines that meet only at $u$ and $u'$, and by the intermediate value theorem $P$ vanishes somewhere on each of them (not at $u$, $u'$). So $Z$ is infinite, a contradiction.

Hence the smallest possible degree is $4$, independently of $n$.

The answer is $\boxed{4}$ for every $n\ge2$.

Degree $4$ is possible. Degree at most $3$ is impossible. Let $P$ satisfy the conditions and have degree at most $3$; its zero set $Z$ consists of the permutations of $p=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$, so it is finite. Choose $i\ne j$ with $a_i\ne a_j$ and let $q$ be $p$ with the entries $i$ and $j$ exchanged; then $q\ne p$ and $q\in Z$.

Exchanging the coordinates $i$ and $j$ maps the point $p+t(q-p)$ to $q+t(p-q)=p+(1-t)(q-p)$, and $P$ is symmetric, so $g(t)=g(1-t)$. A path avoiding $Z$. For each $z\in Z$, the points $w$ for which the segment $uw$ passes through $z$ lie on the line $uz$, and those for which the segment $wu'$ passes through $z$ lie on the line $u'z$. Petya can get $99$. Petya multiplies by $2$ on his first move and by $3$ on all later moves, so after his $j$-th move he has $2\cdot3^{j-1}$. After round $1$ Vasya cannot have $2$, so he has $3$. If before Vasya's $j$-th move he has $3^{j-1}$, then multiplying by $2$ would give $2\cdot3^{j-1}$, Petya's number, which is forbidden; so he must multiply by $3$. Hence at the end Petya has $2\cdot3^{99}$ with $a=2\cdot100=200$ divisors and Vasya has $3^{100}$ with $b=101$ divisors, and Petya gets $|a-b|=99$.

Vasya can keep it at most $99$. Suppose Petya's first move is $\times2$. Then Vasya multiplies by $3$ on his first move and afterwards always uses the same multiplier as Petya used just before. After every round Vasya's exponent of $2$ is exactly one less than Petya's, so his moves are legal. If Petya ends with exponent $p$ ($1\le p\le100$), Vasya ends with $p-1$, and \[a-b=f(p)-f(p-1)=(p+1)(101-p)-p(102-p)=101-2p,\qquad |101-2p|\le 99 .\] If Petya's first move is $\times3$, Vasya does the same with the roles of $2$ and $3$ exchanged (his exponent of $3$ is one less than Petya's); since $f$ is symmetric in the exponents of $2$ and $3$, again $|a-b|=|101-2q|\le99$ with $1\le q\le100$.

So the answer is $n=\boxed{99}$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction · also Grade 9, Problem 6

A hotel has $a>1$ floors, and there are $b$ single rooms on each floor. Exactly $ab$ mathematicians came to a mathematical congress. It turned out that each mathematician likes exactly one room on each floor. Prove that the number of ways to accommodate all the mathematicians in the hotel (one in each room) so that everyone likes his or her room is even.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Number the mathematicians and rooms, and record each person's acceptable rooms in a rectangular array of zeros and ones. Describe an accommodation by choosing one acceptable entry in every row and every column.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the square incidence matrix: the accommodation count is its permanent, whose parity agrees with its determinant because signs disappear modulo $2$. Look for a relation between the columns corresponding to two floors. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Sum the columns for each of two distinct floors and compare the resulting vectors. Use this comparison to investigate column dependence, then connect the determinant to the parity of the accommodation count.
Common trap
The column relation needs two different floors; this is exactly where $a>1$ is used.
Show solution 1 – The determinant modulo 2

Let $N=ab$, number the mathematicians $1,\dots,N$ and the rooms $1,\dots,N$, and let $M$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with $m_{ij}=1$ if mathematician $i$ likes room $j$ and $m_{ij}=0$ otherwise. An admissible accommodation is a permutation $\sigma$ with $m_{1\sigma(1)}=\dots=m_{N\sigma(N)}=1$, so the number of accommodations is \[\operatorname{per}M=\sum_{\sigma}m_{1\sigma(1)}m_{2\sigma(2)}\cdots m_{N\sigma(N)} .\] The determinant $\det M$ is the same sum with signs $\pm1$, so $\operatorname{per}M\equiv\det M\pmod 2$.

Each mathematician likes exactly one room on the first floor, so in every row of $M$ exactly one of the $b$ columns of first-floor rooms contains a $1$: the sum of these $b$ columns is the column consisting of $N$ ones. The same is true for the $b$ columns of the second floor (there is a second floor since $a>1$). Hence \[\bigl(\text{sum of the first-floor columns}\bigr)-\bigl(\text{sum of the second-floor columns}\bigr)=0,\] a nontrivial linear relation between the columns of $M$. Therefore $\det M=0$, and $\operatorname{per}M\equiv0\pmod2$: the number of accommodations is even.

Show solution 2 – Linearity of the permanent

Let $N=ab$ and let $M$ be the $N\times N$ matrix with $m_{ij}=1$ if mathematician $i$ likes room $j$, else $0$. The number of accommodations is the permanent $\operatorname{per}M=\sum_\sigma\prod_i m_{i\sigma(i)}$ over all permutations $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,N\}$. We use two properties of the permanent.

  • It is linear in each column (every product contains exactly one entry of each column).

  • A matrix with two equal columns $r\ne s$ has an even permanent: composing a permutation with the transposition of $r$ and $s$ (exchanging which rows are assigned to columns $r$ and $s$) does not change the product; doing it twice gives back the original permutation, and it never gives the same permutation, so it splits all permutations into pairs with equal products.

Fix a room $r$ on the first floor, and for every room $s$ on the first or second floor let $M_s$ be the matrix $M$ with column $r$ replaced by column $s$; so $M_r=M$. In each row exactly one first-floor entry and exactly one second-floor entry is $1$, hence the sum of all these $2b$ columns is the column of $2$'s. By linearity in column $r$, \[\sum_{s}\operatorname{per}M_s=\operatorname{per}\bigl(M\text{ with column } r \text{ replaced by } (2,\dots,2)^T\bigr)=2\operatorname{per}M',\] where $M'$ is $M$ with column $r$ replaced by ones. For $s\ne r$ the matrix $M_s$ has two equal columns ($r$ and $s$), so $\operatorname{per}M_s$ is even. Therefore \[\operatorname{per}M=2\operatorname{per}M'-\sum_{s\ne r}\operatorname{per}M_s\] is even.

Show solution 3 – Reversing a cycle of preferences

Call an accommodation good if everybody likes his or her room. We pair up the good accommodations: we construct a rule that sends every good accommodation $\sigma$ to a different good accommodation $\sigma'$ and sends $\sigma'$ back to $\sigma$. Then the good accommodations split into pairs $\{\sigma,\sigma'\}$, and their number is even.

Since $a>1$, there are floors $1$ and $2$; fix an order of their $2b$ rooms.

The arrows. Let $\sigma$ be good. For each room $R$ on floor $1$ or $2$, draw an arrow from $R$ to the room $f(R)$ on the other of these two floors that the occupant of $R$ likes. Every one of the $2b$ rooms has exactly one outgoing arrow. Starting from any room and following arrows we must eventually repeat a room, so the arrows contain cycles. A cycle alternates between the two floors, so it has at least two rooms. Let $R_1$ be the first room (in the fixed order) that lies on a cycle, and let \[R_1\to R_2\to\dots\to R_k\to R_1\qquad(k\ge2,\ R_1,\dots,R_k\text{ distinct})\] be its cycle.

The new accommodation. Let $\sigma'$ be obtained by moving the occupant of $R_i$ to $R_{i+1}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$ (with $R_{k+1}=R_1$); everybody else stays. Each room $R_i$ receives exactly one person, and the occupant of $R_i$ likes $R_{i+1}=f(R_i)$, so $\sigma'$ is good. It differs from $\sigma$, because the occupant of $R_1$ has moved to $R_2\ne R_1$.

The arrows of $\sigma'$. The new occupant of $R_{i+1}$ lived in $R_i$ under $\sigma$, so he likes $R_i$; this room lies on the floor (among $1,2$) other than that of $R_{i+1}$, and he likes exactly one room there, so the new arrow from $R_{i+1}$ goes to $R_i$. Rooms off the chosen cycle keep their occupants and their arrows. So the arrows of $\sigma'$ are those of $\sigma$ with the chosen cycle reversed.

Reversing it does not change which rooms lie on cycles. Indeed, take a room $R$ off the chosen cycle. If the path of arrows from $R$ reaches the chosen cycle, it then stays on that cycle forever (before and after the reversal), so it never returns to $R$. If it does not reach the chosen cycle, it uses only unchanged arrows, so it is the same path for $\sigma$ and for $\sigma'$. Hence $R_1$ is again the first room on a cycle, and its cycle for $\sigma'$ is $R_1\to R_k\to\dots\to R_2\to R_1$. Applying the rule to $\sigma'$ moves the occupant of $R_{i+1}$ back to $R_i$ for every $i$, which gives $\sigma$ again.

So the rule pairs every good accommodation with a different one, and the number of good accommodations is even.

Show the solution – Tangency points and angle bookkeeping

Lemma 1. Two spheres lying on opposite sides of a plane $\Pi$ and tangent to $\Pi$ at $T_1$ and $T_2$ touch each other if and only if $T_1=T_2$.

Proof. Let $O_1,O_2$ be the centres and $r_1,r_2$ the radii; the segment $O_1O_2$ crosses $\Pi$ at a point $Z$. Then $O_1Z\ge O_1T_1=r_1$ and $O_2Z\ge r_2$, with equality in both only if $Z=T_1=T_2$. So $O_1O_2=r_1+r_2$ (the spheres touch) exactly when $T_1=T_2$.

Lemma 2. Let a sphere touch the three faces of a trihedral angle with vertex $V$ and edges $VX$, $VY$, $VZ$ at interior points; let $P$ be the point of tangency with the face $XVY$. Then \[\angle XVP=\tfrac12\bigl(\angle XVY+\angle XVZ-\angle YVZ\bigr).\] Proof. Let $P,Q,R$ be the points of tangency with the faces $XVY$, $XVZ$, $YVZ$. Tangent segments from one point to a sphere are equal, so $VP=VQ=VR$ and $XP=XQ$; the triangles $VXP$ and $VXQ$ are congruent (SSS), so $\angle XVP=\angle XVQ=:x$. Similarly define $y$ (at $VY$) and $z$ (at $VZ$). Then $x+y=\angle XVY$, $x+z=\angle XVZ$, $y+z=\angle YVZ$, and solving gives the formula.

The insphere of a tetrahedron touches all four faces at interior points, so Lemma 2 applies at each vertex. Below, $K$ is the intersection of the diagonals; $\angle AKB=\angle BKC=\angle CKD=\angle DKA=90^\circ$.

Step 1: what the first tangency gives. Let $\omega_1,\omega_2$ be the inspheres of $SABC$ and $SACD$, tangent to the plane $SAC$ at $T_1$, $T_2$. By (4) these two differences are equal.

Introduce coordinates in the base plane: $K=(0,0)$, $A=(-a,0)$, $C=(c,0)$, $B=(0,b)$, $D=(0,-d)$, $U_1=(x,y)$ with $0<y<b$, hence $U_2=(x,-y)$ with $y<d$. The ray $BU_1$ deviates from the ray $BD$ towards $C$ by the angle $\varepsilon_B$ with $\tan\varepsilon_B=\frac{x}{b-y}$, so $\angle ABU_1-\angle ABD=\varepsilon_B$; likewise $\angle ADU_2-\angle ADB=\varepsilon_D$ with $\tan\varepsilon_D=\frac{x}{d-y}$ (all these angles lie in $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$). By Lemma 2 at $B$, \[\angle DBT_3=\tfrac12(\angle DBS+\angle DBA-\angle SBA),\qquad \angle DBT_4=\tfrac12(\angle DBS+\angle DBC-\angle SBC),\] which are equal by (5). Rewrite both hypotheses using the differences $a-c$ and $b-d$. Consider separately whether $a$ lies above or below $c$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use that $\arctan$ is increasing and that its change between two points is no greater in magnitude than their distance. The mean value theorem gives this estimate directly from its derivative. (LPS VII, Chapter 38: Inequalities: Convexity and Functional Methods)
Hint 3: approach
In the case $a\le c$, use the second hypothesis to order $b,d$ and bound their arctangent difference in the first hypothesis. In the case $a>c$, bound the arctangent difference involving $a,c$ directly in the second hypothesis, keeping track of the inequality directions.
Common trap
The difference estimate must be applied with the correct ordering before its sign is changed.
Show solution 1 – Two cases and the Lipschitz property of arctan

We use two properties of $\arctan$: it is increasing, and $|\arctan x-\arctan y|\le|x-y|$ for all real $x,y$ (by the mean value theorem, since its derivative $\frac1{1+t^2}$ is at most $1$).

Case $a\le c$. The second inequality gives $b-d>\arctan c-\arctan a\ge0$, so $b>d$, and then $\arctan b-\arctan d\le b-d$, i.e. $\arctan d-\arctan b\ge d-b$. The first inequality gives \[a-c>\arctan d-\arctan b\ge d-b,\qquad\text{so}\qquad a+b>c+d .\] Case $a>c$. Then $\arctan a-\arctan c\le a-c$, i.e. $\arctan c-\arctan a\ge c-a$, and the second inequality gives \[b-d>\arctan c-\arctan a\ge c-a,\qquad\text{so}\qquad a+b>c+d .\] In both cases $a+b>c+d$.

Show solution 2 – A positive combination of the two inequalities

Put $u=a-c$ and $v=b-d$. Define $\lambda=\frac{\arctan b-\arctan d}{b-d}$ if $b\ne d$ and $\lambda=1$ if $b=d$; similarly $\mu=\frac{\arctan a-\arctan c}{a-c}$ (or $\mu=1$ if $a=c$). Since $\arctan$ is increasing and $|\arctan x-\arctan y|\le|x-y|$ (its derivative $\frac1{1+t^2}$ lies in $(0,1]$), we have $0<\lambda,\mu\le1$. The hypotheses read \[u+\lambda v>0,\qquad v+\mu u>0 .\] If $\lambda=1$, the first inequality is $u+v>0$, and if $\mu=1$, the second one is. Otherwise $1-\mu>0$ and $1-\lambda>0$, and \[(1-\mu)(u+\lambda v)+(1-\lambda)(v+\mu u)=(1-\lambda\mu)(u+v)\] (the coefficient of $u$ is $1-\mu+\mu-\lambda\mu$ and that of $v$ is $\lambda-\lambda\mu+1-\lambda$). The left side is positive and $1-\lambda\mu>0$, so $u+v>0$, i.e. $a+b>c+d$.

Show solution 3 – Monotonicity along a fixed sum

For a real number $\sigma$ let $F_\sigma(x)=x+\arctan(\sigma-x)$. We use two properties.

  • (i) $F_\sigma$ is strictly increasing. Its derivative is $1-\frac1{1+(\sigma-x)^2}=\frac{(\sigma-x)^2}{1+(\sigma-x)^2}\ge0$, with equality only at $x=\sigma$. So for $x<y$ the mean value theorem gives $F_\sigma(y)\ge F_\sigma(x)$, and equality would make $F_\sigma$ constant on $[x,y]$, with derivative $0$ on the whole interval, which is false.

  • (ii) For fixed $x$, $F_\sigma(x)$ increases with $\sigma$, because $\arctan$ is increasing.

Let $\sigma=a+b$ and $\tau=c+d$, and suppose that $\sigma\le\tau$. Since $b=\sigma-a$ and $d=\tau-c$, the first hypothesis reads $F_\sigma(a)>F_\tau(c)$. By (ii), $F_\tau(a)\ge F_\sigma(a)>F_\tau(c)$, and by (i), $a>c$. Since $a=\sigma-b$ and $c=\tau-d$, the second hypothesis reads $F_\sigma(b)>F_\tau(d)$, and in the same way $b>d$. Adding, $a+b>c+d$, i.e. $\sigma>\tau$, a contradiction. Hence $a+b>c+d$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Spiral Similarity Centers

In an acute triangle $ABC$ the altitudes $AD$, $BE$ and $CF$ meet at the point $H$. Three circles with centres $D$, $E$, $F$ passing through $H$ meet the lines $BC$, $CA$ and $AB$ respectively at six distinct points. Prove that these six points can be divided into two triples so that for the two triangles formed by these triples there is a circle tangent to all six lines containing the sides of these triangles.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Label the two intersection points on each side consistently according to a clockwise or counterclockwise displacement from the altitude foot. Look at the triangle formed by the three altitude feet and the position of $H$ relative to its sides.
Hint 2: key idea
Express each displacement from an altitude foot as a quarter-turn of the vector from $H$ to that foot. Adding that rotated vector to the original one produces a spiral similarity with centre $H$, and cyclic quadrilaterals help locate $H$ relative to the orthic triangle's angle bisectors. (LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Prove that $H$ is equidistant from the orthic triangle's side lines. Group the intersection points by a common rotation direction, identify the two similarities taking the orthic triangle to these groups, and compare their effects on distances from $H$ to side lines.
Common trap
Tangency is required to the complete side lines, so a contact point need not lie on a side segment.
Show the solution – A spiral similarity of the orthic triangle

$H$ is equidistant from the sides of the orthic triangle $DEF$. The points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, so $\angle EFC=\angle EBC=90^\circ-\angle C$. The points $B,D,H,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BH$, so $\angle HFD=\angle HBD=\angle EBC=90^\circ-\angle C$. Hence the line $FC$ (which contains $H$) bisects the angle $DFE$. In the same way $DA$ bisects the angle $EDF$ and $EB$ bisects $DEF$. So $H$ lies on bisectors of all three angles of $DEF$ and is at the same distance $r>0$ from the lines $EF$, $FD$, $DE$.

The six points. For $s=\pm1$ let $R_s$ be the rotation of vectors by $s\cdot90^\circ$. The vector $\overrightarrow{HD}$ is perpendicular to $BC$, so $R_s\overrightarrow{HD}$ is parallel to $BC$ and has length $HD$; therefore the two points where the circle with centre $D$ through $H$ meets $BC$ are \[D_s=D+R_s\overrightarrow{HD},\qquad s=\pm1 .\] Similarly the six given points are $D_{\pm1}$, $E_{\pm1}$, $F_{\pm1}$ with $E_s=E+R_s\overrightarrow{HE}$ and $F_s=F+R_s\overrightarrow{HF}$. We split them into the triples $\{D_1,E_1,F_1\}$ and $\{D_{-1},E_{-1},F_{-1}\}$.

A spiral similarity. For any vector $w$, the vector $w+R_sw$ is $w$ rotated by $s\cdot45^\circ$ and multiplied by $\sqrt2$. Hence \[\overrightarrow{HD_s}=\overrightarrow{HD}+R_s\overrightarrow{HD}\] is obtained from $\overrightarrow{HD}$ by this rotation and scaling, and the same holds for $E_s$, $F_s$. So the spiral similarity $\Phi_s$ with centre $H$, angle $s\cdot45^\circ$ and ratio $\sqrt2$ maps $D,E,F$ to $D_s,E_s,F_s$, and maps the lines $DE$, $EF$, $FD$ onto the lines $D_sE_s$, $E_sF_s$, $F_sD_s$. In particular $D_sE_sF_s$ is a genuine triangle.

The common circle. $\Phi_s$ fixes $H$ and multiplies all distances by $\sqrt2$, so the distance from $H$ to each line $D_sE_s$, $E_sF_s$, $F_sD_s$ equals $\sqrt2\,r$. This holds for $s=1$ and for $s=-1$. Therefore the circle with centre $H$ and radius $\sqrt2\,r$ is tangent to all six lines containing the sides of the triangles $D_1E_1F_1$ and $D_{-1}E_{-1}F_{-1}$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Basic Graph Properties

A test was held in a class of at least $5$ students, girls and boys. It is known that during the test every two students communicated, and in each pair exactly one of the two students helped the other. It turned out that for every student, among the students he or she helped there are equally many girls and boys. How many students can there be in the class?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Represent each communication by an arrow pointing from the helper to the recipient, and distinguish the two groups of students. Count arrows within each group and between the groups.
Hint 2: key idea
Sum the equal-help condition separately over girls and boys to obtain a numerical constraint on the group sizes. For sufficiency, use near-regular tournaments within each group and distribute the cross-group arrows using a cyclic list. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Simplify the constraint from the three pair counts and parametrize the candidate group sizes. Give the girls near-equal internal scores, assign their cross-group recipients consecutively around the boys' cyclic list, and verify that the resulting required boys' scores can be realised by a near-regular tournament.
Common trap
A necessary numerical constraint alone does not prove that a class with those parameters can be constructed.
Show the answer

any perfect square $N\ge 9$ (that is, $N=m^2$, $m\ge3$)

Show solution 1 – Counting helps, and a construction from near-regular tournaments

Let there be $g$ girls and $b$ boys, $N=g+b\ge5$. The answer: $N$ can be any perfect square $m^2$ with $m\ge3$.

$N$ must be a square. Every girl helped equally many girls and boys, so the total number of helps given by girls to boys equals the total number given by girls to girls. Every pair of girls produced exactly one help, so the latter number is $\binom g2$. Hence girls helped boys $\binom g2$ times, and similarly boys helped girls $\binom b2$ times. Every girl–boy pair produced exactly one help, so \[\binom g2+\binom b2=gb\iff g^2-g+b^2-b=2gb\iff (g-b)^2=g+b=N .\] So $N$ is a perfect square, and $N\ge5$ gives $N\ge9$.

Every $N=m^2$, $m\ge3$, occurs. Take $g=\frac{m(m+1)}2$ girls and $b=\frac{m(m-1)}2$ boys, so $g+b=m^2$ and $(g-b)^2=g+b$.

Near-regular tournaments. Place $s$ players on a circle. If $s$ is odd, let each player beat the next $\frac{s-1}2$ players clockwise; all scores are $\frac{s-1}2$. If $s$ is even, let each player beat the next $\frac s2-1$ players, and in each of the $\frac s2$ diametrically opposite pairs let the player in the first $\frac s2$ positions win. Placing any prescribed $\frac s2$ players in the first $\frac s2$ positions, they score $\frac s2$ and the others $\frac s2-1$. In all cases the scores are $\lfloor\frac{s-1}2\rfloor$ or $\lceil\frac{s-1}2\rceil$, and every such score assignment with total $\binom s2$ is realised (for even $s$ the total forces exactly $\frac s2$ high scores).

Construction. (1) Among the girls, let the helps form a near-regular tournament; girl $v$ helps $h_v\in\{\lfloor\frac{g-1}2\rfloor,\lceil\frac{g-1}2\rceil\}$ girls, and $\sum h_v=\binom g2$.

(2) Write the boys in a cyclic list. Go through the girls one by one: each girl $v$ helps the next $h_v$ boys of the list, continuing cyclically from where the previous girl stopped. Since $h_v\le\frac g2\le b$ (this is $m+1\le2(m-1)$, true for $m\ge3$), girl $v$ helps $h_v$ different boys. In every other girl–boy pair the boy helps the girl. The $\binom g2$ chosen positions are spread cyclically over the $b$ boys, so the numbers $r_w$ of girls helping the boys $w$ differ by at most $1$. Boy $w$ helps $e_w=g-r_w$ girls; these numbers differ by at most $1$ and \[\sum_w e_w=gb-\binom g2=\binom b2\] by the identity above, so their average is $\frac{b-1}2$ and each $e_w$ is $\lfloor\frac{b-1}2\rfloor$ or $\lceil\frac{b-1}2\rceil$.

(3) Among the boys, let the helps form a near-regular tournament in which boy $w$ scores exactly $e_w$.

Now every girl $v$ helps $h_v$ girls and $h_v$ boys, and every boy $w$ helps $e_w$ boys and $e_w$ girls, as required. For example, $m=3$ gives a class of $9$: $6$ girls and $3$ boys.

Show solution 2 – A tournament with prescribed scores, then reversing the mixed pairs

Let there be $g$ girls and $b$ boys, $N=g+b\ge5$, and draw an arrow from each student to every student he or she helped. The answer: $N$ can be any perfect square $m^2$ with $m\ge3$.

$N$ must be a square. The girls helped girls exactly $\binom g2$ times (once in each pair of girls). Each girl helped equally many girls and boys, so the girls helped boys $\binom g2$ times as well; similarly the boys helped girls $\binom b2$ times. Each girl–boy pair gives exactly one help, so \[gb=\binom g2+\binom b2\iff 2gb=g^2-g+b^2-b\iff (g-b)^2=g+b=N .\] So $N=m^2$ with $m=|g-b|$, and $N\ge5$ gives $m\ge3$.

A lemma on tournaments. Let $d_1,\dots,d_N$ be non-negative integers with $\sum_i d_i=\binom N2$ and $\sum_{i\in X}d_i\ge\binom{|X|}2$ for every set $X$ of players. Then there is a tournament (one arrow between every two players) in which player $i$ has exactly $d_i$ outgoing arrows.

Start from any tournament, let $e_i$ be the out-degrees, and let $\Delta=\sum_i|e_i-d_i|$. If $\Delta>0$, then, as $\sum e_i=\sum d_i$, some player $s$ has $e_s>d_s$. Let $X$ be the set of players reachable from $s$ along arrows ($s$ included). If $X$ contains a player $t$ with $e_t<d_t$, take a path of distinct players from $s$ to $t$ and reverse all its arrows: every inner player of the path loses one outgoing arrow and gains one, $s$ loses one and $t$ gains one, so $\Delta$ drops by $2$. Otherwise $e_i\ge d_i$ for all $i\in X$, with $e_s>d_s$. No arrow leaves $X$ (its end would be reachable from $s$), so all outgoing arrows of players of $X$ stay inside $X$, and $\sum_{i\in X}e_i=\binom{|X|}2$. Then $\binom{|X|}2=\sum_{X}e_i>\sum_{X}d_i\ge\binom{|X|}2$, a contradiction. So we can always decrease $\Delta$, and after finitely many reversals $\Delta=0$.

Every $N=m^2$, $m\ge3$, occurs. Take $g=\frac{m(m+1)}2$ girls and $b=\frac{m(m-1)}2$ boys; then $g+b=m^2=N$, $g-b=m$, and $4gb=(g+b)^2-(g-b)^2=N^2-N$, i.e. $2gb=\binom N2$. Apply the lemma with $d_i=b$ for every girl and $d_i=g$ for every boy; the sum is $2gb=\binom N2$. Let $X$ contain $x$ girls and $y$ boys, $k=x+y$. Since $b<g$, for fixed $k$ the sum $xb+yg$ is smallest when $x$ is as large as possible.

  • If $k\le g$: $xb+yg\ge kb\ge\binom k2$, because $k-1\le g-1\le2b$; indeed $2b+1-g=\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}2\ge0$.

  • If $k>g$: let $\ell=N-k\le b-1$. Then $xb+yg\ge gb+(k-g)g=gb+(b-\ell)g=\binom N2-\ell g$, and \[\binom N2-\ell g-\binom{N-\ell}2=\frac{\ell(2N-\ell-1)}2-\ell g=\frac{\ell(2b-\ell-1)}2\ge0 .\]

So there is a tournament on the class in which every girl has $b$ outgoing arrows and every boy has $g$.

Now reverse every arrow between a girl and a boy, and let the final arrows say who helped whom. A girl who had $x$ arrows to girls and $y$ to boys ($x+y=b$) now helps $x$ girls and $b-y=x$ boys. A boy who had $x$ arrows to girls and $y$ to boys ($x+y=g$) now helps $g-x=y$ girls and $y$ boys. So every student helped equally many girls and boys, and a class of $N=m^2$ students exists for every $m\ge3$.

Problem 5

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Counting Integer Configurations

Call a natural number $N>1$ good if there are natural numbers $a_1,\dots,a_N$ such that the greatest common divisors of all the pairs of them are $N(N-1)/2$ consecutive natural numbers (in some order). Is there a good natural number greater than $10^{100}$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a fixed divisor, count how many pairwise gcds are divisible by it in two ways. One count comes from the original numbers and the other from the interval of consecutive gcds.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the divisor $4$: if $n$ original numbers are divisible by it, the number of corresponding gcds is $\binom n2$, whereas multiples of $4$ occupy approximately one quarter of a consecutive interval. Keep the error strictly below one before clearing denominators. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Translate the two counts into a small-error relation involving $n(n-1)$ and $N(N-1)$. Handle $n=0$ separately, complete squares in the remaining case, and factor their difference, using parity to bound the nonzero factor before comparing with the error estimate.
Common trap
The number of multiples in an interval depends on its starting point, so use a strict error bound rather than exact division.
Show the answer

No

Show solution 1 – Counting the gcds divisible by 4

No; in fact no good number exceeds $16$. Let $N$ be good, $a_1,\dots,a_N$ as in the definition, and $M=\frac{N(N-1)}2$.

Let $n$ be the number of indices $i$ with $4\mid a_i$. The gcd of $a_i$ and $a_j$ is divisible by $4$ exactly when both $a_i$ and $a_j$ are, so exactly $\binom n2$ of the $M$ gcds are multiples of $4$. On the other hand the gcds are $M$ consecutive natural numbers, and among $M$ consecutive integers the number of multiples of $4$ is $\lfloor\frac M4\rfloor$ or $\lceil\frac M4\rceil$, in particular it differs from $\frac M4$ by less than $1$. Hence \[\Bigl|\frac{n(n-1)}2-\frac{N(N-1)}8\Bigr|<1\iff \bigl|4n(n-1)-N(N-1)\bigr|<8 .\] If $n=0$ this gives $N(N-1)<8$, so $N\le3$. Let $n\ge1$. Since $4n(n-1)=(2n-1)^2-1$ and $N(N-1)=\frac{(2N-1)^2-1}{4}$, multiplying by $4$: \[\bigl|(4n-2)^2-(2N-1)^2-3\bigr|<32 .\] Here $4n-2>0$ is even and $2N-1$ is odd, so \[(4n-2)^2-(2N-1)^2=\bigl((4n-2)-(2N-1)\bigr)\bigl(4n-2+2N-1\bigr)\] is a product of a nonzero (odd) integer and the positive integer $4n+2N-3\ge 2N+1$; its absolute value is at least $2N+1$. Therefore \[32>\bigl|(4n-2)^2-(2N-1)^2-3\bigr|\ge 2N+1-3=2N-2,\] so $N\le16$. In particular there is no good number greater than $10^{100}$.

Show solution 2 – Multiples of a divisor close to M/5

No; in fact the only good numbers are $2$ and $3$. Let $N$ be good, let $a_1,\dots,a_N$ be as in the definition, and let $M=\frac{N(N-1)}2$.

The counting principle. Fix a natural number $d$ and let $r$ be the number of indices $i$ with $d\mid a_i$. The gcd of $a_i$ and $a_j$ is divisible by $d$ exactly when $d\mid a_i$ and $d\mid a_j$, so exactly $\binom r2$ of the $M$ gcds are multiples of $d$. Hence the number of multiples of $d$ among $M$ consecutive natural numbers must be a number of the form $\binom r2$, that is, one of $0,1,3,6,10,\dots$.

Among $M$ consecutive integers there are $\lfloor\frac Md\rfloor$ or $\lceil\frac Md\rceil$ multiples of $d$: writing $M=Qd+\rho$ with $0\le\rho<d$, the first $Qd$ of them contain exactly $Q$ multiples, and the remaining $\rho<d$ contain at most one.

$N\ge7$ is impossible. Then $M\ge21$. Take $d=\lceil\frac M5\rceil$. Then $\frac M5\le d<\frac M5+1\le\frac M4$ (the last inequality because $M\ge20$), so $4\le\frac Md\le5$, and the number of multiples of $d$ among the gcds is $4$ or $5$. Neither is of the form $\binom r2$, a contradiction.

$N=4,5,6$ are impossible. For $N=4$, $M=6$ and $d=3$: exactly $2$ multiples. For $N=5$, $M=10$ and $d=2$: exactly $5$ multiples. For $N=6$, $M=15$ and $d=3$: exactly $5$ multiples. None of $2,5$ is of the form $\binom r2$.

$N=2,3$ are good: the numbers $1,2$ have the single gcd $1$, and the numbers $2,3,6$ have the gcds $1,2,3$.

So the good numbers are exactly $2$ and $3$; in particular there is no good number greater than $10^{100}$.
