Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition

Let $f(x)=(x^2+4x+3)(x^2-4x+3)$. Over all possible values of $x$, what is the minimum possible value of $f(x)$?

(A) $-16$ (B) $-15$ (C) $-9$ (D) $0$ (E) $9$

Show the answer

(A) $-16$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Regroup the factors)

Factor each quadratic: \[x^2+4x+3=(x+1)(x+3),\qquad x^2-4x+3=(x-1)(x-3).\] Now multiply the four factors in a different order: \[f(x)=(x+1)(x-1)\cdot(x+3)(x-3)=(x^2-1)(x^2-9).\] Write $t=x^2$. Then \[f(x)=(t-1)(t-9)=t^2-10t+9=(t-5)^2-16.\] A square is never negative, so $f(x)\ge-16$, and $f(x)=-16$ exactly when $t=5$, that is $x=\pm\sqrt5$. The minimum value is $\boxed{\textbf{(A)}\ -16}$.

Solution 2 (Fixed sum, largest product)

As in Solution 1, $f(x)=(x^2-1)(x^2-9)$.

If $x^2\le1$ or $x^2\ge9$, both factors have the same sign (or one is $0$), so $f(x)\ge0$.

If $1<x^2<9$, then $x^2-1$ and $9-x^2$ are two positive numbers with sum $8$, and $f(x)=-(x^2-1)(9-x^2)$. Two positive numbers with sum $8$ can be written as $4+d$ and $4-d$, and their product is $(4+d)(4-d)=16-d^2\le16$. Hence $f(x)\ge-16$, with equality when $d=0$, that is $x^2-1=9-x^2=4$, or $x^2=5$. The minimum value is $\boxed{\textbf{(A)}\ -16}$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations

Serafine and Jamie are playing catch with special rules. They start by standing $1500$ cm apart. One throws the ball to the other. If the ball is caught, both players each take a $60$ cm step away from each other. If the ball is not caught, the person who missed the catch takes a $20$ cm step towards the other player. When they stop playing catch, they are $1720$ cm apart. Of the following, which number of throws is not possible?

(A) $17$ (B) $21$ (C) $73$ (D) $45$ (E) $10$

Show the answer

(B) $21$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count catches and misses)

Let $c$ be the number of caught throws and $m$ the number of missed throws, so there are $c+m$ throws. A catch increases the distance by $60+60=120$ cm and a miss decreases it by $20$ cm. Hence \[1500+120c-20m=1720,\qquad 120c-20m=220,\qquad m=6c-11.\] The number of throws is $c+m=7c-11$. Since $m\ge0$ we need $c\ge2$, so the possible numbers of throws are \[3,\ 10,\ 17,\ 24,\ 31,\ 38,\ 45,\ \dots,\ 73,\ \dots\] that is, the numbers $T\ge3$ for which $T+11$ is a multiple of $7$. (Such a game really happens: make all the catches first, so the players never get close to each other.)

Checking the options: $17+11=28$, $73+11=84$, $45+11=56$ and $10+11=21$ are multiples of $7$, but $21+11=32$ is not. The impossible number is $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 21}$.

Solution 2 (Start from all misses)

Suppose there are $T$ throws. If every throw were missed, the final distance would be $1500-20T$. Changing one miss into a catch changes $-20$ into $+120$, which adds $140$ cm. If $c$ throws are caught, then \[1500-20T+140c=1720,\qquad 140c=220+20T,\qquad 7c=T+11.\] So $T+11$ must be a multiple of $7$ (and then $c=(T+11)/7$ is at most $T$ whenever $T\ge2$, so such a game exists). The remainders of $17,21,73,45,10$ when divided by $7$ are $3,0,3,3,3$; we need remainder $3$ (because $11$ has remainder $4$). Only $21$ fails. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 21}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 35: Factorials and Prime Counting

The notation $n!$ (read “$n$ factorial”) is defined as the product of the first $n$ positive integers. (For example, $3!=1\cdot2\cdot3=6$.) Define the superfactorial of a positive integer, denoted by $n^{!}$, to be the product of the factorials of the first $n$ integers. (For example, $3^{!}=1!\cdot2!\cdot3!=12$.) How many factors of $7$ appear in the prime factorization of $51^{!}$, the superfactorial of $51$?

(A) $147$ (B) $150$ (C) $156$ (D) $168$ (E) $171$

Show the answer

(E) $171$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factorial by factorial)

The number of factors $7$ in $k!$ equals the number of multiples of $7$ up to $k$, plus one more for each multiple of $49$ up to $k$ (because $49=7^2$ gives two factors of $7$).

  • For $k=1,\dots,6$ there are $0$ factors; for $k=7,\dots,13$ there is $1$; for $k=14,\dots,20$ there are $2$; and so on, up to $6$ factors for $k=42,\dots,48$. Each of these blocks has $7$ values of $k$.

  • For $k=49,50,51$ there are $7$ multiples of $7$, one of which is $49$, so $7+1=8$ factors.

Adding, \[7(1+2+3+4+5+6)+3\cdot8=147+24=171.\] The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 171}$.

Solution 2 (Follow each multiple of 7)

In $51^{!}=1!\cdot2!\cdots51!$, the number $j$ appears as a factor in each of $j!,(j+1)!,\dots,51!$, that is, $52-j$ times. Only multiples of $7$ contribute factors of $7$:

  • $7,14,21,28,35,42$ each contain one $7$ and appear $45,38,31,24,17,10$ times;

  • $49$ contains two $7$'s and appears $3$ times.

The total is $45+38+31+24+17+10+2\cdot3=171$. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 171}$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 48: Transformations and Symmetry

What is the minimum value of \[f(x)=\sqrt{x^2-4x+5}+\sqrt{x^2-10x+34}\] over all real numbers $x$?

Show the answer

$5$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two distances and a reflection)

Complete the squares: \[x^2-4x+5=(x-2)^2+1^2,\qquad x^2-10x+34=(x-5)^2+3^2.\] So for the point $X=(x,0)$ on the $x$-axis and the points $P=(2,1)$, $Q=(5,3)$, the Pythagorean theorem gives \[f(x)=XP+XQ.\] Reflect $P$ in the $x$-axis to $P'=(2,-1)$. Every point $X$ of the axis is equally far from $P$ and $P'$, so \[f(x)=XP'+XQ\ \ge\ P'Q=\sqrt{(5-2)^2+(3-(-1))^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5,\] because the straight segment $P'Q$ is the shortest path from $P'$ to $Q$. Equality holds when $X$ lies on segment $P'Q$: this segment rises $4$ units over $3$ units, so it crosses the axis $\tfrac34$ to the right of $x=2$, at $x=\tfrac{11}{4}$. The minimum value is $\boxed{5}$.

Solution 2 (Algebra only)

Put $u=x-2$ and $v=3-u=5-x$. Then \[f=\sqrt{u^2+1}+\sqrt{v^2+9},\qquad u+v=3.\] Expanding both sides shows the identity \[(u^2+1)(v^2+9)=(uv+3)^2+(3u-v)^2,\] so $\sqrt{(u^2+1)(v^2+9)}\ge uv+3$. Therefore \[f^2=u^2+1+v^2+9+2\sqrt{(u^2+1)(v^2+9)}\ \ge\ u^2+v^2+10+2uv+6=(u+v)^2+16=9+16=25,\] so $f\ge5$. Equality needs $3u=v=3-u$, that is $u=\tfrac34$ (then $uv+3>0$ too), i.e. $x=\tfrac{11}{4}$, and indeed $f\left(\tfrac{11}4\right)=\tfrac54+\tfrac{15}4=5$. The minimum value is $\boxed{5}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns

Let $f(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ be the quadratic function for which $f(1)=19$, $f(2)=37$, and $f(3)=61$.

For each positive integer $n$, define $S(n)=f(1)+f(2)+f(3)+\dots+f(n)$. For example, $S(3)=f(1)+f(2)+f(3)=19+37+61=117$.

What is the smallest integer $n$ for which $S(n)>1{,}000{,}000$?

(A) $98$ (B) $99$ (C) $100$ (D) $101$ (E) $102$

Show the answer

(B) $99$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A telescoping sum of cubes)

From $a+b+c=19$, $4a+2b+c=37$, $9a+3b+c=61$, subtracting consecutive equations gives $3a+b=18$ and $5a+b=24$, so $a=3$, $b=9$, $c=7$: \[f(x)=3x^2+9x+7.\] Now notice that \[(x+2)^3-(x+1)^3=3(x+1)^2+3(x+1)+1=3x^2+9x+7=f(x).\] Hence \[S(n)=(3^3-2^3)+(4^3-3^3)+\dots+\big((n+2)^3-(n+1)^3\big)=(n+2)^3-8.\] We need $(n+2)^3>1{,}000{,}008$. Since $100^3=1{,}000{,}000$ is too small and $101^3=1{,}030{,}301$ is large enough, the smallest choice is $n+2=101$, so $n=\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 99}$.

Solution 2 (Differences and the sum formulas)

The values $19,37,61$ have differences $18$ and $24$; for a quadratic $ax^2+bx+c$ these differences grow by $2a$ each time, so $2a=6$, $a=3$. Then $b=18-3a=9$ and $c=19-a-b=7$.

Using $1^2+\dots+n^2=\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$ and $1+\dots+n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}2$, \[S(n)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{2}+\frac{9n(n+1)}{2}+7n.\] For $n=98$: $\dfrac{98\cdot99\cdot197}{2}+\dfrac{9\cdot98\cdot99}{2}+686=955{,}647+43{,}659+686=999{,}992$, which is not more than $1{,}000{,}000$. Adding $f(99)=3\cdot9801+891+7=30{,}301$ gives $S(99)=1{,}030{,}293>1{,}000{,}000$. Since every $f(k)$ is positive, $S$ increases, so the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 99}$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction

How many ordered pairs $(a,b)$ of positive integers are there which satisfy \[a\times b+57=29\times\gcd(a,b)+2\times\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)?\] (Note: $\gcd(a,b)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$; $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$ denotes the least common multiple of $a$ and $b$.)

Show the answer

$8$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Use gcd times lcm)

Let $g=\gcd(a,b)$ and $\ell=\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$. A basic fact is $ab=g\ell$. The equation becomes \[g\ell+57=29g+2\ell\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g\ell-29g-2\ell+58=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(g-2)(\ell-29)=1.\] Two integers with product $1$ are both $1$ or both $-1$, so $(g,\ell)=(3,30)$ or $(g,\ell)=(1,28)$.

  • $g=3$, $\ell=30$: write $a=3x$, $b=3y$ with $x,y$ having no common factor; then $\ell=3xy=30$, so $xy=10$ with $x,y$ coprime: $(x,y)=(1,10),(2,5),(5,2),(10,1)$. This gives $(3,30),(6,15),(15,6),(30,3)$.

  • $g=1$, $\ell=28$: $ab=28$ with $a,b$ coprime: $(1,28),(4,7),(7,4),(28,1)$.

There are $\boxed{8}$ ordered pairs.

Solution 2 (Write a = dx, b = dy)

Let $d=\gcd(a,b)$, $a=dx$, $b=dy$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$; then $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)=dxy$ and the equation reads \[d^2xy+57=29d+2dxy\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d(d-2)\,xy=29d-57.\]

  • $d=1$: $-xy=-28$, so $xy=28$.

  • $d=2$: $0=1$, impossible.

  • $d\ge3$: $d$ divides the left side and $29d$, so $d$ divides $57=3\cdot19$; thus $d=3,19$ or $57$. Also $xy\ge1$ gives $d(d-2)\le29d-57$, which fails for $d=57$. For $d=19$: $323\,xy=494$, not an integer. For $d=3$: $3xy=30$, so $xy=10$.

Coprime ordered pairs with $xy=28$: $(1,28),(4,7),(7,4),(28,1)$; with $xy=10$: $(1,10),(2,5),(5,2),(10,1)$. That is $4+4=\boxed{8}$ ordered pairs $(a,b)$.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry

Five distinct points are located on a line. There are ten distances between pairs of points. Nine of the ten distances are $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$, $7$, $9$, $11$, $13$, $16$. The remaining distance is $n$, and it is possible that $n$ is equal to one of the other nine distances. If $n$ is a positive integer from $1$ to $15$ inclusive, how many values of $n$ are possible?

(A) $1$ (B) $2$ (C) $3$ (D) $4$ (E) $5$

Show the answer

(C) $3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pairs that add up to 16)

Since $n\le15$, the largest distance $16$ is between the two outer points. Put them at $0$ and $16$ and the inner points at $0<p<q<r<16$. The ten distances are

  • six outer distances $p,\,16-p,\ q,\,16-q,\ r,\,16-r$, which form three pairs with sum $16$;

  • three inner distances $q-p,\ r-q,\ r-p$, where the last one is the sum of the other two.

Besides $16$, the distances are $2,3,4,5,7,9,11,13$ and $n$. Among the listed numbers the only pairs with sum $16$ are $3+13$, $5+11$, $7+9$ ($2$ and $4$ would need $14$ and $12$).

Case 1: $n$ is an inner distance. Then the six outer distances are $3,13,5,11,7,9$, and the inner ones are $2,4,n$. One of them is the sum of the other two: $n=2+4=6$, or $4=2+2$ with $n=2$.

Case 2: $n$ is an outer distance. Its partner $16-n$ is a listed number ($n=8$ is impossible: its partner would be a second $8$). If $16-n$ is $3,5,7,9,11$ or $13$, the other two outer pairs are two of the pairs above, and the inner distances are $2$, $4$ and the listed partner of $16-n$, which is odd; but $\{2,4,\text{odd}\}$ never has one number equal to the sum of the other two. If $16-n=2$ ($n=14$), the inner distances are $4$ and one pair $\{3,13\}$, $\{5,11\}$ or $\{7,9\}$: impossible. If $16-n=4$ ($n=12$), the inner distances can be $2,7,9$ (as $2+7=9$).

So $n\in\{2,6,12\}$, and all three really occur (see the figure): $0,3,5,7,16$ gives the extra distance $2$; $\ 0,3,5,9,16$ gives $6$; $\ 0,3,5,12,16$ gives $12$. The number of possible values is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 3}$.

Solution 2 (Add up all ten distances)

Place the points at $0<p<q<r<16$ as in Solution 1. Adding the ten distances, \[p+q+r+16+(q-p)+(r-p)+(16-p)+(r-q)+(16-q)+(16-r)=64+2(r-p).\] The nine known distances add up to $70$, so $70+n=64+2(r-p)$, that is \[r-p=\frac{n+6}{2}.\] So $n$ is even, and $r-p$ (a distance less than $16$) is a listed number or equals $n$: $n=2,4,6,8,10,12,14$ give $r-p=4,5,6,7,8,9,10$. For $n=10$ and $n=14$ the values $8$ and $10$ are not available, so these fail.

For $n=4$ and $n=8$: the six distances from the inner points to the two ends form three pairs with sum $16$ ($p$ and $16-p$, and so on). With an extra $4$ or $8$, the only such pairs are still $3+13$, $5+11$, $7+9$, so the other three distances are $2,4,4$ or $2,4,8$. But the three distances between inner points are $q-p$, $r-q$ and their sum $r-p$, and neither triple has this form. So $n=4$ and $n=8$ fail.

The remaining values $2,6,12$ all occur: $0,3,5,7,16$; $\ 0,3,5,9,16$; $\ 0,3,5,12,16$. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 3}$.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry

As shown in the diagram below, $ABCD$ is a rhombus with $\angle D=52^\circ$. Point $M$ is the midpoint of $DC$ and point $E$ lies on segment $BM$ such that $AE\perp BM$. What is the measure, in degrees, of $\angle BEC$?

Show the answer

$116^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Extend BM)

Extend $BM$ beyond $M$ until it meets line $AD$ at $P$. In triangles $MDP$ and $MCB$: $MD=MC$, the angles at $M$ are vertical angles, and $\angle MDP=\angle MCB$ (alternate angles, as $AD\parallel BC$). So the triangles are congruent and $DP=CB=DA$. Thus $D$ is the midpoint of $AP$.

Triangle $AEP$ has a right angle at $E$, and the midpoint of the hypotenuse of a right triangle is equally far from all three vertices. Hence $DE=DA=DP$. Since also $DC=DA$, the points $A$, $E$, $C$ lie on the circle with centre $D$ and radius $DA$.

The point $E$ is inside the rhombus, so inside the angle $ADC$; hence it lies on the short arc $AC$, the one cut off by the central angle $\angle ADC=52^\circ$. So the inscribed angle $\angle AEC$ stands on the other arc, of $360^\circ-52^\circ=308^\circ$: \[\angle AEC=\tfrac12\cdot308^\circ=154^\circ.\] The three angles around $E$ add up to $360^\circ$, and $\angle AEB=90^\circ$, so \[\angle BEC=360^\circ-90^\circ-154^\circ=\boxed{116^\circ}.\]

Solution 2 (Midpoint of AB and isosceles triangles)

Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AB$. Since $DM$ and $NB$ are parallel and both equal half a side, $DMBN$ is a parallelogram, so $DN\parallel BM$. Let $DN$ meet $AE$ at $K$. In triangle $ABE$ the line $NK$ goes through the midpoint of $AB$ parallel to $BE$, so $K$ is the midpoint of $AE$; and $DK\perp AE$ because $AE\perp BM$. So $D$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AE$, and $DE=DA=DC$.

Triangles $DAE$ and $DCE$ are isosceles. Write $\angle DAE=\angle DEA=\alpha$ and $\angle DCE=\angle DEC=\gamma$. The angles of quadrilateral $ADCE$ add up to $360^\circ$: \[52^\circ+\alpha+\gamma+(\alpha+\gamma)=360^\circ,\] since $\angle AEC=\alpha+\gamma$. So $\angle AEC=\alpha+\gamma=154^\circ$, and as in Solution 1 $\angle BEC=360^\circ-90^\circ-154^\circ=\boxed{116^\circ}$.

Problem 9

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry

In an equiangular hexagon, all interior angles measure $120^\circ$. An example of such a hexagon with side lengths of $2$, $3$, $1$, $3$, $2$, and $2$ is shown below, inscribed in equilateral triangle $ABC$. Consider all equiangular hexagons with positive integer side lengths that can be inscribed in $\triangle ABC$, with all six vertices on the sides of the triangle. What is the total number of such hexagons? Hexagons that differ only by a rotation or a reflection are considered the same.

(A) $4$ (B) $5$ (C) $6$ (D) $7$ (E) $8$

Show the answer

(E) $8$

Show the solution

Solution (Corner triangles)

The example fixes the triangle: side $AB=1+3+2=6$.

A convex hexagon with all vertices on the sides of the triangle has exactly two vertices on each side (three vertices of a convex polygon cannot lie on one line), and these two are neighbours, so three sides of the hexagon lie along the sides of the triangle. The other three sides cut off three corner triangles. Each corner triangle has a $60^\circ$ angle at the corner and angles $180^\circ-120^\circ=60^\circ$ at the hexagon, so it is equilateral.

Let the corner triangles have sides $a$ (at $A$), $b$ (at $B$), $c$ (at $C$), positive integers. The hexagon has sides $a,b,c$ and $6-a-b$, $6-b-c$, $6-c-a$, and these must be at least $1$: \[a+b\le5,\qquad b+c\le5,\qquad c+a\le5.\] Conversely, every such triple gives a hexagon. Rotations and reflections of the triangle permute the corners in every possible way, so rearranging $a,b,c$ gives the same hexagon. Different triples (not rearrangements of each other) give different hexagons: a rotation or reflection of a hexagon sends every second side to every second side, so it either keeps the sides $a,b,c$ as a group or swaps them with the other three sides; those three add up to $18-2(a+b+c)$, and if this equals $a+b+c$ then $a+b+c=6$ and the other three sides are $c,a,b$ again. So we count triples $a\le b\le c$ with $b+c\le5$:

  • $c=1$: $(1,1,1)$;

  • $c=2$: $(1,1,2)$, $(1,2,2)$, $(2,2,2)$;

  • $c=3$ (so $b\le2$): $(1,1,3)$, $(1,2,3)$, $(2,2,3)$;

  • $c=4$ (so $b\le1$): $(1,1,4)$.

That is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 8}$ hexagons. (The example is $(1,2,2)$.)

Problem 10

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 11: Divisors and Arithmetic Functions of a Number

What is the fifth smallest positive integer $N$ such that $2026\times N$ has exactly $100$ positive integer divisors?

Show the answer

$11016$

Show the solution

Solution (Exponent casework)

Recall: if $m=p_1^{e_1}p_2^{e_2}\cdots$ then $m$ has $(e_1+1)(e_2+1)\cdots$ divisors.

Since $2026=2\cdot1013$ with $1013$ prime, write $2026N=2^{\alpha}\cdot1013^{\beta}\cdot M$, where $M$ is odd and not divisible by $1013$, and $\alpha,\beta\ge1$. Then \[(\alpha+1)(\beta+1)\,d(M)=100,\] where $d(M)$ is the number of divisors of $M$. We will find five values of $N$ below $11{,}100$, so we may assume $N<11{,}100$. Then $\beta\le2$ (as $\beta\ge3$ would need $1013^2\mid N$), and $\beta=2$ is impossible because $3\nmid100$. So $\beta=1$ and $(\alpha+1)\,d(M)=50$, with $N=2^{\alpha-1}M$. The divisors of $50$ that are at least $2$ give:

  • $\alpha=1$, $d(M)=25$: $M=p^{24}$ or $p^4q^4$, so $N=M\ge3^4\cdot5^4=50{,}625$. Too big.

  • $\alpha=4$, $d(M)=10$: $M=p^9$ or $p^4q$, and $N=8M$.

  • $\alpha=9$, $d(M)=5$: $M=p^4\ge81$, so $N=2^8M\ge20{,}736$. Too big.

  • $\alpha=24$ or $49$: $N\ge2^{23}$. Too big.

So we need the five smallest odd $M$ (without the factor $1013$) of the form $p^9$ or $p^4q$. Since $3^9$ and $5^4\cdot3=1875$ are large, they are $3^4\cdot5=405$, $3^4\cdot7=567$, $3^4\cdot11=891$, $3^4\cdot13=1053$ and $3^4\cdot17=1377$. The five smallest $N$ are \[3240,\ 4536,\ 7128,\ 8424,\ 11016,\] and the fifth is $\boxed{11016}$. Check: $2026\cdot11016=2^4\cdot3^4\cdot17\cdot1013$ has $5\cdot5\cdot2\cdot2=100$ divisors.

Problem 11

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry

As shown in the diagram below, $ABC$ is a right triangle. Points $D$ and $E$ lie on side $BC$ such that $\angle BAD=20^\circ$, $\angle DAE=15^\circ$ and $\angle EAC=20^\circ$. If $EC=4$ cm, what is the length, in cm, of $BD$?

Show the answer

$2$ cm

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reflect D and find isosceles triangles)

We have $\angle BAC=20^\circ+15^\circ+20^\circ=55^\circ$, so $\angle ACB=35^\circ$.

Step 1. $\angle DAC=15^\circ+20^\circ=35^\circ=\angle DCA$, so triangle $ADC$ is isosceles: $DA=DC$.

Step 2. Reflect $D$ in the line $AB$ to the point $D'$. Since $AB\perp BC$, the point $D'$ lies on line $CB$ beyond $B$, with $BD'=BD$, $AD'=AD$ and $\angle D'AB=20^\circ$. In triangle $AD'E$, \[\angle D'AE=20^\circ+20^\circ+15^\circ=55^\circ,\qquad \angle AED'=\angle AEB=90^\circ-35^\circ=55^\circ,\] so it is isosceles: $D'E=D'A=DA$.

Step 3. The points lie on one line in the order $D',B,D,E,C$. Hence \[EC=D'C-D'E=D'C-DA=D'C-DC=D'D=2\,BD.\] So $BD=\tfrac12EC=\boxed{2}$ cm.

Solution 2 (Tangents)

(For students who know the tangent ratio.) Take $AB=1$. In the right triangles with the right angle at $B$, \[BD=\tan20^\circ,\qquad BE=\tan35^\circ,\qquad BC=\tan55^\circ=\frac1{\tan35^\circ}.\] With $t=\tan35^\circ$, \[EC=\frac1t-t=\frac{1-t^2}{t}=\frac{2}{\tan70^\circ}=2\tan20^\circ=2\,BD,\] using the double-angle formula $\tan70^\circ=\dfrac{2t}{1-t^2}$ and $\tan70^\circ\cdot\tan20^\circ=1$. Since the ratio $EC:BD=2$ does not depend on the size of the triangle, $EC=4$ gives $BD=\boxed{2}$ cm.

Problem 12

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting

A $2\times2026$ rectangular board is tiled completely by using $2026$ identical dominoes, each of size $2\times1$. Let $a$ denote the number of such tilings in which no two vertical dominoes share a common side as shown in the diagram below. What is the remainder when $a$ is divided by $6$?

Show the answer

$4$

Show the solution

Solution (A recursion and its remainders)

Read a tiling from left to right. If the left column is covered by a vertical domino, call this block $V$ (width $1$). Otherwise the top-left square is covered by a horizontal domino, and then the bottom-left square must be too; call these two stacked horizontal dominoes a block $H$ (width $2$). So a tiling of a $2\times n$ board is a row of blocks $V$ and $H$ of total width $n$, and the condition says that no two $V$'s are next to each other.

Let $a_n$ be the number of such rows of width $n$ (with $a_0=1$ for the empty row); we want $a=a_{2026}$. Look at the last block: if it is $H$, the rest is any good row of width $n-2$; if it is $V$, the block before it is $H$ (or there is nothing), and the rest is any good row of width $n-3$. So \[a_n=a_{n-2}+a_{n-3},\qquad a_1=1,\ a_2=1,\ a_3=2,\] which gives $1,1,2,2,3,4,5,7,9,12,16,21,28,37,49,65,\dots$

Each term depends only on the three before it, so as soon as three consecutive remainders repeat, all remainders repeat.

Remainders mod $2$, for $n=1,2,\dots,10$: $1,1,0,0,1,0,1,\ 1,1,0$. They repeat with period $7$. Since $2026=7\cdot289+3$, $a_{2026}$ has the same remainder as $a_3=2$: it is even.

Remainders mod $3$, for $n=1,\dots,16$: $1,1,2,2,0,1,2,1,0,0,1,0,1,\ 1,1,2$. Period $13$. Since $2026=13\cdot155+11$, $a_{2026}\equiv a_{11}=16\equiv1\pmod 3$.

An even number that leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $3$ leaves remainder $4$ when divided by $6$. The answer is $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 13

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 39: Perfect Powers and Prime Exponents

It is known that there exists a positive integer $x$ such that each of the following numbers: \[x,\quad 2x,\quad 3x,\quad 4x\quad\text{and}\quad 5x\] is a perfect power. Provide one example of such an integer $x$ and explain how you obtained it.

(Note: $a^i$ is a perfect power where $a$ and $i$ are positive integers and $i>1$.)

Show the answer

for example $x=2^{140}\cdot3^{84}\cdot5^{90}$

Show the solution

Solution (Choose the exponents)

Look for $x=2^a3^b5^c$. A number is a perfect $m$-th power when every exponent in its prime factorisation is a multiple of $m$. Multiplying by $2,3,4,5$ changes only one exponent, so we aim for: \[x\ \text{a square},\quad 2x\ \text{a cube},\quad 3x\ \text{a fifth power},\quad 5x\ \text{a seventh power}.\] Then $4x=2^2\cdot x$ is a square automatically. The conditions are:

  • $a$ is a multiple of $2$, $5$ and $7$, and $a+1$ is a multiple of $3$: $a$ is a multiple of $70$, and $70\to71$ fails, $140\to141=3\cdot47$ works. So $a=140$.

  • $b$ is a multiple of $2$, $3$, $7$ (so of $42$) and $b+1$ is a multiple of $5$: $42\to43$ fails, $84\to85$ works. So $b=84$.

  • $c$ is a multiple of $2$, $3$, $5$ (so of $30$) and $c+1$ is a multiple of $7$: $30\to31$ and $60\to61$ fail, $90\to91=7\cdot13$ works. So $c=90$.

Hence $x=2^{140}\cdot3^{84}\cdot5^{90}$ works: \[x=\left(2^{70}3^{42}5^{45}\right)^2,\quad 2x=\left(2^{47}3^{28}5^{30}\right)^3,\quad 3x=\left(2^{28}3^{17}5^{18}\right)^5,\] \[4x=\left(2^{71}3^{42}5^{45}\right)^2,\quad 5x=\left(2^{20}3^{12}5^{13}\right)^7.\] The same idea works with any assignment of different exponents; for example the roles of $3$, $5$, $7$ may be swapped.

Problem 14

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry

As shown in the diagram below, triangle $ABC$ is acute and $AH$ is the altitude from $A$ to side $BC$. Point $D$ lies on segment $AH$, where $\angle DBA=\angle DAC=20^\circ$ and $\angle DBC=10^\circ$. What is the measure, in degrees, of $\angle DCB$?

Show the answer

$40^\circ$

Show the solution

Solution (The circumcentre lies on BD)

First the angles of the triangle: $\angle ABC=20^\circ+10^\circ=30^\circ$. Since $AH\perp BC$ and $D$ is on $AH$, $\angle HAC=\angle DAC=20^\circ$, so $\angle ACB=90^\circ-20^\circ=70^\circ$ and $\angle BAC=80^\circ$.

Let $O$ be the centre of the circle through $A,B,C$ (inside, as the triangle is acute). A central angle is twice the inscribed angle on the same arc, so \[\angle BOC=2\cdot80^\circ=160^\circ,\qquad\angle AOC=2\cdot30^\circ=60^\circ,\qquad\angle AOB=2\cdot70^\circ=140^\circ.\]

  • Triangle $OBC$ is isosceles ($OB=OC$), so $\angle OBC=10^\circ=\angle DBC$: the point $O$ lies on line $BD$.

  • Triangle $AOC$ is isosceles with a $60^\circ$ angle at $O$, so it is equilateral: $OA=OC=AC$ and $\angle OAC=\angle OCA=60^\circ$.

Since $\angle BAO=80^\circ-60^\circ=20^\circ$ is less than $\angle BAH=60^\circ$, the point $O$ is on the same side of $AH$ as $B$; so $O$ lies between $B$ and $D$. Then \[\angle AOD=180^\circ-\angle AOB=40^\circ,\qquad \angle OAD=\angle OAC-\angle DAC=60^\circ-20^\circ=40^\circ,\] so triangle $AOD$ is isosceles: $DA=DO$.

Both $D$ and $C$ are equally far from $A$ and $O$ (as $CA=CO$), so line $CD$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AO$. In the equilateral triangle $AOC$ this line bisects the angle at $C$: $\angle ACD=30^\circ$. Therefore \[\angle DCB=70^\circ-30^\circ=\boxed{40^\circ}.\]

Problem 15

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking

On an infinite strip of adjacent squares (which can be associated with the integers), there is exactly one ball in each square. Written on these squares is a pattern of numbers that repeats periodically forever. The pattern is formed by a permutation of the numbers from $1$ to $n$ ($n\ge3$) which we denote as $\overline{a_1a_2\ldots a_n}$.

At a given signal, each ball jumps to the right over a number of squares equal to the value written in its starting square. The pattern is called collision-free if, after the jump, there is no square in which two or more balls land. For example, the patterns $\overline{123}$, $\overline{231}$, $\overline{312}$ are collision-free, as shown below. On the other hand, the pattern $\overline{213}$ is not collision-free, as also shown below.

Two patterns are considered identical if they can be obtained from one another through a cyclic shift. For example, $\overline{123}$ produces the same infinite strip as $\overline{231}$ or $\overline{312}$, that is $\overline{123}=\overline{231}=\overline{312}$. For $n=3$, there is only one collision-free pattern.

How many different collision-free patterns exist for $n=5$? Explain your reasoning in complete detail. Simply listing down the correct collision-free patterns will not receive full credit.

Show the answer

$3$

Show the solution

Solution (Landing squares modulo 5)

Number the squares by the integers so that square $k$ carries $a_i$ when $k$ leaves remainder $i$ on division by $5$ (positions $i=0,1,2,3,4$; write the pattern as $a_0a_1a_2a_3a_4$). The ball on square $k$ lands on $k+a_i$.

Key fact: the pattern is collision-free exactly when the five numbers $i+a_i$ ($i=0,\dots,4$) leave five different remainders mod $5$. Indeed, if balls from squares $k$ and $l$ land together, $k+a_i=l+a_j$; then $i\ne j$ (two balls with the same remainder jump by the same amount, so they land on different squares), and $i+a_i\equiv j+a_j\pmod5$. Conversely, if $i+a_i\equiv j+a_j\pmod5$ with $i\ne j$, take square $k$ with remainder $i$; the square $l=k+a_i-a_j$ has remainder $j$, and the balls from $k$ and $l$ both land on $k+a_i$.

A cyclic shift gives the same strip, so it keeps a pattern collision-free, and every pattern has exactly one cyclic shift that starts with $5$. So we count collision-free patterns with $a_0=5$. Its ball has $0+5\equiv0$; the other four landing remainders must be $1,2,3,4$ in some order, so $a_i\ne5-i$: \[a_1\ne4,\quad a_2\ne3,\quad a_3\ne2,\quad a_4\ne1,\] and the remainders of $1+a_1,\ 2+a_2,\ 3+a_3,\ 4+a_4$ must be different. Try $a_1$:

  • $a_1=1$ (remainder $2$). Then $a_2\in\{2,4\}$. If $a_2=4$ (remainder $1$), then $a_3\ne2$ forces $a_3=3$, remainder $1$ again: no. If $a_2=2$ (remainder $4$), then $a_3=4$ gives remainder $2$ again, so $a_3=3$, $a_4=4$, remainders $2,4,1,3$: $51234$ works.

  • $a_1=2$ (remainder $3$). $a_2=1$ gives remainder $3$ again, so $a_2=4$ (remainder $1$). Then $a_4\ne1$ forces $a_4=3$ (remainder $2$) and $a_3=1$ (remainder $4$): $52413$ works.

  • $a_1=3$ (remainder $4$). $a_2=2$ repeats $4$. If $a_2=4$ (remainder $1$), $a_3\ne2$ forces $a_3=1$, remainder $4$ again: no. If $a_2=1$ (remainder $3$), $a_3\ne2$ forces $a_3=4$ (remainder $2$), $a_4=2$ (remainder $1$): $53142$ works.

So there are exactly $\boxed{3}$ collision-free patterns for $n=5$, namely $\overline{12345}$, $\overline{13524}$ $(=\overline{52413})$ and $\overline{14253}$ $(=\overline{53142})$.

Problem 16

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction

A subset $S$ of $\{1,2,3,\ldots,2025,2026\}$ is called good, if for any three elements of $S$, their least common multiple also belongs to $S$. For example, $\{3,9,12,36\}$ is good. What is the maximum possible number of elements in a good subset of $\{1,2,3,\ldots,2025,2026\}$?

Show the answer

$40$

Show the solution

Solution (Everything divides the largest element)

Step 1: a good set is a set of divisors of its largest element. Let $S$ be good with at least $3$ elements and let $M$ be its largest element. Take any $x\in S$, $x\ne M$, and a third element $y$. Then $L=\mathrm{lcm}(x,y,M)$ is in $S$ and $L\ge M$, so $L=M$; hence $x$ divides $M$. So $|S|$ is at most the number $d(M)$ of divisors of $M$, where $M\le2026$.

Conversely, the set of all divisors of any $M$ is good: the lcm of divisors of $M$ is again a divisor of $M$. So the answer is the largest number of divisors of a number $M\le2026$.

Step 2: $1680=2^4\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$ has $5\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=40$ divisors, and no $M\le2026$ has more. Since $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11=2310>2026$, $M$ has at most four prime factors. Giving the largest exponent to $2$, the next to $3$, and so on, keeps the number of divisors and does not make $M$ larger, so take $M=2^a3^b5^c7^e$ with $a\ge b\ge c\ge e$.

  • Four primes: $M=210K$ with $K=2^{a-1}3^{b-1}5^{c-1}7^{e-1}\le9$. With the order of exponents, $K\in\{1,2,4,6,8\}$, giving $16,24,32,36,40$ divisors.

  • Three primes: $M=30K$ with $K=2^{a-1}3^{b-1}5^{c-1}\le67$. If $c\ge2$ then $K\in\{30,60\}$ with $27$ or $36$ divisors. If $c=1$: $b=1$ allows $a\le7$ ($32$ divisors); $b=2$ allows $a\le5$ ($36$); $b=3$ allows $a\le3$ ($32$); $b\ge4$ is impossible.

  • Two primes: $2^a3^b\le2026$ gives at most $28$ divisors ($2^6\cdot3^3=1728$); one prime: at most $11$.

So the maximum number of elements of a good subset is $\boxed{40}$, for example the set of divisors of $1680$.

Problem 17

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants

The numbers $1,2,3,\ldots,32$ and $33$ are written on a board. A student repeatedly performs the following operation: erase two numbers such that one is a multiple of the other, and write down their integer quotient on the board. The process stops when no number on the board is a multiple of another. At least how many numbers remain on the board?

Show the answer

$7$

Show the solution

Solution (An invariant and a construction)

The invariant. Erasing $x$ and $kx$ and writing $k$ divides the product of all numbers on the board by $x^2$. So for every prime $p$, the exponent of $p$ in the product changes by an even number: its parity (even or odd) never changes. At the start the product is $33!$, and the exponents of the primes are \[2:\ 31,\quad 3:\ 15,\quad 5:\ 7,\quad 7:\ 4,\quad 11:\ 3,\quad 13:\ 2,\quad 17,19,23,29,31:\ 1 \text{ each}.\] So at the end the product still has odd exponents of $2,3,5,11,17,19,23,29,31$.

Lower bound. A quotient is never larger than the numbers erased, so all numbers stay at most $33$. For $p=17,19,23,29,31$ some number on the board is divisible by $p$, and as $2p>33$ that number is $p$ itself: five numbers. Some of the other numbers carry the primes $2,3,5,11$; one number cannot carry all four, since $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot11=330>33$. So at least $5+2=7$ numbers remain.

Seven is possible. Write $(a,b)\to c$ for “erase $a$ and $b$, write $c$”. We never erase two equal numbers. Leave $17,19,23,29,31$ and $33$ alone.

  • $(1,2)\to2$, and $(4,8)$, $(10,20)$, $(11,22)$, $(13,26)$, $(14,28)$ each $\to2$; $(7,21)$ and $(9,27)$ each $\to3$; $(5,25)\to5$. Besides the six numbers left alone, the board now holds six $2$'s, three $3$'s, one $5$ and $6,12,15,16,18,24,30,32$.

  • Use up the $3$'s: $(2,24)\to12$, $(2,12)\to6$, $(3,6)\to2$; then $(2,12)\to6$, $(3,6)\to2$; then $(3,6)\to2$. Now $24$, $12$, $6$ and all $3$'s are gone, and there are still six $2$'s.

  • Use up the $2$'s: $(2,32)\to16$, $(2,16)\to8$, $(2,8)\to4$, $(2,4)\to2$ (three $2$'s fewer); then $(2,16)\to8$, $(2,8)\to4$, $(2,4)\to2$ (two $2$'s fewer); then $(2,18)\to9$.

  • Finish: $(5,15)\to3$, $(3,9)\to3$, $(3,30)\to10$.

The board is \[10,\ 17,\ 19,\ 23,\ 29,\ 31,\ 33,\] and no number is a multiple of another, so the process stops. The least number of remaining numbers is $\boxed{7}$.
