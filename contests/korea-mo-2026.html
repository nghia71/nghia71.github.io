Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with orthocenter $H$. Let $D$ and $E$ be the feet of the altitudes from $A$ and $C$ onto the opposite sides, respectively.

Let $P\,(\ne B,E)$ be a point on segment $BE$, and let line $PH$ meet $AC$ at $Q$.

Let $\ell$ be the angle bisector of $\angle BAC$. A line through $H$ parallel to $\ell$ meets the circumcircle of triangle $BDH$ and the circumcircle of triangle $HDC$ at $X\,(\ne H)$ and $Y\,(\ne H)$, respectively.

Show that the three lines $PX$, $QY$, and $\ell$ are concurrent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates with the bisector as an axis)

What $X$ and $Y$ are. Since $\angle BDH=90^\circ$, the circle $BDH$ has diameter $BH$; so the second point $X$ where it meets the line $m$ through $H$ parallel to $\ell$ satisfies $\angle BXH=90^\circ$: $X$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $m$. Likewise ($\angle HDC=90^\circ$) $Y$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $m$.

If $AB=AC$, then $\ell$ is the altitude $AD$, $m=\ell$ and $X=Y=D$, so all three lines pass through $D$. From now on let $b=AC\ne c=AB$.

Coordinates. Put $A$ at the origin and $\ell$ on the $x$-axis; let $\theta=\frac A2$, $s=\sin\theta$, $k=\cos\theta$ and $U=\cos A$, so that $k^2=\frac{1+U}2$, $s^2=\frac{1-U}2$, $4s^2k^2=1-U^2$. Then \[B=(ck,\,-cs),\qquad C=(bk,\,bs),\qquad H=\Bigl(\frac{(b+c)U}{2k},\ h\Bigr)\ \text{ with }\ h=\frac{(c-b)U}{2s}.\] (Check: $H\cdot(C-B)=\frac{(b+c)(b-c)U}2+\frac{(c-b)(b+c)U}2=0$ and $(H-B)\cdot C=bcU-B\cdot C=bcU-bc\cos2\theta=0$, so $AH\perp BC$ and $BH\perp AC$.) The line $m$ is $y=h$, hence $X=(ck,h)$ and $Y=(bk,h)$. These differ from $H$: $ck=\frac{(b+c)U}{2k}$ would mean $c(1+U)=(b+c)U$, i.e. $AB=AC\cos A=AE$, impossible as $E$ is inside segment $AB$; likewise $bk\ne\frac{(b+c)U}{2k}$. The point $Q$ exists: in triangle $AEC$ the line through $H\in EC$ parallel to $AC$ meets $AB$ inside segment $AE$, while $P\ne E$ lies on segment $EB$, so $PH\not\parallel AC$. Write $P=(pk,-ps)$ on line $AB$ and $Q=(qk,qs)$ on line $AC$.

The condition that $P,H,Q$ are collinear. The determinant of $Q-P=((q-p)k,(q+p)s)$ and $H-P$ vanishes: \[(q-p)k\,h+2pq\,sk-(q+p)s\,\frac{(b+c)U}{2k}=0 .\] Multiplying by $4sk$ and substituting $h$, $k^2$, $s^2$: \[(q-p)(c-b)U(1+U)+2pq(1-U^2)-(q+p)(b+c)U(1-U)=0,\] which simplifies (collect the terms with $U$ and with $U^2$) to \[pq\,(1-U^2)=U\,(qb+pc)-U^2\,(qc+pb).\tag{$*$}\] Where $PX$ and $QY$ meet $\ell$. The point $P$ lies on segment $BE$, below the level of $E$, and $E$ is below $H$ ($H$ is inside segment $CE$), so $-ps<h$ and $PX$ is not parallel to $\ell$. Since $P$ is not on $m$ (its level is $-ps<h$), the line $PH$ is not $m$, so it is not parallel to $\ell$; and $Q\ne H$ because $H\notin AC$. So $Q$ is not at level $h$, and $QY$ is not parallel to $\ell$ either. Solving for $y=0$ on the lines $PX$ and $QY$ gives the $x$-coordinates \[x_F=k\,\frac{p\,(h+cs)}{h+ps},\qquad x_G=k\,\frac{q\,(bs-h)}{qs-h}.\] Using $2s^2=1-U$: $h+cs=\frac{c-bU}{2s}$, $bs-h=\frac{b-cU}{2s}$, $h+ps=\frac{p(1-U)+(c-b)U}{2s}$, $qs-h=\frac{q(1-U)-(c-b)U}{2s}$. So $x_F=x_G$ iff \begin{align*}&p(c-bU)\bigl(q(1-U)-(c-b)U\bigr)=q(b-cU)\bigl(p(1-U)+(c-b)U\bigr)\\ \iff\ &pq(1-U)\bigl[(c-bU)-(b-cU)\bigr]=(c-b)U\bigl[p(c-bU)+q(b-cU)\bigr].\end{align*} As $(c-bU)-(b-cU)=(c-b)(1+U)$ and $c\ne b$, this is equivalent to $pq(1-U^2)=U(pc+qb)-U^2(pb+qc)$, which is exactly $(*)$.

Hence $PX$ and $QY$ meet $\ell$ at the same point: the three lines are concurrent.

Solution 2 (Three fixed points of a projectivity)

Let $m$ be the line through $H$ parallel to $\ell$. Since $\angle BDH=90^\circ$, the circle $BDH$ has diameter $BH$, so $\angle BXH=90^\circ$: $X$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $m$. Likewise $Y$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $m$. If $AB=AC$, then $m=\ell=AD$ and $X=Y=D\in\ell$, so the three lines meet at $D$. Assume $AB\ne AC$; then $H\notin\ell$, so $m\ne\ell$. The point $Q$ exists: in triangle $AEC$ the line through $H\in EC$ parallel to $AC$ meets $AB$ inside segment $AE$, while $P\ne E$ lies on segment $EB$, so $PH\not\parallel AC$. Let $B_1$, $C_1$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $B$, $C$ to $\ell$ (so $B,X,B_1$ are collinear and $C,Y,C_1$ are collinear), and let $K$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$. Directed angles are taken modulo $180^\circ$.

Non-degeneracy. $X$ lies neither on $\ell$ (it is on $m$) nor on $AB$. Indeed, if $X\in AB$ and $X\ne B$, then $AB=BX\perp m$, so $AB\perp\ell$, impossible because $\ell$ makes the angle $\frac A2<90^\circ$ with $AB$; and $X=B$ would put $B$ on $m$, so $BH\parallel\ell$, i.e. $\ell\perp AC$, impossible for the same reason. In particular $X\ne B$ and $X\ne E$ (both are on $AB$). In the same way $Y$ lies neither on $\ell$ nor on $AC$, so $Y\ne C$ and $Y\ne K$. Finally $H\notin AB,AC$, and $E\ne A,B$, $K\ne A,C$ because the triangle is acute.

Claim 1: $E$, $X$, $C_1$ are collinear. The points $E,X$ lie on the circle with diameter $BH$ ($\angle BEH=\angle BXH=90^\circ$), and $E,C_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $AC$. Hence \[\measuredangle(EX,AB)=\measuredangle(HX,HB)=\measuredangle(\ell,HB)=\measuredangle(\ell,CA)+90^\circ,\qquad \measuredangle(EC_1,AB)=\measuredangle(CC_1,CA)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(\ell,CA),\] using $HX\parallel\ell$, $HB\perp CA$, $CC_1\perp\ell$. So the lines $EX$ and $EC_1$ coincide.

Claim 2: $K$, $Y$, $B_1$ are collinear. In the same way, with the circles of diameters $CH$ (through $K,Y$) and $AB$ (through $K,B_1$): $\measuredangle(KY,AC)=\measuredangle(HY,HC)=\measuredangle(\ell,AB)+90^\circ$ and $\measuredangle(KB_1,AC)=\measuredangle(BB_1,BA)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(\ell,AB)$.

A projective map. By the non-degeneracy facts, the following central projections are defined \[\pi_1:\ \ell\to AB\ \text{(centre $X$)},\qquad \pi_2:\ AB\to AC\ \text{(centre $H$)},\qquad \pi_3:\ AC\to\ell\ \text{(centre $Y$)},\] on the projective lines (points at infinity included). Their composition $\psi=\pi_3\circ\pi_2\circ\pi_1$ is a projective transformation of the line $\ell$. For a point $P$ of $AB$ with $Q=\pi_2(P)=PH\cap AC$, the point $F=PX\cap\ell$ satisfies $\pi_1(F)=P$, and $\psi(F)=\pi_3(Q)=QY\cap\ell$. So the problem is to show $\psi=\mathrm{id}$.

Three fixed points.

  • $A$: $\pi_1(A)=A$, $\pi_2(A)=A$, $\pi_3(A)=A$.

  • $B_1$: $\pi_1(B_1)=B$ (as $X\in BB_1$), $\pi_2(B)=BH\cap AC=K$, $\pi_3(K)=KY\cap\ell=B_1$ by Claim 2 (as $Y\ne K$).

  • $C_1$: $\pi_1(C_1)=E$ by Claim 1 (line $XC_1=XE$ meets $AB$ only at $E$, as $X\notin AB$), $\pi_2(E)=EH\cap AC=C$, $\pi_3(C)=CY\cap\ell=C_1$.

These are three distinct points ($AB_1=AB\cos\frac A2\ne AC\cos\frac A2=AC_1$). A projective transformation of a line with three fixed points is the identity, so $\psi=\mathrm{id}$: for every $P$, the lines $PX$ and $QY$ meet $\ell$ at the same point.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

A sequence $\{a_n\}$ $(n\ge1)$ satisfies

  • $a_1=a_2=1$,

  • for all positive integers $n$, $\displaystyle a_{n+2}=a_n+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}^2+a_n^2}$.

Determine whether there exist infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that \[a_{2n}^3>\frac{3n}{2}-2026\sqrt n.\]

Show the answer

No: the inequality holds for only finitely many $n$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (The odd terms stay ahead)

Answer: no. Write $x_k=a_{2k-1}$ and $y_k=a_{2k}$ $(k\ge1)$. The recurrence says \[x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac1{x_k^2+y_k^2},\qquad y_{k+1}=y_k+\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y_k^2}.\] All terms are at least $1$ and both sequences are increasing. We must decide whether $y_n^3>\frac{3n}2-2026\sqrt n$ for infinitely many $n$.

Step 1: a gap that never closes. Let $D_k=x_k-y_k$. Then \[D_{k+1}-D_k=\frac1{x_k^2+y_k^2}-\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y_k^2}>0\] because $x_{k+1}>x_k$. From $a_3=\frac32$, $a_4=1+\frac{4}{13}=\frac{17}{13}$ we get $D_2=\frac{5}{26}=:\delta$, so $D_k\ge\delta$ for $k\ge2$, and for every $k\ge1$: $x_{k+1}=y_{k+1}+D_{k+1}>y_k+\delta$.

Step 2: the cube of $y$ grows by less than $\frac32-\frac{\delta}{y}$. Fix $k$ and write $y=y_k$, $\varepsilon=y_{k+1}-y_k$. Then $\varepsilon=\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y^2}<\frac1{(y+\delta)^2+y^2}<\frac1{2y^2}$, so (using $y\ge1$) $3y\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2<\frac3{2y}+\frac1{4y^4}\le\frac7{4y}$ and \[y_{k+1}^3-y_k^3=\varepsilon\,(3y^2+3y\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)<\frac{3y^2+\frac7{4y}}{2y^2+2\delta y}.\] This is at most $\frac32-\frac\delta y$ iff $3y^2+\frac7{4y}\le\bigl(\frac32-\frac\delta y\bigr)(2y^2+2\delta y)=3y^2+\delta y-2\delta^2$, i.e. iff $\frac7{4y}+2\delta^2\le\delta y$, which holds for $y\ge4$ (then the left side is at most $0.44+0.08$ and the right side at least $0.76$, and the left side decreases while the right side increases). So \[y_{k+1}^3\le y_k^3+\frac32-\frac{\delta}{y_k}\qquad\text{whenever } y_k\ge4.\tag{1}\] Step 3: summing. If $(y_k)$ is bounded, then certainly $y_n^3<\frac{3n}2-2026\sqrt n$ for all large $n$, and we are done. Otherwise $y_k\to\infty$ (it is increasing), and there is $K$ with $y_k\ge4$ for all $k\ge K$. From (1), for $k\ge K$, \[y_k^3\le y_K^3+\tfrac32(k-K)\le 2k\qquad\text{as soon as } k\ge J:=\max(K,\,2y_K^3),\] so $\frac1{y_j}\ge(2j)^{-1/3}$ for $j\ge J$ (if $J$ is not an integer, read $\lceil J\rceil$). Summing (1) from $K$ to $k-1$, for $k\ge \max(2J,4)$: \[y_k^3\le y_K^3+\frac32 k-\delta\sum_{j=\lceil k/2\rceil}^{k-1}\frac1{y_j}\le y_K^3+\frac32k-\delta\Bigl(\frac k2-1\Bigr)(2k)^{-1/3} \le \frac32k+y_K^3-c\,k^{2/3},\] with $c=\frac{\delta}{4\cdot 2^{1/3}}>0$ (we used $\frac k2-1\ge\frac k4$ and $(2j)^{-1/3}\ge(2k)^{-1/3}$ for $j<k$).

Conclusion. Since $\frac{k^{2/3}}{\sqrt k}=k^{1/6}\to\infty$, we have $c\,k^{2/3}-y_K^3>2026\sqrt k$ for all large $k$, and then \[a_{2k}^3=y_k^3<\frac{3k}2-2026\sqrt k .\] So the inequality $a_{2n}^3>\frac{3n}2-2026\sqrt n$ holds for only finitely many $n$; the answer is no.

Remark. Computation up to $n=2\cdot10^6$ gives $\frac{3n}2-a_{2n}^3\approx0.67\,n^{2/3}$. If this ratio stays near $0.67$, the deficit exceeds $2026\sqrt n$ only for $n$ beyond about $8\cdot10^{20}$; so the inequality is true for every $n$ one can check by computer, but it fails eventually. This estimate plays no role in the proof.

Solution 2 (The sum of the two cubes)

Answer: no. Write $x_k=a_{2k-1}$ and $y_k=a_{2k}$ $(k\ge1)$. The recurrence says \[x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac1{x_k^2+y_k^2},\qquad y_{k+1}=y_k+\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y_k^2},\] so all terms are at least $1$ and both sequences are increasing.

The gap. Let $D_k=x_k-y_k$. Then $D_1=0$ and $D_{k+1}-D_k=\frac1{x_k^2+y_k^2}-\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y_k^2}>0$, because $x_{k+1}>x_k$. From $a_3=\frac32$, $a_4=1+\frac4{13}=\frac{17}{13}$ we get $D_2=\frac5{26}=:\delta$. Hence $x_k\ge y_k$ for all $k$ and $x_k\ge y_k+\delta$ for $k\ge2$.

The sum of the cubes grows by about $3$. Let $T_k=x_k^3+y_k^3$. Fix $k$, write $x=x_k$, $y=y_k$, $\alpha=x_{k+1}-x=\frac1{x^2+y^2}$ and $\beta=y_{k+1}-y=\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y^2}$, so $0<\beta<\alpha$. Since $t\mapsto t(3y^2+3yt+t^2)$ is increasing for $t>0$, \[T_{k+1}-T_k=\alpha(3x^2+3x\alpha+\alpha^2)+\beta(3y^2+3y\beta+\beta^2)<3\alpha(x^2+y^2)+3\alpha^2(x+y)+2\alpha^3 =3+\frac{3(x+y)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}+\frac2{(x^2+y^2)^3}.\] As $x^2+y^2\ge\frac12(x+y)^2$ and $(x+y)^3\ge x^3+y^3=T_k$, the middle term is at most $\frac{12}{(x+y)^3}\le\frac{12}{T_k}$. Since $x,y\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, we have $x^3+y^3\le(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}$, so $(x^2+y^2)^3\ge T_k^2$, and as $T_k\ge2$ the last term is at most $\frac2{T_k^2}\le\frac1{T_k}$. Hence \[T_{k+1}<T_k+3+\frac{13}{T_k}.\] Also $x_{k+1}^3-x_k^3>3x^2\alpha=\frac{3x^2}{x^2+y^2}\ge\frac32$ (as $x\ge y$), so $x_k^3\ge1+\frac32(k-1)\ge k$ and $T_k>k$. Summing, with $1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1{k-1}\le1+\ln k$, \[T_k<2+3(k-1)+13\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac1j\le3k+13\ln k+12 .\] Conclusion. For $k\ge2$, $x_k^3\ge(y_k+\delta)^3>y_k^3+3\delta y_k^2$, so \[2y_k^3+3\delta y_k^2<T_k<3k+13\ln k+12 .\] If $y_k^3\le k$, then $y_k^3\le k<\frac{3k}2-2026\sqrt k$ as soon as $k>4\cdot2026^2$. If $y_k^3>k$, then $y_k^2>k^{2/3}$ and \[y_k^3<\frac{3k}2-\frac{3\delta}2k^{2/3}+\frac{13}2\ln k+6 ,\] which is less than $\frac{3k}2-2026\sqrt k$ for all large $k$, because $\frac{3\delta}2k^{2/3}-2026\sqrt k-\frac{13}2\ln k-6\to\infty$ (as $k^{2/3}/\sqrt k=k^{1/6}\to\infty$). Either way $a_{2k}^3=y_k^3<\frac{3k}2-2026\sqrt k$ for all large $k$, so the inequality holds for only finitely many $n$; the answer is no.

Solution 3 (A shifted cube for the even terms)

Answer: no. Write $x_k=a_{2k-1}$ and $y_k=a_{2k}$ $(k\ge1)$. The recurrence says \[x_{k+1}=x_k+h_k,\quad h_k=\frac1{x_k^2+y_k^2},\qquad y_{k+1}=y_k+\varepsilon_k,\quad \varepsilon_k=\frac1{x_{k+1}^2+y_k^2},\] so all terms are at least $1$ and both sequences are increasing.

Step 1: the gap. Let $D_k=x_k-y_k$, so $D_1=0$ and $D_{k+1}-D_k=h_k-\varepsilon_k>0$ because $x_{k+1}>x_k$. From $a_3=\frac32$ and $a_4=1+\frac4{13}=\frac{17}{13}$ we get $D_2=\frac5{26}$. Put $d=\frac5{52}$; then $D_k\ge2d$ for $k\ge2$, so for every $k\ge1$ \[x_{k+1}=y_{k+1}+D_{k+1}>y_k+2d.\] The gap is also less than $\frac12$: $D_1=0$, and since $y_{k+1}>y_k$ we have $\varepsilon_k>h_{k+1}$, hence for $k\ge2$ \[D_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(h_j-\varepsilon_j)<\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(h_j-h_{j+1})=h_1-h_k<h_1=\tfrac12 .\] Step 2: a lower bound for $y_k^3$. By Step 1, $x_{k+1}=x_k+h_k<y_k+\frac12+\frac12\le2y_k$, so $\varepsilon_k>\frac1{5y_k^2}$ and $y_{k+1}^3-y_k^3>3y_k^2\varepsilon_k>\frac35$. Hence $y_k^3\ge1+\frac35(k-1)>\frac{3k}5$ for all $k\ge1$.

Step 3: the shifted cube. Let $U_k=y_k+d\ (\ge1)$. By Step 1, \[\varepsilon_k<\frac1{(y_k+2d)^2+y_k^2}=\frac1{2(y_k+d)^2+2d^2}<\frac1{2U_k^2}.\] Therefore, writing $U=U_k$, \[U_{k+1}^3-U_k^3=3U^2\varepsilon_k+3U\varepsilon_k^2+\varepsilon_k^3<\frac32+\frac3{4U^3}+\frac1{8U^6}\le\frac32+\frac1{U^3},\] the last step because $\frac1{8U^6}\le\frac1{4U^3}$ for $U\ge1$. By Step 2, $\frac1{U_k^3}<\frac1{y_k^3}<\frac5{3k}$. Summing from $k=1$ to $n-1$ and using $U_1^3=(1+d)^3<2$ and $1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1{n-1}\le1+\ln n$, \[U_n^3<2+\frac32(n-1)+\frac53(1+\ln n)<\frac{3n}2+2+\frac53\ln n .\] Step 4: the shift costs a term of order $n^{2/3}$. We have $U_n^3-y_n^3=3dy_n^2+3d^2y_n+d^3>3dy_n^2>3d\bigl(\frac{3n}5\bigr)^{2/3}$ by Step 2. Hence \[a_{2n}^3=y_n^3<\frac{3n}2+2+\frac53\ln n-\frac{15}{52}\Bigl(\frac{3n}5\Bigr)^{2/3}.\] Since $n^{2/3}/\sqrt n=n^{1/6}\to\infty$ and $(\ln n)/\sqrt n\to0$, the quantity $\frac{15}{52}\bigl(\frac{3n}5\bigr)^{2/3}-\frac53\ln n-2$ exceeds $2026\sqrt n$ for all large $n$, and then $a_{2n}^3<\frac{3n}2-2026\sqrt n$. So the inequality holds for only finitely many $n$; the answer is no.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Show that there exists a positive integer $M\,(\ge3)$ such that the following holds:

For any integer $n\,(\ge M)$ and any positive integers $a,b,c$ with $1\le a<b<c\le n$, \[\gcd(a+b+c,\ ab+bc+ca,\ abc)<3n-\sqrt{3n}-2^{2026}.\] (Here, for positive integers $p,q,r$, $\gcd(p,q,r)$ denotes the greatest positive integer dividing all of $p,q,r$.)

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Dividing out the common factor)

Write $K=2^{2026}$, $s=a+b+c$, $e_2=ab+bc+ca$, $e_3=abc$ and $g=\gcd(s,e_2,e_3)$. Note that $t\mapsto 3t-\sqrt{3t}$ is increasing for $t\ge1$ (its derivative is $3-\frac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt t}>0$).

Case $g<s$. Then $g\mid s$ gives $g\le\frac s2\le\frac{3n-3}2$, which is less than $3n-\sqrt{3n}-K$ for all $n$ larger than some $M_1$.

Case $g=s$, i.e. $s\mid e_2$ and $s\mid e_3$. Put $x=c-a$, $y=c-b$ (so $x>y\ge1$), $w=x+y$ and $Q=x^2-xy+y^2>0$. Then $s=3c-w$.

Step 1: $s\mid Q$. Modulo $s$ we have $c\equiv-(a+b)$, so $e_2=ab+c(a+b)\equiv ab-(a+b)^2=-(a^2+ab+b^2)$; hence $s\mid a^2+ab+b^2$. From $3a=s+y-2x$ and $3b=s+x-2y$ one computes the exact identity $3(a^2+ab+b^2)=s^2-sw+Q$, so $s\mid Q$; in particular $s\le Q$.

Step 2: $s\le d^3$, where $d=\gcd(a,b,c)$. Write $a=da_1$, $b=db_1$, $c=dc_1$, and let $s_1$, $e_2'$, $e_3'$ be the elementary symmetric functions of $a_1,b_1,c_1$, so $s=ds_1$, $e_2=d^2e_2'$, $e_3=d^3e_3'$. No prime $p$ divides all of $s_1,e_2',e_3'$: it would divide $e_3'=a_1b_1c_1$, say $p\mid a_1$; then $p\mid e_2'-a_1(b_1+c_1)=b_1c_1$, say $p\mid b_1$; then $p\mid s_1-a_1-b_1=c_1$, contradicting $\gcd(a_1,b_1,c_1)=1$. Now $s\mid e_2$ and $s\mid e_3$ say $s_1\mid d\,e_2'$ and $s_1\mid d^2e_3'$. Let $p^k\,\|\,s_1$ with $k\ge1$. If $p\nmid e_2'$, then $p^k\mid d$; if $p\mid e_2'$, then $p\nmid e_3'$ and $p^k\mid d^2$. In both cases $p^k\mid d^2$; hence $s_1\mid d^2$ and $s=ds_1\le d^3$. Since $d$ divides $y=c-b\ge1$, and $s>c$, \[y\ \ge\ d\ \ge\ s^{1/3}\ >\ c^{1/3}.\] Step 3: $w-\sqrt{3c}$ is large. By Step 1, $3c=s+w\le Q+w=w^2-(3xy-w)<w^2$, because $3xy\ge3x>x+y=w$. So $\sqrt{3c}<w<2x$ and \[w-\sqrt{3c}=\frac{w^2-3c}{w+\sqrt{3c}}\ \ge\ \frac{3xy-w}{w+\sqrt{3c}}\ >\ \frac{3xy-w}{2w}\ >\ \frac{3xy-2x}{4x}=\frac{3y-2}4\ >\ \frac{3c^{1/3}-2}4 .\] Hence there is $C_0$ such that $w-\sqrt{3c}>K$ whenever $c\ge C_0$.

Conclusion of the case $g=s$. If $c\ge C_0$, then, as $n\ge c$ and $3t-\sqrt{3t}$ is increasing, \[3n-\sqrt{3n}-K-s\ \ge\ (3c-\sqrt{3c})-K-(3c-w)=w-\sqrt{3c}-K>0 .\] If $c<C_0$, then $s<3C_0<3n-\sqrt{3n}-K$ for all $n$ larger than some $M_2$.

Taking $M=\max(M_1,M_2,3)$, for every $n\ge M$ and every $1\le a<b<c\le n$ we get $\gcd(a+b+c,\,ab+bc+ca,\,abc)<3n-\sqrt{3n}-2^{2026}$. $\blacksquare$

Remark. The term $\sqrt{3n}$ is of the right order of magnitude. For $(a,b,c)=(105,112,126)$ the gcd is $343=a+b+c=7^3$ (here $d=7$ and $s=d^3$), and for $n=126$ the gap $3n-343=35$ is less than twice $\sqrt{3n}\approx19.4$. More generally, for $t\equiv1\pmod3$, $t\ge4$, put $R=t^2-t+1$, $c=\frac{R^3+(t+1)R}3$, $a=c-tR$, $b=c-R$ (for $t=4$: $(702,741,754)$). Here $R\equiv1\pmod3$ and $3\mid R^2+t+1$, so $a,b,c$ are multiples of $R$ and $R^3\mid e_3$; also $s=3c-(t+1)R=R^3=Q$, so $3e_2=s^2-Q=s(s-1)$ and, as $3\nmid s$, $s\mid e_2$. Thus $g=s$, and with $w=(t+1)R$, $w-\sqrt{3c}=\frac{w^2-3c}{w+\sqrt{3c}}=\frac{3tR^2-w}{w+\sqrt{3c}}\approx\frac32R\approx\frac32(3c)^{1/3}$. So the true excess over $\sqrt{3n}$ is of order $n^{1/3}$.

Solution 2 (Reduction to the case gcd equals a+b+c and a quadratic form)

Write $K=2^{2026}$, $s=a+b+c$, $e_2=ab+bc+ca$, $e_3=abc$ and $g=\gcd(s,e_2,e_3)$. Note that $t\mapsto 3t-\sqrt{3t}$ is increasing for $t\ge1$.

Case $g<s$. Then $g\le\frac s2\le\frac{3n-3}2$, which is less than $3n-\sqrt{3n}-K$ for all $n$ larger than some $M_1$.

Case $g=s$, i.e. $s\mid e_2$ and $s\mid e_3$. Put $x=c-a$, $y=c-b$ (so $x>y\ge1$), $w=x+y$, and $Q=x^2-xy+y^2>0$. Then $s=3c-w$.

Two divisibilities. (i) Modulo $s$ we have $c\equiv-(a+b)$, so $e_2=ab+c(a+b)\equiv ab-(a+b)^2=-(a^2+ab+b^2)$; hence $s\mid a^2+ab+b^2$. From $3a=s+y-2x$ and $3b=s+x-2y$ one computes the exact identity \[3(a^2+ab+b^2)=s^2-sw+Q,\] so $s\mid Q$. (ii) $c$ is a root of $t^3-st^2+e_2t-e_3$, all of whose non-leading coefficients are divisible by $s$; hence $s\mid c^3$, so $s\mid 27c^3=(s+w)^3$, and $s\mid w^3$.

The key inequality: $w-\sqrt{3c}\to\infty$ as $c\to\infty$. Let $Q=\mu s$ with $\mu$ a positive integer. Note $Q=w^2-3xy\le w^2$.

  • If $\mu\ge2$: $w^2\ge Q\ge2s=2(3c-w)$, so $(w+1)^2>6c$ and $w>\sqrt{6c}-1$. Then $w-\sqrt{3c}>(\sqrt6-\sqrt3)\sqrt c-1$.

  • If $\mu=1$, i.e. $s=Q$: let $g_0=\gcd(x,y)$, $x=g_0x_1$, $y=g_0y_1$ and $R=x_1^2-x_1y_1+y_1^2$, so $s=g_0^2R$. From $s\mid w^3=g_0^3(x_1+y_1)^3$ we get $R\mid g_0(x_1+y_1)^3$. Now $R=(x_1+y_1)^2-3x_1y_1$; a prime dividing $R$ and $x_1+y_1$ divides $3x_1y_1$ but neither $x_1$ nor $y_1$ (they are coprime), so it is $3$; and if $3\mid x_1+y_1$ then $9\mid(x_1+y_1)^2$, $9\nmid 3x_1y_1$, so $9\nmid R$. Hence $\gcd\bigl(R,(x_1+y_1)^3\bigr)\mid3$, so $R\mid 3g_0$ and $s=g_0^2R\le3g_0^3$, giving \[y\ \ge\ g_0\ \ge\ (s/3)^{1/3}\ \ge\ (c/3)^{1/3}.\] Also $3c=s+w=w^2+w-3xy<w^2$ (since $3xy\ge 3x>x+y=w$), so $\sqrt{3c}<w$ and, using $w<2x$, \[w-\sqrt{3c}=\frac{w^2-3c}{w+\sqrt{3c}}>\frac{3xy-w}{2w}>\frac{3xy-2x}{4x}=\frac{3y-2}4\ \ge\ \frac{3(c/3)^{1/3}-2}{4}.\]

In both cases there is $C_0$ such that $w-\sqrt{3c}>K$ whenever $c\ge C_0$.

Conclusion of the case $g=s$. If $c\ge C_0$, then, as $n\ge c$ and $3t-\sqrt{3t}$ is increasing, \[3n-\sqrt{3n}-K-s\ \ge\ (3c-\sqrt{3c})-K-(3c-w)=w-\sqrt{3c}-K>0 .\] If $c<C_0$, then $s<3C_0<3n-\sqrt{3n}-K$ for all $n$ larger than some $M_2$.

Taking $M=\max(M_1,M_2,3)$, for every $n\ge M$ and every $1\le a<b<c\le n$ we get $\gcd(a+b+c,\,ab+bc+ca,\,abc)<3n-\sqrt{3n}-2^{2026}$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 3 (A small positive multiple of the gcd)

Write $K=2^{2026}$, $s=a+b+c$, $e_2=ab+bc+ca$, $e_3=abc$ and $g=\gcd(s,e_2,e_3)$. Call $(n;a,b,c)$ bad if $1\le a<b<c\le n$ and $g\ge3n-\sqrt{3n}-K$. We show that $n$ is bounded for bad quadruples; then any $M\ge3$ above that bound works.

Let $(n;a,b,c)$ be bad and put $E=3n-g$. Since $g\le s\le3n-3$, we have $0<E\le\sqrt{3n}+K$.

Step 1: $g=s$ for large $n$. Choose $n_1$ with $\sqrt{3n}+K<\frac{3n}2$ for $n>n_1$. If $n>n_1$, then $g=3n-E>\frac{3n}2>\frac s2$; as $g$ divides $s$, this forces $g=s$. From now on $n>n_1$.

Step 2: two divisibilities. Put $x=c-a$, $y=c-b$ (so $x>y\ge1$), $w=x+y$ and $Q=x^2-xy+y^2=w^2-3xy>0$. Then $s=3c-w$. Since $a^2+b^2+c^2-e_2=\frac12\bigl((a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\bigr)=Q$, we get $s^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2e_2=3e_2+Q$; as $s\mid e_2$, this gives $s\mid Q$. Next, modulo $s$ we have $3c\equiv w$, $3a=3c-3x\equiv w-3x$ and $3b\equiv w-3y$, so \[0\equiv27e_3\equiv w(w-3x)(w-3y)=w\bigl(9xy-2w^2\bigr)=-2wQ+3xyw\pmod s .\] Hence $s\mid3xyw$. As $xw=x^2+xy=Q+2xy-y^2$, we have $3xyw=3yQ+3y^2(2x-y)$, so $s$ divides \[N=3y^2(2x-y),\] which is a positive integer because $2x>y$.

Step 3: sizes. As $c\le n$, $w=3c-s\le3n-g=E$, so $s\le Q<w^2\le E^2$ (the first inequality because $s\mid Q$ and $Q>0$). Suppose $E>4K+2$. From $E-K\le\sqrt{3n}$ we get $3n\ge(E-K)^2$, so \[g=3n-E\ge(E-K)^2-E>E^2-(2K+1)E>\frac{E^2}2 .\] Then $0<Q<E^2<2g$ and $g\mid Q$, so $Q=g$. Consequently $w^2>Q=g>\frac{E^2}2$, so $w>\frac E2$, and $x>\frac w2>\frac E4$ (as $x>y$). Also \[3xy=w^2-Q\le E^2-g<(2K+1)E,\qquad\text{so}\qquad y<\frac{(2K+1)E}{3x}<\frac{4(2K+1)}3 .\] Finally $g\mid N$ with $N>0$, and $N<6xy^2<6E\cdot\frac{16(2K+1)^2}{9}$ because $x<w\le E$. Together with $g>\frac{E^2}2$ this gives \[\frac{E^2}2<\frac{32}3(2K+1)^2E,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad E<E_0:=\frac{64}3(2K+1)^2 .\] Since $E_0>4K+2$, in every case $E<E_0$.

Conclusion. For a bad quadruple with $n>n_1$ we have $\frac{3n}2<g=s\le E^2<E_0^2$ (Steps 1 and 3), so $n<\frac23E_0^2$. Hence with $M=\max\bigl(3,\ n_1+1,\ \lceil\tfrac23E_0^2\rceil\bigr)$ there is no bad quadruple with $n\ge M$: for every $n\ge M$ and all $1\le a<b<c\le n$, $\gcd(a+b+c,\,ab+bc+ca,\,abc)<3n-\sqrt{3n}-2^{2026}$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Prove that there does not exist an ordered quadruple of positive integers $(p,q,r,n)$ satisfying both of the following conditions:

  • $p,q,r$ are prime numbers.

  • $p^n+q^n+r^n=2026(p+q)(q+r)(r+p)$.

Show the solution

Solution (Parity, then divisibility by p+q)

Suppose $p^n+q^n+r^n=2026(p+q)(q+r)(r+p)$ with primes $p,q,r$ and $n\ge1$. The equation is symmetric in $p,q,r$, and its right side is even.

How many of $p,q,r$ equal $2$? If none, the left side is odd. If exactly two, say $p=q=2$, the left side $2^{n+1}+r^n$ is odd. If all three, $3\cdot2^n=2026\cdot64=2^7\cdot1013$, impossible since $3\nmid 2^7\cdot1013$. So exactly one of them is $2$; say $p=2$, with $q,r$ odd primes.

$n$ even. Then $n\ge2$, $2^n\equiv0$ and $q^n\equiv r^n\equiv1\pmod 4$, so the left side is $\equiv2\pmod4$. On the right, $2026$ and $q+r$ are both even, so the right side is divisible by $4$. Contradiction.

$n$ odd. Then $x+y\mid x^n+y^n$ for all integers $x,y$. Since $2+q$ divides the right side and $2^n+q^n$, it divides $r^n$; as $2+q>1$ and $r$ is prime, $r\mid q+2$. Symmetrically $2+r$ divides $q^n$, so $q\mid r+2$. If $q=r$, then $q\mid q+2$ gives $q\mid2$, impossible. If, say, $q<r$, then $r\mid q+2$ with $0<q+2<2r$ gives $q+2=r$, and then $q\mid r+2=q+4$ gives $q\mid4$, impossible for an odd prime. The case $q>r$ is symmetric.

So no quadruple $(p,q,r,n)$ satisfies both conditions.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Minsu participates in a quiz show. The host chooses a positive integer less than or equal to $5$ as the “Password of the Day,” and Minsu must determine it by asking questions.

The questions and answers follow these rules:

  • In each question, Minsu chooses a positive integer $m$ and asks whether the secret number is at least $m$.

  • For each question, the host answers either “yes” or “no,” and throughout the entire game, the host may lie at most once.

Find the minimum number of questions required for Minsu to determine the Password of the Day with certainty, regardless of the host's choice.

Show the answer

$6$

Show the solution

Solution (A volume bound and an explicit strategy)

Write $Q_m$ for the question “is the number at least $m$?”. During the game call a number clean if it is consistent with all answers so far, marked if it is consistent with all answers but exactly one, and eliminated otherwise. Minsu knows the number exactly when a single non-eliminated number remains.

Lemma (volume bound). Suppose that at some moment there are $u$ clean and $v$ marked numbers and Minsu can still ask $q$ questions. If he can always finish, then $u(q+1)+v\le2^q$.

Proof. Fix Minsu's strategy; we may assume he always asks exactly $q$ more questions. For a clean number $x$ the host has $q+1$ possible behaviours: answer truthfully throughout, or lie exactly at the $j$-th remaining question ($1\le j\le q$). They produce $q+1$ different answer sequences in $\{\text{yes},\text{no}\}^q$: two behaviours agree before the first question where exactly one of them lies (so the same question is asked there) and differ at it. For a marked number only the truthful behaviour is allowed: one sequence. If one answer sequence came from two different numbers, Minsu could not distinguish them. So all these $u(q+1)+v$ sequences are distinct, and there are at most $2^q$ sequences. $\square$

Five questions are not enough. Suppose Minsu has $5$ questions, and the first is $Q_m$; let $k$ be the number of $x\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ with $x\ge m$. After “yes” there are $k$ clean and $5-k$ marked numbers and $4$ questions left, so the lemma needs $5k+(5-k)=4k+5\le16$, i.e. $k\le2$. After “no” there are $5-k$ clean and $k$ marked numbers, and we need $5(5-k)+k=25-4k\le16$, i.e. $k\ge3$. Both cannot hold, so the host can always prevent Minsu from succeeding with $5$ questions (and with fewer).

Six questions suffice. In the following strategy each line records the clean numbers, then the marked numbers with a star; “decides” means that the remaining candidates are all marked (so no more lies can occur) and the question asked separates them.

  • Ask $Q_3$.

    • Yes: $\{3,4,5;\,1^*,2^*\}$. Ask $Q_5$.

      • Yes: $\{5;\,3^*,4^*\}$. Ask $Q_5$: yes gives $5$; no gives $\{3^*,4^*,5^*\}$, then $Q_4$ (no: $3$) and if needed $Q_5$ decides.

      • No: $\{3,4;\,1^*,2^*,5^*\}$. Ask $Q_4$.

        • Yes: $\{4;\,3^*,5^*\}$. Ask $Q_5$: yes gives $\{4^*,5^*\}$ and $Q_5$ decides; no gives $\{4;\,3^*\}$, then $Q_4$: yes gives $4$, no gives $\{3^*,4^*\}$ and $Q_4$ decides.

        • No: $\{3;\,1^*,2^*,4^*\}$. Ask $Q_3$: yes gives $\{3;\,4^*\}$, then $Q_4$: no gives $3$, yes gives $\{3^*,4^*\}$ and $Q_4$ decides; no gives $\{1^*,2^*,3^*\}$, then $Q_2$ (no: $1$) and if needed $Q_3$ decides.

    • No: $\{1,2;\,3^*,4^*,5^*\}$. Ask $Q_2$.

      • No: $\{1;\,2^*\}$. Ask $Q_2$: no gives $1$; yes gives $\{1^*,2^*\}$ and $Q_2$ decides.

      • Yes: $\{2;\,1^*,3^*,4^*,5^*\}$. Ask $Q_3$: no gives $\{2;\,1^*\}$, then $Q_2$: yes gives $2$, no gives $\{1^*,2^*\}$ and $Q_2$ decides; yes gives $\{2^*,3^*,4^*,5^*\}$, then $Q_4$: no leaves $\{2^*,3^*\}$ and $Q_3$ decides; yes leaves $\{4^*,5^*\}$ and $Q_5$ decides.

Every branch uses at most $6$ questions. (The bookkeeping is mechanical: after an answer, a clean number consistent with it stays clean, an inconsistent clean number becomes marked, a consistent marked number stays marked, and an inconsistent marked number is eliminated.)

Hence the minimum number of questions is $\boxed{6}$.

Remark. The strategy must be adaptive. If all questions are fixed in advance, the answer strings of $x$ and $x+1$ (without lies) differ only at the copies of $Q_{x+1}$; to survive one lie the two strings must differ in at least $3$ places, so each of $Q_2,Q_3,Q_4,Q_5$ must be asked $3$ times, $12$ questions in all.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Let $\mathbb R^+$ be the set of positive real numbers. Determine all possible values of $f(2026)$ for functions $f:\mathbb R^+\to\mathbb R^+$ that satisfy the following condition.

(Condition) For any real numbers $x\,(>0)$ and $y\,(>1)$, \[f(f(x))+\frac1y=\bigl(f(f(xy))+1\bigr)f(y).\]

Show the answer

every number in the interval $\left[\frac1{2026},\ \frac{2027}{4052}\right]$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two ways to compute g at xyz)

Let $g=f\circ f$ and write $E(x,y)$ for the condition $g(x)+\frac1y=(g(xy)+1)f(y)$, $x>0$, $y>1$. It gives \[g(xy)=\frac{g(x)+\frac1y}{f(y)}-1\qquad(x>0,\ y>1).\tag{1}\] Case 1: $g$ is not constant. Let $y,z>1$. Computing $g(xyz)$ by (1) directly with the factor $yz$, and in two steps (first $y$, then $z$): \[\frac{g(x)+\frac1{yz}}{f(yz)}-1=\frac{\frac{g(x)+\frac1y}{f(y)}-1+\frac1z}{f(z)}-1 .\] Both sides are affine functions of $G=g(x)$, and they agree for at least two different values of $G$; so the coefficients agree: \[\frac1{f(yz)}=\frac1{f(y)f(z)},\qquad \frac1{yz\,f(yz)}=\frac{1}{y\,f(y)f(z)}+\frac{\frac1z-1}{f(z)} .\] Using the first relation in the second and multiplying by $f(z)$: $\frac1{yz f(y)}=\frac1{yf(y)}+\frac1z-1$, i.e. $\bigl(\frac1{yf(y)}-1\bigr)\bigl(\frac1z-1\bigr)=0$. As $z>1$, $f(y)=\frac1y$ for every $y>1$. In particular $f(2026)=\frac1{2026}$, and $f(x)=\frac1x$ is indeed a solution ($g(x)=x$ and $x+\frac1y=\frac{xy+1}y$).

Case 2: $g\equiv\kappa$ is constant. Then $\kappa>0$ and (1) gives $f(y)=\frac{\kappa+1/y}{\kappa+1}$ for $y>1$. Since $\kappa=g(x)=f(f(x))$ is a value of $f$, also $f(\kappa)=f(f(f(x)))=g(f(x))=\kappa$. If $\kappa>1$ this would mean $\frac{\kappa+1/\kappa}{\kappa+1}=\kappa$, i.e. $\kappa+\frac1\kappa=\kappa^2+\kappa$, i.e. $\kappa^3=1$, contradicting $\kappa>1$. So $0<\kappa\le1$ and \[f(2026)=\frac{\kappa+\frac1{2026}}{\kappa+1}.\] Conversely, for every $\kappa\in(0,1]$ the function \[f(y)=\frac{\kappa+1/y}{\kappa+1}\ (y>1),\qquad f(y)=\kappa\ (0<y\le1)\] is a solution: for $y>1$, $f(y)\in\bigl(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+1},1\bigr)\subset(0,1)$, so $f(f(y))=\kappa$; for $y\le1$, $f(y)=\kappa\le1$, so $f(f(y))=\kappa$. Thus $g\equiv\kappa$ and $E(x,y)$ reads $\kappa+\frac1y=(\kappa+1)\frac{\kappa+1/y}{\kappa+1}$, which is true.

The set of values. The map $\kappa\mapsto\frac{\kappa+1/2026}{\kappa+1}$ is continuous and increasing on $(0,1]$ (its derivative is $\frac{1-1/2026}{(\kappa+1)^2}>0$), with limit $\frac1{2026}$ at $0$ and value $\frac{2027}{4052}$ at $1$; so Case 2 gives exactly the values in $\bigl(\frac1{2026},\frac{2027}{4052}\bigr]$, and Case 1 adds $\frac1{2026}$.

All possible values of $f(2026)$ form the interval $\boxed{\left[\frac1{2026},\ \frac{2027}{4052}\right]}$.

Solution 2 (Exchanging the variables)

Let $g=f\circ f$, $c=g(1)>0$, and write $E(x,y)$ for the condition $g(x)+\frac1y=\bigl(g(xy)+1\bigr)f(y)$ ($x>0$, $y>1$).

Step 1: the form of $f$ on $(1,\infty)$. For $x,y>1$, the conditions $E(x,y)$ and $E(y,x)$ contain the same factor $g(xy)+1$, so \[\frac{g(x)+\frac1y}{f(y)}=\frac{g(y)+\frac1x}{f(x)},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad g(x)f(x)+\frac{f(x)}y=g(y)f(y)+\frac{f(y)}x .\] For $x>1$, $E(1,x)$ reads $c+\frac1x=(g(x)+1)f(x)$, i.e. $g(x)f(x)=c+\frac1x-f(x)$. Substituting this for $x$ and for $y$ and writing $u(t)=f(t)-\frac1t$, everything cancels except \[u(x)\Bigl(1-\frac1y\Bigr)=u(y)\Bigl(1-\frac1x\Bigr)\qquad(x,y>1).\] With $y=2$ this gives $u(x)=\lambda\bigl(1-\frac1x\bigr)$, where $\lambda=2u(2)$ is a constant. Hence, for $t>1$, \[f(t)=\lambda+\frac{1-\lambda}t,\qquad g(t)=\frac{c+\frac1t}{f(t)}-1=\frac{ct+1}{\lambda t+1-\lambda}-1 .\] Step 2: two possibilities. Put these formulas into $E(x,y)$ for $x,y>1$ (then $xy>1$ too). Clearing the (positive) denominators, the difference of the two sides becomes \[-\lambda\,xy\,(x-1)(y-1)\bigl(\lambda(c+1)-c\bigr)=0 ,\] so $\lambda=0$ or $\lambda=\frac c{c+1}$.

  • If $\lambda=0$, then $f(t)=\frac1t$ for $t>1$, so $f(2026)=\frac1{2026}$; and $f(x)=\frac1x$ is indeed a solution ($g(x)=x$ and $x+\frac1y=\frac{xy+1}y$).

  • If $\lambda=\frac c{c+1}$, then $f(t)=\frac{c+1/t}{c+1}$ for $t>1$, and $\lambda t+1-\lambda=\frac{ct+1}{c+1}$ gives $g(t)=c$ for $t>1$. For any $x>0$, take $y>\max\bigl(1,\frac1x\bigr)$; then $xy>1$ and $E(x,y)$ reads $g(x)+\frac1y=(c+1)f(y)=c+\frac1y$, so $g(x)=c$. Thus $g\equiv c$.

Step 3: the constant case. Let $g\equiv c$. Since $c=f(f(x))$ is a value of $f$, also $f(c)=f(f(f(x)))=g(f(x))=c$. If $c>1$, the formula $f(t)=\frac{c+1/t}{c+1}$ ($t>1$) gives $\frac{c+1/c}{c+1}=c$, i.e. $c+\frac1c=c^2+c$, i.e. $c^3=1$, a contradiction. So $0<c\le1$ and $f(2026)=\frac{c+1/2026}{c+1}$. Conversely, for every $c\in(0,1]$ the function $f(y)=\frac{c+1/y}{c+1}$ $(y>1)$, $f(y)=c$ $(0<y\le1)$ is a solution: for $y>1$, $f(y)\in\bigl(\frac c{c+1},1\bigr)\subset(0,1)$, so $f(f(y))=c$; for $y\le1$, $f(f(y))=f(c)=c$ as $c\le1$. So $g\equiv c$, and $E(x,y)$ reads $c+\frac1y=(c+1)\frac{c+1/y}{c+1}$, which is true.

The set of values. The map $c\mapsto\frac{c+1/2026}{c+1}$ is continuous and increasing on $(0,1]$ (its derivative is $\frac{1-1/2026}{(c+1)^2}>0$), with limit $\frac1{2026}$ at $0$ and value $\frac{2027}{4052}$ at $1$. Together with $\frac1{2026}$ from $\lambda=0$, the possible values of $f(2026)$ form the interval $\boxed{\left[\frac1{2026},\ \frac{2027}{4052}\right]}$.
