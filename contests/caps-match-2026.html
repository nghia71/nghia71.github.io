Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

In a sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ of $100$ mutually different real numbers, we say that an index $i\in\{2,3,\dots,99\}$ is good if \[a_1+\dots+a_{i-1}=a_{i+1}+\dots+a_{100}.\] What is the largest possible number of good indices?

Show the answer

$66$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Consecutive good indices and an explicit sequence)

For $2\le i\le 99$ let $D_i=(a_1+\dots+a_{i-1})-(a_{i+1}+\dots+a_{100})$; the index $i$ is good iff $D_i=0$. Moving from $i$ to $i+1$, the term $a_i$ joins the left sum and $a_{i+1}$ leaves the right sum, so \[ D_{i+1}-D_i=a_i+a_{i+1}. \]

Upper bound. If $i$, $i+1$, $i+2$ were all good, then $a_i+a_{i+1}=0=a_{i+1}+a_{i+2}$, so $a_i=a_{i+2}$, contradicting distinctness. Split the $98$ candidates into $32$ blocks $\{2,3,4\},\{5,6,7\},\dots,\{95,96,97\}$ and the block $\{98,99\}$. Each block of three contains at most $2$ good indices, so there are at most $32\cdot 2+2=66$ good indices.

Example with $66$. Let \[ a_{3j+1}=2j+3\ (0\le j\le 33),\qquad a_{3j+2}=c_j,\ \ a_{3j+3}=-c_j\ (0\le j\le 32),\qquad c_j=1226-2(j+2)^2 . \] Since $a_{3j+2}+a_{3j+3}=0$, we have $D_{3j+3}=D_{3j+2}$, so it suffices to check $D_{3j+2}=0$. The pairs cancel in all sums, so the first $3j+1$ terms add up to $\sum_{t=0}^{j}(2t+3)=(j+2)^2-1$, and the total is $\sum_{t=0}^{33}(2t+3)=35^2-1=1224$. Hence \[ D_{3j+2}=\bigl((j+2)^2-1\bigr)-\bigl(1224-((j+2)^2-1)-c_j\bigr)=2(j+2)^2-1226+c_j=0 . \] So all $66$ indices $i\not\equiv 1\pmod 3$ with $2\le i\le 99$ are good. The terms are distinct: the $a_{3j+1}$ are distinct odd numbers; the $c_j$ are even, nonzero ($613$ is not a square) and strictly decreasing; and $c_j=-c_l$ would mean $(j+2)^2+(l+2)^2=1226$ with $2\le j+2,\,l+2\le 34$. This is impossible: the larger square would be at least $613$, so the larger base would be at least $25$, but $1226-25^2,\dots,1226-34^2$ are $601,550,497,442,385,326,265,202,137,70$, none of which is a square.

The largest possible number of good indices is $\boxed{66}$.

Solution 2 (Prefix sums that alternate in sign)

Let $s_0=0$ and $s_i=a_1+a_2+\dots+a_i$ for $1\le i\le 100$, so $a_i=s_i-s_{i-1}$. The left sum in the definition is $s_{i-1}$ and the right sum is $s_{100}-s_i$, so the index $i$ is good if and only if \[ s_{i-1}+s_i=s_{100}. \]

Upper bound. If $i$, $i+1$, $i+2$ were all good, then $s_{i-1}+s_i=s_i+s_{i+1}=s_{i+1}+s_{i+2}$, so $s_{i-1}=s_{i+1}$ and $s_i=s_{i+2}$. Hence $a_i+a_{i+1}=s_{i+1}-s_{i-1}=0$ and $a_{i+1}+a_{i+2}=s_{i+2}-s_i=0$, which give $a_i=a_{i+2}$, contradicting distinctness. So no three consecutive indices are good. Split the $98$ candidates $2,3,\dots,99$ into the $32$ blocks $\{2,3,4\},\{5,6,7\},\dots,\{95,96,97\}$ and the block $\{98,99\}$. Each block of three contains at most $2$ good indices, so there are at most $32\cdot2+2=66$ good indices.

Example with $66$. We prescribe the prefix sums and aim at total $s_{100}=0$, so that $i$ is good exactly when $s_{i-1}=-s_i$. For $j=0,1,\dots,32$ put \[ s_{3j+1}=10^j,\qquad s_{3j+2}=-10^j,\qquad s_{3j+3}=10^j, \] and $s_0=s_{100}=0$; this defines $s_1,\dots,s_{99}$, and we set $a_i=s_i-s_{i-1}$. Then $s_{3j+1}+s_{3j+2}=0$ and $s_{3j+2}+s_{3j+3}=0$, so the indices $3j+2$ and $3j+3$ ($0\le j\le 32$), that is, all $66$ indices $i\not\equiv1\pmod 3$ with $2\le i\le 99$, are good. The terms are \[ a_1=1,\qquad a_{100}=-10^{32},\qquad a_{3j+2}=-2\cdot10^j,\quad a_{3j+3}=2\cdot10^j\quad(0\le j\le32), \] and $a_{3j+4}=s_{3j+4}-s_{3j+3}=9\cdot10^j$ for $0\le j\le31$. They are distinct: the positive terms are $1$, the numbers $2\cdot10^j$ and the numbers $9\cdot10^j$, which differ in their leading digit or in their number of digits; the negative terms are $-2\cdot10^j$ and $-10^{32}$, which differ in their leading digit.

The largest possible number of good indices is $\boxed{66}$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes

A positive integer $k$ is called divisive if there exist only finitely many quadruples $(x,y,z,w)$ of positive integers satisfying \[x^2+y^2+z^2+w^2=xyzw\] and \[k\nmid x^2+y^2+z^2+w^2.\] Find all divisive positive integers.

Show the answer

$k\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (All entries are even; a Vieta family modulo $32$ and modulo $p$)

Write $S=x^2+y^2+z^2+w^2=xyzw$ for a solution.

All entries are even. If all four were odd, then $S\equiv 4\pmod 8$ while $xyzw$ is odd. Otherwise $xyzw$ is even, so the number of odd entries is even ($S$ is even); if it is $2$, then $S\equiv 2\pmod 4$ while $xyzw$ has two even factors and is divisible by $4$. Hence there are no odd entries, and $16\mid xyzw=S$ for every solution. So every divisor of $16$ is divisive (there are no quadruples at all with $k\nmid S$).

A family. Let $b_1=b_2=1$, $b_{n+1}=4b_n-b_{n-1}$: $1,1,3,11,41,153,\dots$. We claim $b_n^2+b_{n+1}^2+2=4b_nb_{n+1}$. It holds for $n=1$. If it holds for $n-1$, then $b_{n-1}$ is a root of $X^2-4b_nX+b_n^2+2=0$; the other root is $4b_n-b_{n-1}=b_{n+1}$ (Vieta), which gives the claim for $n$. Multiplying by $4$, \[ (2,\,2,\,2b_n,\,2b_{n+1})\ \text{is a solution with}\ S_n=16\,b_nb_{n+1}. \] The $b_n$ ($n\ge2$) are strictly increasing ($b_{n+1}-b_n=3b_n-b_{n-1}>0$), so these are infinitely many different quadruples.

$32\mid k$ is not divisive. All $b_n$ are odd ($b_{n+1}\equiv b_{n-1}\pmod 2$), so $32\nmid S_n$ and hence $k\nmid S_n$ for infinitely many quadruples.

$k$ with an odd prime factor $p$ is not divisive. The pairs $(b_n,b_{n+1})\bmod p$ determine each other in both directions ($b_{n+1}=4b_n-b_{n-1}$, $b_{n-1}=4b_n-b_{n+1}$), and there are finitely many pairs, so the sequence of pairs is purely periodic. As $(b_1,b_2)\equiv(1,1)$, infinitely many $n$ have $b_nb_{n+1}\equiv1\pmod p$; for them $p\nmid 16b_nb_{n+1}=S_n$, so $k\nmid S_n$.

Every $k$ that does not divide $16$ has an odd prime factor or is divisible by $32$. Hence the divisive integers are exactly $\boxed{1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,16}$.

Solution 2 (Exact power of two by descent; coprime consecutive terms)

Every solution has $S=16\cdot(\text{odd})$. Let $S=x^2+y^2+z^2+w^2=xyzw$ and let $t$ be the number of odd entries; then $S\equiv t\pmod 4$. If $t=4$, then $S\equiv 4\pmod 8$ but $xyzw$ is odd. If $1\le t\le 3$, then $xyzw$ is even, so $t$ is even, $t=2$, and $S\equiv 2\pmod 4$ while $4\mid xyzw$. So $t=0$ and $(x,y,z,w)=2(x',y',z',w')$ with \[ x'^2+y'^2+z'^2+w'^2=4x'y'z'w'. \tag{1} \] Claim: for $j\ge 2$ the equation $X^2+Y^2+Z^2+W^2=4^jXYZW$ has no positive solutions. If some entry is odd, the right side is divisible by $16$, so the number of odd entries is $\equiv 0\pmod 4$, i.e. all four are odd, and then the left side is $\equiv4\pmod 8$: impossible. So all entries are even, and halving them gives a smaller solution of the equation with $4^{j+1}$. Repeating, we would get an infinite decreasing sequence of positive integers. Now in (1), if some entry were even, the right side would be divisible by $8$, the number of odd entries would be $0$ or $4$, hence $0$, and halving would give a solution with $4^2$, impossible by the claim. So $x',y',z',w'$ are all odd and $S=16\,x'y'z'w'$ with $x'y'z'w'$ odd.

Consequently every divisor $k$ of $16$ divides every $S$ (divisive), and for $32\mid k$ no solution has $k\mid S$. There are infinitely many solutions, all with $32\nmid S$ (see the family below), so $32\mid k$ is not divisive.

A family. Let $b_1=b_2=1$ and $b_{n+1}=4b_n-b_{n-1}$. Then $b_n^2+b_{n+1}^2+2=4b_nb_{n+1}$: this is true for $n=1$, and if it is true for $n-1$, then $b_{n-1}$ is a root of $X^2-4b_nX+b_n^2+2$, whose other root is $4b_n-b_{n-1}=b_{n+1}$ (Vieta). Multiplying by $4$, the quadruples $(2,2,2b_n,2b_{n+1})$ are solutions, starting with $(2,2,2,2)$, with $S_n=16b_nb_{n+1}$; since $b_{n+1}-b_n=3b_n-b_{n-1}>0$ for $n\ge2$, they are infinitely many different solutions.

Odd primes. By the identity, a common divisor of $b_n,b_{n+1}$ divides $2$, and the $b_n$ are odd ($b_{n+1}\equiv b_{n-1}\pmod 2$), so $\gcd(b_n,b_{n+1})=1$. Let $p$ be an odd prime and suppose $p\mid b_nb_{n+1}$ for all $n\ge n_0$. Take $n\ge n_0$ with $p\mid b_n$ (one of two consecutive terms is divisible by $p$). By coprimality $p\nmid b_{n+1}$, so $p\mid b_{n+2}$; but $b_{n+2}=4b_{n+1}-b_n\equiv 4b_{n+1}\not\equiv 0\pmod p$. This contradiction gives infinitely many $n$ with $p\nmid S_n$, so no multiple of $p$ is divisive.

Therefore the divisive integers are exactly $\boxed{1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,16}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $ABCD$ be a quadrilateral possessing an incenter $I$. Points $K$ and $L$ are chosen on the segments $IA$ and $IC$, respectively, such that $CK\perp ID$ and $AL\perp IB$. Points $P$ and $Q$ are chosen on the segments $AD$ and $BC$, respectively, such that \[\angle AKP=\angle ICD\qquad\text{and}\qquad\angle CLQ=\angle IAB.\] Prove that $IP=IQ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A concyclicity lemma and a symmetric formula)

Let $r$ be the inradius and $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ the half-angles at $A,B,C,D$, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=180^\circ$, $\angle IAB=\angle IAD=\alpha$, $\angle ICB=\angle ICD=\gamma$, $IA=r/\sin\alpha$, $IC=r/\sin\gamma$. In triangle $AIB$ the angle at $I$ is $180^\circ-\alpha-\beta$, and similarly $\angle BIC=180^\circ-\beta-\gamma$, $\angle CID=180^\circ-\gamma-\delta=\alpha+\beta$, $\angle DIA=180^\circ-\delta-\alpha=\beta+\gamma$.

Let $T$, $U$ be the points where the incircle touches $AD$ and $BC$; then $AT=r\cot\alpha$, $CU=r\cot\gamma$, $IT\perp AD$, $IU\perp BC$. We measure $AP$, $AT$ from $A$ along ray $AD$ and $CQ$, $CU$ from $C$ along ray $CB$, so that $TP=|AP-AT|$ and $UQ=|CQ-CU|$; hence \[ IP^2=r^2+(AP-AT)^2,\qquad IQ^2=r^2+(CQ-CU)^2 . \] It suffices to show $AP-AT=CQ-CU$.

Lemma: $IK\cdot IA=IL\cdot IC$. Since $CK\perp ID$, the points $K$ and $C$ have the same orthogonal projection onto line $ID$; measuring along the ray $ID$, $IK\cos\angle AID=IC\cos\angle CID$, i.e. $IK\cos(\beta+\gamma)=IC\cos(\alpha+\beta)$. Here $\cos(\beta+\gamma)\ne0$: otherwise $IA\perp ID$ and the perpendicular from $C$ to $ID$ would be parallel to $IA$ and could not meet it (unless $C$ lies on line $IA$; then $ABCD$ is symmetric about $AC$ and has $AB\parallel CD$, so it is a rhombus, $K$ and $L$ are not determined by the conditions, and the statement fails, for example for a square; we exclude this case). In the same way $AL\perp IB$ gives $IL\cos\angle CIB=IA\cos\angle AIB$; since $\angle CIB=180^\circ-(\beta+\gamma)$ and $\angle AIB=180^\circ-(\alpha+\beta)$, this reads $-IL\cos(\beta+\gamma)=-IA\cos(\alpha+\beta)$. Hence \[ m:=IK\cdot IA=IL\cdot IC=\frac{IA\cdot IC\,\cos(\alpha+\beta)}{\cos(\beta+\gamma)} . \] (So $A,C,K,L$ are concyclic.)

Computing $AP$. $K$ lies on segment $IA$, so in triangle $AKP$ the angle at $A$ is $\angle IAD=\alpha$, the angle at $K$ is $\gamma$, and the angle at $P$ is $180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma$. By the law of sines and $AK=IA-IK=(IA^2-m)/IA$, \[ AP=\frac{AK\sin\gamma}{\sin(\alpha+\gamma)}=\frac{(r^2-m\sin^2\alpha)\sin\gamma}{r\sin\alpha\,\sin(\alpha+\gamma)} . \] Using $\sin\gamma-\cos\alpha\sin(\alpha+\gamma)=-\sin\alpha\cos(\alpha+\gamma)$ (expand $\sin\gamma=\sin((\alpha+\gamma)-\alpha)$), \[ AP-AT=\frac{r^2\bigl(\sin\gamma-\cos\alpha\sin(\alpha+\gamma)\bigr)-m\sin^2\alpha\sin\gamma}{r\sin\alpha\sin(\alpha+\gamma)} =-\,\frac{r^2\cos(\alpha+\gamma)+m\sin\alpha\sin\gamma}{r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)} . \]

Computing $CQ$. Triangle $CLQ$ has angle $\gamma$ at $C$ and $\alpha$ at $L$, and $CL=(IC^2-m)/IC$ by the lemma. The same computation with $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ exchanged gives \[ CQ-CU=-\,\frac{r^2\cos(\gamma+\alpha)+m\sin\gamma\sin\alpha}{r\sin(\gamma+\alpha)} . \] The two expressions are equal, so $IP=IQ$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Contact chords and a circle through $I$, $E$, $F$)

Let the incircle (centre $I$, radius $r$) touch $AB$, $BC$, $CD$, $DA$ at $S$, $U$, $V$, $T$, and let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ be the half-angles at $A,B,C,D$, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=180^\circ$. Label the quadrilateral so that $A,B,C,D$ (hence also $S,U,V,T$) are in counterclockwise order. We write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for the angle of the counterclockwise rotation taking line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$.

The line from the centre to the meeting point of two tangents is perpendicular to the chord of contact: \[ IA\perp TS,\qquad IB\perp SU,\qquad IC\perp UV,\qquad ID\perp VT. \] Hence $CK\parallel VT$ and $AL\parallel SU$.

The case $AD\parallel BC$. Then $\alpha+\beta=\gamma+\delta=90^\circ$, so $\angle CID=\angle AIB=90^\circ$ and $K=L=I$. Now $\angle AIP=\gamma$ and $\angle CIQ=\alpha$, so the exterior angles of triangles $AIP$ and $CIQ$ are $\angle IPD=\alpha+\gamma=\angle IQB$. Since $I$ is at distance $r$ from both $AD$ and $BC$, we get $IP=r/\sin(\alpha+\gamma)=IQ$.

The general case. Now $AD\nparallel BC$; let $F=AD\cap BC$. Then $\angle CID\ne90^\circ$, so $K\ne I$, and likewise $L\ne I$. Also $AB\nparallel CD$: otherwise $\angle AID=90^\circ$, so $CK\parallel IA$, which is impossible for a point $K$ of $IA$ (unless $C$ lies on $IA$, when $K$ is not determined; we exclude this case). Let $E=AB\cap CD$. As before, $IE\perp SV$ and $IF\perp UT$.

Step 1: $E,C,I,K$ are concyclic, and $E,A,I,L$ are concyclic. Using $KI=IA\perp TS$ and $KC\parallel VT$, \[ \measuredangle(KI,KC)=\measuredangle(IA,TS)+\measuredangle(TS,TV)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(TS,TV). \] Using $EI\perp SV$ and the tangent–chord theorem at $V$ (the tangent is $CD$), $\measuredangle(VS,CD)=\measuredangle(TS,TV)$, so \[ \measuredangle(EI,EC)=\measuredangle(EI,SV)+\measuredangle(VS,CD)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(TS,TV). \] The two angles are equal, so $E,C,I,K$ lie on a circle. In the same way, with $LI=IC\perp UV$, $LA\parallel SU$ and the tangent $AB$ at $S$, \[ \measuredangle(LI,LA)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(UV,US)=\measuredangle(EI,SV)+\measuredangle(SV,AB)=\measuredangle(EI,EA), \] so $E,A,I,L$ lie on a circle.

Step 2: $P$ lies on line $EK$ and $Q$ lies on line $EL$. From the first circle, $\measuredangle(KA,KE)=\measuredangle(KI,KE)=\measuredangle(CI,CE)=\measuredangle(CI,CD)$. At the vertex $C$ of the counterclockwise quadrilateral, ray $CI$ is ray $CD$ turned counterclockwise by $\gamma$, so $\measuredangle(CI,CD)=-\gamma$. On the other hand ray $AP=AD$ is ray $AK=AI$ turned counterclockwise by $\alpha$, so triangle $AKP$ is counterclockwise, and its angle at $K$, which equals $\gamma$ by hypothesis, is swept counterclockwise from ray $KP$ to ray $KA$: $\measuredangle(KA,KP)=-\gamma$. Hence the lines $KE$ and $KP$ coincide. Similarly $\measuredangle(LC,LE)=\measuredangle(LI,LE)=\measuredangle(AI,AE)=\measuredangle(AI,AB)=-\alpha$, and triangle $CLQ$ is counterclockwise (ray $CQ=CB$ is ray $CL=CI$ turned counterclockwise by $\gamma$) with angle $\alpha$ at $L$, so $\measuredangle(LC,LQ)=-\alpha$ and $Q$ lies on $EL$.

Step 3: $\measuredangle(IE,IF)=\alpha+\gamma$. Since $IE\perp SV$ and $IF\perp UT$, this angle equals $\measuredangle(SV,UT)$. The chords $SV$, $UT$ are the diagonals of the convex quadrilateral $SUVT$; let them meet at $G$. By the exterior angle of triangle $GST$ and the inscribed angle theorem, \[ \angle VGT=\angle VST+\angle UTS=\tfrac12\angle VIT+\tfrac12\angle UIS=(90^\circ-\delta)+(90^\circ-\beta)=\alpha+\gamma, \] and ray $GT$ is ray $GV$ turned counterclockwise by this angle. So $\measuredangle(SV,UT)=\alpha+\gamma$.

Step 4: $P$ and $Q$ lie on the circle $\omega$ through $E$, $I$, $F$. (These three points are not collinear by Step 3.) Triangle $AKP$ is counterclockwise with angle $180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma$ at $P$, swept counterclockwise from ray $PA$ to ray $PK$; therefore $\measuredangle(PE,PF)=\measuredangle(PK,PA)=\alpha+\gamma=\measuredangle(IE,IF)$, and $P$ lies on $\omega$. Triangle $CLQ$ is counterclockwise with angle $180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma$ at $Q$, swept from ray $QC$ to ray $QL$; therefore $\measuredangle(QE,QF)=\measuredangle(QL,QC)=\alpha+\gamma$, and $Q$ lies on $\omega$.

Step 5. The point $I$ is equidistant from the lines $FP=AD$ and $FQ=BC$, so line $FI$ makes the same angle $\theta$ with both. If $\rho$ is the radius of $\omega$, the chords $IP$ and $IQ$ are seen from $F$ under the angle $\theta$ (or $180^\circ-\theta$), so $IP=2\rho\sin\theta=IQ$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Tom and Jerry play the following game on a $16\times 16$ chessboard. Jerry starts by selecting some, but not all, of the squares, and placing one red pebble on each of the selected squares. Tom then places his one black pebble on an empty square and chooses whether he wants to be the hunter or the prey. After that, Tom and Jerry alternate turns with Jerry starting. In each turn, a player chooses one of his pebbles and moves it to a square sharing an edge with the current square. If such a turn would move the pebble outside of the board, the pebble appears on the other side of the board (i.e. if the pebble is on the left border, it appears in the same row on the right border and similarly for the other cases). If at any point in the game a pebble of the hunter is on the same square as a pebble of the prey, the hunter wins and the game ends. An infinite game is a win for the prey. Which of the two players has a winning strategy?

Show the answer

Jerry wins

Show the solution

Solution (One placement that works for both roles)

Jerry has a winning strategy.

Use coordinates $(x,y)$ modulo $16$ ($x$ = column, $y$ = row); neighbours differ by $1$ in one coordinate (cyclically). Split the board into sixteen $4\times4$ blocks; the centre of a block is its middle $2\times2$ square $\{4i+1,4i+2\}\times\{4j+1,4j+2\}$. Jerry places one red pebble on the cell $(4i+1,4j+1)$ of every block ($16$ pebbles, not all squares).

A capture can happen only on a move that brings a hunter pebble onto the prey (or the prey onto a hunter, which a sensible prey never does). So the hunter can capture on his turn exactly when one of his pebbles is adjacent to a prey pebble.

Tom chooses to be the hunter. Jerry keeps every red pebble inside the centre of its block. Two facts:

  • (a) Cells of two different centres are at distance at least $3$ (in each coordinate the centres use residues $1,2$ mod $4$). Hence no square is equal or adjacent to cells of two different centres, so at any moment at most one red pebble is adjacent to Tom.

  • (b) If Tom's square $t$ is adjacent to a red pebble on $r$, the two neighbours of $r$ inside its centre are diagonal to each other. If $t$ is one of them, the other one is neither $t$ nor adjacent to $t$. If $t$ is outside the centre, neither of them is adjacent to $t$ (a square adjacent to both $r$ and a neighbour of $r$ would form a triangle, and the grid has no triangles).

Jerry's move: if a red pebble is adjacent to Tom, move it as in (b); otherwise move a red pebble at distance at least $3$ from Tom (one exists: only $13$ squares are within distance $2$ of Tom, and there are $16$ pebbles) to a neighbouring cell of its centre, which is at distance at least $2$ from Tom. After Jerry's move no red pebble is adjacent to Tom (by (a) at most one was, and it was moved away), and Jerry never lands on Tom. So Tom can never capture, the game is infinite, and the prey (Jerry) wins.

Tom chooses to be the prey. Call a square unsafe if it carries a red pebble or is adjacent to one. Jerry's rule: whenever a red pebble is adjacent to Tom, capture. Hence, as long as the game goes on, after each of Tom's moves Tom stands on a square that is safe for the current position of the red pebbles. Otherwise Jerry follows a fixed schedule of single steps.

Phase 1 (building two walls). Move the pebbles, one step at a time and one pebble after another, along fixed paths, each going first horizontally and then vertically (so no two red pebbles ever share a square): from $(1,4j+1)$ to $(0,4j)$, from $(5,4j+1)$ to $(0,4j+2)$, from $(9,4j+1)$ to $(8,4j)$ and from $(13,4j+1)$ to $(8,4j+2)$. If a step would land on Tom, the pebble is adjacent to Tom and Jerry captures instead, so the schedule can always be carried out. Afterwards the pebbles stand on the even rows of columns $0$ and $8$, and every square of these two columns is unsafe. So Tom is in columns $1$–$7$ or $9$–$15$; by symmetry (shift by $8$ columns) say $1$–$7$; in the other case the wall of column $8$ is swept towards column $15$ in the same way.

Phase 2 (sweeping). For $s=0,1,\dots,6$ in turn, move the wall from column $s$ to column $s+1$: move the pebbles $(s,0),(s,2),\dots, (s,14)$, in this order, one step to the right. After $k$ of these $8$ steps ($0\le k\le 8$) the pebbles of this wall stand on $(s+1,2t)$ for $t<k$ and on $(s,2t)$ for $t\ge k$. Directly from this description:

  • column $s+1$, even rows, and column $s$, even rows, are unsafe;

  • $(s+1,2t+1)$ is safe only if $t\ge k$ and ($t\le 6$ or $k=0$) (otherwise it touches $(s+1,2t)$ or $(s+1,2t+2)$);

  • $(s,2t+1)$ is safe only if $t\le k-2$, or $t=7$ and $k=8$ (it must not touch $(s,2t)$ or $(s,2t+2)$).

Claim: during stage $s$, Tom is always in a column from $s+1$ to $7$. Column $8$ is always unsafe, so Tom can leave the strip only by stepping from column $s+1$ into column $s$, from some $(s+1,2t+1)$, safe when the wall had $k-1$ moved pebbles, to $(s,2t+1)$, safe after the $k$-th step. The two conditions above give $t\ge k-1$ and ($t\le6$ or $k=1$), together with $t\le k-2$ or ($t=7$, $k=8$); these are incompatible. At the end of stage $s$ column $s+1$ is entirely unsafe, so Tom is then in columns $s+2$ to $7$.

After stage $6$ the wall stands in column $7$, so no safe square is left between the walls; Tom must have been captured before. Phase 1 and phase 2 together take $64+56=120$ steps, so the hunter wins after finitely many moves.

Since Jerry wins whichever role Tom chooses, Jerry has a winning strategy.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $M$ be the midpoint of side $AD$ of a cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$. Points $K$ and $L$ are chosen on the rays $MB$ and $MC$ respectively such that \[\angle DAK+\angle ACM=\angle ABM\] and \[\angle LDA+\angle MBD=\angle MCD.\] Prove that the points $B$, $C$, $K$, and $L$ lie on one circle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Law of sines and distances to an auxiliary line)

Let $\Gamma$ (radius $R$) be the circumcircle; $ABCD$ is convex with $M$ on segment $AD$, so at $B$ the rays $BA,BM,BD,BC$ come in this order, and at $C$ the rays $CB,CA,CM,CD$.

The two given angles are equal. Let $\varphi=\angle DAK=\angle ABM-\angle ACM$. Since $\angle ABD=\angle ACD$ (inscribed, on arc $AD$), \[ \angle ABM+\angle MBD=\angle ACM+\angle MCD\ \Longrightarrow\ \angle LDA=\angle MCD-\angle MBD=\angle ABM-\angle ACM=\varphi . \] Moreover $\varphi>0$. By the law of sines in triangles $ABM$, $DBM$ (with $AM=DM$) and $ABD$, \[ \frac{\sin\angle ABM}{\sin\angle MBD}=\frac{\sin\angle BAD}{\sin\angle BDA}=\frac{BD}{AB},\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad \frac{\sin\angle ACM}{\sin\angle MCD}=\frac{CD}{AC}. \] Both pairs of angles have the same sum $\omega=\angle ABD=\angle ACD$, and $x\mapsto\sin x/\sin(\omega-x)$ is increasing on $(0,\omega)$, so $\angle ABM>\angle ACM$ is equivalent to $AC\cdot BD>AB\cdot CD$, which is true by Ptolemy's theorem ($AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+AD\cdot BC$). Since also $\theta+\varphi<180^\circ$ and $\theta'+\varphi<180^\circ$ (see below), the points $K$ and $L$ exist and are unique.

Reduction. Let $\theta=\angle AMB$ and $\theta'=\angle DMC$. In triangle $AMK$ the angles at $A$ and $M$ are $\varphi$ and $\theta$, so by the law of sines $MK=MA\sin\varphi/\sin(\theta+\varphi)$; likewise $ML=MD\sin\varphi/\sin(\theta'+\varphi)$. Since $K$, $L$ lie on the rays $MB$, $MC$, the points $B,C,K,L$ are concyclic iff $MK\cdot MB=ML\cdot MC$ (if $K=B$, then $L=C$ and there is nothing to prove; otherwise $MK:MC=ML:MB$, triangles $MKL$ and $MCB$ are similar, and $\angle MKL=\angle MCB$). With $MA=MD$ this becomes \[ MB\sin(\theta'+\varphi)=MC\sin(\theta+\varphi). \tag{$*$} \]

The angles. From triangle $ABM$, $\theta=180^\circ-\angle BAD-\angle ABM$, so, using $\angle BAD=180^\circ-\angle BCD$, \[ \theta+\varphi=\angle BCD-\angle ACM=\angle BCA+\angle MCD,\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad \theta'+\varphi=\angle ABM+\angle DBC . \]

An auxiliary line. Let $b$ be the line through $B$ obtained by rotating ray $BA$ away from $BD$ by the angle $\angle DBC$. The angle between ray $BM$ and this ray is $\angle ABM+\angle DBC=\theta'+\varphi$, and between ray $BD$ and it is $\angle ABD+\angle DBC=\angle ABC<180^\circ$; so neither ray of $b$ enters the angle $ABD$, and $A$, $D$ lie on the same side of $b$. As $M$ is the midpoint of $AD$, \[ 2\,MB\sin(\theta'+\varphi)=2\,\mathrm{dist}(M,b)=\mathrm{dist}(A,b)+\mathrm{dist}(D,b)=AB\sin\angle DBC+DB\sin\angle ABC . \] The same construction at $C$ (exchange $A\leftrightarrow D$, $B\leftrightarrow C$) gives \[ 2\,MC\sin(\theta+\varphi)=DC\sin\angle ACB+AC\sin\angle DCB . \] By the extended law of sines in $\Gamma$: $AB=2R\sin\angle ACB$, $DB=2R\sin\angle DCB$, $DC=2R\sin\angle DBC$, $AC=2R\sin\angle ABC$. Both right-hand sides equal $2R\bigl(\sin\angle ACB\sin\angle DBC+\sin\angle DCB\sin\angle ABC\bigr)$, which proves $(*)$. Hence $B$, $C$, $K$, $L$ lie on one circle. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Two auxiliary circles and the butterfly theorem)

As in Solution 1, $ABCD$ is convex, $\angle ABD=\angle ACD$, and therefore \[ \varphi:=\angle DAK=\angle ABM-\angle ACM=\angle MCD-\angle MBD=\angle LDA ; \] by Solution 1 (Ptolemy's theorem), $\varphi>0$. We write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let the lines $CM$ and $BM$ meet the circumcircle $\Gamma$ again at $E$ and $F$; both points lie on the arc $AD$ not containing $B$ and $C$.

The points $X$ and $Y$. The inscribed angles on arc $AE$ give $\angle ABE=\angle ACE=\angle ACM<\angle ABM$, so ray $BE$ runs inside angle $ABM$ and meets segment $AM$ at an interior point $X$, with \[ \angle XBM=\angle ABM-\angle ACM=\varphi . \] In the same way $\angle DCF=\angle DBF=\angle MBD<\angle MCD$, so ray $CF$ meets segment $DM$ at an interior point $Y$ with $\angle YCM=\varphi$.

$A,X,K,B$ are concyclic. Place the figure with $AD$ horizontal, $A$ on the left, and $B$, $C$ above $AD$. The point $K$ is on ray $MB$, hence above $AD$, so line $AK$ is line $AX$ turned counterclockwise about $A$ by $\angle DAK=\varphi$. Seen from $B$, the point $X$ is to the left of $M$, so line $BK=BM$ is line $BX$ turned counterclockwise about $B$ by $\angle XBM=\varphi$. Thus the directed angles $\measuredangle(AX,AK)$ and $\measuredangle(BX,BK)$ are equal, and $A$, $X$, $K$, $B$ lie on one circle (if $K=B$, the equality says that $MB$ is tangent to circle $AXB$). The power of $M$ with respect to this circle, computed along $MA$ and along $MB$, gives \[ MK\cdot MB=MX\cdot MA . \] The mirror-image argument at $D$ and $C$ (line $DL$ is line $DY$ turned clockwise by $\varphi$, and line $CM$ is line $CY$ turned clockwise by $\varphi$) shows that $D,Y,L,C$ are concyclic, so \[ ML\cdot MC=MY\cdot MD . \]

The butterfly. The chords $BF$ and $CE$ of $\Gamma$ pass through the midpoint $M$ of the chord $AD$, and the lines $BE$ and $CF$ meet $AD$ at $X$ and $Y$. By the butterfly theorem, $MX=MY$. Together with $MA=MD$, \[ MK\cdot MB=MX\cdot MA=MY\cdot MD=ML\cdot MC . \] Since $K$ and $L$ lie on the rays $MB$ and $MC$, the converse of the power-of-a-point theorem shows that $B$, $C$, $K$, $L$ lie on one circle. $\blacksquare$

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer and let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ be non-zero real numbers such that \[a_1+\frac{1}{a_2},\ a_2+\frac{1}{a_3},\ \dots,\ a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{a_n},\ a_n+\frac{1}{a_1}\] are all integers. Prove that \[a_1a_2\cdots a_n+\frac{1}{a_1a_2\cdots a_n}\] is also an integer.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An eigenvector of a product of integer matrices)

Let $m_i=a_i+\frac1{a_{i+1}}\in\mathbb Z$ (indices modulo $n$), $P=a_1a_2\cdots a_n$, and \[ M_i=\begin{pmatrix} m_i & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_i=\begin{pmatrix} a_i\\ 1\end{pmatrix}. \] Each $M_i$ has integer entries and determinant $1$. Since $a_i=m_i-\frac1{a_{i+1}}$, \[ M_iv_{i+1}=\begin{pmatrix} m_ia_{i+1}-1\\ a_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}=a_{i+1}\begin{pmatrix} a_i\\ 1\end{pmatrix}=a_{i+1}v_i . \] Let $W=M_1M_2\cdots M_n$. Using $v_{n+1}=v_1$ and applying the relation $n$ times, \[ Wv_1=M_1\cdots M_{n-1}(a_1v_n)=a_1a_n\,M_1\cdots M_{n-2}v_{n-1}=\dots=a_1a_na_{n-1}\cdots a_2\,v_1=P\,v_1 . \] As $v_1\ne 0$, $P$ is an eigenvalue of $W$. The matrix $W$ has integer entries and $\det W=1$, so its characteristic polynomial is $t^2-(\operatorname{tr}W)\,t+1$, and $P^2-(\operatorname{tr}W)P+1=0$. Dividing by $P\ne0$, \[ P+\frac1P=\operatorname{tr}W\in\mathbb Z . \qquad\blacksquare \]

Solution 2 (A domino expansion with cancellation in pairs)

Indices are taken modulo $n$. Let $s_i=a_i+\frac1{a_{i+1}}$ (an integer) and $P=a_1a_2\cdots a_n$. Arrange $1,2,\dots,n$ around a circle; a domino is a pair of neighbours $(i,i+1)$, $1\le i\le n$ (for $n=2$ the dominoes $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ count as different). A tiling is a set $\mathcal D$ of dominoes no two of which share a number; the numbers in no domino of $\mathcal D$ are free. We prove \[ P+\frac1P=\sum_{\mathcal D}(-1)^{|\mathcal D|}\prod_{i\ \mathrm{free}}s_i , \tag{1} \] the sum being over all tilings, including the empty one. The right-hand side is an integer, so (1) solves the problem. For example, $n=2$ gives $P+\frac1P=s_1s_2-2$ and $n=3$ gives $P+\frac1P=s_1s_2s_3-s_1-s_2-s_3$.

Expanding. In each product, every free $i$ contributes either $a_i$ (we mark $i$ with the letter $N$) or $\frac1{a_{i+1}}$ (letter $D$). So the right-hand side of (1) is the sum, over all configurations (a tiling together with a letter $N$ or $D$ on every free number), of the terms \[ (-1)^{|\mathcal D|}\prod_{i\ \mathrm{marked}\ N}a_i\prod_{i\ \mathrm{marked}\ D}\frac1{a_{i+1}} . \]

Pairing. Call the place $(i,i+1)$ active in a configuration if it is a domino, or if $i$ is marked $D$ and $i+1$ is marked $N$. Two active places never share a number: if $(i,i+1)$ is active, then $i+1$ is covered or marked $N$, so it is not the left end of another active place, and $i$ is covered or marked $D$, so it is not the right end of one. If a configuration has an active place, take the one with the smallest $i$ and switch it: replace the domino $(i,i+1)$ by the letters $D$ on $i$ and $N$ on $i+1$, or the other way round. Nothing changes outside $i$, $i+1$, so the set of active places stays the same and switching twice gives back the original configuration. The two configurations of a pair have the same product, because the letters $D$ on $i$ and $N$ on $i+1$ contribute $\frac1{a_{i+1}}\cdot a_{i+1}=1$, and opposite signs, because the number of dominoes changes by one. Hence all configurations with an active place cancel in pairs.

What is left. A configuration without active places has no domino, and no $D$ is followed by an $N$ around the circle. If both letters occurred, then going round the circle some $D$ would be followed by an $N$. So only two configurations remain: all letters $N$, with term $a_1a_2\cdots a_n=P$, and all letters $D$, with term $\frac1{a_2}\cdot\frac1{a_3}\cdots\frac1{a_1}=\frac1P$, both with sign $+$. This proves (1). $\blacksquare$
