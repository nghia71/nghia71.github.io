Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Piotrek is playing with pebbles. He starts with an empty stack. In $i$-th move, Piotrek removes $i$ pebbles from the stack if he can. If he can't, he adds $i$ pebbles to the stack. For example, after first $5$ moves, Piotrek has $1,3,0,4,9$ pebbles respectively. Find all positive integers $n$ such that Piotrek has $0$ pebbles after $n$ moves.

Show the answer

$n=\dfrac{3^k-3}{2}$ with $k\ge2$, i.e. $n=3,12,39,120,363,\dots$

Show the solution

Solution (From one empty stack to the next)

Let $s_i$ be the number of pebbles after move $i$, with $s_0=0$. In move $i$ Piotrek removes $i$ pebbles if $s_{i-1}\ge i$ and adds $i$ otherwise.

Claim. If $s_n=0$ for some $n\ge0$, then $s_i>0$ for $n<i<3n+3$ and $s_{3n+3}=0$.

Proof. Move $n+1$ adds: $s_{n+1}=n+1$. Move $n+2$ adds (as $n+1<n+2$): $s_{n+2}=2n+3$. We show by induction on $j=1,2,\dots,n+1$ that \[s_{n+2j}=2n+2+j\qquad\text{and}\qquad s_{n+2j+1}=n+1-j .\] For $j=1$ the first formula holds. If $s_{n+2j}=2n+2+j$ with $j\le n+1$, then $s_{n+2j}\ge n+2j+1$ (this is $j\le n+1$), so move $n+2j+1$ removes and $s_{n+2j+1}=2n+2+j-(n+2j+1)=n+1-j$. If moreover $j\le n$, then $s_{n+2j+1}=n+1-j<n+2j+2$, so move $n+2j+2$ adds and $s_{n+2j+2}=2n+3+j=2n+2+(j+1)$. Hence all values $s_{n+1},\dots,s_{3n+2}$ are positive ($s_{n+2j+1}=n+1-j\ge1$ for $j\le n$), and $s_{n+2(n+1)+1}=s_{3n+3}=0$. $\square$

Starting from $s_0=0$, the moves after which the stack is empty are therefore exactly $n_1,n_2,\dots$ with $n_0=0$ and $n_{k+1}=3n_k+3$. Since $n_{k+1}+\frac32=3\bigl(n_k+\frac32\bigr)$, we get $n_k=\frac32(3^k-1)=\frac{3^{k+1}-3}2$. So Piotrek has $0$ pebbles after $n$ moves exactly when \[\boxed{n=\frac{3^k-3}{2}\ \ (k\ge2)},\qquad n=3,12,39,120,363,\dots\] (The example in the statement: $s_1,\dots,s_5=1,3,0,4,9$, and indeed $n_1=3$.)

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $I$ be the incenter of an acute triangle $ABC$. Circle $o$ passes through $I$ and is tangent to $BC$ at $C$. Ray $BI$ meets $o$ again at $D\ne I$. Ray $BA$ meets the circumcircle of $ADI$ again at $E\ne A$, which lies outside the segment $AB$. Prove that the intersection of lines $DE$ and $AI$ lies on $o$.

Show the solution

Solution (A directed angle chase)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $X$ be the intersection of the lines $DE$ and $AI$. Since $o$ passes through $C,D,I$, it suffices to show \[\measuredangle(XD,XI)=\measuredangle(CD,CI). \tag{1}\] (If $X=D$ or $X=I$ there is nothing to prove.)

Left side. The lines $XD$ and $XI$ are $DE$ and $AI$. Since $A,D,I,E$ are concyclic, $\measuredangle(ED,EA)=\measuredangle(ID,IA)$; here $EA$ is the line $AB$ and $ID$ is the line $BI$, so $\measuredangle(DE,AB)=\measuredangle(BI,AI)$. Hence \[\measuredangle(XD,XI)=\measuredangle(DE,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,AI)=\measuredangle(BI,AI)+\measuredangle(AB,AI).\]

Right side. The circle $o$ is tangent to $BC$ at $C$, so by the tangent–chord angle $\measuredangle(CB,CD)=\measuredangle(IC,ID)=\measuredangle(IC,BI)$. Hence \[\measuredangle(CD,CI)=\measuredangle(CD,CB)+\measuredangle(CB,CI)=\measuredangle(BI,IC)+\measuredangle(CB,CI).\]

Comparison. Since $AI$ and $CI$ bisect the angles at $A$ and $C$, $\measuredangle(AB,AI)=\measuredangle(AI,AC)$ and $\measuredangle(CB,CI)=\measuredangle(CI,CA)$. Therefore \[\measuredangle(IC,AI)=\measuredangle(CI,CA)+\measuredangle(CA,AI)=\measuredangle(CB,CI)-\measuredangle(AB,AI),\] and the difference of the two sides is \[\bigl[\measuredangle(BI,AI)-\measuredangle(BI,IC)\bigr]+\measuredangle(AB,AI)-\measuredangle(CB,CI)=\measuredangle(IC,AI)+\measuredangle(AB,AI)-\measuredangle(CB,CI)=0 .\] This proves (1), so the intersection $X$ of $DE$ and $AI$ lies on $o$.

Remark. The proof does not use that $E$ lies outside segment $AB$ (that holds exactly when $BC>BA$, because $BA\cdot BE=BI\cdot BD=BC^2$ by powers of the point $B$).

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Positive integer $n$ and real numbers $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{2n+1}$ are given. Assume that $x_i+x_{i+1}\ge 0$ for $i=1,2,\dots,2n+1$, where $x_{2n+2}=x_1$. Prove that \[\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}|x_i|\le(2n+1)\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}x_i.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Each term is at most the total)

Indices are cyclic ($x_{2n+2}=x_1$), and let $S=x_1+\dots+x_{2n+1}$. We show $|x_i|\le S$ for every $i$; adding these $2n+1$ inequalities gives the claim.

Fix $i$. The other $2n$ terms $x_{i+1},x_{i+2},\dots,x_{i+2n}$ split into the $n$ consecutive pairs $(x_{i+1},x_{i+2}),\dots,(x_{i+2n-1},x_{i+2n})$, each with non-negative sum, so $S-x_i\ge0$, i.e. $x_i\le S$.

Also, the $2n+2$ terms $x_i,x_{i+1},\dots,x_{i+2n},x_{i+2n+1}=x_i$ (that is, all terms, with $x_i$ counted twice) split into the $n+1$ consecutive pairs $(x_i,x_{i+1}),(x_{i+2},x_{i+3}),\dots,(x_{i+2n},x_i)$, each with non-negative sum, so $S+x_i\ge0$, i.e. $-x_i\le S$.

Hence $|x_i|\le S$ for all $i$, and \[\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}|x_i|\le(2n+1)S=(2n+1)\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}x_i .\] The constant $2n+1$ cannot be improved: for $(x_1,\dots,x_{2n+1})=(1,-1,1,-1,\dots,-1,1)$ all neighbour sums are $0$ or $2$, $S=1$ and $\sum|x_i|=2n+1$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Positive integers $a,b$ are given. Assume that $a^2+ab$ is a product of two consecutive integers. Prove that if $b>1$, then \[b\ge 1+\sqrt{4a+1}\] and find all $a,b$ for which equality occurs.

Show the answer

Equality holds exactly for $(a,b)=(m(m-1),\,2m)$ with $m\ge2$, i.e. $(2,4),(6,6),(12,8),(20,10),\dots$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Difference of two squares)

Let $a^2+ab=t(t+1)$; since $t(t+1)=(-t-1)(-t)$ we may assume $t\ge0$. Multiplying by $4$ and adding $1$, and comparing with $(2a+b)^2$: \[4a^2+4ab+1=(2t+1)^2,\qquad (2a+b)^2-(2t+1)^2=b^2-1 .\] As $b>1$, the right side is positive, so $u=2a+b-(2t+1)$ and $v=2a+b+(2t+1)$ are positive integers with \[uv=b^2-1,\qquad u+v=2(2a+b),\qquad v-u=4t+2>0 .\] Since $u,v\ge1$, $(u-1)(v-1)\ge0$, i.e. $uv\ge u+v-1$: \[b^2-1\ge4a+2b-1\iff(b-1)^2\ge4a+1\iff b\ge1+\sqrt{4a+1}\] (using $b-1>0$). This proves the inequality.

Equality. Equality means $(u-1)(v-1)=0$, i.e. $u=1$ (as $v>u$). Then $(b-1)^2=4a+1$ is odd, so $b=2m$ is even, and $(2m-1)^2=4a+1$ gives $a=m(m-1)$; $a\ge1$ forces $m\ge2$. Conversely, for $a=m(m-1)$, $b=2m$ ($m\ge2$) we have $a^2+ab=a(a+b)=m(m-1)\cdot m(m+1)=(m^2-1)m^2$, a product of two consecutive integers, and $1+\sqrt{4a+1}=1+(2m-1)=b$. So equality holds exactly for \[\boxed{(a,b)=(m(m-1),\,2m),\ m\ge2}.\]

Solution 2 (Write $t=a+c$)

Let $a^2+ab=t(t+1)$ with $t\ge0$ (replace $t$ by $-t-1$ if necessary). Since $b\ge2$, $t(t+1)=a(a+b)\ge a(a+2)>a(a+1)$, so $t>a$; write $t=a+c$ with $c\ge1$. Expanding $(a+c)(a+c+1)=a^2+ab$ gives \[a(2c+1)+c(c+1)=ab,\qquad\text{so}\qquad x:=b-2c-1=\frac{c(c+1)}a .\] Thus $x$ is a positive integer, $b-1=2c+x$ and $a=\frac{c(c+1)}x$. Therefore \[4a+1=\frac{4c(c+1)}x+1\le4c(c+1)+1=(2c+1)^2\le(2c+x)^2=(b-1)^2,\] which is $b\ge1+\sqrt{4a+1}$.

Equality holds in both steps exactly when $x=1$, i.e. $a=c(c+1)$ and $b=2c+2$. With $m=c+1\ge2$ this is $(a,b)=(m(m-1),2m)$, and these pairs do satisfy the hypothesis: $a^2+ab=(m^2-1)m^2$. So equality occurs exactly for $\boxed{(a,b)=(m(m-1),\,2m),\ m\ge2}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Find all positive integers $k,n$ such that \[3^k+n^3+6=\prod_{i=k}^{k+n-1}i.\]

Show the answer

$(k,n)=(3,3)$

Show the solution

Solution (Parity, then powers of $3$)

Let $P=k(k+1)\cdots(k+n-1)$, a product of $n$ consecutive positive integers; it is divisible by $n!$, since $P/n!=\binom{k+n-1}{n}$.

$n=1$. The equation $3^k+7=k$ is impossible, as $3^k>k$.

$n$ is odd. For $n\ge2$, $P$ is even, while $3^k+n^3+6\equiv1+n\pmod2$. So $n$ is odd.

$3\mid n$. For $n\ge3$, $3\mid P$, while $3^k+n^3+6\equiv n^3\equiv n\pmod 3$. So $3\mid n$, and $n\in\{3,9,15,\dots\}$.

$n\ge9$ is impossible. Then $3^4\mid9!\mid P$. But $27\mid n^3$, so: if $k\ge2$, $3^k+n^3+6\equiv6\pmod9$ is not divisible by $9$; if $k=1$, $3+n^3+6\equiv9\pmod{27}$ is not divisible by $27$. Either way the left side is not divisible by $81$, a contradiction.

$n=3$. The equation is $3^k+33=k(k+1)(k+2)$. For $k=1,2,3,4$ the two sides are $36$ vs $6$, $42$ vs $24$, $60$ vs $60$, $114$ vs $120$. For $k\ge5$ even $3^k>k(k+1)(k+2)$: at $k=5$, $243>210$, and when $k$ increases by $1$, $3^k$ is multiplied by $3$ while $k(k+1)(k+2)$ is multiplied by $\frac{k+3}{k}\le\frac85<3$. So for $k\ge5$ the left side $3^k+33$ is larger than the right side.

Hence the only solution is $\boxed{(k,n)=(3,3)}$: $27+27+6=60=3\cdot4\cdot5$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

A sequence of $2025$ real numbers with sum $0$ is given. We will modify the sequence using the following procedure: Let $A$ be the number of positive terms in this sequence, $B$ be the number of negative terms in this sequence and let $S$ be the sum of the positive terms. From every positive term, we subtract $\frac{S}{A}$ and we add $\frac{S}{B}$ to every negative term. Assume that after every application of this procedure, at least one term of the sequence is nonzero. Prove that after a finite number of such procedures, absolute values of all the terms will be smaller than $\frac{1}{2025}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The sum of squares shrinks geometrically)

Let $N=2025$, let $x_1,\dots,x_N$ be the current terms and $Q=\sum x_i^2$.

One step. If all terms are $0$ there is nothing to prove; otherwise, as the sum is $0$, there are positive and negative terms, so $A,B\ge1$ and $S>0$, and the negative terms sum to $-S$. The step replaces each positive term $p$ by $p-\mu_+$ with $\mu_+=\frac SA$ (the mean of the positive terms), each negative term $q$ by $q-\mu_-$ with $\mu_-=-\frac SB$ (the mean of the negative terms), and keeps the zeros. The sum stays $0$. Using $\sum(p-\mu)^2=\sum p^2-A\mu^2$ for $A$ numbers with mean $\mu$, the new sum of squares is \[Q'=Q-A\mu_+^2-B\mu_-^2=Q-S^2\Bigl(\frac1A+\frac1B\Bigr).\]

Estimates. Since $A+B\le N$, $\frac1A+\frac1B\ge\frac4{A+B}\ge\frac4N$. Also $\sum|x_i|=2S$, so $Q\le\bigl(\sum|x_i|\bigr)^2=4S^2$. Therefore \[Q'\le Q-\frac4N\cdot\frac Q4=\Bigl(1-\frac1N\Bigr)Q .\]

Conclusion. After $t$ steps the sum of squares is at most $\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)^tQ_0$, which tends to $0$. Choose $t$ with $\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)^tQ_0<\frac1{N^2}$. Then every term satisfies $|x_i|\le\sqrt{Q_t}<\frac1N=\frac1{2025}$.

Solution 2 (The largest absolute value shrinks)

Let $N=2025$. We show that one step multiplies the largest absolute value of the terms by a factor smaller than $1-\frac1N$.

One step. Let $x_1,\dots,x_N$ be the current terms, with sum $0$. If all of them are $0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise there are positive and negative terms (the sum is $0$), so $A,B\ge1$, hence $A\le N-1$ and $B\le N-1$. Let $\mathcal M=\max_i|x_i|>0$. The sum of the positive terms is $S$, and since the total is $0$, the sum of the absolute values of the negative terms is also $S$. The term of largest absolute value belongs to one of these two groups, so $S\ge\mathcal M$.

Positive terms. Each positive term $p$ becomes $p-\mu$, where $\mu=\frac SA$ is the mean of the positive terms. On the one hand \[p-\mu\le\mathcal M-\frac{\mathcal M}{A}\le\mathcal M-\frac{\mathcal M}{N-1}=\frac{N-2}{N-1}\,\mathcal M .\] On the other hand, let $p_0$ be the smallest positive term. Then \[\mu-p\le\mu-p_0=\frac1A\sum_{p'>0}(p'-p_0)\le\frac{A-1}{A}\,(\mathcal M-p_0)\le\frac{N-2}{N-1}\,\mathcal M ,\] because the summand for $p'=p_0$ is $0$, each of the other $A-1$ summands is at most $\mathcal M-p_0<\mathcal M$, and $\frac{A-1}A=1-\frac1A\le1-\frac1{N-1}$. Finally $\frac{N-2}{N-1}<\frac{N-1}{N}$, since $N(N-2)=(N-1)^2-1$. Hence $|p-\mu|<\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)\mathcal M$ for every positive term $p$.

Negative terms. Each negative term $q$ becomes $q+\frac SB$. Applying the same argument to the numbers $-q$ (there are $B\le N-1$ of them, with sum $S$, largest value at most $\mathcal M$, and mean $\frac SB\ge\frac{\mathcal M}{B}$) gives $\bigl|q+\frac SB\bigr|<\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)\mathcal M$.

Zero terms stay $0$. So after the step every term has absolute value less than $\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)\mathcal M$, and the sum is still $0$.

Conclusion. By the hypothesis the sequence is never all zeros after a step, so the step can be repeated, and by induction the largest absolute value after $t$ steps is less than $\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)^t\mathcal M_0$, where $\mathcal M_0$ is the initial largest absolute value. As $\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)^t\to0$, for some $t$ this bound is below $\frac1N=\frac1{2025}$, and then all terms have absolute value smaller than $\frac1{2025}$.

Problem 7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $AD$ be an altitude in an acute triangle $ABC$. Points $E,F$ are the projections of $D$ onto lines $AB$ and $AC$ respectively. Points $K,L,M$ are the midpoints of $BD,BC,DC$ respectively. Prove that the lines $EK,AL,FM$ are concurrent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangents and the symmedian)

Let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $AD$; it passes through $E$ and $F$ (right angles $AED$, $AFD$). Let $\alpha=\angle BAC<90^\circ$.

$KE$ and $MF$ are tangents to $\omega$. $K$ is the midpoint of the hypotenuse $BD$ of the right triangle $BED$, so $KE=KD$. As $KD\perp AD$, the line $KD$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $D$, so the power of $K$ is $KD^2=KE^2$. If the line $KE$ met $\omega$ again at $E'$, then $KE\cdot KE'=KE^2$ would give $E'=E$; so $KE$ is the tangent at $E$. Likewise $MF$ is the tangent at $F$.

These tangents meet at a point $T$: $EF$ is not a diameter of $\omega$ because the inscribed angle $\angle EAF=\alpha$ is not right. As $\alpha<90^\circ$, $A$ lies on the major arc $EF$ and $T$ on the other side of $EF$; by the tangent–chord angle $\angle TEF=\angle TFE=\alpha$. So the angles of quadrilateral $AETF$ at $E$ and $F$ are $\angle AEF+\alpha=180^\circ-\angle AFE$ and $180^\circ-\angle AEF$, both less than $180^\circ$; the quadrilateral is convex and the diagonal $AT$ meets segment $EF$ at an interior point $S$.

$T$ lies on $AL$. Comparing the triangles $AES$, $AFS$ and using $ET=FT$, \[\frac{ES}{SF}=\frac{[AET]}{[AFT]}=\frac{AE\cdot ET\sin\angle AET}{AF\cdot FT\sin\angle AFT}=\frac{AE\sin\angle AFE}{AF\sin\angle AEF}=\frac{AE^2}{AF^2},\] by the law of sines in $AEF$. The median $AL$ meets segment $EF$ at an interior point $S'$ with \[\frac{ES'}{S'F}=\frac{AE\sin\angle BAL}{AF\sin\angle CAL}=\frac{AE}{AF}\cdot\frac{AC}{AB},\] because $BL=LC$ means $AB\sin\angle BAL=AC\sin\angle CAL$ (equal areas of $ABL$ and $ACL$). Finally, in the right triangles $ADB$ and $ADC$, $AE\cdot AB=AD^2=AF\cdot AC$, so $\frac{AC}{AB}=\frac{AE}{AF}$ and $\frac{ES'}{S'F}=\frac{AE^2}{AF^2}=\frac{ES}{SF}$. Hence $S'=S$, the lines $AL$ and $AT$ coincide, and $T\in AL$.

So the lines $EK$, $FM$ (the tangents at $E$, $F$) and $AL$ all pass through $T$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(-b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a,b,c>0$. The angle at $A$ is acute, i.e. $\vec{AB}\cdot\vec{AC}=a^2-bc>0$.

The foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AB$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $AE:EB=AD^2:BD^2=a^2:b^2$, so \[E=\frac{b^2A+a^2B}{a^2+b^2}=\frac{(-a^2b,\ ab^2)}{a^2+b^2},\qquad F=\frac{(a^2c,\ ac^2)}{a^2+c^2},\] and $K=(-\frac b2,0)$, $L=(\frac{c-b}2,0)$, $M=(\frac c2,0)$. Consider the point \[T=\Bigl(\frac{a^2(c-b)}{2(a^2-bc)},\ -\frac{abc}{a^2-bc}\Bigr).\]

  • $T\in AL$: with $\lambda=\frac{a^2}{a^2-bc}$, $A+\lambda(L-A)=\bigl(\lambda\frac{c-b}2,\ a(1-\lambda)\bigr)=T$.

  • $T\in EK$: $\vec{KE}=\Bigl(\frac{b(b^2-a^2)}{2(a^2+b^2)},\frac{ab^2}{a^2+b^2}\Bigr)$ is parallel to $(b^2-a^2,\,2ab)$, and $\vec{KT}=\Bigl(\frac{c(a^2-b^2)}{2(a^2-bc)},\,-\frac{abc}{a^2-bc}\Bigr)=-\frac{c}{2(a^2-bc)}\,(b^2-a^2,\ 2ab)$ is parallel to it as well.

  • $T\in FM$: the whole configuration is symmetric under $x\mapsto-x$, $b\leftrightarrow c$ (which exchanges $B\leftrightarrow C$, $E\leftrightarrow F$, $K\leftrightarrow M$ and fixes $A,D,L$), and $T$ is mapped to itself; so $T\in FM$ follows from $T\in EK$.

Hence $EK$, $AL$, $FM$ pass through $T$ and are concurrent.

Problem 8

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities

Positive integer $n$ is given. We will call every injective function $f:\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}$ a permutation. Pairwise distinct permutations $f_1,\dots,f_N$ are given. Assume that for every pair of indices (not necessarily distinct) $i,j$, there exists $k$ such that $f_i\circ f_j=f_k$. For $1\le i<j\le n$ let $Z(i,j)$ denote the number of indices $l$ such that $f_l(i)>f_l(j)$. Prove that \[\frac{N}{2}\cdot\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}Z(i,j)=\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}Z(i,j)^2.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Count pairs inverted by two permutations)

For a permutation $g$ let $I(g)$ be the set of pairs $\{i,j\}$ ($i<j$) with $g(i)>g(j)$ (inversions), and $\operatorname{inv}(g)=|I(g)|$. Let $G=\{f_1,\dots,f_N\}$.

$G$ is a group. It is closed under composition. For $g\in G$ the powers $g,g^2,\dots$ lie in the finite set $G$, so $g^a=g^b$ for some $a<b$; then $g^{b-a}=\mathrm{id}\in G$, and $g^{-1}=g^{b-a-1}\in G$ (this is $\mathrm{id}$ if $b-a=1$).

The two sides. $Z(i,j)$ counts the $g\in G$ with $\{i,j\}\in I(g)$, so \[\sum_{i<j}Z(i,j)=\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{inv}(g),\qquad \sum_{i<j}Z(i,j)^2=\sum_{g,h\in G}|I(g)\cap I(h)| .\]

Lemma. $|I(g)\,\triangle\,I(h)|=\operatorname{inv}(gh^{-1})$. Indeed, $g$ and $h$ disagree on the pair $\{i,j\}$ exactly when the order of $g(i),g(j)$ differs from the order of $h(i),h(j)$, i.e. when $\sigma=gh^{-1}$ (which maps $h(i)\mapsto g(i)$, $h(j)\mapsto g(j)$) reverses the pair $\{h(i),h(j)\}$. As $\{i,j\}$ runs over all pairs, so does $\{h(i),h(j)\}$; hence the number of disagreements is $\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)$.

Therefore $|I(g)\cap I(h)|=\frac12\bigl(\operatorname{inv}(g)+\operatorname{inv}(h)-\operatorname{inv}(gh^{-1})\bigr)$. Summing over $g,h\in G$ and writing $J=\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{inv}(g)$: the first two terms give $NJ$ each, and for each fixed $h$ the map $g\mapsto gh^{-1}$ is a bijection of $G$ (as $G$ is a group), so the third term also gives $NJ$. Hence \[\sum_{i<j}Z(i,j)^2=\frac12(NJ+NJ-NJ)=\frac N2\,J=\frac N2\sum_{i<j}Z(i,j).\]

Problem 9

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ of positive integers is given. Assume that $\gcd(a_2-a_1,a_3-a_2,\dots)=1$. Prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that the sum of some $N$ elements (not necessarily distinct) of the sequence is equal to the sum of some $N+1$ elements (not necessarily distinct) of the sequence.

Show the solution

Solution (An integer combination with coefficient sum $1$)

Let $d_k=a_{k+1}-a_k$. The numbers $\gcd(d_1,\dots,d_K)$ form a non-increasing sequence of positive integers whose eventual value is the gcd of all differences, $1$; so $\gcd(d_1,\dots,d_K)=1$ for some $K$. By Bezout there are integers $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_K$ with $\sum\lambda_kd_k=1$; put $\mu_k=-a_1\lambda_k$, so $\sum_{k=1}^K\mu_k(a_{k+1}-a_k)=-a_1$.

Define integers $e_1,\dots,e_{K+1}$ by \[\sum_{j=1}^{K+1}e_j\,y_j=y_1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_k\,(y_{k+1}-y_k)\qquad\text{(identically in the variables } y_j).\] Taking all $y_j=1$ gives $\sum e_j=1$; taking $y_j=a_j$ gives $\sum e_ja_j=a_1-a_1=0$.

Let $\mathcal P=\{j:e_j>0\}$ and $\mathcal N=\{j:e_j<0\}$. Then $\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}e_ja_j=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N}|e_j|a_j$. Put $N=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N}|e_j|$; from $\sum e_j=1$ we get $\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}e_j=N+1$. Moreover $N\ge1$: if $\mathcal N$ were empty, then $\sum e_ja_j$ would be positive (non-negative coefficients with sum $1$ and positive $a_j$).

So the $N$ numbers obtained by taking $a_j$ exactly $|e_j|$ times for $j\in\mathcal N$, and the $N+1$ numbers obtained by taking $a_j$ exactly $e_j$ times for $j\in\mathcal P$, are elements of the sequence with equal sums, as required.

Problem 10

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

A cyclic convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ is given. Rays $AB$ and $DC$ meet at $X$. Rays $AD$ and $BC$ meet at $Y$. Let $I,J$ be the incenters of $BCX$ and $CDY$ respectively. Circumcircles of $BCX$ and $CDY$ meet again at $Z\ne C$. Assume that $CX=CY$. Prove that $ZI=ZJ$.

Show the solution

Solution (Equal circles and a rotation about $Z$)

Let $\omega_1=(BCX)$ and $\omega_2=(CDY)$ with radii $r_1,r_2$. Note that $B,C,Y$ are collinear and $X,C,D$ are collinear.

Step 1: $r_1=r_2$. Since $X$ lies on ray $AB$ beyond $B$, $\angle CBX=180^\circ-\angle ABC$; since $Y$ lies on ray $AD$ beyond $D$, $\angle CDY=180^\circ-\angle ADC=\angle ABC$ ($ABCD$ is cyclic). By the law of sines, $CX=2r_1\sin\angle CBX=2r_1\sin\angle ABC$ and $CY=2r_2\sin\angle CDY=2r_2\sin\angle ABC$. So $CX=CY$ gives $r_1=r_2$.

Step 2: a rotation about $Z$. For $U\in\omega_1$, $U\ne C$, let $U'$ be the second intersection of line $UC$ with $\omega_2$. The directed angles $\measuredangle(UZ,UU')=\measuredangle(UZ,UC)$ and $\measuredangle(U'Z,U'U)=\measuredangle(U'Z,U'C)$ do not depend on $U$ (inscribed angles on the chord $ZC$ of $\omega_1$ and of $\omega_2$). So all triangles $ZUU'$ are directly similar, and $U\mapsto U'$ is a spiral similarity with centre $Z$. It maps $\omega_1$ onto $\omega_2$, so its ratio is $r_2/r_1=1$: it is a rotation $\mathcal R$ about $Z$. It maps $B\mapsto Y$ (line $BC$ meets $\omega_2$ again at $Y$) and $X\mapsto D$.

Step 3: the bisector. The angles $\angle BCX$ and $\angle YCD$ are vertical angles (ray $CY$ is opposite to ray $CB$, ray $CD$ opposite to ray $CX$), so their bisectors lie on one line $\ell$, and $I,J\in\ell$ lie on opposite sides of $C$. Let $N$ and $N'$ be the second intersections of $\ell$ with $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$. By the incenter–excenter lemma, $N$ is the midpoint of the arc $BX$ not containing $C$, $NB=NI$, and $I$ lies between $C$ and $N$; similarly $N'Y=N'J$ and $J$ lies between $C$ and $N'$. Thus $C$ lies between $N$ and $N'$.

Step 4. By Step 2 (with $U=N$), $\mathcal R(N)=N'$, because line $NC=\ell$ meets $\omega_2$ again at $N'$. As $\mathcal R$ is a rotation, \[ZN=ZN'\qquad\text{and}\qquad NI=NB=N'Y=N'J=:\rho .\] Put a coordinate on $\ell$ with $C=0$, $N=\nu>0$, $N'=-\nu'<0$. Then $I=\nu-\rho$ and $J=-\nu'+\rho$, so the midpoint of $IJ$ is $\frac{\nu-\nu'}2$, the midpoint of $NN'$. Since $ZN=ZN'$, the point $Z$ lies on the perpendicular to $\ell$ through this midpoint, which is the perpendicular bisector of $IJ$. Hence $ZI=ZJ$.

Problem 11

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 38: Inequalities: Convexity and Functional Methods

A sequence of positive real numbers $a_0,a_1,\dots,a_n$ satisfies $a_k^2\ge a_{k-1}a_{k+1}$ for $1\le k\le n-1$. Assume that the sequence $b_0,b_1,\dots,b_n$ is geometric, has positive terms and satisfies \[\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_k=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_k\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{n}ka_k=\sum_{k=0}^{n}kb_k.\] Prove that \[\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^2a_k\le\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^2b_k.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Two sign changes and a quadratic weight)

Let $b_k=b_0r^k$ and $c_k=\ln a_k-\ln b_k$. The hypothesis $a_k^2\ge a_{k-1}a_{k+1}$ says $2\ln a_k\ge\ln a_{k-1}+\ln a_{k+1}$, and $\ln b_k$ is linear in $k$, so \[c_{k+1}-c_k\le c_k-c_{k-1}\qquad(1\le k\le n-1):\] the differences $\Delta_k=c_{k+1}-c_k$ are non-increasing.

The set where $a_k\ge b_k$ is an interval. Let $i<j<l$ with $c_i\ge0$ and $c_l\ge0$. Since the $\Delta$'s are non-increasing, \[\frac{c_j-c_i}{j-i}=\frac{\Delta_i+\dots+\Delta_{j-1}}{j-i}\ge\Delta_{j-1}\ge\Delta_j\ge\frac{\Delta_j+\dots+\Delta_{l-1}}{l-j}=\frac{c_l-c_j}{l-j},\] which rearranges to $c_j\ge\frac{(l-j)c_i+(j-i)c_l}{l-i}\ge0$. So $\{k:\ a_k\ge b_k\}=\{p,p+1,\dots,q\}$ for some $p\le q$; it is non-empty because $\sum a_k=\sum b_k$.

The weight. Let $\varphi(x)=\bigl(x-p+\frac12\bigr)\bigl(x-q-\frac12\bigr)$. For integers $k$ with $p\le k\le q$ the first factor is positive and the second negative, so $\varphi(k)<0$, while $a_k-b_k\ge0$; for the other integers $k$ both factors have the same sign, so $\varphi(k)>0$, while $a_k-b_k<0$. In all cases $\varphi(k)(a_k-b_k)\le0$. Summing and using $\sum(a_k-b_k)=0$ and $\sum k(a_k-b_k)=0$: \[0\ge\sum_{k=0}^n\varphi(k)(a_k-b_k)=\sum k^2(a_k-b_k)-(p+q)\sum k(a_k-b_k)+\bigl(p-\tfrac12\bigr)\bigl(q+\tfrac12\bigr)\sum(a_k-b_k)=\sum_{k=0}^nk^2(a_k-b_k).\] Hence $\sum k^2a_k\le\sum k^2b_k$.

Problem 12

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

If $X,Y$ are subsets of $\mathbb{Z}$, by $X+Y$ we will denote the set of all numbers of the type $x+y$ where $x\in X$ and $y\in Y$. In particular $X+Y+Z=(X+Y)+Z$. Non-empty and finite subsets $A,B,C$ of $\mathbb{Z}$ are given. Prove that \[2|A+B+C|+1\ge|A+B|+|B+C|+|C+A|.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Two explicit subsets of $A+B+C$)

Let $a_0=\min A$, $a_1=\max A$, $b_1=\max B$, $c_0=\min C$, and $s=b_1+c_0\in B+C$. Split $B+C$ into $U=\{y\in B+C:\ y>s\}$ and $V=\{y\in B+C:\ y<s\}$, so $|U|+|V|=|B+C|-1$. All sets below are subsets of $A+B+C$.

First subset. $X_1=(A+B+c_0)\cup(a_1+U)$. Every element of $A+B+c_0$ is at most $a_1+b_1+c_0=a_1+s$, and every element of $a_1+U$ is larger than $a_1+s$. So the union is disjoint and \[|A+B+C|\ge|X_1|=|A+B|+|U| .\]

Second subset. $X_2=(a_0+V)\cup(A+C+b_1)$. Every element of $a_0+V$ is smaller than $a_0+s=a_0+b_1+c_0$, and every element of $A+C+b_1$ is at least $a_0+c_0+b_1$. So the union is disjoint and \[|A+B+C|\ge|X_2|=|V|+|A+C| .\]

Adding, \[2|A+B+C|\ge|A+B|+|A+C|+|U|+|V|=|A+B|+|B+C|+|C+A|-1,\] which is the claim. (Equality holds, for example, when $A=B=C=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}$.)
