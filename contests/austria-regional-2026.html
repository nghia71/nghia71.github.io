Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $a$ and $b$ be positive real numbers with \[(a+4)(b+1) = 24.\] Prove the inequality \[a^2+b^2 \ge 13.\] When does equality hold?

Show the answer

$a^2+b^2\ge 13$, with equality exactly for $a=2$, $b=3$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Cauchy-Schwarz, then AM-GM)

Put $x=a+4$ and $y=b+1$. Then $x>4$, $y>1$ and $xy=24$.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \[ (a^2+b^2)(2^2+3^2)\ge(2a+3b)^2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad a^2+b^2\ge\frac{(2a+3b)^2}{13}. \] It remains to show $2a+3b\ge 13$. By AM-GM, \[ 2a+3b=2(x-4)+3(y-1)=2x+3y-11\ge 2\sqrt{2x\cdot 3y}-11=2\sqrt{144}-11=13 . \] Since $2a+3b\ge13>0$, we get $a^2+b^2\ge 13^2/13=13$.

Equality. We need equality in AM-GM, i.e. $2x=3y$; with $xy=24$ this gives $x=6$, $y=4$, so $a=2$, $b=3$. Then also $a:b=2:3$, which is the equality case of Cauchy-Schwarz. Indeed $(2+4)(3+1)=24$ and $2^2+3^2=13$. So equality holds exactly for $\boxed{a=2,\ b=3}$.

Solution 2 (Eliminating $b$)

From $(a+4)(b+1)=24$ we get $b=\dfrac{24}{a+4}-1=\dfrac{20-a}{a+4}$, and $b>0$ means $0<a<20$. Then \[ (a+4)^2\,(a^2+b^2-13)=a^2(a+4)^2+(20-a)^2-13(a+4)^2=a^4+8a^3+4a^2-144a+192 . \] We expect equality at $a=2$, so we try to split off $(a-2)^2=a^2-4a+4$; division gives \[ a^4+8a^3+4a^2-144a+192=(a-2)^2\,(a^2+12a+48). \] The second factor equals $(a+6)^2+12>0$. Hence $(a+4)^2(a^2+b^2-13)\ge 0$, so $a^2+b^2\ge 13$, with equality exactly when $a=2$, that is, for $\boxed{a=2,\ b=3}$ (then $b=\frac{18}{6}=3$).

Solution 3 (Shifting to the equality point)

We expect equality at $(a,b)=(2,3)$, so put $a=2+x$ and $b=3+y$. The constraint becomes \[ (6+x)(4+y)=24\iff 24+6y+4x+xy=24\iff 4x+6y=-xy . \] Hence \[ a^2+b^2-13=(4+4x+x^2)+(9+6y+y^2)-13=x^2+y^2+(4x+6y)=x^2-xy+y^2 . \] Now $x^2-xy+y^2=\bigl(x-\frac y2\bigr)^2+\frac34y^2\ge0$, with equality only for $y=0$ and $x=\frac y2=0$. Therefore $a^2+b^2\ge13$, with equality exactly for $\boxed{a=2,\ b=3}$. (This argument does not even use $a,b>0$.)

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $k$ be the circumcircle of a square $ABCD$. Let $P$ be a point on the shorter arc $CD$ of $k$ with $P \ne C$ and $P \ne D$. Let the intersection of the line $BP$ with the line $AC$ be $Q$ and let the intersection of the line $CP$ with the line $AD$ be $R$.

Prove that the line $RQ$ is perpendicular to the line $AC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle through $A$, $Q$, $P$, $R$)

We use directed angles between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$; write $\angle(\ell,m)$ for the angle that rotates line $\ell$ onto line $m$ counterclockwise. Recall that four points $W,X,Y,Z$ (no three collinear) are concyclic if and only if $\angle(XW,XY)=\angle(ZW,ZY)$.

Because $ABCD$ is a square, the diagonal $AC$ bisects the right angle at $A$, and turning $AB$ to $AC$ and then $AC$ to $AD$ are rotations by $45^\circ$ in the same direction. Hence $\angle(AB,AC)=\angle(AC,AD)$.

The point $P$ lies on $k$, so by the inscribed angle theorem $\angle(PB,PC)=\angle(AB,AC)$. Since $Q$ lies on line $BP$ and on line $AC$, and $R$ lies on line $CP$ and on line $AD$, \[ \angle(PQ,PR)=\angle(PB,PC)=\angle(AB,AC)=\angle(AC,AD)=\angle(AQ,AR). \] Therefore $A$, $Q$, $P$, $R$ lie on one circle. (They are four distinct points: $Q$ lies strictly inside $k$ on chord $BP$, and $R$ lies on line $AD$ beyond $D$, since $P$ lies on the arc $CD$.)

$AC$ is a diameter of $k$, so $\angle(PA,PC)=90^\circ$. Using the new circle, \[ \angle(QA,QR)=\angle(PA,PR)=\angle(PA,PC)=90^\circ . \] So $QR\perp QA$, i.e. the line $RQ$ is perpendicular to $AC$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Choose coordinates with the centre of $k$ at the origin and radius $1$: \[ A=(-1,0),\quad B=(0,-1),\quad C=(1,0),\quad D=(0,1). \] The shorter arc $CD$ consists of the points $P=(\cos t,\sin t)$ with $0<t<90^\circ$. Write $c=\cos t$, $s=\sin t$; then $0<c,s<1$.

The point $Q$. The line $AC$ is the $x$-axis. The line $BP$ consists of the points $(\lambda c,\,-1+\lambda(s+1))$; it meets the $x$-axis for $\lambda=\frac1{1+s}$, so $Q=\left(\frac{c}{1+s},\,0\right)$.

The point $R$. The line $AD$ is $y=x+1$. The line $CP$ consists of the points $(1+\mu(c-1),\,\mu s)$. Substituting into $y=x+1$ gives $\mu(1+s-c)=2$ (note $1+s-c>0$), so \[ x_R=1+\frac{2(c-1)}{1+s-c}=\frac{s+c-1}{1+s-c}. \]

Comparison. The line $RQ$ is perpendicular to $AC$ (the $x$-axis) exactly when $x_R=x_Q$, i.e. when $(s+c-1)(1+s)=c(1+s-c)$. Expanding, and using $s^2=1-c^2$: \[ (s+c-1)(1+s)=s^2+c+sc-1=c+sc-c^2=c(1+s-c). \] So $x_R=x_Q$ and $RQ\perp AC$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A set of positive integers is called airy, if it does not contain two consecutive numbers. For each airy subset of the set $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ compute the product of its elements, then add the squares of all these products where the empty subset is assigned the product $1$.

Determine the sum $s_n$.

Example: $n = 3$.
The airy subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$ are $\{\}, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{3\}, \{1,3\}$ with products $1, 1, 2, 3, 3$.
Therefore, the sum of the squares equals $s_3 = 1^2+1^2+2^2+3^2+3^2 = 24$.

Show the answer

$s_n=(n+1)!$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A two-term recurrence)

Put $s_0=1$ (only the empty set). Directly, $s_1=1^2+1^2=2$.

Let $n\ge2$ and split the airy subsets $S$ of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into two kinds.

  • $n\notin S$: these are exactly the airy subsets of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$; they contribute $s_{n-1}$.

  • $n\in S$: then $n-1\notin S$, and $S\setminus\{n\}$ is an arbitrary airy subset of $\{1,\dots,n-2\}$. The square of the product of $S$ is $n^2$ times that of $S\setminus\{n\}$, so these contribute $n^2s_{n-2}$ (for $n=2$ the only such set is $\{2\}$, with $4=2^2s_0$).

Hence $s_n=s_{n-1}+n^2s_{n-2}$ for $n\ge 2$.

We show $s_n=(n+1)!$ by induction. It holds for $n=0$ and $n=1$. If it holds for $n-1$ and $n-2$, then \[ s_n=n!+n^2\,(n-1)!=(n-1)!\,(n+n^2)=(n-1)!\,n\,(n+1)=(n+1)! . \] Therefore $\boxed{s_n=(n+1)!}$; for example $s_3=24$, as in the statement.

Solution 2 (Splitting $i^2=i+i(i-1)$: a bijection)

For a finite set $T$ of positive integers write $\pi(T)$ for the product of its elements ($\pi(\varnothing)=1$). Expanding the product $\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+j)$ gives one term $\pi(T)$ for each subset $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, so \[ \sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}\pi(T)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+j)=(n+1)! . \] We show that $s_n=\sum_S\pi(S)^2$ (over airy $S$) equals the same sum.

Expanding the squares. Write $i^2=i+i(i-1)$ for every $i\in S$ and multiply out: \[ \pi(S)^2=\prod_{i\in S}\bigl(i+i(i-1)\bigr)=\sum_{R\subseteq S}\ \prod_{i\in S\setminus R}i\ \prod_{i\in R}(i-1)\,i , \] where $R$ is the set of elements for which we choose the term $i(i-1)$. The terms with $1\in R$ vanish, so we may restrict to $R\subseteq S\setminus\{1\}$. Hence $s_n$ is the sum of the weights \[ w(S,R)=\prod_{i\in S\setminus R}i\ \prod_{i\in R}(i-1)\,i \] over all pairs $(S,R)$ with $S$ an airy subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ and $R\subseteq S\setminus\{1\}$.

The map. To such a pair assign the set \[ T=(S\setminus R)\ \cup\bigcup_{i\in R}\{i-1,i\}\ \subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}: \] every element of $R$ is “doubled” into the pair $\{i-1,i\}$, every other element of $S$ stays a single. These pieces are pairwise disjoint: for $i\in R$ the number $i-1\ge1$ is not in $S$ (as $S$ is airy), and different $i$ give different $i-1$. Therefore $\pi(T)=w(S,R)$.

The inverse. Let $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ be arbitrary and split it into maximal runs of consecutive integers. Cut each run $\{m,m+1,\dots,M\}$ from the top into pairs $\{M-1,M\},\{M-3,M-2\},\dots$; if the run has odd length, its lowest element $m$ is left as a single. Let $S$ be the set of tops of all pieces (the larger element of each pair and each single), and $R$ the set of tops of the pairs. Inside a run the tops differ by $2$; for two runs, the largest top of the lower run is at most its maximum, which is at least $2$ less than the minimum of the upper run, hence than every top there. So $S$ is airy, $R\subseteq S\setminus\{1\}$, and the pair $(S,R)$ is mapped to $T$.

It remains to see that no other pair $(S,R)$ is mapped to $T$, i.e. that the pieces of $T$ coming from any $(S,R)$ are exactly those of the top-down cutting. Each piece consists of consecutive numbers of $T$, so it lies inside one run, and its top is an element of $S$. Take a run and the piece containing its current highest element $j$ (first $j=M$, then the highest element not yet covered). The number $j$ is the top of its piece, because $j+1$ is either not in $T$ or already covered by a higher piece; all elements of the run below $j$ are still uncovered. If $j-1\in T$, the piece cannot be the single $\{j\}$: then $j-1$ would be the top of its own piece, so $j-1$ and $j$ would both lie in $S$, against airiness; hence the piece is $\{j-1,j\}$. If $j-1\notin T$, the piece is the single $\{j\}$. This is exactly the top-down cutting, so $(S,R)$ is determined by $T$.

Conclusion. $(S,R)\mapsto T$ is a bijection from the pairs onto all subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $\pi(T)=w(S,R)$. Therefore \[ s_n=\sum_{(S,R)}w(S,R)=\sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}\pi(T)=(n+1)!, \] so $\boxed{s_n=(n+1)!}$. (In counting language: an element $i$ of an airy set carries one of $i^2$ labels, an element $j$ of an arbitrary set one of $j$ labels, and the map matches the labelled objects one-to-one.)

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Prove the following two assertions.

  • (a) There are infinitely many square numbers of the form $3^k+3^n$ with positive integers $k$ and $n$.

  • (b) There are no square numbers of the form $7^k+7^n$ with positive integers $k$ and $n$.

Show the answer

proof: (a) $3^{2m}+3^{2m+1}=(2\cdot 3^m)^2$; (b) $7^k+7^n$ is never a square

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factoring out the power of 7)

(a) For every positive integer $m$ take $k=2m$ and $n=2m+1$. Then \[ 3^k+3^n=3^{2m}(1+3)=4\cdot 3^{2m}=(2\cdot 3^m)^2 . \] Different $m$ give different squares, so there are infinitely many.

(b) By symmetry let $k\le n$ and suppose $7^k+7^n=y^2$ with a positive integer $y$.

If $n=k$, then $y^2=2\cdot 7^k$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$, which is impossible for a square.

If $n>k$, put $d=n-k\ge1$, so $y^2=7^k(1+7^d)$. The factor $1+7^d$ is not divisible by $7$, so the exponent of $7$ in $y^2$ is $k$; it is even, and $1+7^d=(y/7^{k/2})^2=x^2$ for a positive integer $x$. Then \[ (x-1)(x+1)=7^d , \] so $x-1$ and $x+1$ are both powers of $7$. Two powers of $7$ that differ by $2$ cannot both be divisible by $7$, so $x-1=1$; then $x=2$ and $7^d=3$, a contradiction.

So $7^k+7^n$ is never a perfect square.

Solution 2 (Working modulo 3)

(a) As in Solution 1: for every positive integer $m$, $3^{2m}+3^{2m+1}=4\cdot3^{2m}=(2\cdot3^m)^2$, and these squares are all different, so there are infinitely many.

(b) Since $7\equiv1\pmod 3$, every power of $7$ is $\equiv 1\pmod 3$, so \[ 7^k+7^n\equiv 1+1=2\pmod 3 . \] A square $y^2$ is $\equiv 0\pmod 3$ if $3\mid y$ and $\equiv 1\pmod 3$ if $y\equiv\pm1\pmod 3$; it is never $\equiv 2$. Hence $7^k+7^n$ is not a square for any positive integers $k,n$.
