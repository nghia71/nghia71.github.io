Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement · Height-Based and Base-Based Comparisons

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a regular hexagon with center $O$. Distinct points $A$ and $B$ lie on the boundary of $\mathcal{P}$. The segments $OA$ and $OB$ divide the interior of $\mathcal{P}$ into two polygons $\mathcal{R}_1$ and $\mathcal{R}_2$. Let $a_1$ and $a_2$ be the areas of $\mathcal{R}_1$ and $\mathcal{R}_2$ respectively, and let $p_1$ and $p_2$ be the perimeters of $\mathcal{R}_1$ and $\mathcal{R}_2$ respectively. Given that $a_1/p_1=a_2/p_2$, prove that $a_1=a_2$.

Show the solution

Solution (Area as apothem times boundary length)

Let $h$ be the apothem of $\mathcal P$, the distance from $O$ to each side, and let $s=OA+OB>0$. For $i=1,2$ let $\ell_i$ be the length of the part of the boundary of $\mathcal P$ that belongs to the boundary of $\mathcal R_i$; then the boundary of $\mathcal R_i$ consists of the segments $OA$, $OB$ and this part, so \[p_i=\ell_i+s .\]

The areas. Cut the boundary part of $\mathcal R_i$ at the vertices of the hexagon that it contains. Each resulting piece $XY$ lies on one side of $\mathcal P$, and the triangles $OXY$ fill $\mathcal R_i$ without overlapping (they are the fan of triangles seen from $O$). The triangle $OXY$ has base $XY$ on a side of $\mathcal P$ and height $h$, so its area is $\frac12h\cdot XY$. Adding, \[a_i=\tfrac12 h\,\ell_i .\]

Conclusion. The hypothesis $a_1p_2=a_2p_1$ becomes \[\tfrac12h\,\ell_1(\ell_2+s)=\tfrac12h\,\ell_2(\ell_1+s)\iff \tfrac12hs\,(\ell_1-\ell_2)=0 .\] Since $h>0$ and $s>0$, we get $\ell_1=\ell_2$, and therefore $a_1=\frac12h\ell_1=\frac12h\ell_2=a_2$. (Equivalently: $a_i/p_i=\frac h2\cdot\frac{\ell_i}{\ell_i+s}$ is a strictly increasing function of $\ell_i$.)

Problem 2

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Constraint-Based Enumeration

Jessica the jackrabbit wants to climb down a wall. The wall consists of $2026$ horizontal layers stacked vertically. The $n$th layer from the top is partitioned into $2^n-1$ identical rectangular bricks arranged side by side. Jessica begins in the topmost layer, which contains a single brick. A move consists of Jessica going down one layer to a brick that shares a side with the brick she is currently on.

Determine, with proof, the total number of distinct sequences of moves Jessica can take to reach the $2026$th layer.

Show the answer

$3^{2025}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Locating the three bricks below)

Take the width of the wall as the unit of length, so that brick $k$ ($0\le k\le N-1$) of layer $n$, where $N=2^n-1$, occupies the horizontal interval $\bigl[\frac kN,\frac{k+1}N\bigr]$. Two bricks in consecutive layers share a side exactly when their intervals overlap in a segment of positive length (touching at a single corner is not sharing a side).

Claim. Every brick of layer $n$ shares a side with exactly $3$ bricks of layer $n+1$.

Layer $n+1$ has $N'=2^{n+1}-1=2N+1$ bricks. Measure lengths in units of $\frac1{N'}$; then brick $j$ of layer $n+1$ is $[j,j+1]$ and brick $k$ of layer $n$ is \[\Bigl[\frac{k(2N+1)}N,\ \frac{(k+1)(2N+1)}N\Bigr]=\Bigl[2k+\frac kN,\ 2k+2+\frac{k+1}N\Bigr].\] Because $0\le\frac kN<1$ and $0<\frac{k+1}N\le1$, the left end lies in $[2k,2k+1)$ and the right end lies in $(2k+2,2k+3]$. Hence $[j,j+1]$ overlaps it in positive length exactly for $j=2k,\,2k+1,\,2k+2$: for $j\le 2k-1$ we have $j+1\le 2k\le$ left end, and for $j\ge 2k+3$ we have $j\ge$ right end. This proves the claim.

Counting. A sequence of moves is a choice of a brick in each of the layers $2,3,\dots,2026$, each sharing a side with the previous one. By the claim there are $3$ choices at each of the $2025$ moves, whatever was chosen before, so the number of sequences is $\boxed{3^{2025}}$.

Solution 2 (Double counting the common refinement)

Take the width of the wall as the unit of length; brick $k$ ($0\le k\le N-1$) of layer $n$, with $N=2^n-1$, is the interval $\bigl[\frac kN,\frac{k+1}N\bigr]$, and two bricks of consecutive layers share a side exactly when their intervals overlap in positive length. Fix $n\le 2025$ and put $N'=2^{n+1}-1=2N+1$, the number of bricks of layer $n+1$.

Each brick has at least $3$ neighbours below. A brick of layer $n$ has width $\frac1N>\frac2{2N+1}=\frac2{N'}$. If it overlapped in positive length at most two bricks of layer $n+1$, those bricks would be adjacent (the overlapped bricks form a run) and would cover it, so its width would be at most $\frac 2{N'}$, a contradiction.

In total there are exactly $3N$ neighbouring pairs. The interior cut points of the two layers are $\frac kN$ ($0<k<N$) and $\frac j{N'}$ ($0<j<N'$). They are all different: $\frac kN=\frac j{N'}$ would give $kN'=jN$, and since $\gcd(N,N')=\gcd(N,2N+1)=1$ this forces $N\mid k$, impossible for $0<k<N$. So together they cut $[0,1]$ into $(N-1)+(N'-1)+1=N+N'-1=3N$ pieces. Each piece lies in exactly one brick of each layer, and conversely the overlap of a neighbouring pair contains no cut point in its interior, so it is exactly one piece. Thus the neighbouring pairs correspond one-to-one to the $3N$ pieces.

Conclusion. $N$ bricks, each with at least $3$ neighbours below, and $3N$ neighbouring pairs in total: every brick has exactly $3$ neighbours in the next layer. A sequence of moves chooses one of these $3$ neighbours at each of the $2025$ moves, so there are $\boxed{3^{2025}}$ sequences.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Factorization and Algebraic Cleanup

Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be complex numbers such that $\alpha\beta+\alpha+\beta+100=0$. Suppose that $|\alpha|=|\beta|=M$ for some nonnegative real number $M$. Determine, with proof, all possible values of $M$.

Show the answer

$M=10$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Solving for beta and comparing moduli)

The only possible value is $M=10$; it occurs, for example, for $\alpha=10$, $\beta=-10$: indeed $-100+10-10+100=0$.

Now let $\alpha,\beta$ satisfy the conditions. The equation reads $(\alpha+1)(\beta+1)=-99$, so $\alpha\ne-1$ and \[\beta=-\frac{\alpha+100}{\alpha+1}.\] Also $M\ne0$, since $\alpha=\beta=0$ does not satisfy the equation. Write $\alpha=M(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ and $c=\cos\theta\in[-1,1]$. The condition $|\beta|=M$ is $|\alpha+100|^2=M^2|\alpha+1|^2$, that is, \[M^2+200Mc+10000=M^2\bigl(M^2+2Mc+1\bigr).\] Rearranging, $2Mc\,(100-M^2)=M^4-10000=(M^2-100)(M^2+100)$, that is, \[(M^2-100)\bigl(M^2+100+2Mc\bigr)=0 .\] The second factor is positive: $M^2+100+2Mc\ge M^2-2M+100=(M-1)^2+99>0$. Hence $M^2=100$, and the only possible value is $\boxed{M=10}$.

Solution 2 (Conjugating the equation)

For $\alpha=10$, $\beta=-10$ we get $-100+10-10+100=0$ and $|\alpha|=|\beta|=10$, so $M=10$ occurs. We show no other value does.

$M=0$ is impossible, because $\alpha=\beta=0$ does not satisfy the equation. So $M>0$, and $\overline\alpha=M^2/\alpha$, $\overline\beta=M^2/\beta$. Let $s=\alpha+\beta$ and $p=\alpha\beta$; the equation says $p+s=-100$. Conjugating the equation gives $\overline\alpha\,\overline\beta+\overline\alpha+\overline\beta+100=0$, that is, \[\frac{M^4}{p}+\frac{M^2 s}{p}+100=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^4+M^2s+100p=0 .\] Substituting $s=-100-p$: \[M^4-100M^2-p(M^2-100)=0\iff (M^2-100)(M^2-p)=0 .\] Suppose $M^2\ne100$. Then $\alpha\beta=p=M^2=\alpha\overline\alpha$, so $\beta=\overline\alpha$. Then $s=\alpha+\overline\alpha=2\operatorname{Re}\alpha$, and $p+s=-100$ gives $M^2+2\operatorname{Re}\alpha+100=0$. But $2\operatorname{Re}\alpha\ge-2M$, so $0\ge M^2-2M+100=(M-1)^2+99>0$, a contradiction. Therefore $M^2=100$, and the answer is $\boxed{M=10}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

A set of rational numbers $S$ is called inclusive if $0$ is not an element of $S$, and for any (not necessarily distinct) elements $x$, $y$, and $z$ of $S$, the number $xy+z$ is also an element of $S$. Determine, with proof, all rational numbers $a$ for which there exists an inclusive set containing $a$.

Show the answer

All rational $a$ except $a=0$ and $a=-\frac1n$ for positive integers $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Repeated addition of a square, and valuation classes)

We show that an inclusive set containing $a$ exists exactly when $a\ne0$ and $a\ne-\frac1n$ for every positive integer $n$.

$a=0$ and $a=-\frac1n$ are impossible. $0$ is never in an inclusive set. Let $a=-\frac1n$ lie in an inclusive set $S$. Taking $x=y=a$ shows that $z\in S$ implies $z+a^2=z+\frac1{n^2}\in S$. Starting from $z=a$ and applying this $n$ times, $S$ contains \[-\frac1n+n\cdot\frac1{n^2}=0,\] which is impossible.

Positive $a$. The set $S=\mathbb Q_{>0}$ of positive rationals is inclusive: if $x,y,z>0$ then $xy+z>0$. It contains every positive $a$.

Negative $a$ not of the form $-\frac1n$. Write $a=-\frac uw$ in lowest terms with $u,w$ positive integers. Since $a\ne-\frac1n$, we have $u\ge2$; let $q$ be a prime dividing $u$, and let $k=v_q(a)\ge1$ be the exponent of $q$ in $a$ (for a nonzero rational $x$, $v_q(x)$ is the exponent of $q$ in its factorisation, negative if $q$ divides the denominator). Let \[S=\{x\in\mathbb Q:\ x\ne0,\ v_q(x)=k\}.\] Then $a\in S$ and $0\notin S$. If $x,y,z\in S$, then $v_q(xy)=2k>k=v_q(z)$, so $xy$ has a higher power of $q$ than $z$; writing $xy=q^{k}\cdot q^{k}r$ and $z=q^{k}t$ with $v_q(r)\ge0$, $v_q(t)=0$, we get $xy+z=q^k(q^kr+t)$, where $q^kr+t$ has numerator and denominator prime to $q$ (its value modulo $q$ is that of $t$). So $xy+z\ne0$ and $v_q(xy+z)=k$, i.e. $xy+z\in S$. Thus $S$ is inclusive.

Answer. Inclusive sets containing $a$ exist exactly for the rationals $a$ other than $0$ and $-\frac11,-\frac12,-\frac13,\dots$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

The numbers $1, 2, \ldots, 2026$ are written on a blackboard. An operation consists of replacing any number on the blackboard with the positive difference between the largest and smallest numbers currently on the blackboard. Determine, with proof, the least number of operations required to make all the numbers on the blackboard equal.

Show the answer

$3037$

Show the solution

Solution (The window at the first write of the final value)

Think of the board as $2026$ slots; an operation replaces the content of one slot by $D=\max-\min$. Two basic facts:

  • As long as the numbers are not all equal, $D\ge1$; so all numbers stay positive integers.

  • The maximum never increases, since $D=\max-\min<\max$.

Construction with $3037$ operations. First replace the current minimum $1012$ times: replacing $i$ (for $i=1,2,\dots,1012$) when the board contains $i,i+1,\dots,2026$ together with earlier written values, all of which lie between $i$ and $2026$, writes $2026-i$. After these $1012$ operations the minimum is $1013$, the maximum is $2026$, and $D=1013$. Now replace, one at a time, every number different from $1013$ except one copy of $2026$ by $1013$; during this the minimum stays $1013$ and the maximum stays $2026$, so each operation writes $1013$. Finally replace the last $2026$ by $D=1013$. The numbers different from $1013$ after the first phase are $1014,\dots,2026$ and the written values $1014,\dots,2025$, that is $1013+1012=2025$ numbers, so the second phase uses $2025$ operations. In total $1012+2025=3037$ operations, and all numbers become $1013$.

Lower bound. Consider any sequence of operations making all numbers equal; cutting it at the first moment when all numbers are equal can only shorten it, so we may assume the numbers are not all equal before its last operation. Let $v$ be the final common value. Before the last operation the numbers are not all equal and all but one equal $v$; let the remaining one be $y\ne v$. The operation writes $D=|y-v|$ into the slot of $y$, and $D=v$; since $y>0$ this gives $y=2v$. So at that moment the maximum is $2v$, and since the maximum never increases, $2v\le2026$, i.e. $v\le1013$.

Let $T$ be the first operation that writes $v$ (the last operation writes $v$, so $T$ exists). Just before $T$, $\max-\min=v$, so all numbers lie in a window $[m,m+v]$, which contains $v+1$ integers. Let $w\le v+1$ be the number of slots whose initial value lies in this window. Classify the slots:

  • A slot whose initial value is outside the window was overwritten before $T$, by an operation writing a value different from $v$ (no earlier operation writes $v$). Since it ends with $v$, it is written at least once more: at least $2$ operations.

  • A slot whose initial value is in the window but different from $v$ is written at least once.

At most one initial value equals $v$, so the number of operations is at least \[2(2026-w)+(w-1)=4051-w\ge 4051-(v+1)=4050-v\ge 4050-1013=3037 .\]

Hence the least number of operations is $\boxed{3037}$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral with circumcenter $O$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$. Suppose that $\angle CMD=90^\circ$ and $\overline{AD}\perp\overline{BC}$. Prove that the reflection of $O$ over $\overline{AB}$ lies on line $CD$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Axes along the two perpendicular sides)

The lines $AD$ and $BC$ are perpendicular, so they meet at a point $P$. No vertex equals $P$: for instance $P=A$ would put $A$ on line $BC$, and three distinct points of a circle are never collinear. Take $P$ as origin, line $AD$ as the $x$-axis and line $BC$ as the $y$-axis: \[A=(a,0),\quad D=(d,0),\quad B=(0,b),\quad C=(0,c),\qquad a,b,c,d\ne0 .\] The power of $P$ with respect to the circle gives (with signs) $ad=bc$. The center $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors $x=\frac{a+d}2$ of $AD$ and $y=\frac{b+c}2$ of $BC$, so $O=\bigl(\frac{a+d}2,\frac{b+c}2\bigr)$. Also $M=\bigl(\frac a2,\frac b2\bigr)$.

Since $OM\perp AB$ and $M\in AB$, the reflection of $O$ over $AB$ is $O'=2M-O=\bigl(\frac{a-d}2,\frac{b-c}2\bigr)$.

The right angle. $\angle CMD=90^\circ$ means $(C-M)\cdot(D-M)=0$: \[-\frac a2\Bigl(d-\frac a2\Bigr)-\frac b2\Bigl(c-\frac b2\Bigr)=0\iff a^2+b^2=2(ad+bc)=4ad .\]

$O'$ on $CD$. The line $CD$ is $\frac xd+\frac yc=1$. For $O'$, \[\frac{a-d}{2d}+\frac{b-c}{2c}=\frac12\Bigl(\frac ad+\frac bc\Bigr)-1=\frac12\cdot\frac{a^2+b^2}{ad}-1=\frac12\cdot4-1=1,\] where we used $\frac bc=\frac{b^2}{bc}=\frac{b^2}{ad}$. Hence $O'$ lies on line $CD$.

Solution 2 (Vectors from the corner and equal-length vectors)

Let $P$ be the intersection of the perpendicular lines $AD$ and $BC$; it is not a vertex, because three distinct points of a circle are not collinear. Use vectors with origin $P$. Then $A,D$ lie on one line through $P$ and $B,C$ on the perpendicular line, so $A\cdot B=A\cdot C=D\cdot B=D\cdot C=0$, and the power of $P$ gives $A\cdot D=B\cdot C$.

The center. The projection of $O$ on line $AD$ is the midpoint $\frac{A+D}2$ and its projection on line $BC$ is $\frac{B+C}2$; as these lines are perpendicular through the origin, $O=\frac{A+D}2+\frac{B+C}2$. With $M=\frac{A+B}2$ and $N=\frac{C+D}2$ (the midpoint of $CD$) this reads $O=M+N$. The reflection of $O$ over $AB$ is $O'=2M-O=M-N$ (as $OM\perp AB$), so \[O'-N=u+w,\qquad u=M-N,\quad w=-N .\]

Three facts.

  • $|w|=\frac{CD}2$: since $C\cdot D=0$, $|C+D|^2=|C|^2+|D|^2=|C-D|^2$.

  • $|u|=\frac{CD}2$: $\angle CMD=90^\circ$ puts $M$ on the circle with diameter $CD$, centered at $N$ ($M\ne C,D$, because $M$ is inside the circumcircle).

  • $u-w=M$ is perpendicular to $CD$: $2M\cdot(D-C)=A\cdot D-A\cdot C+B\cdot D-B\cdot C=A\cdot D-B\cdot C=0$.

Conclusion. From $|u|=|w|$ we get $(u+w)\cdot(u-w)=|u|^2-|w|^2=0$, so $u+w\perp M$. Also $M\ne0$ (otherwise $P=M$ would lie on $AB$ and on $AD$, forcing $P=A$). In the plane, the two vectors $u+w$ and $D-C$ are both perpendicular to the nonzero vector $M$, so they are parallel. Hence $O'-N\parallel D-C$, and as $N$ lies on $CD$, so does $O'$.

Problem 7

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

An infinite sequence $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ of integers is called $r$-close if every integer appears in the sequence exactly once and $|a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}|\ge r|a_{n+1}-a_n|$ for all integers $n\ge 1$. Determine, with proof, all nonnegative real numbers $r$ for which an $r$-close sequence exists.

Show the answer

$0\le r<2$

Show the solution

Solution (A distance lemma for r at least 2 and a block construction)

Write $d_n=|a_{n+1}-a_n|$; since the terms are distinct, $d_n\ge1$. We prove that $r$-close sequences exist exactly for $0\le r<2$.

No sequence for $r\ge2$. Then $d_{n+1}\ge2d_n$ for all $n$. First, for $m\ge n$, \[d_n+d_{n+1}+\dots+d_m\le d_{m+1}-d_n ,\] by induction on $m$: for $m=n$ it is $2d_n\le d_{n+1}$, and if it holds for $m$ then $d_n+\dots+d_{m+1}\le 2d_{m+1}-d_n\le d_{m+2}-d_n$. Consequently, for $k\ge n+2$, \[|a_k-a_n|\ \ge\ d_{k-1}-(d_n+\dots+d_{k-2})\ \ge\ d_{k-1}-(d_{k-1}-d_n)=d_n ,\] and also $|a_{n+1}-a_n|=d_n$. Apply this with $n=2$: every term $a_k$ with $k\ne2$, $k\ge 3$, is at distance at least $d_2\ge 2d_1\ge2$ from $a_2$. So the two integers $a_2-1$ and $a_2+1$ can only appear as $a_1$, which is impossible for both. Hence no $r$-close sequence exists.

A sequence for $0\le r<2$. Fix a rational $q=\frac uv$ ($u>v\ge1$ integers) with $\max(r,1)<q<2$. Since $\sum_{j\ge1}q^{-j}=\frac1{q-1}>1>r-1$, we can fix an integer $m\ge3$ with \[\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}q^{-j}>r-1 .\] Start with $a_1=0$ and build the sequence in blocks. Each block starts at the last term $p$ written so far and ends at a target $t$: the integer of smallest absolute value not yet in the sequence (taking the nonnegative one in case of a tie). Let $\Delta=t-p$. Choose a positive integer $X$ and put \[x_i=X\,v^{m-1-i}u^{i-1}\ (1\le i\le m-1),\qquad x_m=x_1+\dots+x_{m-1}-\Delta .\] The block consists of the steps $+x_1,+x_2,\dots,+x_{m-1}$ followed by $-x_m$; it ends at $p+\sum_{i<m}x_i-x_m=p+\Delta=t$. We take $X$ so large that

  • $p+x_1$ exceeds every term written so far and also the target $t$,

  • $x_1\ge r\cdot$(the last step of the previous block), and

  • $(\sigma-(r-1))\,x_{m-1}\ge\Delta$.

Then the ratios inside the block are fine: $x_{i+1}=q\,x_i\ge r\,x_i$, and, as $x_1+\dots+x_{m-2}=\sigma x_{m-1}$, \[x_m=x_{m-1}+\sigma x_{m-1}-\Delta\ \ge\ x_{m-1}+(r-1)x_{m-1}=r\,x_{m-1}>0 .\] Between blocks the condition holds by the second requirement. The new terms $p+x_1<p+x_1+x_2<\dots<p+x_1+\dots+x_{m-1}$ are larger than every earlier term and than $t$, hence new, distinct from each other and from $t$; and the last new term $t$ was not yet used. So no integer is repeated, and $|a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}|\ge r|a_{n+1}-a_n|$ holds throughout.

Finally every integer $w$ appears: as long as $w$ is missing, each block's target has absolute value at most $|w|$, and there are only $2|w|+1$ such integers, each targeted at most once; so after at most $2|w|+1$ blocks $w$ has appeared.

Therefore an $r$-close sequence exists if and only if $\boxed{0\le r<2}$.

Problem 8

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Monovariants and Directional Change

Let $n\ge 2$ be a positive integer and let $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ be a permutation of the numbers $1, 2, \ldots, n$. Marin makes a single move on this permutation by performing the following steps:

  • First, he chooses an integer $1\le k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. This integer can be different for different moves.

  • Then, he picks two nonintersecting sets $I=\{i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_k\}$ and $J=\{j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_k\}$ such that $1\le i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_k\le n$ and $1\le j_1<j_2<\cdots<j_k\le n$.

  • Finally, he swaps the numbers $a_{i_s}$ and $a_{j_s}$ in the current permutation for all integers $1\le s\le k$.

Let $f(n)$ denote the smallest positive integer such that it is possible for Marin to reach $(1,2,\ldots,n)$ from any starting permutation in at most $f(n)$ moves. Prove that $\lceil\log_3 n\rceil\le f(n)\le\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$.

Show the solution

Solution (Longest decreasing subsequence and halving)

Call a move with sets $I,J$ as in the statement; it moves the entry in position $i_s$ to position $j_s$ and the entry in position $j_s$ to position $i_s$, and leaves the other positions fixed.

Lower bound $f(n)\ge\lceil\log_3n\rceil$. Let $L(\pi)$ be the length of the longest decreasing subsequence of a permutation $\pi$. We claim one move decreases $L$ by at most a factor $3$: if $\pi'$ is obtained from $\pi$ by a move, then $L(\pi')\ge L(\pi)/3$.

Split the positions into three classes: $I$, $J$, and the rest $K$. On each class the move preserves the order of positions: positions of $K$ stay; $i_s\mapsto j_s$ with $i_s<i_t\Rightarrow j_s<j_t$; and similarly $j_s\mapsto i_s$. Take a decreasing subsequence of $\pi$ of length $L(\pi)$; one of the three classes contains at least $L(\pi)/3$ of its positions. The entries in these positions keep their values and the relative order of their positions after the move, so they still form a decreasing subsequence of $\pi'$. This proves the claim.

Start from $(n,n-1,\dots,1)$, with $L=n$. After $t$ moves $L\ge n/3^t$, while the identity has $L=1$. So reaching the identity needs $3^t\ge n$, i.e. $t\ge\lceil\log_3n\rceil$.

Upper bound $f(n)\le\lceil\log_2n\rceil$.

One split move. Let a block of consecutive positions of length $\ell\ge2$ contain some set of values, and let $z=\lceil \ell/2\rceil$. Call the $z$ smallest values of the block small. Let $I$ be the set of positions among the first $z$ positions of the block holding a value that is not small, and $J$ the set of positions among the last $\ell-z$ holding a small value. Counting small values gives $|I|=|J|$, and $|I|\le\min(z,\ell-z)$. Swapping $I$ with $J$ in increasing order puts all small values into the first $z$ positions.

Doing many blocks at once. If the positions $1,\dots,n$ are cut into consecutive blocks, the split moves of all blocks can be performed as a single move: take $I$ and $J$ to be the unions over blocks. Because all positions of an earlier block precede those of a later block, listing $I$ and $J$ in increasing order pairs the $I$-positions of each block with the $J$-positions of the same block, as required; $I\cap J=\varnothing$; and $|I|=|J|\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ since the blocks are disjoint and each contributes at most half of its length. (If $I$ is empty, skip the move.)

The algorithm. Start with the single block $\{1,\dots,n\}$ holding the values $1,\dots,n$. In each round, perform the combined split move and then cut every block of length $\ell\ge2$ into its first $\lceil \ell/2\rceil$ and its last $\lfloor \ell/2\rfloor$ positions. By induction, after each round every block holds exactly the values equal to its positions (as a set): this is true at the start, and a split puts the smallest $\lceil\ell/2\rceil$ values of a block into its first $\lceil \ell/2\rceil$ positions. Block lengths go from $\ell$ to at most $\lceil\ell/2\rceil$, and $\lceil\lceil x\rceil/2\rceil=\lceil x/2\rceil$, so after $t$ rounds all blocks have length at most $\lceil n/2^t\rceil$. After $t=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ rounds all blocks have length $1$, so each position holds its own value: the permutation is $(1,2,\dots,n)$. Hence $f(n)\le\lceil\log_2n\rceil$.

Together, $\lceil\log_3 n\rceil\le f(n)\le\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two circles centred on BC, then a tangent computation)

Both conditions and the claim are unchanged by the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ (it swaps $B$ and $C$ and the angles $\angle ABC$, $\angle ACB$). Since the triangle is scalene, $D\ne M$, and we may assume that $D$ lies between $M$ and $C$. Choose coordinates with \[M=(0,0),\quad B=(-1,0),\quad C=(1,0),\quad A=(\delta,h),\quad D=(\delta,0),\qquad 0<\delta<1,\ h>0 .\] ($|\delta|<1$ because the triangle is acute, so $D$ lies inside $BC$.)

Finding $P$. The condition $\angle DPM=90^\circ$ says that $P$ lies on the circle with diameter $DM$: $x^2+y^2=\delta x$. The condition $PB/PC=AB/AC$ reads $AC^2\cdot PB^2=AB^2\cdot PC^2$. Here $AB^2=(1+\delta)^2+h^2$, $AC^2=(1-\delta)^2+h^2$, so $AC^2-AB^2=-4\delta$ and $AC^2+AB^2=2(1+\delta^2+h^2)$. Expanding $AC^2\bigl((x+1)^2+y^2\bigr)=AB^2\bigl((x-1)^2+y^2\bigr)$: \[(AC^2-AB^2)(x^2+y^2+1)+2x(AC^2+AB^2)=0\iff -4\delta(x^2+y^2+1)+4x(1+\delta^2+h^2)=0 .\] Substituting $x^2+y^2=\delta x$ gives $-4\delta^2x-4\delta+4x(1+\delta^2+h^2)=0$, so $x=\frac{\delta}{1+h^2}$, and then $y^2=x(\delta-x)=\frac{\delta^2h^2}{(1+h^2)^2}$. As $P$ is inside the triangle, $y>0$: \[P=(t,\,th),\qquad t=\frac{\delta}{1+h^2}\in(0,1).\]

The angle. Let $x=\angle PBC$ and $y=\angle PCB$. Then $\tan x=\frac{th}{1+t}$ and $\tan y=\frac{th}{1-t}$, so \[\tan(x+y)=\frac{\frac{2th}{1-t^2}}{1-\frac{t^2h^2}{1-t^2}}=\frac{2th}{1-t^2(1+h^2)}=\frac{2th}{1-t\delta}=\frac{2\delta h}{1+h^2-\delta^2},\] using $t(1+h^2)=\delta$. On the other hand $\tan\angle ABC=\frac h{1+\delta}$ and $\tan\angle ACB=\frac h{1-\delta}$, so \[\tan(\angle ACB-\angle ABC)=\frac{\frac h{1-\delta}-\frac h{1+\delta}}{1+\frac{h^2}{1-\delta^2}}=\frac{2\delta h}{1-\delta^2+h^2}.\] Since the triangle is acute, $0<\angle ACB-\angle ABC<90^\circ$, so this common tangent is positive and finite. The angle $x+y$ lies in $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ and has the same positive tangent, so $x+y=\angle ACB-\angle ABC$. Therefore \[\angle BPC=180^\circ-(x+y)=180^\circ-\bigl|\angle ABC-\angle ACB\bigr| .\]

Solution 2 (A spiral similarity with angle ninety degrees)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$, and orient $ABC$ counterclockwise, so that $\angle(BA,BC)=-\angle B$ and $\angle(CA,CB)=\angle C$ (writing $\angle B=\angle ABC$, $\angle C=\angle ACB$).

At most two candidates. The points $X$ with $\angle DXM=90^\circ$ lie on the circle with diameter $DM$; the points with $XB/XC=AB/AC$ lie on the Apollonius circle of $B,C$ through $A$. Both circles have their centers on line $BC$, and they are different (the Apollonius circle passes through $A$, while $\angle ADM=90^\circ$ shows $A$ is not on the first circle). So they have at most two common points, symmetric in $BC$.

A point satisfying both conditions. Let $A'$ be the reflection of $A$ in $BC$; the line $AA'$ meets $BC$ at $D$, the midpoint of $AA'$. Let $Q$ be the center of the spiral similarity $S$ with $S(A)=B$, $S(A')=C$; its angle is the angle from $AA'$ to $BC$, which is $90^\circ$. Then:

  • $S$ maps the midpoint $D$ of $AA'$ to the midpoint $M$ of $BC$, so $\angle DQM=90^\circ$ ($Q\ne D,M$ because $S(D)=M\ne D$).

  • The spiral similarity with center $Q$ taking $A$ to $A'$ takes $B$ to $C$ (triangles $QAB$ and $QA'C$ are similar), so $\frac{QB}{QC}=\frac{AB}{A'C}=\frac{AB}{AC}$.

Hence the given point $P$ is $Q$ or the reflection of $Q$ in $BC$.

The angle at $Q$. Since $\angle(QA,QB)$ equals the angle of $S$, which equals $\angle(DA,DB)$, the points $Q,D,A,B$ are concyclic; likewise $Q,D,A',C$ are concyclic. The similarity $QAB\sim QA'C$ gives $\angle(QB,QC)=\angle(QA,QA')$, and \[\angle(QA,QA')=\angle(QA,QD)+\angle(QD,QA')=\angle(BA,BD)+\angle(CD,CA')=\angle(BA,BC)+\angle(CA,CB),\] where $\angle(CB,CA')=\angle(CA,CB)$ because $A'$ is the reflection of $A$ in $BC$. So $\angle(QB,QC)=\angle C-\angle B$. A reflection in $BC$ only changes the sign of a directed angle, so in both cases $\angle(PB,PC)=\pm(\angle C-\angle B)$, and the undirected angle $\angle BPC$ equals $|\angle B-\angle C|$ or $180^\circ-|\angle B-\angle C|$.

Choosing the right value. $P$ is strictly inside the triangle, so $\angle BPC>\angle BAC$. Since $\angle B,\angle C<90^\circ$, we have $\angle BAC=180^\circ-\angle B-\angle C>|\angle B-\angle C|$. Therefore $\angle BPC\ne|\angle B-\angle C|$, and \[\angle BPC=180^\circ-|\angle ABC-\angle ACB| .\]

Show the solution

Solution (A recursion with step three)

Let $S_n=\varepsilon_1F_1+\dots+\varepsilon_nF_n$ be the position after $n$ steps ($\varepsilon_i=\pm1$ independent and fair) and $E_n=\mathbb E|S_n|$. We use:

  • (a) for $x\ge0$, $F>0$: the average of $|x+F|$ and $|x-F|$ is $\max(x,F)=F+(x-F)^+$;

  • (b) $|S_k|\le F_1+\dots+F_k=F_{k+2}-1$.

So $\mathbb E\bigl[(|S_{n-2}|-F_{n-2})^+\bigr]=\frac12E_{n-3}$. Altogether, for $n\ge4$, \[E_n=F_n+\tfrac14E_{n-3}.\] With $E_1=1$: $E_4=3+\frac14=\frac{13}4$, $E_7=13+\frac{13}{16}=\frac{221}{16}$, and \[E_{10}=55+\frac{221}{64}=\boxed{\frac{3741}{64}}.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Gaussian periods and symmetric sums)

Setup. The powers $\zeta,\dots,\zeta^{18}$ form a basis of $\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ over $\mathbb Q$, which explains the uniqueness. The automorphisms of $\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ are $\sigma_t:\zeta\mapsto\zeta^t$, $t=1,\dots,18$; they form a cyclic group generated by $\sigma=\sigma_2$ ($2$ is a primitive root mod $19$). The cubic $f(x)=x^3+16x^2+3x-229$ has no rational roots (none of $\pm1,\pm229$ is a root), so it is irreducible; its roots lie in $\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ (one of them does, and $\sigma$ maps roots to roots). The cyclic group of order $18$ acts transitively on the three roots, through a cyclic group of order $3$; so $\sigma^3=\sigma_8$ fixes every root and $\sigma$ permutes the roots cyclically.

The subgroup $H=\{1,7,8,11,12,18\}$ of cubes mod $19$ is generated by $8$, with cosets $H$, $2H$, $4H$. A number $\sum a_k\zeta^k$ is fixed by $\sigma_8$ iff $a_k$ is constant on these cosets. Let $\eta_0,\eta_1,\eta_2$ be the sums of $\zeta^k$ over $H$, $2H$, $4H$ (Gaussian periods); $\sigma$ maps $\eta_0\to\eta_1\to\eta_2\to\eta_0$. So the largest root is $\rho=u\eta_0+v\eta_1+w\eta_2$ with rational $u,v,w$, and the three roots are $\rho$ and its cyclic shifts. The answer is $6(u^2+v^2+w^2)$.

Period sums. $\eta_0+\eta_1+\eta_2=\sum_{k=1}^{18}\zeta^k=-1$. Next, $\sum_i\eta_i^2$ is a sum of $3\cdot36=108$ terms $\zeta^{a+b}$ ($a,b$ in the same coset). Since $-1=18\in H$, exactly $18$ of them have $a+b\equiv0$; the map $(a,b)\mapsto(2a,2b)$ permutes the pairs and multiplies $a+b$ by $2$, so each nonzero residue occurs equally often, $\frac{90}{18}=5$ times. Hence $\sum\eta_i^2=18-5=13$, and $\eta_0\eta_1+\eta_1\eta_2+\eta_2\eta_0=\frac{1-13}2=-6$.

Matching. The sum of the roots is $-(u+v+w)=-16$. Conjugates. For $k=1,\dots,18$ let $\sigma_k$ be the map $\sum c_j\zeta^j\mapsto\sum c_j\zeta^{jk}$ ($c_j$ rational). It respects sums and products (it replaces $\zeta$ by $\zeta^k$, another root of $t^{18}+\dots+t+1$), so $f(\sigma_k(x))=\sigma_k(f(x))=0$: every $x_k=\sigma_k(x)$ is a root of $f$, hence real.

We show that each root of $f$ appears exactly six times among $x_1,\dots,x_{18}$. The set $G=\{k:\ x_k=x\}$ is closed under multiplication mod $19$, so it is a subgroup of the nonzero residues, and $x_k=x_l$ exactly when $l\equiv km$ for some $m\in G$. So each value taken by the $x_k$ is taken exactly $|G|$ times; let $v_1,\dots,v_d$ be the distinct values, so $d\,|G|=18$ and $d\le3$. Each $\sigma_m$ permutes $v_1,\dots,v_d$ (as $\sigma_m(x_k)=x_{km}$), so it fixes every coefficient of $g(t)=(t-v_1)\cdots(t-v_d)$. A number $\sum c_j\zeta^j$ fixed by all $\sigma_m$ has $c_{jm}=c_j$ for all $j,m$, so all $c_j$ equal some $c$, and the number is $c(\zeta+\dots+\zeta^{18})=-c$, rational. Hence $g$ has rational coefficients and the root $x$. Now $f$ has no rational root (modulo $2$ it is $t^3+t+1$, which has no root $0$ or $1$; a rational root of this monic integer cubic would be an integer), so $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ (a cubic with a factorization has a linear factor). Let $h$ be a nonzero rational polynomial of least degree with root $x$. Dividing any rational polynomial with root $x$ by $h$, the remainder has root $x$ and smaller degree, so it is $0$: $h$ divides every such polynomial. In particular $h\mid f$, so $h$ is a constant multiple of $f$ by irreducibility, and $h\mid g$ gives $\deg g\ge3$. Thus $d=3$ and $|G|=6$.

Solution 1 (Triangular coordinates and sliding windows)

Let $\mathbf u$ point from the center to $A$, and let $\mathbf v,\mathbf w$ be $\mathbf u$ turned by $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$ clockwise; $\mathbf u+\mathbf v+\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$. Hops of lengths $a,b,c$ end at \[a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v+c\mathbf w=(a-c)\mathbf u+(b-c)\mathbf v=(a-c)\mathbf u-(b-c)\mathbf e,\] where $\mathbf e=-\mathbf v$ is the unit vector at $60^\circ$ from $\mathbf u$. In the coordinates $X\mathbf u+Y\mathbf e$ the hexagon (vertices $100\mathbf u,100\mathbf e,100(\mathbf e-\mathbf u),\dots$) is $|X|,|Y|,|X+Y|\le100$. With $X=a-c$, $Y=c-b$, $X+Y=a-b$, Kelvin ends inside iff \[\max(a,b,c)-\min(a,b,c)\le100 .\] Different triples give different paths, so we count triples in $\{0,\dots,200\}^3$ with range at most $100$. Each such triple lies in a window $\{m,\dots,m+100\}$ with $0\le m\le100$, and the windows containing it are consecutive. So, counting windows minus overlaps of consecutive windows, \[101\cdot101^3-100\cdot100^3=101^4-100^4=\boxed{4060401}.\]

Solution 2 (Counting by the range)

Let $\mathbf u$ be the unit vector from the center towards $A$ and $\mathbf e$ the unit vector at $60^\circ$ from it, towards $B$ (the case of the opposite orientation is symmetric). The three hop directions are $\mathbf u$, $-\mathbf e$ and $\mathbf e-\mathbf u$ (each turned $120^\circ$ clockwise from the previous), so hops of lengths $a,b,c$ end at \[a\mathbf u-b\mathbf e+c(\mathbf e-\mathbf u)=(a-c)\mathbf u+(c-b)\mathbf e .\] The hexagon, with vertices $\pm100\mathbf u$, $\pm100\mathbf e$, $\pm100(\mathbf e-\mathbf u)$, is the set of points $X\mathbf u+Y\mathbf e$ with $|X|,|Y|,|X+Y|\le100$. With $X=a-c$, $Y=c-b$, $X+Y=a-b$, Kelvin ends in the hexagon iff $\max(a,b,c)-\min(a,b,c)\le100$, and different triples are different paths.

Count the triples by their range $r=\max-\min$. The number of triples with minimum exactly $m$ and maximum exactly $m+r$ is $1$ for $r=0$ and, by inclusion-exclusion, $(r+1)^3-2r^3+(r-1)^3=6r$ for $r\ge1$; there are $201-r$ choices of $m$. So the count is \[201+\sum_{r=1}^{100}6r(201-r)=201+6\Bigl(201\cdot5050-\frac{100\cdot101\cdot201}6\Bigr)=201+6\cdot676700=\boxed{4060401}.\]
