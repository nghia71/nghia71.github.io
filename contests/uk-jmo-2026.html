Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

In the following sum, the letters $Y$, $U$, $M$ and $S$ stand for single digits. \[\begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c} & Y & U & M & S\\ + & & Y & U & M\\ \hline & 2 & 0 & 2 & 6 \end{array}\]

  • (a) Find one possibility for the values of $Y$, $U$, $M$ and $S$. Only the answer is required.

  • (b) Prove that this is the only possible solution. A full explanation is required.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Line up the two addends by place value. Mark the places where each letter occurs in each addend.
Hint 2: key idea
Write each addend as a sum of its digit values and collect the two contributions from each repeated letter. This gives one equation with a different weight on each digit. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the smallest and largest possible contributions from the other digits to restrict $Y$. Subtract the contribution of each digit once it is determined and repeat the bounds for the next digit. Check the remaining units digit against the original addition.
Common trap
Each unknown fits in one digit, so the largest allowance for any unfixed digit is $9$.
Show the answer

$Y=1$, $U=8$, $M=4$, $S=2$ ($1842+184=2026$)

Show solution 1 – Place value and size

(a) $Y=1$, $U=8$, $M=4$, $S=2$: indeed $1842+184=2026$.

(b) Write the two numbers using place value: \[(1000Y+100U+10M+S)+(100Y+10U+M)=1100Y+110U+11M+S=2026 .\]

  • If $Y\ge2$ the left side is at least $2200>2026$. If $Y=0$ the left side is at most $110\cdot9+11\cdot9+9=1098<2026$. So $Y=1$ and $110U+11M+S=926$.

  • If $U\le7$ then $110U+11M+S\le770+99+9=878<926$; and $U=9$ gives $990>926$. So $U=8$ and $11M+S=46$.

  • If $M\le3$ then $S\ge46-33=13$, impossible for a digit; if $M\ge5$ then $11M\ge55>46$. So $M=4$ and $S=2$.

Each step left only one possibility, so $Y=1$, $U=8$, $M=4$, $S=2$ is the only solution: $\boxed{1842+184=2026}$.

Show solution 2 – Eleven times the smaller number

(a) $1842+184=2026$, so $Y=1$, $U=8$, $M=4$, $S=2$.

(b) Let $N$ be the number with digits $Y,U,M$, that is, $N=100Y+10U+M$. The top number $YUMS$ is $10N+S$, so the sum is \[10N+S+N=11N+S=2026 .\] Here $S=2026-11N$ is a digit, $0\le S\le9$, so the multiple $11N$ of $11$ lies between $2017$ and $2026$. The multiples of $11$ near $2026$ are $11\cdot183=2013$, $11\cdot184=2024$ and $11\cdot185=2035$, and only $2024$ lies in that range. So $11N=2024$, $N=184$ and $S=2$.

Finally $100Y+10U+M=184$ with digits $Y,U,M$ forces $Y=1$, $U=8$, $M=4$ (a number has only one decimal representation). Hence the solution $\boxed{1842+184=2026}$ is unique.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Divisibility Tests

Prove that every number that can be written as the product of three consecutive positive integers can also be written as the sum of three consecutive positive integers.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a few sums of three consecutive positive integers. Compare each total with the middle integer of its three terms.
Hint 2: key idea
Express the sum in terms of its middle integer. Then examine the remainders modulo $3$ of the three consecutive factors in the product. (LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization)
Hint 3: approach
Use the consecutive factors to show that the product divided by $3$ is an integer. Compare the least possible product with the lower limit on the middle term of a positive triple.
Common trap
The proposed middle term must exceed one so that its predecessor is positive.
Show solution 1 – Three times the middle number

The sum of three consecutive positive integers $k-1$, $k$, $k+1$ is $3k$, and these are all positive exactly when $k\ge2$. So we must show: if $P=n(n+1)(n+2)$ with $n\ge1$, then $P=3k$ for some integer $k\ge2$.

Among any three consecutive integers exactly one is a multiple of $3$ (the remainders on division by $3$ are $0,1,2$ in some order). Hence $P$ is a multiple of $3$; let $k=\tfrac P3$, an integer. Since $n\ge1$, $P\ge1\cdot2\cdot3=6$, so $k\ge2$. Therefore \[n(n+1)(n+2)=(k-1)+k+(k+1)\qquad\text{with }k=\tfrac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3}\ge2,\] a sum of three consecutive positive integers. For example $2\cdot3\cdot4=24=7+8+9$.

Show solution 2 – Using a choice number

The number of ways to choose $3$ objects from $n+2$ objects is \[\binom{n+2}{3}=\frac{(n+2)(n+1)n}{6},\] and a number of ways is a whole number. So $n(n+1)(n+2)=6m$ for the positive integer $m=\binom{n+2}{3}$ (for $n\ge1$ there is at least one way to choose, so $m\ge1$).

Now take the three consecutive integers $2m-1$, $2m$, $2m+1$. They are positive because $m\ge1$, and their sum is \[(2m-1)+2m+(2m+1)=6m=n(n+1)(n+2).\] So every product of three consecutive positive integers is a sum of three consecutive positive integers; the middle one is $\tfrac13$ of the product.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 25: Algebraic Word Problem Models · Money, Coins, and Value

Blackbeard and Brownbeard each have a pile of gold coins. One night, Blackbeard takes $3$ gold coins from Brownbeard's pile and adds them to his pile, and Brownbeard responds by taking half of the coins in Blackbeard's pile and adding them to his pile. The next night, Brownbeard takes $3$ coins from Blackbeard's pile and adds them to his pile, and Blackbeard responds by taking half of the coins in Brownbeard's pile and adding them to his pile. Both pirates now have the same number of gold coins as they had at the beginning.

Prove that the total number of gold coins is divisible by $3$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Label the two starting piles $x$ and $y$. Record the sizes of both piles after the first transfer before applying the next instruction.
Hint 2: key idea
Follow the transfers forward in order, updating both piles at each step. Since the total stays fixed, it suffices to impose the return condition on just one pirate's pile. (LPS VI, Chapter 25: Algebraic Word Problem Models)
Hint 3: approach
Find Brownbeard's pile just before Blackbeard takes half of it on the second night. Equate the amount Brownbeard then keeps to $y$, and solve the resulting relation for $x$. Use that relation to rewrite the original total.
Common trap
Each half is taken from the current pile after that night's three-coin transfer.
Show solution 1 – Following the piles forwards

Let Blackbeard start with $x$ coins and Brownbeard with $y$ coins. We follow the two piles (Blackbeard, Brownbeard):

  • Blackbeard takes $3$: $\ (x+3,\ y-3)$.

  • Brownbeard takes half of Blackbeard's pile: $\ \bigl(\tfrac{x+3}{2},\ y-3+\tfrac{x+3}{2}\bigr)$.

  • Brownbeard takes $3$: $\ \bigl(\tfrac{x+3}{2}-3,\ y+\tfrac{x+3}{2}\bigr)=\bigl(\tfrac{x-3}{2},\ y+\tfrac{x+3}{2}\bigr)$.

  • Blackbeard takes half of Brownbeard's pile: Brownbeard is left with $\tfrac12\bigl(y+\tfrac{x+3}{2}\bigr)=\tfrac{2y+x+3}{4}$.

At the end Brownbeard has $y$ coins again, so \[\frac{2y+x+3}{4}=y,\qquad 2y+x+3=4y,\qquad x=2y-3 .\] (Blackbeard then also has $x$ again, because the total never changes.) The total number of coins is \[x+y=(2y-3)+y=3y-3=3(y-1),\] which is divisible by $3$.

Show solution 2 – Working modulo 3

Let Blackbeard start with $x$ coins and Brownbeard with $y$ coins, and follow Brownbeard's pile modulo $3$ only.

  • Moving $3$ coins from one pile to the other does not change either pile modulo $3$.

  • Taking half of a pile: if a pile has $2h$ coins, then $h\equiv4h=2\cdot2h\pmod 3$, so half of a pile is congruent to twice the pile modulo $3$.

On the first night, after Blackbeard takes $3$ coins, his pile is $x+3\equiv x$ and Brownbeard's is $y-3\equiv y$. Brownbeard then adds half of Blackbeard's pile, which is $\equiv2x$, so he has $\equiv y+2x$. On the second night Brownbeard takes $3$ coins, which changes nothing modulo $3$, and then Blackbeard takes half of Brownbeard's pile, leaving Brownbeard with half of it, that is, \[\equiv2(y+2x)=2y+4x\equiv2y+x\pmod 3 .\] At the end Brownbeard again has $y$ coins, so $2y+x\equiv y$, that is, $x+y\equiv0\pmod 3$. The total number of coins is divisible by $3$.

Problem 4

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Complementary Counting

Suppose the vertices of a cube are labelled from $1$ to $8$. How many ways are there to choose three of these labels so that the corresponding three vertices are not all on the same face? As this is an Olympiad problem, you must demonstrate that all possible choices are included in your count.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the cube and try a few selections of three vertices. Mark which selections fit on a single face.
Hint 2: key idea
Count all three-label choices and count the unwanted ones separately. For the unwanted choices, start with a fixed face and choose three of its vertices. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
Set up the total number of unordered triples and the number of triples on one face. Compare the intersection of two faces with the size of a triple to see whether counting by face repeats any choice.
Common trap
Choose subsets of labels, so different orders of the same three labels count once.
Show the answer

$32$

Show solution 1 – Count the complement

There are $\binom83=\dfrac{8\cdot7\cdot6}{6}=56$ ways to choose three of the eight labels.

Now count the choices whose three vertices are on one face. A face has $4$ vertices, so it contains $\binom43=4$ triples, and the cube has $6$ faces, giving $6\cdot4=24$ triples. No triple is counted twice: two different faces share at most one edge, that is, at most two vertices, so three vertices cannot lie on two faces.

Hence the number of choices with the three vertices not all on one face is $56-24=\boxed{32}$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put the cube with its vertices at the eight points $(x,y,z)$ with each coordinate $0$ or $1$. A face is the set of vertices with one coordinate fixed (for example $z=1$ is the top face). So three vertices lie on a common face exactly when, in some coordinate, all three have the same value. We want triples in which every coordinate takes both values $0$ and $1$.

First count ordered lists $(P,Q,R)$ of vertices (not yet required to be different) with this property. In each coordinate the three values must not be all equal: of the $2^3=8$ ways to give the three values, $2$ are excluded, leaving $6$. The coordinates are independent, so there are $6^3=216$ lists.

Remove lists with a repeated vertex. If $P=Q$, then in each coordinate $P$ and $Q$ agree and $R$ must differ from them, so $R$ is the opposite corner of $P$: $8$ lists. Likewise $8$ lists with $P=R$ and $8$ with $Q=R$, and all three equal is impossible. That leaves $216-24=192$ lists of three different vertices, and each set of three vertices appears in $3!=6$ orders. So the answer is $\dfrac{192}{6}=\boxed{32}$.

Problem 5

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · Regular Polygons

$ABCDEF$ is a regular hexagon. The square $ABGH$ lies inside $ABCDEF$ and the vertex $J$ of the square $EIFJ$ lies inside $ABCDEF$. Prove that $H$ lies on the line $FJ$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the angles at $A$ and $F$ and identify which segment of $EIFJ$ is a diagonal. Draw the triangle formed by $A,F,H$.
Hint 2: key idea
The square and the regular hexagon give equal lengths $AH$ and $AF$. Use the isosceles triangle $AFH$ and the hexagon angles to determine how ray $FH$ is inclined to $FE$. (LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Find $\angle FAH$ from the angles at $A$, then use the two equal base angles of $AFH$ to find $\angle HFE$. Compare it with the angle between the side $FJ$ and diagonal $FE$, and check which side of $FE$ contains both points.
Common trap
Equal angles with $FE$ can describe two different rays unless their sides relative to $FE$ are also checked.
Show solution 1 – Two angles of 45 degrees

Each interior angle of a regular hexagon is $120^\circ$, and all sides are equal; the square $ABGH$ has side $AB$, so $AH=AB=AF$.

The angle $\angle HFE$. In the square, $\angle HAB=90^\circ$, so $\angle FAH=\angle FAB-\angle HAB=120^\circ-90^\circ=30^\circ$ (the point $H$ lies inside the angle $FAB$ since the square is inside the hexagon). Triangle $AFH$ is isosceles with $AF=AH$, so \[\angle AFH=\angle AHF=\frac{180^\circ-30^\circ}{2}=75^\circ .\] Since $H$ is inside the hexagon, the ray $FH$ lies between the rays $FA$ and $FE$, so $\angle HFE=\angle AFE-\angle AFH=120^\circ-75^\circ=45^\circ$.

The angle $\angle JFE$. In the square $EIFJ$ the vertices $E$ and $F$ are opposite, so $FE$ is a diagonal and $FJ$ is a side; a diagonal of a square makes $45^\circ$ with each side, so $\angle JFE=45^\circ$.

Conclusion. The points $H$ and $J$ both lie inside the hexagon, hence on the same side of the line $FE$. The rays $FH$ and $FJ$ make the same angle $45^\circ$ with the ray $FE$ on the same side, so they are the same ray. Therefore $H$ lies on the line $FJ$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Take the side length to be $1$ and put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$. Going round the regular hexagon (each turn is $60^\circ$), \[F=\Bigl(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr),\qquad E=\bigl(0,\sqrt3\bigr).\] The square on $AB$ inside the hexagon has $H=(0,1)$.

In the square $EIFJ$, the diagonal $EF$ has midpoint $M=\bigl(-\tfrac14,\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}\bigr)$, and $J$ is obtained from $M$ by going half a diagonal at right angles to $EF$. Half of $\overrightarrow{EF}$ is $\bigl(-\tfrac14,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\bigr)$; turning it through a right angle gives $\pm\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4},-\tfrac14\bigr)$. The whole hexagon lies on one side of the line $EF$, the side of its centre $O=\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\bigr)$, and $\overrightarrow{MO}=\bigl(\tfrac34,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}4\bigr)$ has positive scalar product $\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{16}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{16}$ with $\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4},-\tfrac14\bigr)$. So the choice $+$, which gives $J=\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3-1}{4},\tfrac{3\sqrt3-1}{4}\bigr)$, is on the side of the hexagon, and the choice $-$ is on the other side of $EF$, outside the hexagon (it is $I$). Since $J$ is inside, $J$ is the point given by $+$.

Now compare directions from $F$: \[\overrightarrow{FH}=\Bigl(\tfrac12,\ 1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr),\qquad \overrightarrow{FJ}=\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3+1}{4},\ \tfrac{\sqrt3-1}{4}\Bigr).\] The slope of $FH$ is $\dfrac{1-\sqrt3/2}{1/2}=2-\sqrt3$, and the slope of $FJ$ is \[\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}=\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{(\sqrt3+1)(\sqrt3-1)}=\frac{4-2\sqrt3}{2}=2-\sqrt3 .\] The lines $FH$ and $FJ$ pass through $F$ and have the same slope, so they are the same line: $H$ lies on $FJ$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · Recursive Counting Rules

A stone is placed in the central square of a $3\times3$ grid. Each second, it moves one space vertically or horizontally to an adjacent square. After $2n$ seconds, where $n$ is a positive integer, it is back to the centre. It is allowed to revisit cells that have been previously visited. Rotations and reflections of paths are considered different. In terms of $n$, how many possible paths could it have followed? For example, after $2$ seconds there are $4$ such paths.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the centre, corners and four other cells on a sketch of the grid. Track which types of cell can be reached after one move and after two.
Hint 2: key idea
Keep separate counts of paths ending at the centre and at any corner at even times. From either type, count the two-step continuations to each type of endpoint. (LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting)
Hint 3: approach
Write two recurrence equations using the continuations from the centre and from one corner. Compare their coefficients to express the corner count in terms of the centre count. Substitute into the centre recurrence and use the two-second case to start it.
Common trap
The corner count collects paths to all four corners, so the recurrence needs the number of continuations from one particular corner.
Show the answer

$2^{3n-1}$

Show solution 1 – Two kinds of position at even times

Call the centre square $C$, the four squares next to it edge squares $E$, and the four corners $K$ (see the figure). From $C$ the stone can go to any of the $4$ edge squares; from an edge square it can go to $C$ or to one of $2$ corners; from a corner it can go to $2$ edge squares. In particular every move goes between an edge square and a non-edge square, so after an even number of seconds the stone is at $C$ or at a corner.

Let $c_n$ be the number of paths of $2n$ seconds that end at $C$, and $k_n$ the number that end at a corner (any corner). Look at two consecutive moves starting from $C$ or a corner:

  • from $C$: $4$ ways to an edge square, then $1$ way back to $C$ or $2$ ways to a corner, so $4$ two-move continuations end at $C$ and $8$ end at a corner;

  • from a corner: $2$ ways to an edge square, then $1$ way to $C$ or $2$ ways to a corner, so $2$ end at $C$ and $4$ end at a corner.

Hence \[c_{n+1}=4c_n+2k_n,\qquad k_{n+1}=8c_n+4k_n=2c_{n+1}.\] So $k_n=2c_n$ for every $n\ge1$, and then $c_{n+1}=4c_n+2\cdot2c_n=8c_n$. Since $c_1=4$ (go to any edge square and back), $c_n=4\cdot8^{n-1}$: \[\boxed{2^{3n-1}}\ \text{paths}.\]

Show solution 2 – Count the paths that stop one second early

Colour the squares like a chessboard with the centre black. Every move changes colour, the four squares next to the centre are the only white squares, and so at every odd time the stone is on one of these four edge squares.

A path of $2n$ seconds that ends at the centre is the same as a path of $2n-1$ seconds (ending, necessarily, on an edge square) followed by the single move from that edge square to the centre. So we only need the number of all paths of $2n-1$ seconds from the centre.

The first move has $4$ choices. After that, look at the moves in pairs, each pair starting from an edge square. From an edge square the stone either goes to the centre ($1$ way) and then to one of $4$ edge squares, or goes to one of $2$ corners and then to one of the $2$ edge squares next to that corner. That is $1\cdot4+2\cdot2=8$ ways in every case. A path of $2n-1$ seconds is the first move followed by $n-1$ such pairs, so there are $4\cdot8^{n-1}$ of them, and the answer is \[4\cdot8^{n-1}=\boxed{2^{3n-1}}.\] For $n=1$ this gives $4$, as in the example.
