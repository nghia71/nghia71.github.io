Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Winning and Losing Positions

Ana and Beto play on an initially empty $n\times n$ board. Ana chooses $k$ squares and writes a letter from the set $\{O,N,E,M\}$ in each chosen square; she may repeat letters. Then, Beto writes a letter from the same set in each empty square, without altering the letters written by Ana. Beto wins if, upon completion of the board, the word ONEM can be read somewhere on the board, in four consecutive squares within a row or column, in either direction. Otherwise, Ana wins.

  • (a) Show that, for $n=5$ and $k=5$, Beto can win regardless of the squares and letters Ana chooses.

  • (b) Show that, for $n=6$ and $k=12$, Ana can choose the squares and letters so that she wins regardless of how Beto completes the board.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a window of four consecutive cells. Mark which letters it would have to contain to read the target word from either end.
Hint 2: key idea
In part (a), look for a window with no written letters by counting written cells in rows and columns. In part (b), ask which positions of a single letter inside a window block both readings, and look for a placement that puts the same number of marks in every row and column. (LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
For the smaller board, handle empty rows and columns first, then inspect the row containing the mark in the first column. For the larger board, space the marks of one letter periodically along every row and column, with a period short enough that every window contains a mark. Check each possible position of the marks inside a window against both readings of the word.
Common trap
A placement that blocks one direction must also block the reverse spelling $MENO$.
Show the solution – An empty window, and a mod 3 pattern

Call four consecutive squares of a row or column a window. Beto wins exactly when some window can be completed to ONEM or to MENO.

(a) $n=k=5$. If some row contains none of Ana's squares, Beto writes ONEM in four of its squares and wins; the same for a column. Otherwise each of the $5$ rows and each of the $5$ columns contains at least one of Ana's $5$ squares, so each row contains exactly one, and column $1$ contains one of them, say in row $r$. Then Ana's only square in row $r$ is its first square, and squares $2,3,4,5$ of row $r$ are empty: Beto writes ONEM there.

(b) $n=6$, $k=12$. Number the rows from the top and the columns from the left, $1,\dots,6$. Ana writes the letter $O$ in the $12$ squares $(r,c)$ with $c-r\equiv1\pmod3$ (shaded in the figure): two squares in every row and every column, three apart.

Consider any window. Its four squares have consecutive indices along the row (or column), and along that line Ana's squares are those with index in one fixed residue class modulo $3$. Four consecutive indices contain this class either once, in position $2$ or $3$ of the window, or twice, in positions $1$ and $4$.

  • One $O$ in position $2$ or $3$: ONEM and MENO have $N$ or $E$ in these positions, so neither word fits.

  • $O$ in positions $1$ and $4$: ONEM would need $M$ in position $4$, and MENO would need $M$ in position $1$; neither fits.

So no window can show ONEM in either direction, whatever Beto writes, and Ana wins.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Quadratic Residues

Prove that for every integer $n$ with $2\le n\le60$, the number $3^n-3n$ is not a perfect square.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Check the first few values of $3^n-3n$. Note when $3^n$ itself is a square and how much the subtraction moves it below that value.
Hint 2: key idea
For even $n$, compare with the square immediately below $3^n$. For odd $n$, start with residues modulo $4$, then use the fact that every prime exponent in a square is even. (LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences)
Hint 3: approach
Separate the smallest even case and bound the subtraction by the gap between the two consecutive squares for larger even $n$. In the odd class that survives modulo $4$, factor out $3$ and compare the two valuations inside the difference to restrict $n$. Use the bound $n\le60$ to list candidates, then try modulo $3$ after dividing out the square factor and modulo $7$ on the remaining case.
Common trap
Apply the residue test modulo $3$ after removing the square power of $3$ that divides the whole expression.
Show the solution – Squeeze for even n, residues and 3-adic valuations for odd n

Let $N=3^n-3n$.

$n$ even. For $n=2$, $N=3$ is not a square. For even $n\ge4$, write $3^n=(3^{n/2})^2$; then \[(3^{n/2}-1)^2=3^n-2\cdot3^{n/2}+1<3^n-3n<(3^{n/2})^2,\] because $3n<2\cdot3^{n/2}-1$ for $n\ge4$: this holds for $n=4$ ($12<17$), and when $n$ grows by $2$ the left side increases by only $6$ while the right side $R$ becomes $3R+2>R+6$. So $N$ lies strictly between consecutive squares.

$n$ odd, $n\equiv3\pmod4$. Then $3^n\equiv3$ and $3n\equiv9\equiv1\pmod4$, so $N\equiv2\pmod4$: not a square.

$n$ odd, $n\equiv1\pmod4$. The exponent of $3$ in $N=3(3^{n-1}-n)$ is $1+v_3(n)$: indeed if $3^e\,\|\,n$ then $3^{n-1}-n$ has exactly $e$ factors $3$, since $n-1>e$. A square has an even exponent, so $v_3(n)$ must be odd. For $n\le60$ with $n\equiv1\pmod4$ this means $v_3(n)=1$ ($27$ and $54$ are not $\equiv1\pmod4$), so $n=3m$ with $3\nmid m$; the candidates are $n=21,33,57$. Then $N=9(3^{n-2}-m)$, and $3^{n-2}-m\equiv-m\pmod3$ must be a square modulo $3$, i.e. $m\equiv2\pmod3$. This excludes $m=7$ and $m=19$ and leaves $n=33$. Finally, modulo $7$: $3^6\equiv1$, so $3^{33}\equiv3^3\equiv6$, and $3\cdot33=99\equiv1$, giving $N\equiv5\pmod7$. The squares modulo $7$ are $0,1,2,4$, so $N$ is not a square.

Hence $3^n-3n$ is not a perfect square for $2\le n\le60$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Power of a Point

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with incenter $I$ and circumcircle $\omega$. Let $L$ be a point on the arc $BC$ of $\omega$ not containing $A$, distinct from $B$ and $C$. The line $LI$ intersects $\omega$ again at $D$. Suppose $D\ne A$ and that the circle with center $D$ and radius $DA$ intersects $\omega$ at $A$ and at another point $X$, distinct from $L$. Let $Y$ be the intersection of lines $LX$ and $BC$, and let $M$ be the reflection of $I$ with respect to $L$. Prove that the points $Y$, $I$, $A$ and $M$ lie on the same circle.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $L,I,D$ in order along their line and mark how $M$ is related to $I$. Add the midpoint of the arc $BC$ that contains $L$ and mark its position relative to the angle bisector at $A$.
Hint 2: key idea
Invert at that arc midpoint using its common distance to $B,C,I$. Track the images of $L,D$ and of line $LI$, then use power of a point to look for a circle through $L,I$ and the image of $L$ that is tangent to $AI$. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use directed angles and $DA=DX$ to test whether $Y$ lies on the auxiliary tangent circle. Use tangent–chord angles there to seek a similarity that relates $LY$, $LI$ and $LA$. Reflect $A$ across the perpendicular bisector of $IM$ and compare chord products through $L$.
Common trap
Check the order along each line before using a chord product, since the reflected point is on the opposite side of $L$ from $Y$.
Show solution 1 – Inversion at the arc midpoint and a spiral similarity at L

Let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of $\omega$ not containing $A$; it lies on line $AI$, and $NB=NC=NI$ (incenter lemma). We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ modulo $180^\circ$. Note $L\ne N$ (otherwise $LI=NA$ and $D=A$) and $D\ne N$ (otherwise $L$ would lie on line $NI=AN$).

Step 1: the inversion at $N$. The inversion $\Phi$ with center $N$ and radius $NB=NI$ fixes $B$, $C$, $I$ and maps $\omega$ (a circle through $N$) to the line $BC$. Let $K=\Phi(L)$ and $T=\Phi(D)$: these are the points where the rays $NL$ and $ND$ meet line $BC$, and \[NK\cdot NL=NT\cdot ND=NI^2 .\] (i) Since $N$, $K$, $L$ are collinear with $K$, $L$ on the same side of $N$, the power of $N$ with respect to the circle $(LIK)$ is $NK\cdot NL=NI^2$; as $I$ is on this circle, line $NI$, i.e. line $AI$, is tangent to circle $(LIK)$ at $I$.

(ii) The line $LD$ does not pass through $N$, so $\Phi$ maps it to a circle through $N$ containing $\Phi(I)=I$, $\Phi(L)=K$, $\Phi(D)=T$: the points $N$, $I$, $K$, $T$ are concyclic.

Step 2: $Y$ lies on circle $(LIK)$. Let $Y'$ be the second intersection of line $BC$ with circle $(LIK)$ (with $Y'=K$ if $BC$ is tangent). Using this circle, then the circle $NIKT$ of (ii), then $\omega$: \[\angle(LY',LD)=\angle(KY',KI)=\angle(KT,KI)=\angle(NT,NI)=\angle(ND,NA)=\angle(LD,LA).\] On the other hand $DA=DX$, so the equal chords $DA$, $DX$ of $\omega$ give $\angle(LX,LD)=\angle(LD,LA)$: line $LX$ is the reflection of line $LA$ in line $LD$. Hence lines $LY'$ and $LX$ coincide, and $Y'$, which is on $BC$, equals $Y=LX\cap BC$.

Step 3: $LY\cdot LA=LI^2$, with $LI$ bisecting the angle $YLA$. In circle $(LIKY)$ the tangent at $I$ is $AI$, so (tangent-chord) $\angle(YL,YI)=\angle(IL,IA)$, and by Step 2 $\angle(LY,LI)=\angle(LI,LA)$. So triangles $YLI$ and $ILA$ are directly similar, and the spiral similarity with center $L$ that maps $Y\mapsto I$ also maps $I\mapsto A$. Therefore $\frac{LI}{LY}=\frac{LA}{LI}$, i.e. $LY\cdot LA=LI^2$, and the same rotation takes ray $LY$ to ray $LI$ and ray $LI$ to ray $LA$: the reflection $\sigma$ in line $LI$ maps ray $LY$ to ray $LA$.

Step 4: the circle $AIM$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $L$ perpendicular to $LI$; it is the perpendicular bisector of $IM$. Let $\rho$ be the reflection in $\ell$ and $A'=\rho(A)$. Since $\rho\circ\sigma$ is the half-turn about $L$, the point $A'$ lies on line $LY$, on the opposite side of $L$ from $Y$, and $LA'=LA$. So $L$ lies between $Y$ and $A'$ and between $I$ and $M$, and \[LY\cdot LA'=LI^2=LI\cdot LM .\] By the converse of the intersecting chords theorem, $Y$, $A'$, $I$, $M$ are concyclic (the lines $YA'$ and $IM$ are different). The center of this circle lies on the perpendicular bisector $\ell$ of $IM$, so the circle is symmetric in $\ell$ and also contains $\rho(A')=A$. Hence $Y$, $I$, $A$, $M$ lie on one circle.

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers on the circumcircle

Take $\omega$ as the unit circle of the complex plane and denote the coordinate of each point by the corresponding lower-case letter; for a point $z$ of $\omega$ we have $\bar z=1/z$. Rotate so that the midpoint $S$ of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$ is $1$. Then $B$ and $C$ are symmetric in the real axis, so $c=\bar b=1/b$.

Two tools. (i) For $P,Q$ on $\omega$, a point $z$ lies on line $PQ$ if and only if \[z+pq\,\bar z=p+q ,\] because $\frac{z-p}{q-p}$ must be real, and conjugating with $\bar p=1/p$, $\bar q=1/q$ turns $\frac{z-p}{q-p}=\frac{\bar z-1/p}{1/q-1/p}$ into this equation. In particular line $BC$ is $z+\bar z=k$ with $k=b+c$ (a real number). (ii) The reflection in a line through $0$ and a point $d$ of $\omega$ is $z\mapsto d^2\bar z$.

The incenter. $I$ lies on segment $SA$, so $i=1+\tau$ with $\tau=t(a-1)$ for some real $t\in(0,1)$, and $\bar\tau=t(\frac1a-1)=-\frac{\tau}{a}$. By the incenter lemma $SI=SB$ (in triangle $SBI$ both $\angle SBI=\angle SBC+\angle CBI$ and $\angle SIB=\angle IAB+\angle IBA$ equal $\frac{\angle A+\angle B}2$). Hence \[2-k=(1-b)(1-\bar b)=|b-1|^2=|i-1|^2=\tau\bar\tau=-\frac{\tau^2}{a},\qquad\text{so}\qquad k=2+\frac{\tau^2}{a}.\]

The points $D$ and $X$. Put $p=l-i$ and $q=l\bar i-1$; here $q\ne0$ since $|l\bar i|=|i|<1$. As $I$ lies on chord $LD$, tool (i) gives $i+ld\,\bar i=l+d$, so $d=\frac pq$. The circles $\omega$ and $(D,DA)$ meet at $A$ and $X$, which are therefore symmetric in the line of centres $OD$ ($O=0$); by tool (ii), $x=d^2\bar a=\frac{p^2}{aq^2}$.

A key identity. Let $u=a-l$ and $h=k-l-\frac1l$. Since $p=(l-1)-\tau$ and $q=(l-1)+l\bar\tau=(l-1)-\frac{l\tau}a$, expanding (the terms linear in $\tau$ cancel) gives \[aq^2-lp^2=(a-l)(l-1)^2+l\tau^2\Bigl(\frac la-1\Bigr)=(a-l)\Bigl[(l-1)^2-\frac{l\tau^2}{a}\Bigr]=-l\,u\,h,\tag{1}\] where the last step uses $-lh=(l-1)^2-l(k-2)$ and $k-2=\frac{\tau^2}a$. Here $u\ne0$ (as $A\ne L$) and $h\ne0$ (otherwise $l+\bar l=k$, i.e. $L$ lies on line $BC$, so $L\in\{B,C\}$). Thus $1-lx=\frac{aq^2-lp^2}{aq^2}\ne0$.

The point $Y$. Let $w=y-l$. From $Y\in BC$: $y+\bar y=k$, i.e. $w+\bar w=h$. From $Y\in LX$, tool (i) gives $y+lx\,\bar y=l+x$, i.e. $w+lx\,\bar w=0$. Subtracting, $\bar w(1-lx)=h$, and by (1) \[\bar w=\frac{h\,aq^2}{aq^2-lp^2}=-\frac{aq^2}{lu},\qquad w=-lx\,\bar w=\frac{p^2}{u}.\]

Concyclicity. Measure all points from $L$: the displacements of $I$, $M$, $A$, $Y$ are $v=-p$, $-v=p$, $u$, $w$, and \[\bar v=\bar i-\frac1l=\frac ql,\qquad \bar u=\frac1a-\frac1l=-\frac{u}{al}.\] Every circle through the points with displacements $\pm v$ has an equation $|z|^2-|v|^2=\lambda(z\bar v-\bar zv)$ with a constant $\lambda$. Indeed, writing it as $|z-o|^2=R^2$, i.e. $|z|^2-o\bar z-\bar oz+|o|^2-R^2=0$, and substituting $z=\pm v$ gives $o\bar v+\bar ov=0$ and $|o|^2-R^2=-|v|^2$; so $o=\mu v$ with $\bar\mu=-\mu$, and $o\bar z+\bar oz=-\mu(z\bar v-\bar zv)$.

The point $A$ is not on line $LI$ (that line meets $\omega$ only at $L$ and $D\ne A$), so $u\bar v-\bar uv\ne0$ and the circle $AIM$ has $\lambda=\frac{|u|^2-|v|^2}{u\bar v-\bar uv}$. Hence $Y$ lies on it if and only if \[(|w|^2-|v|^2)(u\bar v-\bar uv)=(|u|^2-|v|^2)(w\bar v-\bar wv).\] Now compute each factor: \[|v|^2=v\bar v=-\frac{pq}{l},\qquad |u|^2=u\bar u=-\frac{u^2}{al},\qquad |w|^2=w\bar w=-\frac{ap^2q^2}{lu^2},\] \[u\bar v-\bar uv=\frac{u(aq-p)}{al},\qquad w\bar v-\bar wv=\frac{pq(p-aq)}{lu},\] \[|w|^2-|v|^2=\frac{pq(u^2-apq)}{lu^2},\qquad |u|^2-|v|^2=\frac{apq-u^2}{al}.\] Both sides of the criterion equal $\dfrac{pq\,(u^2-apq)(aq-p)}{al^2u}$. Therefore $Y$, $I$, $A$ and $M$ lie on the same circle.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Let $\mathbb{R}^+$ be the set of positive real numbers. Let $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ be a function such that \[xy(x+y)\le f(xy)f(x+y)\le x^2f(x)+y^3\] for all positive real numbers $x$ and $y$.

  • (a) Prove that $f(1)=1$.

  • (b) Prove that $f(x)=x$ for all positive real numbers $x$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Substitute $y=x$ and compare the two outer expressions in the double inequality. Also write down what the upper bound says when both inputs are one.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the diagonal substitution to bound $f$ below by the identity and settle the value at one. Then normalize by writing $g(x)=f(x)/x$ and use the same substitution to compare the excess of $g$ over one at $x^2$ and at $x$. (LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Derive a separate upper bound for $g$ by choosing the first input as one. For a fixed $z>0$, iterate the squaring estimate from $z^{1/2^k}$ up to $z$. Apply the separate upper bound at the starting point and examine what happens as $k$ grows.
Common trap
The inputs approaching one do not justify any limit of $f$ by themselves, since continuity was not assumed.
Show the solution – Squeezing g = f(x)/x toward 1

Write $E(x,y)$ for the double inequality.

$f(x)\ge x$. From $E(x,x)$: $2x^3\le f(x^2)f(2x)\le x^2f(x)+x^3$, so $x^2f(x)\ge x^3$, i.e. $f(x)\ge x$ for all $x>0$.

(a) $E(1,1)$ gives $f(1)f(2)\le f(1)+1$. Since $f(2)\ge2$, $2f(1)\le f(1)+1$, so $f(1)\le1$; together with $f(1)\ge1$ we get $f(1)=1$.

(b) Let $g(x)=\frac{f(x)}x\ge1$.

Squaring step. From $E(x,x)$ and $f(2x)\ge2x$: $f(x^2)\cdot2x\le x^2f(x)+x^3$, so \[g(x^2)\le\frac{g(x)+1}2,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad g(x^2)-1\le\frac{g(x)-1}2 .\] A crude bound. From $E(1,y)$, $f(1)=1$ and $f(1+y)\ge1+y$: $f(y)(1+y)\le1+y^3$, so \[g(y)\le\frac{1+y^3}{y(1+y)}=y-1+\frac1y\qquad(y>0).\] Conclusion. Fix $z>0$ and apply the squaring step to $z^{1/2},z^{1/4},\dots$: \[0\le g(z)-1\le\frac{g(z^{1/2})-1}{2}\le\dots\le\frac{g\bigl(z^{1/2^k}\bigr)-1}{2^k}\le\frac{z^{1/2^k}+z^{-1/2^k}-2}{2^k}.\] As $k\to\infty$, $z^{\pm1/2^k}\to1$, so the right side tends to $0$. Hence $g(z)=1$, i.e. $f(z)=z$ for every $z>0$.

(Conversely $f(x)=x$ satisfies the condition: $xy(x+y)\le x^3+y^3$ is $(x+y)(x-y)^2\ge0$.)
