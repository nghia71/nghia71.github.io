Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

What is the greatest possible number of distinct remainders modulo $2026$ that can be attained by the elements of the set \[\{\pi(1),2\pi(2),3\pi(3),\dots,2026\pi(2026)\},\] if $\pi$ is a permutation of the set $\{1,2,\dots,2026\}$?

Show the answer

$2025$

Show the solution

Solution (Wilson's theorem on the odd half, and a construction by residues)

Let $p=1013$, a prime, so $2026=2p$. By the Chinese remainder theorem a residue modulo $2p$ is determined by its parity and its residue modulo $p$. For $r\in\mathbb Z_p$ let $o(r)$ and $e(r)$ be the odd and the even number in $\{1,\dots,2p\}$ congruent to $r$ modulo $p$; the odd numbers $1,3,\dots,2p-1$ are pairwise incongruent modulo $p$ (two of them differ by an even number of absolute value less than $2p$), so every number in $\{1,\dots,2p\}$ is exactly one of the $o(r)$, $e(r)$. Note $o(0)=p$, $e(0)=2p$. The product $o(r)o(s)$ is odd and congruent to $rs$ modulo $p$; any product involving an $e(\cdot)$ is even.

Not all $2026$ residues. Suppose the $2026$ products $k\pi(k)$ were pairwise distinct modulo $2p$. Then exactly two of them are divisible by $p$ (the residues $0$ and $p$). A product $k\pi(k)$ is divisible by $p$ iff $k\in\{p,2p\}$ or $\pi(k)\in\{p,2p\}$; this already holds for $k=p,2p$, so $\pi$ maps $\{p,2p\}$ onto itself. If $\pi(p)=2p$, both products $p\cdot2p$ and $2p\cdot p$ are $\equiv0$, a repetition; so $\pi(p)=p$ and $\pi(2p)=2p$.

Now consider the $p-1$ odd residues not divisible by $p$. Each must be some $k\pi(k)$ with $k$ and $\pi(k)$ odd and different from $p$. There are only $p-1$ odd numbers $k\ne p$, so every odd $k\ne p$ has $\pi(k)$ odd, and $\pi$ permutes the set $O$ of odd numbers other than $p$, whose residues modulo $p$ are $1,2,\dots,p-1$. On one hand, by Wilson's theorem, \[ \prod_{k\in O}k\pi(k)=\Bigl(\prod_{k\in O}k\Bigr)^2\equiv\bigl((p-1)!\bigr)^2\equiv1\pmod p . \] On the other hand these $p-1$ products are the $p-1$ odd residues not divisible by $p$, whose residues modulo $p$ are again $1,\dots,p-1$, so their product is $\equiv(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. Thus $1\equiv-1\pmod p$, which is false. Hence at most $2025$ residues occur.

A permutation with $2025$ residues. For $r\ne0$ let $r^{-1}$ be the inverse of $r$ modulo $p$. Define

  • $\pi(o(r))=o(1+r^{-1})$ for $r\ne0,-1$, and $\pi(e(r))=e(1-r^{-1})$ for $r\ne0,1$;

  • $\pi(o(-1))=e(1)$, $\pi(o(0))=o(1)$, $\pi(e(0))=o(0)$, $\pi(e(1))=e(0)$.

As $r$ runs over $\mathbb Z_p\setminus\{0,-1\}$, so does $r^{-1}$, hence $1+r^{-1}$ runs over $\mathbb Z_p\setminus\{0,1\}$; similarly $1-r^{-1}$ runs over $\mathbb Z_p\setminus\{0,1\}$ for $r\ne0,1$. Together with the four special values, every number $1,\dots,2p$ is hit exactly once, so $\pi$ is a permutation. The products are:

  • odd: $o(r)\,o(1+r^{-1})\equiv r+1$ for $r\ne0,-1$, giving every class except $0$ and $1$; and $o(0)\,o(1)\equiv0$. So $p-1$ distinct odd residues.

  • even: $e(r)\,e(1-r^{-1})\equiv r-1$ for $r\ne0,1$, giving every class except $-1$ and $0$; $o(-1)\,e(1)\equiv-1$; and $e(0)\,o(0)\equiv e(1)\,e(0)\equiv0$. So all $p$ even residues.

In total $(p-1)+p=2p-1=2025$ distinct residues. The answer is $\boxed{2025}$.

Remark. The Wilson step can be replaced by an additive one. Fix a primitive root $g$ modulo $p$ and write the elements of $O$ as $o(g^u)$, $u\in\mathbb Z_{p-1}$. If $\pi(o(g^u))=o(g^{\sigma(u)})$ and the products are pairwise incongruent, then both $u\mapsto\sigma(u)$ and $u\mapsto u+\sigma(u)$ are bijections of $\mathbb Z_{p-1}$; summing over $u$ gives $\sum u+\sum u\equiv\sum u$, so $\sum_{u=0}^{p-2}u=\frac{(p-1)(p-2)}2\equiv0\pmod{p-1}$, which is false because $p-2$ is odd. (A cyclic group of even order has no complete mapping.)

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $\triangle ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$. Let $D$ and $E$ be the feet of the altitudes from vertices $A$ and $B$, respectively, let $H$ be the orthocenter of $\triangle ABC$, and let $F$ be the intersection of the bisector of the interior angle at vertex $A$ with the line $BC$. Let $I$ be the intersection point of the lines $AF$ and $BE$. If the circumcircles of triangles $\triangle BHC$ and $\triangle DFI$ intersect at points $M$ and $P$, prove that the quadrilateral $AEMP$ is cyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (A circle of the pencil through $M$ and $P$)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ the angles of triangle $ABC$. Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $BHC$, $\omega_2$ the circle $DFI$, and $p_1(Z)$, $p_2(Z)$ the powers of a point $Z$ with respect to them.

Step 1: a criterion. In coordinates, $p_1(Z)=|Z|^2+(\text{linear})$ and $p_2(Z)=|Z|^2+(\text{linear})$. Hence for any real $\lambda$ the function $\Phi=\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2$ has the form $|Z-O|^2-\kappa$, and it vanishes at $M$ and $P$. Suppose that \[ \frac{p_1(A)}{p_2(A)}=\frac{p_1(E)}{p_2(E)}=\rho\ne1 . \] Taking $\lambda=\frac1{1-\rho}$ gives $\Phi(A)=\Phi(E)=0$. Then $\kappa=|A-O|^2$, so the zero set of $\Phi$ is the circle with centre $O$ through $A$; it contains $A,E,M,P$, and its radius is positive because $A\ne E$. So it suffices to show that both ratios equal $\frac{b+c}{c}$.

Step 2: the point $A$. The reflection of $H$ in $BC$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$, so $\omega_1$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $BC$; it contains the reflection $A'$ of $A$, which lies on line $AD$, as does $H$. Both $H$ and $A'$ are on ray $AD$, so $p_1(A)=AH\cdot AA'=2\,AH\cdot AD$. Since $\angle HDC=\angle HEC=90^\circ$, the points $D,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $HC$, and the power of $A$ gives $AH\cdot AD=AE\cdot AC=c\cos\alpha\cdot b$. Hence $p_1(A)=2bc\cos\alpha$.

Line $AF$ meets $\omega_2$ at $I$ and $F$, both on ray $AF$, so $p_2(A)=AI\cdot AF$. In the right triangle $AEI$ the angle at $A$ is $\frac\alpha2$, so $AI=\frac{AE}{\cos(\alpha/2)}=\frac{c\cos\alpha}{\cos(\alpha/2)}$. Comparing areas, $[ABF]+[AFC]=[ABC]$ gives $\frac12(b+c)\,AF\sin\frac\alpha2=\frac12bc\sin\alpha$, so $AF=\frac{2bc\cos(\alpha/2)}{b+c}$. Therefore $p_2(A)=\frac{2bc^2\cos\alpha}{b+c}$ and $\frac{p_1(A)}{p_2(A)}=\frac{b+c}{c}$.

Step 3: the point $E$ and $\omega_1$. Line $BE$ meets $\omega_1$ at $B$ and $H$, and $H$ lies between $B$ and $E$, so $p_1(E)=EH\cdot EB$. Here $EB=c\sin\alpha$, and in the right triangle $AEH$ the angle at $A$ is $\angle CAD=90^\circ-\gamma$, so $EH=AE\cot\gamma=c\cos\alpha\cot\gamma$. Thus $p_1(E)=c^2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cot\gamma$.

Step 4: the point $E$ and $\omega_2$. Measure positions on line $BE$ from $B$ towards $E$. Line $BE$ meets $\omega_2$ at $I$ and at a second point $J$; let $t_I=BI$, $t_J$, $t_E=BE$ be their positions. Line $BC$ meets $\omega_2$ at $D$ and $F$, both on ray $BC$, so the power of $B$ is $t_It_J=BD\cdot BF$, and $p_2(E)=(t_E-t_I)(t_E-t_J)$.

In triangle $ABI$ we have $\angle BAI=\frac\alpha2$ and $\angle ABI=90^\circ-\alpha$, hence $\angle AIB=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$ and, by the law of sines, $BI=\frac{c\sin(\alpha/2)}{\cos(\alpha/2)}=c\tan\frac\alpha2$. Also $t_E-t_I=EI=AE\tan\frac\alpha2=c\cos\alpha\tan\frac\alpha2$. With $BD=c\cos\beta$ and $BF=\frac{ac}{b+c}$ (angle bisector theorem), \[ t_J=\frac{BD\cdot BF}{BI}=\frac{ac\cos\beta}{(b+c)\tan(\alpha/2)},\qquad p_2(E)=\frac{c^2\cos\alpha}{b+c}\Bigl((b+c)\sin\alpha\tan\tfrac\alpha2-a\cos\beta\Bigr). \] With $a=2R\sin\alpha$, $b=2R\sin\beta$, $c=2R\sin\gamma$ and $(\sin\beta+\sin\gamma)\tan\frac\alpha2=2\cos\frac\alpha2\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2\tan\frac\alpha2=2\sin\frac\alpha2\cos\frac{\beta-\gamma}2=\cos\beta+\cos\gamma$, the bracket equals $2R\sin\alpha\,(\cos\beta+\cos\gamma-\cos\beta)=2R\sin\alpha\cos\gamma=c\sin\alpha\cot\gamma$. Hence \[ p_2(E)=\frac{c^3\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cot\gamma}{b+c}\quad(\text{positive since }\gamma<90^\circ),\qquad \frac{p_1(E)}{p_2(E)}=\frac{b+c}{c}. \]

Conclusion. Both ratios equal $\frac{b+c}{c}\ne1$ (all powers involved are positive because the triangle is acute), so by Step 1 the points $A,E,M,P$ lie on one circle: the quadrilateral $AEMP$ is cyclic.

Remark (the official route, sketched only). Let $Z$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$. The inversion centred at $A$ with power $AH\cdot AD=AE\cdot AC=AZ\cdot AB$ swaps $H\leftrightarrow D$, $E\leftrightarrow C$, $B\leftrightarrow Z$, so it maps the circle $BHC$ to the nine-point circle $ZDE$, and the circle through $A,E,M,P$ to a line through $C$. The claim becomes: $C$ has equal powers with respect to the nine-point circle and the image of the circle $DFI$. The official marking scheme completes this with two auxiliary points and a coaxality lemma; we do not reproduce that argument here.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

A regular $2n$-gon $A_1A_2\cdots A_{2n}$, with $n\ge 3$, is given, whose sides are colored with $n$ colors so that each pair of opposite sides is colored the same color. Determine the smallest $m\in\mathbb{N}$ for which it is possible to choose points $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_m$ in the interior of this polygon, and then draw certain pairwise nonintersecting segments whose endpoints are points from the set \[\{A_1,A_2,\dots,A_{2n}\}\cup\{B_1,B_2,\dots,B_m\},\] and color each of these segments with one of the given $n$ colors, in such a way that the original polygon is partitioned into convex quadrilaterals, each side of each quadrilateral being one of the drawn segments or a side of the polygon, with the property that in each of them the opposite sides are colored the same color.

Show the answer

$m_{\min}=\dfrac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$

Show the solution

Solution (Colour strips, an angle count, and a rhombus tiling)

By the statement, each side of each quadrilateral is one of the drawn segments or a side of the $2n$-gon, and it carries that colour. Let $F$ be the number of quadrilaterals. Say the two opposite sides $A_iA_{i+1}$ and $A_{i+n}A_{i+n+1}$ have colour $c_i$.

Step 1: $F\le m+n-1$. The angles of all quadrilaterals add up to $2\pi F$. Group them by vertex. At a vertex $A_i$ of the polygon they fill the polygon's angle $\pi-\frac\pi n$ (no quadrilateral can have $A_i$ inside one of its sides, since that needs a straight angle there). At a point $B_j$ they add up to at most $2\pi$. Hence $2\pi F\le 2n\bigl(\pi-\frac\pi n\bigr)+2\pi m$, i.e. $F\le m+n-1$. (Alternatively, by Euler's formula: if $m'\le m$ of the points $B_j$ are used, the subdivision has $V=2n+m'$ vertices and, since every interior edge borders two quadrilaterals and every polygon side borders one, $E=\frac{4F+2n}{2}=2F+n$ edges; $V-E+F=1$ gives $F=n+m'-1\le n+m-1$.)

Step 2: $F\ge\binom n2$. Fix a colour $c$ and call an edge (drawn segment or polygon side) of colour $c$ a $c$-edge. If a quadrilateral has one side of colour $c$, the opposite side also has colour $c$. Form a graph $\Gamma_c$ whose vertices are the $c$-edges, joining two $c$-edges when they are opposite sides of a quadrilateral. A $c$-edge inside the polygon is a side of two quadrilaterals and has degree $2$; the two polygon sides of colour $c$ have degree $1$. So $\Gamma_c$ is a union of paths and cycles, and the two polygon sides of colour $c$ are the two ends of one path. Following this path, in each quadrilateral it passes draw the segment joining the midpoints of the two opposite $c$-edges. We get a curve $\gamma_c$ inside the polygon from the midpoint of one side of colour $c$ to the midpoint of the opposite side. It meets edges only at midpoints of $c$-edges; inside a quadrilateral it consists of segments joining midpoints of opposite sides.

Let $c\ne c'$. The two sides of colour $c$ are opposite, so they split the boundary of the polygon into two arcs, and each arc contains exactly one side of colour $c'$. Thus the endpoints of $\gamma_c$ and $\gamma_{c'}$ alternate along the boundary, and two curves inside a convex polygon with alternating endpoints on the boundary must meet. A common point is not on an edge (on edges $\gamma_c$ only passes midpoints of $c$-edges and $\gamma_{c'}$ only midpoints of $c'$-edges), so it lies inside a quadrilateral $Q$ that has a pair of opposite sides of colour $c$ and a pair of colour $c'$. The two pairs of $Q$ therefore have colours exactly $c$ and $c'$, so different pairs $\{c,c'\}$ give different quadrilaterals, and $F\ge\binom n2$.

Step 3: lower bound. From Steps 1 and 2, $m\ge F-(n-1)\ge\binom n2-(n-1)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$.

Step 4: construction. Let $v_k$ ($1\le k\le n$) be the unit vector at angle $\frac{(k-1)\pi}{n}$, and for $2\le j\le n$ let $Z_j$ be the convex polygon with sides $v_1,\dots,v_j,-v_1,\dots,-v_j$ in this order, starting at the origin. Its exterior angles all equal $\frac\pi n$ except two, and $Z_n$ (with exterior angles all $\frac\pi n$) is a regular $2n$-gon of side $1$; we label it so that the sides parallel to $v_k$ are the two sides of colour $c_k$. We tile $Z_j$ by $\binom j2$ rhombi with sides parallel to two of the $v_k$, edge to edge, with $\frac{(j-1)(j-2)}{2}$ interior vertices, by induction on $j$. For $j=2$, $Z_2$ is one rhombus. For $j\ge3$, let $L$ be the chain $0\to v_1\to v_1+v_2\to\dots\to v_1+\dots+v_{j-1}$, formed by the first $j-1$ sides of $Z_{j-1}$. Since $v_j$ makes an angle in $(0,\pi)$ with each $v_i$ ($i<j$), translating $L$ by $v_j$ sweeps the strip of $j-1$ rhombi \[ R_i=v_1+\dots+v_{i-1}+\{xv_i+yv_j:\ 0\le x,y\le1\},\qquad 1\le i\le j-1, \] which lies between $L$ and $L+v_j$, while $Z_{j-1}+v_j$ lies on the other side of $L+v_j$. Going around the union we meet the sides $v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}$ (along $L$), $v_j$, then $-v_1,\dots,-v_{j-1}$ (the rest of $Z_{j-1}+v_j$) and $-v_j$: the union is exactly $Z_j$. Tiling $Z_{j-1}+v_j$ by the translated tiling of $Z_{j-1}$ and adding the strip gives a tiling of $Z_j$ by $\binom{j-1}2+(j-1)=\binom j2$ rhombi, edge to edge. Its interior vertices are those of $Z_{j-1}+v_j$ plus the $j-2$ inner vertices of the chain $L+v_j$, so their number is $\frac{(j-2)(j-3)}{2}+(j-2)=\frac{(j-1)(j-2)}{2}$.

For $j=n$ take $B_1,\dots,B_m$ to be the $m=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$ interior vertices, draw the rhombus sides that are not sides of the polygon, and colour each segment parallel to $v_k$ with colour $c_k$. The segments meet only at endpoints, every piece is a convex quadrilateral, and opposite sides of each rhombus are parallel, hence of the same colour. So $m=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$ points suffice, and the answer is \[ \boxed{m_{\min}=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}} . \]

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

In the plane, $n=2k+1$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, points are given, no three of which are collinear. Maja and Kosta play the following game: at the beginning, Maja connects every two of these $n$ points by a segment, and assigns a direction to each segment toward one of its two endpoints, in any way she wishes. After that, they play a game consisting of $k$ consecutive rounds. In each round, Kosta first chooses one of the points that has not been chosen earlier in the game, after which Maja does the same, and then the loser of the round is the player whose chosen point is the endpoint toward which the segment between the two points chosen in that round is directed. Maja's goal is to direct the segments at the beginning so that, if she plays optimally in the remainder of the game, regardless of how Kosta chooses points, he cannot win any of the $k$ rounds. In how many different ways can she achieve this goal?

Show the answer

$(2k)!$

Show the solution

Solution (Good orientations are exactly the rotational ones)

Write $u\to w$ if the segment $uw$ is directed toward $w$; then whoever chose $w$ loses that round. Maja wins a round in which Kosta chose $x$ and she chose $y$ exactly when $y\to x$. After some rounds, the rest of the game is the same game on the remaining points. Call an orientation of the segments between $2j+1$ points good if Maja can win all $j$ rounds against every play of Kosta. In-neighbours of $w$ are the points $u$ with $u\to w$.

Lemma 1. In a good orientation on $2j+1$ points every point has exactly $j$ in-neighbours and $j$ out-neighbours.

Proof. Let $w$ have $d$ in-neighbours. In each round, while some in-neighbour of $w$ is left, let Kosta choose one, say $x$. Maja cannot answer with $w$, because $x\to w$. So $w$ stays, and after at most $d$ rounds no in-neighbour of $w$ is left. If $d\le j-1$, a round is still to come; Kosta chooses $w$, and whatever Maja chooses, $w$ beats it, so Kosta wins that round. Hence $d\ge j$ for every point; as the in-degrees add up to $\binom{2j+1}{2}=j(2j+1)$, all equal $j$. $\square$

Rotational orientations. Place the points on a circle, labelled by $\mathbb Z_{2j+1}$ in clockwise order, and let $u\to w$ iff $w-u\in\{1,\dots,j\}$: each point beats the next $j$ points clockwise. Call such an orientation rotational.

Lemma 2. Rotational orientations are good.

Proof. When Kosta chooses $x$, Maja chooses $y=x-j$; then $y\to x$. For a remaining point $u$, its out-neighbours $u+1,\dots,u+j$ form a block of $j$ consecutive points. No such block contains both $x$ and $x-j$ (going around the circle they are $j$ and $j+1$ steps apart), and the only block avoiding both is $\{x+1,\dots,x+j\}$, which belongs to $u=x$. So each remaining $u$ loses exactly one out-neighbour, and its remaining out-neighbours are the next $j-1$ remaining points clockwise. Thus the remaining $2j-1$ points carry a rotational orientation again, and Maja repeats the strategy until one point is left. $\square$

Lemma 3. Every good orientation is rotational.

Proof. Induction on $j$. For $j=1$, Lemma 1 says each of the three points has one in-neighbour: the triangle is a directed cycle, which is rotational. Let $j\ge2$ and let $T$ be good on $2j+1$ points. Let Kosta choose any point $x$ and let $y$ be a winning answer of Maja; then $y\to x$ and the remaining orientation $T'$ on $2j-1$ points is good, so by induction it is rotational: its points are $w_0,\dots,w_{2j-2}$ (indices modulo $2j-1$) with $w_s\to w_t$ iff $t-s\in\{1,\dots,j-1\}$. By Lemma 1 every point has out-degree $j$ in $T$ and $j-1$ in $T'$, so every $w_i$ beats exactly one of $x,y$. Let $P$ be the set of $w_i$ that beat $x$ and $Q$ the set of those that beat $y$. Since $x$ has $j$ in-neighbours, one of which is $y$, $|P|=j-1$ and $|Q|=j$; moreover $x$ beats every point of $Q$, and $y$ beats every point of $P$.

A local fact. Let $w$ be any point of $T$. If Kosta chooses $w$ first, Maja has an answer $z\to w$ after which the remaining $2j-1$ points form a good orientation; by Lemma 1 every point there has out-degree $j-1$, so every point $u\ne w,z$ beats exactly one of $w,z$. In particular $z$ beats every other in-neighbour of $w$.

Apply this to $w=w_i\in P$. Its in-neighbours are $y$ and the $j-1$ points $W_i=\{w_{i-j+1},\dots,w_{i-1}\}$. The point $z$ beating all other in-neighbours is either $y$, which then beats all of $W_i$, so $W_i\subseteq P$ and $P=W_i$ (both have $j-1$ elements); or $w_{i-j+1}$ (any other point of $W_i$ loses to $w_{i-j+1}$), which then beats $y$, so $w_{i-j+1}\in Q$. Note $i-j+1\equiv i+j\pmod{2j-1}$.

Suppose $P$ is not a block of $j-1$ consecutive points. Then $w_i\in P$ implies $w_{i+j}\in Q$. Since $2j\equiv1$, the sequence of indices $i_t=i_0+tj$ ($t=0,1,\dots,2j-2$) runs through all of $\mathbb Z_{2j-1}$, with $i_{t+2}=i_t+1$, and no two cyclically consecutive terms both index points of $P$. With $j-1$ terms in $P$ among $2j-1$, the gaps between consecutive $P$-terms are all $2$ except one gap of $3$; starting the count right after that gap, the $P$-terms are $i_{t_0},i_{t_0+2},\dots,i_{t_0+2(j-2)}$, i.e. the consecutive indices $i_{t_0},i_{t_0}+1,\dots,i_{t_0}+j-2$. So $P$ is a block after all, a contradiction.

Hence $P$ is a block; after relabelling, $P=\{w_j,\dots,w_{2j-2}\}$ and $Q=\{w_0,\dots,w_{j-1}\}$. Put the $2j+1$ points on a circle in the order $x,w_0,\dots,w_{j-1},y,w_j,\dots,w_{2j-2}$. Then $x$ beats the next $j$ points ($Q$); $y$ beats the next $j$ points ($P$ and $x$); a point $w_i\in Q$ beats $w_{i+1},\dots,w_{i+j-1}$ and $y$, which are its next $j$ points; and a point $w_i\in P$ beats $w_{i+1},\dots,w_{i+j-1}$ (indices modulo $2j-1$) and $x$, again its next $j$ points. So $T$ is rotational. $\square$

Counting. By Lemmas 2 and 3, Maja achieves her goal exactly for the rotational orientations. A rotational orientation comes from a cyclic order, and the cyclic order can be read off from it: the point following $u$ is the out-neighbour of $u$ that beats all other out-neighbours of $u$. So different cyclic orders give different orientations. The number of cyclic orders (with a direction) of $n=2k+1$ labelled points is $(n-1)!$, so the number of ways is $\boxed{(2k)!}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Determine all functions $f:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)$ such that \[f\!\left(\frac{x}{y}+\frac{y}{x}\right)=\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}+\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}\] for all $x,y\in(0,+\infty)$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x$ for all $x$, and $f(x)=2$ for all $x$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Logarithms, a Cauchy equation and a lower bound)

Let $c=f(1)$ and $\varphi(x)=\ln\frac{f(x)}{c}$, so $\varphi(1)=0$ and $\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}=e^{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}$. The equation becomes \[ f\Bigl(\frac xy+\frac yx\Bigr)=2\cosh\bigl(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\bigr). \tag{1} \] With $y=1$ and $x=t$: $f\bigl(t+\frac1t\bigr)=2\cosh\varphi(t)$. Applying this with $t=\frac xy$ and comparing with (1), and since $\cosh$ is even and injective on $[0,\infty)$, we get $|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|=|\varphi(x/y)|$. Let $G(s)=\varphi(e^s)$ for real $s$; then $G(0)=0$ and \[ |G(a)-G(b)|=|G(a-b)|\qquad\text{for all real } a,b. \tag{2} \]

Step 1: $G$ is additive. By (2), $|G(a+b)-G(b)|=|G(a)|$ and $|G(a+b)-G(a)|=|G(b)|$, so $G(a+b)\in\{G(a)+G(b),\,G(b)-G(a)\}$ and $G(a+b)\in\{G(a)+G(b),\,G(a)-G(b)\}$. If $G(a+b)\ne G(a)+G(b)$, then $G(b)-G(a)=G(a)-G(b)=G(a+b)$, so $G(a)=G(b)$ and $G(a+b)=0$; also $G(a)\ne0$ (otherwise $G(a+b)=0=G(a)+G(b)$). Call such a pair $(a,b)$ bad; for a bad pair $G(a)=G(b)=:g\ne0$ and $G(a+b)=0$.

Suppose $(a,b)$ is bad. By (2), $|G(z+a+b)-G(z)|=|G(a+b)|=0$, so $G$ has period $a+b$, and $G(-a)=G(b)=g$. Then (2) gives $|G(2a)|=|G(a)-G(-a)|=0$. Let $w=G(a/2)$. The pair $(\frac a2,\frac a2)$ is not bad, since $G(a)=g\ne0$, so $g=2w$. The pair $(a,\frac a2)$ is not bad, since $G(a)=2w\ne w=G(\frac a2)$, so $G(\frac{3a}2)=3w$. The pair $(\frac{3a}2,\frac a2)$ is not bad, since $3w\ne w$, so $G(2a)=4w\ne0$. This contradicts $G(2a)=0$. Hence there are no bad pairs: $G(a+b)=G(a)+G(b)$ for all $a,b$.

Step 2: $G$ is linear. Every $t\ge2$ equals $u+\frac1u$ for some $u>0$, and then $f(t)=2\cosh\varphi(u)\ge2$. Hence $G(s)=\ln\frac{f(e^s)}{c}\ge\ln\frac2c=:L$ for all $s\ge\delta:=\ln2$. For $0\le s\le\delta$ we get $G(s)=G(s+\delta)-G(\delta)\ge L-G(\delta)$ and (because $\delta-s$ also lies in $[0,\delta]$, so $G(\delta-s)\ge L-G(\delta)$ by the same bound) $G(s)=G(\delta)-G(\delta-s)\le 2G(\delta)-L$. So $G$ is bounded on $[0,\delta]$. Let $\lambda=G(\delta)/\delta$ and $K(s)=G(s)-\lambda s$. Then $K$ is additive, $K(\delta)=0$, so $K$ has period $\delta$; being bounded on $[0,\delta]$, it is bounded on $\mathbb R$. For every integer $N$, $NK(s)=K(Ns)$ is bounded, so $K(s)=0$. Thus $G(s)=\lambda s$, that is, $f(x)=cx^\lambda$.

Step 3: the exponent. Now $f\bigl(u+\frac1u\bigr)=\frac{f(u)}{c}+\frac{c}{f(u)}$ reads $c\bigl(u+\frac1u\bigr)^\lambda=u^\lambda+u^{-\lambda}$ for all $u>0$. At $u=1$: $c\cdot2^\lambda=2$.

  • If $\lambda<0$, the left side tends to $0$ as $u\to\infty$, while the right side is at least $2$. Impossible.

  • If $\lambda>0$, divide by $u^\lambda$: $c(1+u^{-2})^\lambda=1+u^{-2\lambda}$; letting $u\to\infty$ gives $c=1$, and then $2^\lambda=2$, so $\lambda=1$: $f(x)=x$.

  • If $\lambda=0$, then $c=2$: $f(x)=2$.

Both functions work: for $f(x)=x$ both sides equal $\frac xy+\frac yx$, and for $f\equiv2$ both sides equal $2$. Therefore \[ \boxed{f(x)=x\ \text{ for all } x>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x)=2\ \text{ for all } x>0.} \]

Solution 2 (Injective and non-injective cases)

Let $h(r)=r+\frac1r$ for $r>0$ and $c=f(1)$; recall that $h(r)=h(s)$ holds iff $s=r$ or $s=\frac1r$. The equation reads $f\bigl(h(\frac xy)\bigr)=h\bigl(\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}\bigr)$, and with $y=1$ it gives $f(h(t))=h\bigl(\frac{f(t)}{c}\bigr)$. Comparing the two (with $t=\frac xy$), for all $x,y>0$ \[ \frac{f(x)}{f(y)}=\frac{f(x/y)}{c}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}=\frac{c}{f(x/y)}. \tag{$\star$} \]

Periods. If $f(a)=f(b)$, then $f\bigl(\frac ab z\bigr)=f(z)$ for all $z>0$. Indeed, $(\star)$ with $x=a$, $y=b$ gives $f(\frac ab)=c$, and then $(\star)$ with $x=\frac ab z$, $y=z$ gives $\frac{f(\frac ab z)}{f(z)}\in\{1\}$.

Case 1: $f$ is injective. Let $y,z>0$ and $w=\sqrt{yz}$, so $\frac yw=\frac wz=:q$. By $(\star)$ both $\frac{f(y)}{f(w)}$ and $\frac{f(w)}{f(z)}$ lie in $\bigl\{\frac{f(q)}c,\frac c{f(q)}\bigr\}$. If they are equal, $f(w)^2=f(y)f(z)$. Otherwise they are reciprocal, so $f(y)=f(z)$, hence $y=z=w$ and again $f(w)^2=f(y)f(z)$. So $g(s)=\ln f(e^s)$ satisfies Jensen's equation $g\bigl(\frac{a+b}2\bigr)=\frac{g(a)+g(b)}2$; comparing the pairs $(a,b)$ and $(a+b,0)$, which have the same midpoint, gives $g(a+b)+g(0)=g(a)+g(b)$. Since $g(0)=\ln c$, this means \[ c\,f(xy)=f(x)f(y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y>0. \tag{M} \] In particular $f(t)f(\frac1t)=cf(1)=c^2$, so $\frac c{f(t)}=\frac{f(1/t)}c$, and $f(h(t))=h\bigl(\frac{f(t)}c\bigr)$ becomes $c\,f\bigl(t+\frac1t\bigr)=f(t)+f\bigl(\frac1t\bigr)$. Multiplying by $f(w)$ and using (M) for each product gives $c\,f\bigl(wt+\frac wt\bigr)=f(wt)+f\bigl(\frac wt\bigr)$ for all $w,t>0$. For $u,v>0$ take $w=\sqrt{uv}$, $t=\sqrt{u/v}$: then $c\,f(u+v)=f(u)+f(v)$. So $B=cf$ is additive on $(0,\infty)$: $B(u+v)=c^2f(u+v)=c\bigl(f(u)+f(v)\bigr)=B(u)+B(v)$. As $B>0$, $B$ is increasing ($B(u+v)>B(u)$), and additivity gives $B(qx)=qB(x)$ for rational $q>0$. With $\beta=B(1)$ and rationals $q_1<x<q_2$ we get $q_1\beta\le B(x)\le q_2\beta$, hence $B(x)=\beta x$. Thus $f(x)=\frac\beta c x$, and $f(1)=c$ gives $f(x)=cx$. Finally $c\,f(u+v)=f(u)+f(v)$ becomes $c^2(u+v)=c(u+v)$, so $c=1$ and $f(x)=x$.

Case 2: $f$ is not injective. Let $f(a)=f(b)$ with $a\ne b$ and $k=\max\bigl(\frac ab,\frac ba\bigr)>1$. By the periods fact (replacing $z$ by $kz$ if $\frac ab<1$), $f(kz)=f(z)$ for all $z$. Then for every $t>0$, \[ f\bigl(h(kt)\bigr)=h\Bigl(\frac{f(kt)}c\Bigr)=h\Bigl(\frac{f(t)}c\Bigr)=f\bigl(h(t)\bigr), \] so, again by the periods fact, $\lambda(t)=\frac{h(kt)}{h(t)}=\frac{k^2t^2+1}{k(t^2+1)}$ satisfies $f(\lambda(t)z)=f(z)$ for all $z$. The function $\lambda$ is continuous, tends to $\frac1k$ as $t\to0$ and to $k$ as $t\to\infty$, so every $\lambda\in(\frac1k,k)$ is a period. Products of periods are periods. For any $x>0$ choose $N$ with $x^{1/N}\in(\frac1k,k)$; then $x=(x^{1/N})^N$ is a period, so $f(x)=f(x\cdot1)=f(1)$. Thus $f$ is constant, and the equation $f\equiv C$ gives $C=1+1=2$.

Both $f(x)=x$ and $f\equiv2$ satisfy the equation, so the solutions are \[ \boxed{f(x)=x\ \text{ for all } x>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x)=2\ \text{ for all } x>0.} \]

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes

Let $k$ and $l$ be nonnegative integers, and let $r$ and $s$ be positive integers. Denote by $S$ the set of all pairs of positive integers $(a,b)$ such that \[a\mid b^2+kb+1,\qquad b\mid a^2+al+1.\] Prove that the pair $(r,s)$ belongs to the set $S$ if and only if the set \[G=\{\gcd(a-r,b-s)\mid (a,b)\in S\}\] is infinite.

Show the solution

Solution (Vieta jumping, a bounded index, and periodicity modulo $d$)

Let $\Phi(a,b)=a^2+b^2+la+kb+1$.

Step 0: an equation for $S$. If $(a,b)\in S$, a common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $b^2+kb+1$ and $b$, hence $1$; so $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Since $a\mid a^2+la$ and $a\mid b^2+kb+1$, we get $a\mid\Phi(a,b)$; likewise $b\mid\Phi(a,b)$, so $ab\mid\Phi(a,b)$. Conversely, if $\Phi(a,b)=tab$ for an integer $t$, then $a\mid b^2+kb+1$ and $b\mid a^2+la+1$. So $S$ is the set of positive pairs with $\Phi(a,b)=tab$ for some integer $t$, which we call the index of $(a,b)$.

Step 1: jumps. Let $(a,b)\in S$ have index $t$. As a quadratic in $a$, $X^2+(l-tb)X+(b^2+kb+1)=0$ has the root $a$; the other root is $a'=tb-a-l=\frac{b^2+kb+1}{a}$, a positive integer, so $(a',b)\in S$ with index $t$. Similarly $b'=ta-b-k=\frac{a^2+la+1}{b}$ is a positive integer and $(a,b')\in S$ with index $t$.

Step 2: finitely many indices. Given an index $t$, choose $(a,b)\in S$ of index $t$ with $a+b$ minimal. Then $a'\ge a$ and $b'\ge b$, i.e. $b^2+kb+1\ge a^2$ and $a^2+la+1\ge b^2$. If $a\le b$, \[ t=\frac{\Phi(a,b)}{ab}\le\frac{2a^2+2la+2+kb}{ab}=\frac{2a}{b}+\frac{2l}{b}+\frac{2}{ab}+\frac ka\le 4+2l+k , \] and if $b\le a$, similarly $t\le4+2k+l$. So every index is at most $4+2(k+l)$: only finitely many indices occur.

Step 3: if $G$ is infinite, then $(r,s)\in S$. Let $N_t=\Phi(r,s)-trs$. If $g\in G$, $g\ge1$, comes from $(a,b)\in S$ of index $t$, then $a\equiv r$ and $b\equiv s\pmod g$, so $N_t\equiv\Phi(a,b)-tab=0\pmod g$. If $G$ is infinite it contains arbitrarily large $g$; since only finitely many indices exist, one index $t_0$ serves for arbitrarily large $g$, and $N_{t_0}$ is divisible by arbitrarily large numbers. Hence $N_{t_0}=0$, i.e. $\Phi(r,s)=t_0rs$, and $(r,s)\in S$ by Step 0.

Step 4: if $(r,s)\in S$, then $G$ is infinite. Let $t$ be the index of $(r,s)$ and define $J(a,b)=(a_1,b_1)$ with $a_1=tb-a-l$, $b_1=ta_1-b-k$. By Step 1, $J$ maps solutions of index $t$ to solutions of index $t$. $J$ is a bijection of $\mathbb Z^2$ given by integer formulas, with inverse $(a_1,b_1)\mapsto(tb-a_1-l,\,b)$ where $b=ta_1-b_1-k$, also given by integer formulas. Hence for every $d\ge1$, $J$ induces a permutation of the finite set $(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^2$, and some power $J^N$ with $N\ge1$ acts on it as the identity: $J^N(r,s)\equiv(r,s)\pmod d$.

The orbit of $(r,s)$ is infinite. Indeed, write $z_0=r$, $z_1=s$, $(z_2,z_1)$ for the first jump, $(z_2,z_3)$ for the second, and so on; then $J^i(r,s)=(z_{2i},z_{2i+1})$, and by Step 1, $z_{i+1}z_{i-1}=z_i^2+c_iz_i+1$ with $c_i\in\{k,l\}$. Hence \[ \frac{z_{i+1}}{z_i}=\frac{z_i^2+c_iz_i+1}{z_iz_{i-1}}>\frac{z_i}{z_{i-1}}, \] so the ratios $z_{i+1}/z_i$ increase strictly. If all of them were at most $1$, the positive integers $z_i$ would be non-increasing, hence eventually constant, $z_{i-1}=z_i=z_{i+1}=z$, and then $z^2=z^2+c_iz+1$, impossible. So from some point on the ratios exceed $1$ and the $z_i$ increase strictly; the orbit is infinite, and in particular $J^N(r,s)\ne(r,s)$ for every $N\ge1$.

So for every $d$ there is $(a,b)=J^N(r,s)\in S$, $(a,b)\ne(r,s)$, with $d\mid a-r$ and $d\mid b-s$; then $\gcd(a-r,b-s)$ is a positive multiple of $d$. Thus $G$ contains numbers at least $d$ for every $d$, and $G$ is infinite.

Steps 3 and 4 together prove that $(r,s)\in S$ if and only if $G$ is infinite.
