Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Factorization

The composite numbers from the set $\{1,2,3,\ldots,30\}$ are chosen and written on a whiteboard. After erasing $3$ numbers on the whiteboard, is it possible to color the remaining numbers blue, red and green so that the products of the numbers of each color are equal?

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the composite entries and record their prime factorizations. Look for primes that appear in very few entries.
Hint 2: key idea
If the three colors have equal products, the product of all unerased entries is a cube. Use the resulting conditions on prime exponents to restrict what can be erased. (LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization)
Hint 3: approach
Use primes appearing in only one entry to force some erasures. Compare the remaining exponent totals modulo $3$ to identify candidates for the other erasure. Then decide whether the surviving entries can be split into three equal products, using the target prime exponents for one colour as a guide.
Common trap
Making the total product a cube still leaves the task of splitting the entries into three equal products.
Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – Make the product a cube

The composite numbers up to $30$ are \[4,6,8,9,10,12,14,15,16,18,20,21,22,24,25,26,27,28,30 .\] If the remaining numbers are split into three groups with equal products $m$, the product of all remaining numbers is $m^3$, so every prime exponent in it must be divisible by $3$. This tells us what to erase: $11$ divides only $22$ and $13$ divides only $26$, so $22$ and $26$ must be erased. In the product of the other $17$ numbers the exponent of $2$ is $23$, of $3$ is $13$, of $5$ is $6$ and of $7$ is $3$; so the third erased number must have $2$-exponent $\equiv2$ and $3$-exponent $\equiv1\pmod 3$, and no factor $5$ or $7$: it is $12$.

After erasing $12$, $22$, $26$, the product is $2^{21}3^{12}5^67^3=m^3$ with $m=2^7\cdot3^4\cdot5^2\cdot7=1814400$, and indeed \[30\cdot28\cdot27\cdot20\cdot4\;=\;25\cdot24\cdot21\cdot18\cdot8\;=\;16\cdot15\cdot14\cdot10\cdot9\cdot6\;=\;1814400 .\] Colour the three groups blue, red and green. So the answer is Yes (and $12,22,26$ is the only possible choice of erased numbers).

Problem 2

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Multi-Method Diophantine Problems

Find all prime numbers $p,q,r,s$ such that $p-2$, $q-2$, $r-2$, $s-2$ and $pqrs-2$ are all perfect squares.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write each candidate prime as a square plus two. Try a few small square roots and record which candidates are prime.
Hint 2: key idea
Use square residues modulo $8$ and $4$ to restrict how often the even prime can occur. Then reduce the remaining product equation modulo $3$ and check what happens if none of the odd primes is $3$. (LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking)
Hint 3: approach
Order the two remaining primes as $r\le s$ and write $s=b^2+2$. If $2n$ is the square root of $pqrs-2$, factor $4n^2-b^2$ and write whichever factor is divisible by $s$ as $ms$. Use the resulting squared expression to bound $m$, treating $r=3$ separately and using reduction modulo $r$ in the other case.
Common trap
Zero is a perfect square, so the prime $2$ must be considered.
Show the answer

$\{p,q,r,s\}=\{2,3,3,11\}$ or $\{2,3,11,83\}$ (in any order)

Show solution 1 – Congruences and a difference of squares

(We use the convention that $0=0^2$ is a perfect square; without it no prime can be $2$ and, by Step 1, there are no solutions.)

Step 1: reduction. Each prime has the form $k^2+2$. If $k$ is even, the prime is even, so it is $2$ ($k=0$). If $k$ is odd, then $k^2\equiv1\pmod8$ and the prime is $\equiv3\pmod8$. Also, if $3\nmid k$ then $3\mid k^2+2$, so the prime is $3$ ($k=1$); otherwise $3\mid k$.

Let $j$ be the number of $2$'s among $p,q,r,s$. If $j=0$, all four primes are $\equiv3\pmod 8$, so $pqrs\equiv3^4\equiv1\pmod8$ and $pqrs-2\equiv7\pmod8$, not a square. If $j\ge2$, then $pqrs-2\equiv2\pmod4$, not a square. So exactly one prime is $2$; say $p=2$. Then $2qrs-2=m^2$ forces $m=2n$ and $qrs=2n^2+1$. If none of $q,r,s$ is $3$, then each is $\equiv2\pmod3$, so $qrs\equiv2\pmod3$ and $2n^2\equiv1$, i.e. $n^2\equiv2\pmod3$, impossible. So say $q=3$; by symmetry let $r\le s$, and write $r=a^2+2$, $s=b^2+2$ with $1\le a\le b$ (neither is $2$). Then \[3rs=2n^2+1 .\]

Step 2: the key factorization. Since $4n^2=6rs-2$ and $b^2=s-2$, \[(2n-b)(2n+b)=4n^2-b^2=6rs-s=(6r-1)\,s .\] The right side is positive, so $2n>b$ and both factors are positive. As $s$ is prime, it divides $2n-\varepsilon b$ for some $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$: write $2n-\varepsilon b=ms$ and $2n+\varepsilon b=k$ with positive integers $m,k$, $mk=6r-1$. Then $k=ms+2\varepsilon b$, and \[6r-1=mk=m^2(b^2+2)+2\varepsilon mb\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (mb+\varepsilon)^2+2m^2=6r=6a^2+12 .\qquad(*)\]

Step 3: $r=3$ ($a=1$). Then $(*)$ reads $(mb+\varepsilon)^2+2m^2=18$, so $m\le3$; $m=3$ gives $3b+\varepsilon=0$ and $m=2$ gives $(2b+\varepsilon)^2=10$, both impossible, and $m=1$ gives $(b+\varepsilon)^2=16$, $b\in\{3,5\}$. Now $s=27$ is not prime, while $b=3$ gives $s=11$ and $3\cdot3\cdot11=99=2\cdot7^2+1$.

Step 4: $r\ne3$ ($a\ge2$, and by Step 1, $3\mid a$, so $a\ge3$). If $m\ge3$, then by $a\le b$, \[(mb+\varepsilon)^2+2m^2\ \ge\ (3b-1)^2+18\ =\ 6b^2+12+(3b^2-6b+7)\ >\ 6b^2+12\ \ge\ 6a^2+12,\] contradicting $(*)$ (note $3b^2-6b+7=3(b-1)^2+4>0$). If $m=2$, $(*)$ gives $(2b+\varepsilon)^2=6a^2+4$, odd $=$ even, impossible. So $m=1$ and \[c^2=6a^2+10,\qquad c=b+\varepsilon .\] Now work modulo the prime $r=a^2+2$: $c^2=6(a^2+2)-2\equiv-2\equiv a^2$, so $r\mid(c-a)(c+a)$. But $0<c-a<r$ (because $c^2=6a^2+10<(a^2+a+2)^2$), so $r\mid c+a$; and $0<c+a<2r$ (because $c^2<(2a^2-a+4)^2$), so $c+a=a^2+2$. Then $(a^2-a+2)^2=6a^2+10$. For $a=3$ both sides equal $64$, while for $a\ge4$ we have $a^2-a\ge3a$, so $(a^2-a+2)^2\ge(3a+2)^2>6a^2+10$. So $a=3$, $r=11$, $c=8$, $b=c-\varepsilon\in\{7,9\}$. As $3\mid b$, $b=9$ and $s=83$; indeed $3\cdot11\cdot83=2739=2\cdot37^2+1$.

Answer. $2\cdot3\cdot3\cdot11-2=196=14^2$ and $2\cdot3\cdot11\cdot83-2=5476=74^2$, and $0,1,1,9$ resp. $0,1,9,81$ are squares. So $\{p,q,r,s\}=\{2,3,3,11\}$ or $\{2,3,11,83\}$, in any order.

Show solution 2 – Congruences, then unique factorization in $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]$

(We use the convention that $0=0^2$ is a perfect square; without it no prime can be $2$ and, by Step 1, there are no solutions.)

Step 1: which primes. Each prime has the form $k^2+2$. If $k$ is even then the prime is even, so it is $2$ ($k=0$). If $k$ is odd then $k^2\equiv1\pmod8$ and the prime is $\equiv3\pmod8$. Also, if $3\nmid k$ then $k^2+2\equiv0\pmod3$, so the prime is $3$ ($k=1$); otherwise it is $\equiv2\pmod3$.

Let $j$ be the number of $2$'s among $p,q,r,s$. If $j=0$, then $pqrs\equiv3^4\equiv1\pmod8$ and $pqrs-2\equiv7\pmod8$, not a square. If $j\ge2$, then $pqrs-2\equiv2\pmod4$, not a square. So exactly one prime is $2$; say $p=2$. Then $2qrs-2=m^2$ forces $m=2n$ and \[qrs=2n^2+1 .\] If none of $q,r,s$ is $3$, then $qrs\equiv2^3\equiv2\pmod3$ and $2n^2\equiv1$, i.e. $n^2\equiv2\pmod3$, impossible. So say $q=3$. If $r=s=3$, then $2n^2=26$, impossible.

Step 2: the ring $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]$. Write $w=\sqrt{-2}$. The ring $\mathbb Z[w]$ is Euclidean for the norm $N(x+yw)=x^2+2y^2$, so it has unique factorization, and its units are $\pm1$. A prime $\ell=k^2+2$ ($k\ge1$) factors as $\pi_\ell\bar\pi_\ell$ with $\pi_\ell=k+w$, a prime element (its norm is prime), not associate to $\bar\pi_\ell$. Let $\nu=1+nw$, so $N(\nu)=\nu\bar\nu=2n^2+1=3rs$. Every prime factor of $\nu$ divides $N(\nu)$, hence is associate to some $\pi_\ell$ or $\bar\pi_\ell$ with $\ell\in\{3,r,s\}$; and $\pi_\ell$, $\bar\pi_\ell$ never both divide $\nu$ (otherwise $\ell\mid\nu$, i.e. $\ell\mid1$). So for each such prime $\ell$ exactly one of $\pi_\ell,\bar\pi_\ell$ divides $\nu$, to the full power of $\ell$ in $3rs$.

Step 3a: $r=3$, $s\ne3$. Then $9s=2n^2+1$, $s=b^2+2$ with $b\ge3$, and $\nu=\pm(1+\varepsilon w)^2(b+\delta w)$ with signs $\varepsilon,\delta$. As $(1+\varepsilon w)^2=-1+2\varepsilon w$, the real part of $\nu$ is $\pm(-b-4\varepsilon\delta)$, which must equal $1$. So $b=4\pm1$, i.e. $b=3$ or $b=5$. For $b=5$, $s=27$ is not prime; for $b=3$, $s=11$ and $9\cdot11=2\cdot7^2+1$.

Step 3b: $r,s\ne3$. Then $r=a^2+2$, $s=b^2+2$ with $a,b\ge3$, and $\nu=\pm(1+\varepsilon_1w)(a+\varepsilon_2w)(b+\varepsilon_3w)$. Since $(1+\varepsilon_1w)(a+\varepsilon_2w)=(a-2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2)+(\varepsilon_1a+\varepsilon_2)w$, the real part of the product is \[(a-2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2)b-2\varepsilon_3(\varepsilon_1a+\varepsilon_2)=(a-v)(b-u)-\tfrac32uv,\qquad u=2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_3,\ v=2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2,\] (using $2\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3=uv/2$). It must be $\pm1$, and $uv=\pm4$, so $(a-v)(b-u)\in\{\pm5,\pm7\}$. Here $a-v\ge1$ and $b-u\ge1$, so the product is $5$ or $7$ and one factor is $1$; say $a-v=1$, i.e. $a=3$, $r=11$. Then $b-u\in\{5,7\}$ gives $b\in\{3,5,7,9\}$, and $3\mid b$ (Step 1) leaves $b=3$ or $b=9$. Now $3\cdot11\cdot11=363=2n^2+1$ would give $n^2=181$, impossible, while $3\cdot11\cdot83=2739=2\cdot37^2+1$.

Answer. $2\cdot3\cdot3\cdot11-2=196=14^2$ and $2\cdot3\cdot11\cdot83-2=5476=74^2$, and all of $0,1,1,9$ resp. $0,1,9,81$ are squares. So $\{p,q,r,s\}=\{2,3,3,11\}$ or $\{2,3,11,83\}$, in any order.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Invariant-Based Game Analysis · also 2026-azerbaijan-senior-nmo: Day 1, Problem 2

Two players $A$ and $B$ play the following game on an $8\times 8$ chessboard with one knight. A knight's “direction” is defined as the direction he moves two cells. For example: If a knight moves two cells upward and one cell left, then his “direction” is up. If it moves two cells right and one cell down then his “direction” is right. First, $A$ places the knight on some cell of the board and every round goes as follows: Firstly $B$ advises a “direction” for the move, if there exist two possible moves with that “direction” then $A$ chooses one of them and changes the knight's position, if there is only one possible move then $A$ can either reject or accept to do that move. It is known that $B$ cannot advise the same “direction” twice in a round and $A$ cannot reject $B$'s propositions infinitely many times. The round ends when the knight's position has been changed. When a knight moves to a cell that cell gets marked (including the beginning cell). Find the least positive integer $k$, such that regardless of the advices of $B$, $A$ can make the number of marked cells in the board at most $k$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the legal moves from an interior cell and from a corner. Group them by the directions $B$ could name and mark when $A$ can reject a proposal.
Hint 2: key idea
Look for a set with an internal move from each of its cells and an internal reply to every direction offering two moves. For a candidate, look for a periodic pattern of cells, described by a simple condition on the coordinates, that suitable knight moves preserve. (LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
Check that each chosen cell has a legal move staying in the set and that every direction with two moves offers one inside it. For a lower bound, start from a winning state with as many marked cells as possible, then show its internal safe set must meet every interior five-cell X by examining moves from the nearest safe cell to any missed X. In the top-left quarter, use the Xs centred at $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$, and test whether omitting the rest would force all of the X at $(2,1)$ to be absent.
Common trap
A direction with two legal moves must be answered, even when both destinations are new cells.
Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution – A residue class modulo $5$, and three cells in every quarter

Reading of the rules. In a round, $B$ advises different directions one after another (a direction with no legal move is not advised). If the advised direction has two moves, $A$ must make one of them, of his choice. If it has one move, $A$ may reject it, and then $B$ advises another direction; $A$ must accept when $B$ has no further direction left (he cannot reject forever).

Self-safe sets. Call a set $T$ of cells self-safe if for every $u\in T$:

  • (i) whenever a direction has two moves from $u$, at least one of them ends in $T$, and

  • (ii) at least one move from $u$ ends in $T$.

If the knight stands on a cell of a self-safe set $T$, then $A$ can keep it in $T$ for the whole round: a two-move direction is answered by a move into $T$; a one-move direction is accepted if it ends in $T$ and rejected otherwise. $A$ is never forced to accept a move out of $T$, because the direction from (ii) (whose move into $T$ is accepted, or chosen) is still available to $B$ as long as $A$ has only rejected moves out of $T$. Conversely, if (i) fails at $u$, then $B$ advises that direction; if (ii) fails, $B$ advises directions until $A$ must accept; either way the knight leaves $T$.

Upper bound $12$. Let $T$ be the set of the $12$ cells $(r,c)$ ($0\le r,c\le7$, row $r$ from the top, column $c$ from the left) with \[r+2c\equiv3\pmod5 .\] The knight moves $(+1,+2),(-1,-2),(+2,-1),(-2,+1)$ keep $r+2c$ modulo $5$, and each direction (right, left, down, up) contains exactly one of them: right $=(\pm1,+2)$, left $=(\pm1,-2)$, down $=(+2,\pm1)$, up $=(-2,\pm1)$. So in every direction with two moves, one of them stays in the class of $r+2c$ modulo $5$, i.e. in $T$; this is (i). Each of the $12$ cells has a class-preserving move inside the board, which is (ii): \[\begin{array}{llll} (0,4)\to(2,3), & (1,1)\to(2,3), & (1,6)\to(3,5), & (2,3)\to(1,1),\\ (3,0)\to(4,2), & (3,5)\to(4,7), & (4,2)\to(3,0), & (4,7)\to(3,5),\\ (5,4)\to(6,6), & (6,1)\to(4,2), & (6,6)\to(5,4), & (7,3)\to(5,4). \end{array}\] So $A$ puts the knight on a cell of $T$ (this first cell is marked too, and it is in $T$) and keeps it in $T$ forever: at most $12$ cells are marked (see the left part of the figure).

Reduction of the lower bound. Suppose $A$ can guarantee at most $k$ marked cells. Call a position (knight's cell $v$, set $M$ of marked cells) winning if from it $A$ can guarantee that at most $k$ cells are ever marked. Choose a winning position $(v,M)$ with $|M|$ as large as possible. From a winning position $A$ can force the next position to be winning; from $(u,M)$ a move to a new cell $w\notin M$ would give a winning position with more marked cells, which is impossible, so $A$ can force the knight to stay in $M$ and on a cell $w$ with $(w,M)$ winning. Hence $T=\{u\in M:(u,M)\text{ winning}\}$ is non-empty and self-safe, with $|T|\le k$. So $k$ is at least the size of the smallest self-safe set.

Every self-safe set has at least $12$ cells. Let $T$ be a non-empty self-safe set. Cells are $(r,c)$ with row $r$ counted from the top; “up” from $(r,c)$ means the moves to $(r-2,c\pm1)$, “left” the moves to $(r\pm1,c-2)$, and so on. We use (i) in this form: if a direction has two moves from $u$ and neither target is in $T$, then $u\notin T$. The reflections of the board in its two axes and in a diagonal permute the four directions, so they map self-safe sets to self-safe sets.

For $1\le r,c\le6$ let $X(r,c)$ be the set of the five cells $(r,c)$, $(r\pm1,c\pm1)$ (a cell and its four diagonal neighbours).

Lemma 1. $T$ contains a cell of every $X(m)$. Suppose $T\cap X(m)=\emptyset$, $m=(m_r,m_c)$. For a cell $v$ let $d(v)=(v_r-m_r)^2+(v_c-m_c)^2$, and write $m+(x,y)$ for the cell $(m_r+x,m_c+y)$. Choose $u\in T$ with $d(u)$ as small as possible. After reflections (which keep the form of the sets $X$) we may assume $u=m+(x,y)$ with $x\ge y\ge0$. Since $u\notin X(m)$, $(x,y)\ne(0,0),(1,1)$, so $(x,y)=(1,0)$ or $x\ge2$. Note $d(m+(x',y'))-d(u)=x'^2+y'^2-x^2-y^2$.

  • $(x,y)=(1,0)$. “Up” from $u$ has the two moves to $m+(-1,\pm1)$, both in $X(m)$, so $u\notin T$: contradiction.

  • $x\ge2$ and $u$ is not in the last column. “Up” from $u$ has two moves, to $m+(x-2,y-1)$ and $m+(x-2,y+1)$, and one of them is in $T$. Their values of $d$ are $d(u)-(4x+2y-5)$ and $d(u)-(4x-2y-5)$. The first is smaller than $d(u)$; the second is smaller than $d(u)$ unless $(x,y)=(2,2)$ (for $x\ge3$, $4x-2y-5\ge2x-5>0$; for $x=2$, $y\le1$, $3-2y>0$). By the choice of $u$ we must have $(x,y)=(2,2)$ and $w=m+(0,3)\in T$. But “left” from $w$ has the two moves to $m+(\pm1,1)$, both in $X(m)$: contradiction.

  • $x\ge2$ and $u$ is in the last column (so $y=7-m_c\ge1$). If $u$ is the corner $(7,7)$, by (ii) one of its two moves, to $m+(x-2,y-1)$ or $m+(x-1,y-2)$, ends in $T$; the values of $d$ are $d(u)-(4x+2y-5)$ and $d(u)-(2x+4y-5)$, both smaller than $d(u)$: contradiction. Otherwise “left” from $u$ has two moves, so $w=m+(x+\varepsilon,y-2)\in T$ for some $\varepsilon=\pm1$. The cell $w$ is in column $5$ and in a row $\ge m_r+1\ge2$, so “up” from $w$ has two moves and $z=m+(x+\varepsilon-2,\ y-2+\delta)\in T$ for some $\delta=\pm1$. The four possibilities for $z$ are \[m+(x-1,y-1),\quad m+(x-1,y-3),\quad m+(x-3,y-1),\quad m+(x-3,y-3),\] with $d(z)-d(u)=-(2x+2y-2),\ -(2x+6y-10),\ -(6x+2y-10),\ -(6x+6y-18)$. For $x\ge2$, $y\ge1$ these are all negative, except that the second and the fourth vanish for $(x,y)=(2,1)$. So $(x,y)=(2,1)$, hence $m_c=6$ and $d(u)=5$. Now $\varepsilon=-1$ would give $w=m+(1,-1)\in X(m)$, so $w=m+(3,-1)$, and $z=m+(1,0)$ (with $d=1$, impossible) or $z=m+(1,-2)\in T$. This cell is in column $4$ and in row $m_r+1\le6$, so “right” from it has two moves, to $m+(0,0)\in X(m)$ and to $m+(2,0)$ with $d=4<5$. Neither is in $T$: contradiction.

This proves Lemma 1.

Lemma 2. $T$ contains a cell of $R=\{(1,0),(1,3),(3,0),(3,1),(3,2),(3,3)\}$. Suppose not. “Down” from $(1,2)$ has the two moves to $(3,1),(3,3)$, so $(1,2)\notin T$; “right” from $(2,1)$ has the two moves to $(1,3),(3,3)$, so $(2,1)\notin T$. Then $T$ contains no cell of $X(2,1)=\{(2,1),(1,0),(1,2),(3,0),(3,2)\}$, against Lemma 1.

Counting. The top-left $4\times4$ quarter of the board is the disjoint union of \[X(1,1)=\{(1,1),(0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(2,2)\},\qquad X(1,2)=\{(1,2),(0,1),(0,3),(2,1),(2,3)\},\qquad R\] (see the right part of the figure), so by the two lemmas it contains at least $3$ cells of $T$. By reflection the same holds for the other three quarters. Hence every self-safe set has at least $12$ cells, so $k\ge12$, and with the upper bound the answer is $\boxed{12}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

Find all pairs of positive integers $(n,k)$ such that

  • (i) $k$ is the smallest positive integer that does not divide $n$,

  • (ii) $n\mid 3k^2+1$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Test a small value of $n$ by listing its divisors in order. Notice what knowing the first missing divisor tells you about every smaller positive integer.
Hint 2: key idea
Every positive integer below $k$ divides $3k^2+1$ because it divides $n$. Use $k-1$ in particular and replace $k$ by its remainder modulo $k-1$. (LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction)
Hint 3: approach
Use the divisibility condition on $k-1$ to make a short list of possible $k$. For each one, list the divisors of $3k^2+1$ that are multiples of the least common multiple of the smaller positive integers. Check the defining condition on $k$ for those candidates.
Common trap
Having every smaller integer divide $n$ is only half the condition, since $k$ itself must fail to divide it.
Show the answer

$(n,k)\in\{(1,2),(13,2),(2,3),(4,3),(14,3),(28,3)\}$

Show solution 1 – $k-1$ divides $4$

Since $1\mid n$, we have $k\ge2$, and $1,2,\dots,k-1$ all divide $n$, hence divide $3k^2+1$.

$k-1\mid 3k^2+1$ and $3k^2+1\equiv3+1=4\pmod{k-1}$, so $k-1\mid4$: $k\in\{2,3,5\}$.

  • $k=2$: $n$ is odd and $n\mid13$: $n=1$ or $n=13$; both have $2$ as smallest non-divisor.

  • $k=3$: $n$ is even, $3\nmid n$, $n\mid28$: $n\in\{2,4,14,28\}$; all work.

  • $k=5$: $12\mid n\mid76$, impossible since $3\nmid76$.

The pairs are $\boxed{(1,2),(13,2),(2,3),(4,3),(14,3),(28,3)}$.

Show solution 2 – Residues modulo $3$

If $k\ge4$, then $3<k$, so $3\mid n$ by (i); but $n\mid 3k^2+1$ and $3k^2+1\equiv1\pmod3$, a contradiction. Also $k\ne1$ because $1\mid n$. So $k=2$ or $k=3$.

  • $k=2$ means exactly that $n$ is odd; with $n\mid13$ this gives $n\in\{1,13\}$.

  • $k=3$ means $2\mid n$ and $3\nmid n$; with $n\mid28$ this gives $n\in\{2,4,14,28\}$.

Hence $(n,k)\in\boxed{\{(1,2),(13,2),(2,3),(4,3),(14,3),(28,3)\}}$.

Problem 5

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Let $a,b,c$ be positive real numbers such that $a+b+c=1$. Prove that \[\sqrt a+\sqrt b+\sqrt c\geq 3\sqrt3\,(ab+bc+ca).\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the terms containing $a$ on both sides, then repeat for $b$ and $c$. Look at what the condition on their sum says about the two variables other than $a$.
Hint 2: key idea
Rewrite twice the sum of pairwise products as the sum of $x(1-x)$ over $x=a,b,c$. This suggests looking for a bound comparing one such term with $\sqrt x$. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Divide the proposed one-variable comparison by $\sqrt x$ and square the nonnegative expression that remains to be bounded. Apply AM-GM to $2x$, $1-x$ and $1-x$, and track the constant that this gives in the one-variable estimate.
Common trap
Each pairwise product is counted twice when you group the terms by variable.
Show solution 1 – One variable at a time

Since $a+b+c=1$, $2(ab+bc+ca)=\sum a(b+c)=\sum a(1-a)$. So it suffices to show, for $0<x<1$, \[\sqrt x\ \ge\ \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}\,x(1-x)\iff \sqrt x\,(1-x)\le\frac{2}{3\sqrt3},\] and add the three inequalities for $x=a,b,c$. By AM-GM for $2x,\ 1-x,\ 1-x$: \[2x(1-x)^2\le\Bigl(\frac{2x+2(1-x)}{3}\Bigr)^3=\frac8{27},\] so $x(1-x)^2\le\frac4{27}$ and $\sqrt x\,(1-x)\le\frac2{3\sqrt3}$. Adding, $\sqrt a+\sqrt b+\sqrt c\ge\frac{3\sqrt3}2\sum a(1-a)=3\sqrt3(ab+bc+ca)$, with equality for $a=b=c=\frac13$.

Show solution 2 – Hölder and one variable

By Hölder's inequality (or the power-mean inequality), \[(\sqrt a+\sqrt b+\sqrt c)^2\,(a^2+b^2+c^2)\ \ge\ (a+b+c)^3=1 ,\] since $\bigl(\sqrt a\cdot\sqrt a\cdot a^2\bigr)^{1/3}=a$ and similarly for $b,c$. Put $u=a^2+b^2+c^2$; then $\frac13\le u<1$ (as $u\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}3$) and $ab+bc+ca=\frac{1-u}2$. So $\sqrt a+\sqrt b+\sqrt c\ge\frac1{\sqrt u}$, and it suffices to prove \[\frac1{\sqrt u}\ \ge\ \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}(1-u)\iff \sqrt u\,(1-u)\le\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}\qquad\Bigl(\tfrac13\le u<1\Bigr).\] Put $u=t^2$ with $\frac1{\sqrt3}\le t<1$. The function $t(1-t^2)$ is decreasing there, because for $\frac1{\sqrt3}\le t<t'$, \[t'(1-t'^2)-t(1-t^2)=(t'-t)\bigl(1-t'^2-t't-t^2\bigr)<(t'-t)(1-3t^2)\le0 .\] Hence $\sqrt u\,(1-u)\le\frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac23=\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}$, as needed. Equality requires $u=\frac13$, i.e. $a=b=c=\frac13$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Spiral Similarity Centers · also 2026-azerbaijan-senior-nmo: Day 2, Problem 5

Let $ABC$ be a triangle, and let $D$ be a point on the segment $BC$, different from $B$, $C$ and the midpoint of $BC$. The lines through $D$ parallel to $AB$ and $AC$ meet $AC$ and $AB$ at $E$ and $F$, respectively. Let the circumcircles of triangles $AEF$ and $ABC$ intersect again at $X$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. The line $XM$ meets the circumcircle of $ABC$ again at $Y$. Let $AY$ intersect $BC$ at $K$. Prove that $MD=MK$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the parallel segments through $D$ and mark both circles through $A$ and $X$. Record which of $E,F$ lies on each side through $A$.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare directed angles at $B,C$ on one circle and at $E,F$ on the other. Look for a spiral similarity with centre $X$ that takes the pair $F,E$ to the pair $B,C$. (LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Put $M$ at the complex origin and choose $B=-1$, $C=1$, then express the coordinates of $E,F$ using the parallel segments. Write the similarity as an equality of complex ratios to solve for $X$, and use the power of $M$ to express $Y$ through $X$. Set up a real-ratio test for collinearity with $A,Y$ and the reflection of $D$ across $M$.
Common trap
Since $M$ is between $X$ and $Y$, their coordinates lie on opposite rays from the origin.
Show solution 1 – Complex numbers

The point $X$. The point $D$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$ (if $D=B$ or $D=C$, one of $E,F$ is $A$ and the circle $AEF$ is not defined), and $D\ne M$ (if $D=M$ the two circles are tangent at $A$ and there is no $X$). The point $X\ne A$ lies on both circles, so, with directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, $\angle(FX,FB)=\angle(FX,FA)=\angle(EX,EA)=\angle(EX,EC)$ and $\angle(BX,BF)=\angle(BX,BA)=\angle(CX,CA)=\angle(CX,CE)$. Hence the triangles $XFB$ and $XEC$ are directly similar, and $X$ is the centre of the spiral similarity that maps $F\mapsto B$ and $E\mapsto C$.

Use complex numbers with $BC$ on the real axis, $M=0$, $B=-1$, $C=1$, $D=d$ real with $-1<d<1$, $d\ne0$, and $A=a$ not real. Since $DF\parallel AC$ and $DE\parallel AB$, Thales gives $BF:BA=BD:BC=\frac{1+d}2$ and $CE:CA=CD:CB=\frac{1-d}2$, so \[f=-1+\tfrac{1+d}{2}(a+1),\qquad e=1+\tfrac{1-d}{2}(a-1),\qquad e+f=a+d,\qquad f-e=da-1 .\] The spiral similarity condition reads $\frac{b-x}{f-x}=\frac{c-x}{e-x}$ with $b=-1$, $c=1$, i.e. $(-1-x)(e-x)=(1-x)(f-x)$, which is linear in $x$: \[x=\frac{e+f}{2+f-e}=\frac{a+d}{1+da}\] (here $1+da\ne0$, because $a$ is not real). The point $Y$. $M=0$ is inside the circumcircle and its power is $-MB\cdot MC=-1$, so $Y$ is on the line $XM$, on the other side of $M$, with $MX\cdot MY=1$: that is, $y=-\frac{x}{|x|^2}=-\frac1{\bar x}=-\frac{1+d\bar a}{\bar a+d}$.

The point $K$. We show that $-d$ lies on the line $AY$: \[y+d=\frac{-1-d\bar a+d\bar a+d^2}{\bar a+d}=\frac{d^2-1}{\bar a+d},\qquad \frac{y-(-d)}{a-(-d)}=\frac{d^2-1}{(\bar a+d)(a+d)}=\frac{d^2-1}{|a+d|^2}\in\mathbb R .\] Since $a$ is not real, the line $AY$ meets the real axis in a single point, so $K=-d$. Therefore $MK=|d|=MD$.

Show solution 2 – Spiral similarity and a midline

Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AD$. Since $AEDF$ is a parallelogram ($DE\parallel AF$, $DF\parallel AE$), $N$ is also the midpoint of $EF$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$. We may assume $D\ne M$ (for $D=M$ the circles $AFE$ and $ABC$ are tangent at $A$ and $X$ does not exist).

The spiral similarity. The point $X\ne A$ lies on both circles $AFE$ and $ABC$, so $\measuredangle(FX,FB)=\measuredangle(FX,FA)=\measuredangle(EX,EA)=\measuredangle(EX,EC)$ and $\measuredangle(BX,BF)=\measuredangle(BX,BA)=\measuredangle(CX,CA)=\measuredangle(CX,CE)$. So the triangles $XFB$ and $XEC$ are directly similar, and $X$ is the centre of the spiral similarity $s$ with $s(F)=B$ and $s(E)=C$. A spiral similarity maps midpoints to midpoints, so $s(N)=M$. Since $s$ maps $F\mapsto B$ and $N\mapsto M$ with centre $X$, the triangles $XFB$ and $XNM$ are directly similar (same rotation angle, $XB:XF=XM:XN$), so \[\measuredangle(MX,MN)=\measuredangle(BX,BF)=\measuredangle(BX,BA).\] Parallel lines. $A,B,X,Y$ lie on the circumcircle, so $\measuredangle(BX,BA)=\measuredangle(YX,YA)$. The lines $MX$ and $YX$ coincide (as $X,M,Y$ are collinear), hence \[\measuredangle(YX,MN)=\measuredangle(YX,YA),\] so $MN\parallel YA$, i.e. $MN\parallel AK$.

Midline. In triangle $ADK$ the line through the midpoint $N$ of $AD$ parallel to $AK$ meets $DK$ (the line $BC$) at the midpoint of $DK$. That point is $M$, because $M$ lies on $BC$ and on this line. Hence $MD=MK$.

Show solution 3 – Oblique coordinates and the reflected point

The point $D$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$: if $D=B$ or $D=C$, one of $E,F$ coincides with $A$ and the circle $AEF$ is not defined. Also $D\ne M$: for $D=M$ the circles $AEF$ and $ABC$ are tangent at $A$ and $X$ does not exist.

Coordinates. Let $\mathbf p=\overrightarrow{AB}$, $\mathbf q=\overrightarrow{AC}$, and give the point $A+\xi\mathbf p+\eta\mathbf q$ the coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$. Then $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(0,1)$, $M=(\frac12,\frac12)$, and $D=(1-t,t)$ with $0<t<1$, $t\ne\frac12$. Since $DE\parallel AB$ and $DF\parallel AC$, we get $E=(0,t)$ and $F=(1-t,0)$. Put $b=AB^2$, $c=AC^2$, $g=\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$. The squared distance from $A$ to $(\xi,\eta)$ is \[\varphi(\xi,\eta)=b\xi^2+2g\xi\eta+c\eta^2,\] which is positive for $(\xi,\eta)\ne(0,0)$.

Powers of a point. If a circle with centre $O$ passes through $A$, put $\mathbf w=\overrightarrow{AO}$; the power of a point $P$ is $|\overrightarrow{AP}-\mathbf w|^2-|\mathbf w|^2=AP^2-2\,\mathbf w\cdot\overrightarrow{AP}$. For the circle $ABC$, the points $B$ and $C$ give $2\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf p=b$ and $2\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf q=c$; for the circle $AEF$, the points $F=A+(1-t)\mathbf p$ and $E=A+t\mathbf q$ give $2\mathbf w'\cdot\mathbf p=(1-t)b$ and $2\mathbf w'\cdot\mathbf q=tc$. So the two powers are \[f_1=\varphi(\xi,\eta)-b\xi-c\eta,\qquad f_2=\varphi(\xi,\eta)-(1-t)b\xi-tc\eta .\] At $X$ both vanish, so $f_1-f_2=-tb\xi-(1-t)c\eta=0$ there: $X$ lies on the line through $A$ with direction $U=\bigl(c(1-t),\,-bt\bigr)$.

Second intersections. For a direction $w=(w_1,w_2)\ne(0,0)$, the point $\lambda w$ is on the circle $ABC$ exactly when $\lambda^2\varphi(w)-\lambda(bw_1+cw_2)=0$. So the line through $A$ with direction $w$ meets the circle again at \[R(w)=\frac{bw_1+cw_2}{\varphi(w)}\,w .\] For $U$ we have $L_U:=bU_1+cU_2=bc(1-2t)\ne0$, so $X=R(U)=\frac{L_U}{D_U}U$ with $D_U=\varphi(U)$.

The reflected point. Let $K'=(t,1-t)$; it lies on $BC$ (its coordinates add up to $1$), and $D+K'=(1,1)$ in coordinates, so $K'$ is the reflection of $D$ in $M$. Let $V=(t,1-t)$, $L_V=bt+c(1-t)>0$, $D_V=\varphi(V)$, and $Z=R(V)=\frac{L_V}{D_V}V$: the second intersection of the line $AK'$ with the circle $ABC$; $Z\ne A$ as $L_V\ne0$. We show that $Z$ lies on the line $XM$.

Write $[P,Q]=P_1Q_2-P_2Q_1$; then $P,M,Q$ are collinear exactly when $[P-M,Q-M]=0$, and \[[P-M,Q-M]=[P,Q]-[P,M]+[Q,M],\qquad [P,M]=\tfrac12(P_1-P_2).\] Let $\Delta=[U,V]=c(1-t)^2+bt^2>0$. Directly, \[D_U=bc^2(1-t)^2-2gbc\,t(1-t)+b^2c\,t^2,\qquad D_V=bt^2+2g\,t(1-t)+c(1-t)^2,\] so the terms with $g$ cancel in \[D_U+bc\,D_V=2bc\bigl(c(1-t)^2+bt^2\bigr)=2bc\,\Delta. \tag{1}\] Moreover $U_1-U_2=c(1-t)+bt=L_V$ and $V_1-V_2=2t-1$. Hence \[[X-M,Z-M]=\frac{L_UL_V\Delta}{D_UD_V}-\frac{L_UL_V}{2D_U}+\frac{L_V(2t-1)}{2D_V},\] and, using $L_U=bc(1-2t)$ and (1), \[2D_UD_V\,[X-M,Z-M]=(1-2t)L_V\bigl(2bc\,\Delta-bc\,D_V-D_U\bigr)=0 .\] So $X$, $M$, $Z$ are collinear.

Conclusion. $M$ is inside the circle $ABC$ (it is an interior point of the chord $BC$), so $X\ne M$ and the line $XM$ meets the circle in exactly the two points $X$ and $Y$. The point $Z$ is on the circle and on this line. Also $Z\ne X$: both differ from $A$, and they lie on the lines through $A$ with directions $U$ and $V$, which are different lines since $[U,V]=\Delta\ne0$. Hence $Z=Y$, and the line $AY$ is the line $AK'$. This line is not parallel to $BC$ (its direction $V$ satisfies $[V,(-1,1)]=1\ne0$), so it meets $BC$ only at $K'$. Therefore $K=K'$, the reflection of $D$ in $M$, and $MD=MK$.
