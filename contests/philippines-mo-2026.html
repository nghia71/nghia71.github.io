Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle such that $AC-AB=BC-AC=d>0$. Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, $D$ the point on $\Omega$ which bisects the minor arc $AC$, and $E$ the foot of the altitude from $B$ to $AC$. The segment $BD$ meets $AC$ at a point $F$. Show that $EF=3d/2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Projections onto $AC$ and the angle bisector theorem)

Write $b=AC$, so $AB=b-d$ and $BC=b+d$ (the triangle inequality forces $b>2d$).

$BD$ is the bisector of $\angle B$. $AC$ is the middle side, so $\angle B$ is not the largest angle and is acute. The arc $AC$ not containing $B$ is twice $\angle B<90^\circ$, so it is the minor arc, and $D$ is its midpoint. Equal arcs $AD$ and $DC$ give $\angle ABD=\angle DBC$, so $F$ is the foot of the bisector from $B$.

Coordinates along $AC$. Measure signed distances along line $AC$ from $A$, positive towards $C$. The foot $E$ of the altitude from $B$ is at \[ x_E=AB\cos A=\frac{AB^2+AC^2-BC^2}{2\,AC}=\frac{(b-d)^2+b^2-(b+d)^2}{2b}=\frac{b^2-4bd}{2b}=\frac{b-4d}{2} \] (by the law of cosines; this is correct also when $E$ falls outside the segment $AC$). By the angle bisector theorem $AF:FC=AB:BC=(b-d):(b+d)$, so \[ x_F=AF=\frac{b-d}{(b-d)+(b+d)}\cdot b=\frac{b-d}{2}. \] Therefore \[ EF=x_F-x_E=\frac{b-d}{2}-\frac{b-4d}{2}=\frac{3d}{2}. \]

Solution 2 (Ptolemy: the incentre is the midpoint of $BD$)

Write $b=AC$, so $AB=b-d$, $BC=b+d$, the semiperimeter is $s=\frac{3b}{2}$, and let $I$ be the incentre, $r$ the inradius, $T$ the point where the incircle touches $AC$, and $M$ the midpoint of $AC$. As $AC$ is the middle side, $\angle B<90^\circ$, so the minor arc $AC$ is the one not containing $B$; its midpoint $D$ satisfies $DA=DC$ and $\angle ABD=\angle DBC$, so $I$ lies on segment $BD$.

$DI=DA$. $\angle DAC=\angle DBC=\frac B2$ (same arc $DC$), so $\angle DAI=\frac B2+\frac A2$; and $\angle AID$, an exterior angle of triangle $ABI$, equals $\frac A2+\frac B2$. So triangle $DAI$ is isosceles with $DI=DA$.

$I$ is the midpoint of $BD$. Ptolemy's theorem for the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ gives \[ AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA=DA\,(AB+BC)=DA\cdot 2b, \] so $BD=2DA=2DI$.

Heights above $AC$. Let $h$ be the height of $B$ above $AC$. With $\Delta$ the area, $h=\frac{2\Delta}{b}$ and $r=\frac{\Delta}{s}=\frac{2\Delta}{3b}=\frac h3$. Since $I$ is the midpoint of $BD$, the signed height of $D$ is $2r-h=-\frac h3$. The point $F$ of segment $BD$ at height $0$ therefore satisfies $BF:FD=h:\frac h3=3:1$, so $F$ is the midpoint of $ID$.

Projecting onto $AC$. Orthogonal projection onto line $AC$ keeps midpoints. It sends $B\mapsto E$, $I\mapsto T$, $D\mapsto M$ (as $DA=DC$), and fixes $F$. Using signed coordinates from $A$ towards $C$: $x_M=\frac b2$ and $x_T=s-BC=\frac{3b}{2}-(b+d)=\frac b2-d$ (tangent length from $A$). Since $T$ is the midpoint of $EM$ and $F$ is the midpoint of $TM$, \[ x_E=2x_T-x_M=\frac b2-2d,\qquad x_F=\frac{x_T+x_M}{2}=\frac b2-\frac d2, \] and $EF=x_F-x_E=\frac{3d}{2}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Alice and Bob are playing a game on a grid of squares with $m$ rows and $n$ columns, which initially is empty. At the start of the game, each player owns $mn$ coins. When it is a player's turn to move, they choose a row or column with at least one empty cell, and place one of their coins in each of the empty cells in that row or column. Starting with Alice, the players take turns making moves until there are no more empty cells in the grid. Both players want to maximize the number of coins they have left at the end of the game. If they both play optimally, for how many ordered pairs $(m,n)$ of positive integers will Alice be left with $2025$ coins?

Show the answer

$60$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A recursion for the difference of payments)

Reformulation. A cell is filled exactly when its row or its column has already been chosen. So a position is described by the numbers $r$ and $c$ of rows and columns not chosen yet; the empty cells are the $rc$ cells where these meet. While $r,c\ge1$, a row with an empty cell is an unchosen row, and choosing it costs $c$ coins and leads to $(r-1,c)$; choosing an unchosen column costs $r$ and leads to $(r,c-1)$. The game ends when $r=0$ or $c=0$. In total the players place $mn$ coins, so maximizing one's coins left is the same as minimizing one's own payment, and the game is zero-sum.

Let $D(r,c)$ be (payment of the player to move) minus (payment of the other player) from position $(r,c)$ on, under optimal play. Then $D(r,c)=0$ if $r=0$ or $c=0$, and otherwise \[ D(r,c)=\min\big(c-D(r-1,c),\ r-D(r,c-1)\big). \]

Claim. $D(r,c)=\min(r,c)$ if $r\equiv c\pmod 2$, and $D(r,c)=0$ otherwise.

We use induction on $r+c$. If $r=0$ or $c=0$ both sides are $0$. Let $r,c\ge1$; by the symmetry between rows and columns we may assume $r\le c$.

  • $r+c$ odd: then $r<c$. The positions $(r-1,c)$ and $(r,c-1)$ have even sums, so $D(r-1,c)=r-1$ and $D(r,c-1)=r$ (as $r\le c-1$). The two options give $c-r+1>0$ and $r-r=0$, so $D(r,c)=0$.

  • $r+c$ even: $(r-1,c)$ and $(r,c-1)$ have odd sums, so both $D$-values are $0$, and $D(r,c)=\min(c,r)=r$.

Alice's coins. If Alice pays $A$ and Bob pays $B$, then $A+B=mn$ and $A-B=D(m,n)$. Alice is left with $mn-A=B=\frac{mn-D(m,n)}{2}$. So we need $mn-D(m,n)=4050=2\cdot3^4\cdot5^2$. Note that $4050$ has $2\cdot5\cdot3=30$ divisors and exactly one factor $2$.

  • $m\not\equiv n\pmod 2$: we need $mn=4050$. In every factorization of $4050$ exactly one factor is even, so all $30$ ordered factorizations qualify: $30$ pairs.

  • $m\equiv n\pmod 2$, say $m\le n$: we need $m(n-1)=4050$. Again exactly one of $m$, $n-1$ is even, so $m\equiv n$ automatically. With $k=n-1=4050/m$ we need $m\le k+1$. As $4050$ is not a square, $m\ne k$; and $m=k+1$ would mean $m(m-1)=4050$, impossible ($63\cdot64=4032$, $64\cdot65=4160$). So $m<k$: this happens for $15$ of the $30$ divisors $m$. Each gives a pair with $m<n$, and swapping gives $15$ more; $m=n$ is impossible ($m^2-m\ne4050$). So $30$ pairs.

In total there are $\boxed{60}$ ordered pairs.

Solution 2 (Explicit strategies: greedy and copycat)

As in any analysis of this game, a position is given by the numbers $r,c$ of unchosen rows and columns: choosing a row costs $c$ and leads to $(r-1,c)$, choosing a column costs $r$ and leads to $(r,c-1)$, and the game ends when $r=0$ or $c=0$. Each player minimizes his own payment; the total payment is fixed, so this is a zero-sum game. Let $D(r,c)$ be the payment of the player to move minus that of the other, under optimal play. We find $D$ by exhibiting strategies.

Copycat. The player who does not move first from a position can ensure that the first player pays at least as much as he does: whenever the first player chooses a row (column) and the game is not over, he also chooses a row (column). Choosing a row costs the number of unchosen columns, which the opponent's row move did not change, so he pays exactly what the opponent just paid. The opponent may have one extra last move. Hence $D(r,c)\ge 0$ for every position.

Greedy. From a position with $r+c$ odd (so $r\ne c$), the player to move can ensure that he pays at most as much as the opponent: he always pays $\min(r,c)$, choosing a line of the longer direction. If, say, $r<c$, he pays $r$ and moves to $(r,c-1)$ with $c-1\ge r\ge1$, so the game is not over; the opponent then pays $c-1\ge r$ or $r$, and the new position again has odd sum with our player to move. So his moves pair off with opponent moves that cost at least as much, and $D(r,c)\le 0$.

Values. If $r+c$ is odd, the two strategies give $D(r,c)=0$. If $r+c$ is even, say $r\le c$:

  • the player to move pays $r$ (choosing a column); if the game goes on, he faces an odd-sum position as the second player and uses copycat, so in the rest the opponent pays at least as much as he does. Hence $D(r,c)\le r$.

  • whatever the first move, it costs $c$ or $r$, both at least $r$; if the game goes on, the opponent faces an odd-sum position as the player to move and uses greedy, paying at most as much as the first player in the rest. Hence $D(r,c)\ge r$.

So $D(r,c)=\min(r,c)$ if $r\equiv c\pmod 2$ and $D(r,c)=0$ otherwise.

Counting. Alice pays $A$, Bob pays $B$, with $A+B=mn$ and $A-B=D(m,n)$; Alice keeps $mn-A=\frac{mn-D(m,n)}2$ coins. So $mn-D(m,n)=4050=2\cdot3^4\cdot5^2$, a number with $30$ divisors and a single factor $2$. If $m,n$ have different parities, $mn=4050$; every one of the $30$ ordered factorizations has exactly one even factor, giving $30$ pairs. If they have the same parity and $m\le n$, then $m(n-1)=4050$ (parities then match automatically), and $m\le n$ means $m\le 4050/m+1$, i.e. $m^2-m\le4050$, i.e. $m\le 64$; as $m(m-1)\ne 4050$ and $4050$ is not a square, this holds exactly for the $15$ divisors $m<\sqrt{4050}$. These give $15$ pairs with $m<n$ and, swapped, $15$ with $m>n$. Altogether $\boxed{60}$ ordered pairs.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $d\ge 2$ be an integer. An integer $n=\overline{a_1a_2\cdots a_d}$ with $a_1\ne 0$ is left-rotatable if $a_2\ne 0$. In this case, define $L(n)=\overline{a_2a_3\cdots a_da_1}$, which also has $d$ digits like $n$. Similarly, an integer $n=\overline{a_1a_2\cdots a_d}$ with $a_1\ne 0$ is right-rotatable if $a_d\ne 0$. In this case, define $R(n)=\overline{a_da_1a_2\cdots a_{d-1}}$, which also has $d$ digits like $n$.

  • (a) Show that there exists a $d$ for which there are at least $24$ left-rotatable $d$-digit integers $n$ such that $L(n)\mid n$.

  • (b) Does there exist a $d$ for which there are at least $24$ right-rotatable $d$-digit integers $n$ such that $R(n)\mid n$?

Show the answer

(a) proof ($d=18$ works); (b) $\boxed{\text{No}}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A closed formula for the rotations)

Formulas. If $n=\overline{a_1a_2\cdots a_d}$, then $10n=a_1\cdot10^d+\overline{a_2\cdots a_d0}$, so \[ L(n)=10n-a_1\big(10^d-1\big),\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad 10\,R(n)=n+a_d\big(10^d-1\big). \] If $L(n)\mid n$ (resp. $R(n)\mid n$) with $L(n)$ (resp. $R(n)$) a $d$-digit number, then $n=k\,L(n)$ (resp. $n=k\,R(n)$) with an integer $1\le k\le 9$, because $n<10^d\le 10\,L(n)$.

(a) From $n=kL(n)$ we get $(10k-1)\,n=k\,a_1(10^d-1)$. Conversely we show: if $1\le k\le a\le 9$ and $N=\dfrac{ka(10^d-1)}{10k-1}$ is an integer, then $N$ is a left-rotatable $d$-digit number with first digit $a$ and $L(N)=N/k$.

  • $a\cdot10^{d-1}\le N<(a+1)10^{d-1}$: multiplying by $10k-1$, the left inequality becomes $a\cdot10^{d-1}\ge ka$, true as $10^{d-1}\ge 9\ge k$; the right one becomes $-ka<(10k-1-a)10^{d-1}$, true as $10k-1-a\ge 0$. So $N$ has $d$ digits and first digit $a$.

  • By the formula, $L(N)=10N-a(10^d-1)=\dfrac{\big(10ka-a(10k-1)\big)(10^d-1)}{10k-1}=\dfrac{a(10^d-1)}{10k-1}=\dfrac Nk$.

  • $L(N)=N/k\ge a\cdot10^{d-1}/k\ge 10^{d-1}$, so $L(N)$ has $d$ digits, i.e. the second digit of $N$ is not $0$.

Different pairs $(k,a)$ give different $N$ (as $a$ is the first digit and $k=N/L(N)$). Now take $d=18$. Note $10^{18}-1=(10^6-1)(10^{12}+10^6+1)$ and $10^6-1=999999=3^3\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot37$.

  • $k=1$: $9\mid 10^{18}-1$; $a=1,\dots,9$ give $9$ numbers (the repdigits).

  • $k=2$: $19\mid 10^{18}-1$ by Fermat's little theorem; $a=2,\dots,9$ give $8$ numbers, e.g. $210526315789473684=2\cdot105263157894736842$.

  • $k=4$: $39=3\cdot13\mid 10^6-1\mid 10^{18}-1$; $a=4,\dots,9$ give $6$ numbers, e.g. $410256410256410256$.

  • $k=5$: $49\mid 5\cdot7\cdot(10^{18}-1)$ because $7\mid 10^{18}-1$; $a=7$ gives $1$ number, $714285714285714285=5\cdot 142857142857142857$.

That is $9+8+6+1=24$ left-rotatable $18$-digit numbers $n$ with $L(n)\mid n$.

(b) From $n=kR(n)$ and $10R(n)=n+b(10^d-1)$, where $b=a_d\ge1$ is the last digit: $(10-k)\,n=kb\,(10^d-1)$. If $kb\ge 11-k$, then \[ (10-k)n=kb(10^d-1)\ge(11-k)(10^d-1)=(10-k)10^d+\big(10^d-(11-k)\big)>(10-k)10^d, \] so $n>10^d$, impossible. Hence $k(b+1)\le 10$. The pair $(k,b)$ determines $n=\frac{kb(10^d-1)}{10-k}$, and the pairs with $k,b\ge1$ and $k(b+1)\le10$ are: $k=1$, $b\le 9$ ($9$ pairs); $k=2$, $b\le4$ ($4$); $k=3$, $b\le 2$ ($2$); $k=4$, $b=1$; $k=5$, $b=1$. That is at most $17$ numbers for every $d$. So the answer to (b) is $\boxed{\text{No}}$.

Solution 2 (Part (b) by comparing leading digits)

This solution treats part (b) only.

Let $n=\overline{a_1a_2\cdots a_d}$ be right-rotatable with $R(n)\mid n$, and let $b=a_d\ge1$ be its last digit. Then $R(n)=\overline{b\,a_1\cdots a_{d-1}}$ has $d$ digits and leading digit $b$, so $R(n)\ge b\cdot10^{d-1}$. Write $n=k\,R(n)$ with a positive integer $k$. Then \[ k\,b\cdot10^{d-1}\le k\,R(n)=n<10^d , \] so $kb<10$, i.e. $kb\le 9$.

The pair $(k,b)$ determines $n$. Since $n=10\cdot\overline{a_1\cdots a_{d-1}}+b$, we have $R(n)=b\cdot10^{d-1}+\frac{n-b}{10}$. From $n=kR(n)$ we get $10n=k\big(b\cdot 10^d+n-b\big)$, i.e. $(10-k)\,n=kb\,(10^d-1)$, and $k\le 9$, so $n$ is determined by $k$ and $b$.

Counting pairs. The pairs of positive integers with $kb\le 9$ number \[ 9\ (k=1)+4\ (k=2)+3\ (k=3)+2\ (k=4)+1+1+1+1+1\ (k=5,\dots,9)=23 . \] So for every $d$ there are at most $23<24$ right-rotatable $d$-digit integers $n$ with $R(n)\mid n$, and the answer to (b) is $\boxed{\text{No}}$.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Let $\mathbb{N}_0$ be the set of nonnegative integers. Find all nondecreasing functions $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{N}_0$ such that \[f(x)=f\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)+f\left(\frac{x+1}{n}\right)+f\left(\frac{x+2}{n}\right)+\dots+f\left(\frac{x+n-1}{n}\right)\] for all positive real numbers $x$ and positive integers $n$. (A function $f$ is nondecreasing if $f(x)\le f(y)$ for all $x$ and $y$ in the domain such that $x\le y$.)

Show the answer

$f(x)=\alpha\lfloor x\rfloor+\beta\big(\lceil x\rceil-1\big)$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Large $n$: count the arguments in $(1,2)$)

These functions work. For $t>0$, $\lfloor t\rfloor=\#\{j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}: j\le t\}$ and $\lceil t\rceil-1=\#\{j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}: j<t\}$. Hence \[ \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Big\lfloor\frac{x+i}{n}\Big\rfloor=\#\{(i,j):0\le i\le n-1,\ j\ge1,\ nj-i\le x\}=\#\{m\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}:m\le x\}=\lfloor x\rfloor, \] because $(i,j)\mapsto nj-i$ is a bijection from $\{0,\dots,n-1\}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$ onto $\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$ (division with remainder). The same computation with $<$ gives the identity for $\lceil\cdot\rceil-1$. The equation is linear, so every $f=\alpha\lfloor x\rfloor+\beta(\lceil x\rceil-1)$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0$ satisfies it; it is nondecreasing with values in $\mathbb N_0$.

Conversely, let $f$ be a solution.

Step 1: $f=0$ on $(0,1)$. Let $0<x<1$ and choose $n\ge 1+\frac{2}{1-x}$. We have $\frac{x+i}n\ge x$ exactly for the integers $i$ with $(n-1)x\le i\le n-1$; there are $n-\lceil (n-1)x\rceil>(n-1)(1-x)\ge 2$ of them. All terms of the sum are nonnegative and these terms are at least $f(x)$, so $f(x)\ge 2f(x)$, and $f(x)=0$.

Step 2: the values. Since $f$ is integer-valued and nondecreasing, it is constant, say equal to $c$, on some interval $(1,1+\delta')$. Let $x>0$ and $n>x$ so large that $\frac{x+n-1}{n}=1+\frac{x-1}{n}<1+\delta'$. Every argument $\frac{x+i}{n}$ is then below $1+\delta'$; it is less than $1$ for $i<n-x$ (so the term is $0$ by Step 1), equal to $1$ for $i=n-x$, and in $(1,1+\delta')$ for $i>n-x$.

  • If $x\notin\mathbb Z$, the indices $i>n-x$ are $n-\lfloor x\rfloor,\dots,n-1$, so $f(x)=c\lfloor x\rfloor$.

  • If $x=k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$, one index gives the argument $1$ and $k-1$ indices give arguments in $(1,1+\delta')$, so $f(k)=f(1)+(k-1)c$.

Put $\beta=c-f(1)$. Monotonicity gives $0=f(\tfrac12)\le f(1)\le f(\tfrac32)=c$, so $\beta,\alpha:=c-\beta$ are nonnegative integers, and $f(x)=(\alpha+\beta)\lfloor x\rfloor$ for $x\notin\mathbb Z$, $f(k)=\alpha k+\beta(k-1)$ for $k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$. This is exactly \[ \boxed{f(x)=\alpha\lfloor x\rfloor+\beta\big(\lceil x\rceil-1\big),\quad \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0.} \]

Solution 2 (Telescoping: $f(x+1)-f(x)$ is constant)

These functions work by the identities $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lfloor\frac{x+i}n\rfloor=\lfloor x\rfloor$ (Hermite) and $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big(\lceil\frac{x+i}n\rceil-1\big)=\lceil x\rceil-1$; the second follows from the first by replacing $x$ with $x-\varepsilon$ and letting $\varepsilon\to0^+$, since $\lfloor t-\varepsilon\rfloor\to\lceil t\rceil-1$. The equation is linear, so all $\alpha\lfloor x\rfloor+\beta(\lceil x\rceil-1)$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0$ are solutions.

Conversely, let $f$ be a solution. Being nondecreasing and $\mathbb N_0$-valued, $f$ is constant on some interval $(0,\delta)$, with value $L$; $L\ge1$ would give $f(1)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\frac{1+i}{n})\ge n$ for all $n$, so $L=0$. Likewise $f$ is constant, say $c$, on some $(1,1+\delta')$.

The difference $f(x+1)-f(x)$. Write the equation for $x$ and for $x+1$ with the same $n$ and subtract. The arguments for $x+1$ are $\frac{x+1}{n},\dots,\frac{x+n}{n}$ and those for $x$ are $\frac xn,\dots,\frac{x+n-1}n$; all but one cancel: \[ f(x+1)-f(x)=f\Big(\frac{x+n}{n}\Big)-f\Big(\frac xn\Big)=f\Big(1+\frac xn\Big)-f\Big(\frac xn\Big). \] For $n$ large, $\frac xn<\delta$ and $1+\frac xn<1+\delta'$, so the right side is $c-0$. Hence $f(x+1)=f(x)+c$ for all $x>0$.

Values on $(0,1)$. Let $0<x<1$ and $n\ge 1+\frac{2}{1-x}$. The argument $\frac{x+i}{n}$ is at least $x$ exactly for the integers $i$ with $(n-1)x\le i\le n-1$; there are $n-\lceil (n-1)x\rceil>(n-1)(1-x)\ge2$ of them. All terms are nonnegative and these are at least $f(x)$, so $f(x)\ge 2f(x)$ and $f(x)=0$.

Conclusion. For $x\notin\mathbb Z$: $f(x)=f(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)+\lfloor x\rfloor c=c\lfloor x\rfloor$. For $k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$: $f(k)=f(1)+(k-1)c$. Monotonicity gives $0\le f(1)\le c$, so $\beta=c-f(1)$ and $\alpha=f(1)$ are nonnegative integers, and $f(k)=\alpha k+\beta(k-1)$, $f(x)=(\alpha+\beta)\lfloor x\rfloor$ off the integers. So \[ \boxed{f(x)=\alpha\lfloor x\rfloor+\beta\big(\lceil x\rceil-1\big),\quad \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0.} \]

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

Prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $k$ satisfying the following property: there exist positive integers $a_1,\dots,a_k$, not all equal, such that \[\frac{1}{a_1(a_1+1)}+\frac{1}{a_2(a_2+1)}+\dots+\frac{1}{a_k(a_k+1)}=1.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Telescoping: every even $k\ge4$)

Since $\dfrac1{a(a+1)}=\dfrac1a-\dfrac1{a+1}$, for every integer $N\ge2$ \[ \sum_{a=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{a(a+1)}=1-\frac1N,\qquad\text{and}\qquad (N+1)\cdot\frac{1}{N(N+1)}=\frac1N . \] So the $k=(N-1)+(N+1)=2N$ numbers \[ 1,\ 2,\ \dots,\ N-1,\ \underbrace{N,\ N,\ \dots,\ N}_{N+1\ \text{times}} \] satisfy the equation, and they are not all equal (they contain $1$ and $N\ge2$). Hence every even $k\ge4$ has the property, and there are infinitely many such $k$.

Solution 2 (One term $\tfrac12$ and many equal terms)

For an integer $b\ge2$ take $a_1=1$ and $a_2=\dots=a_k=b$ with $k-1=\frac{b(b+1)}2$ (an integer). Then \[ \frac{1}{1\cdot2}+\frac{b(b+1)}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{b(b+1)}=\frac12+\frac12=1, \] and the numbers are not all equal. So every $k=1+\frac{b(b+1)}2$ with $b\ge2$ has the property; these are $4,7,11,16,\dots$, an increasing and hence infinite list.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

What is the smallest value of $n$ such that if you color any $n$ squares of an $11\times 11$ grid black, there is guaranteed to be at least one $1\times 5$ or $5\times 1$ tile in the grid such that its first, third, and fifth squares are black?

Show the answer

$82$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Four parity classes and line-by-line bounds)

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,11$. The first, third and fifth squares of a tile are three cells of one row or column in positions $j,j+2,j+4$. Call a black set safe if it contains no such triple; we find the largest safe set.

Splitting. Sort the cells into four classes by the parities of their row and column: odd–odd ($6\times6$ cells), odd–even ($6\times5$), even–odd ($5\times6$), even–even ($5\times5$). Inside a class, renumber the odd indices $2u-1\mapsto u$ and the even indices $2u\mapsto u$. A triple $j,j+2,j+4$ in a line has equal parities, so it lies in one class and becomes three consecutive cells of a row or column of that class's grid, and conversely. So the largest safe set has size $F(6,6)+F(6,5)+F(5,6)+F(5,5)$, where $F(p,q)$ is the largest number of cells of a $p\times q$ grid with no three consecutive chosen cells in a row or column.

Lines. A line of $6$ cells has at most $4$ chosen cells (each half of three cells has at most $2$). A line of $5$ cells has at most $4$ (split as $3+2$), and if it has $4$ then, splitting as $3+2$ and as $2+3$, cells $1,2,4,5$ are chosen and cell $3$ is not: the pattern $11011$.

Bounds. $F(6,6)\le 6\cdot4=24$ (rows). For $6\times5$ and $5\times6$, the $5$ lines of length $6$ give $F\le 5\cdot4=20$. For the $5\times5$ grid, suppose $18$ cells are chosen. If at most two rows had $4$ cells, the total would be at most $2\cdot4+3\cdot3=17$; so at least three rows are $11011$ and have the middle cell empty. Then the middle column has at most $2$ cells, and $18\le 2+4\cdot4$ forces each of the other four columns to have $4$ cells, i.e. to be $11011$ vertically. So the middle row is empty outside the middle column, and rows $1,2,4,5$ already contain cells in columns $1,2,4,5$; a cell in their middle column would complete five in a row. Thus the middle column has at most one cell and the total is at most $16+1=17$, a contradiction. So $F(5,5)\le17$, and every safe set has at most $24+20+20+17=81$ cells.

An example with $81$. In the three classes with a side of length $6$, choose the cell $(u,v)$ iff $u+v\not\equiv0\pmod3$: in every line the unchosen cells occur every third position, so no three consecutive cells are chosen; each line of length $6$ has exactly $4$ chosen cells, giving $24$, $20$ and $20$. In the even–even class choose $(u,v)$ iff $u\ne3\ne v$, or $u=v=3$: rows and columns read $11011$ or $00100$, giving $17$. The resulting $81$ black cells are shown in the figure; they contain no bad tile.

So $81$ cells may avoid the pattern, while any $82$ black cells contain it: the answer is $\boxed{82}$.

Solution 2 (Covering each class by straight trominoes)

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,11$; a bad tile means three black cells in one line at positions $j,j+2,j+4$.

Splitting. Split the cells into the four classes of row and column parity, of sizes $6\times6$, $6\times5$, $5\times6$, $5\times5$, and renumber inside each class ($2u-1\mapsto u$ and $2u\mapsto u$). The triples $j,j+2,j+4$ in a line are exactly the triples of three consecutive cells (“straight trominoes”) in a row or column of one class.

Upper bound by partitions. A straight tromino of a class contains at most $2$ black cells of a safe set. Partition each class into straight trominoes as far as possible:

  • $6\times6$: each of the $6$ rows splits into two trominoes: $12$ trominoes, at most $24$ black cells;

  • $6\times5$ and $5\times6$: each of the $5$ lines of length $6$ splits into two trominoes: $10$ trominoes, at most $20$ each;

  • $5\times5$: the $24$ cells other than the centre split into $8$ trominoes arranged as a pinwheel (second figure): two horizontal trominoes in rows $1,2$, columns $1$–$3$; two vertical ones in columns $4,5$, rows $1$–$3$; two horizontal ones in rows $4,5$, columns $3$–$5$; two vertical ones in columns $1,2$, rows $3$–$5$. So at most $8\cdot2+1=17$ black cells.

In total at most $24+20+20+17=81$ black cells avoid a bad tile.

Sharpness. In the classes with a side $6$ colour $(u,v)$ black iff $u+v\not\equiv0\pmod3$ ($24+20+20$ cells; in every line the white cells are every third cell), and in the $5\times5$ class colour $(u,v)$ black iff $u\ne3\ne v$ or $u=v=3$ ($17$ cells; lines read $11011$ or $00100$). These $81$ cells contain no bad tile.

Hence the smallest $n$ is $\boxed{82}$.

Problem 7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

Let $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ be a square inscribed in convex quadrilateral $B_1B_2B_3B_4$, such that for each $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$, point $A_i$ lies on $B_iB_{i+1}$, where we take $B_5=B_1$. Let $m$ be an integer. Let $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ be a quadrilateral such that for each $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$, point $C_i$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $B_i$ to line $A_{i+m}A_{i+m+1}$, where we take indices modulo $4$. Find all integers $m$ such that the following holds: if $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ is a square, then $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ is also a square.

Show the answer

exactly the even integers $m$

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates adapted to the square)

Indices are taken modulo $4$, and $C_i$ depends only on $m$ modulo $4$.

Set-up. Reflecting the figure if necessary (this changes nothing in the problem), let $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ be counterclockwise; scale it to side $2$ and put $A_1=(1,-1)$, $A_2=(1,1)$, $A_3=(-1,1)$, $A_4=(-1,-1)$. Let $\rho(x,y)=(-y,x)$ be the rotation by $90^\circ$ about the centre; then $\rho(A_i)=A_{i+1}$, and $\rho$ maps the line $s_i=A_iA_{i+1}$ to $s_{i+1}$. We read “inscribed” as: each $A_i$ is an interior point of the side $B_iB_{i+1}$. The chord $A_{i-1}A_i$ of the convex quadrilateral cuts off the triangle $A_{i-1}B_iA_i$, so $B_i$ lies beyond the line $s_{i-1}$, on the side away from the square. Hence we can write \[ B_i=\rho^{\,i-2}(1+p_i,\ q_i)\quad\text{with } p_i>0: \] $B_1=(q_1,-1-p_1)$, $B_2=(1+p_2,q_2)$, $B_3=(-q_3,1+p_3)$, $B_4=(-1-p_4,-q_4)$; here $p_i$ is the distance from $B_i$ to $s_{i-1}$.

The side through $A_i$. $A_2$ lies strictly between $B_2$ and $B_3$, so $B_2-A_2=(p_2,q_2-1)$ and $B_3-A_2=(-1-q_3,p_3)$ point in opposite directions. Comparing signs of coordinates gives $q_3>-1$ and $q_2<1$, and parallelism gives $p_2p_3=(1-q_2)(1+q_3)$. Applying $\rho^{\,i-2}$ to the same computation, for every $i$: \[ p_ip_{i+1}=(1-q_i)(1+q_{i+1}),\qquad -1<q_i<1. \tag{$\ast$} \] If all $p_i$ are equal and all $q_i$ are equal, then $B_{i+1}=\rho(B_i)$ for all $i$, so $\rho$ maps each side of $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ to the next one; consecutive sides are equal and perpendicular, and $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ is a square.

The feet. Apply $\rho^{-(i-2)}$: it moves $B_i$ to $(1+p_i,q_i)$ and the line $s_{i+m}$ to $s_{m+2}$, which is $y=1$, $x=-1$, $y=-1$, $x=1$ for $m\equiv0,1,2,3$. Hence \[ C_i=\rho^{\,i-2}(1+p_i,\ 1),\quad \rho^{\,i-2}(-1,\ q_i),\quad \rho^{\,i-2}(1+p_i,\ -1),\quad \rho^{\,i-2}(1,\ q_i) \] for $m\equiv 0,1,2,3\pmod 4$ respectively.

Odd $m$: the property fails. Take $p_1=p_3=\tfrac12$, $p_2=p_4=2$, all $q_i=0$; $(\ast)$ holds. Then $B_1=(0,-\tfrac32)$, $B_2=(3,0)$, $B_3=(0,\tfrac32)$, $B_4=(-3,0)$: a convex rhombus, not a square, and $A_1=(1,-1)$ lies inside the side $B_1B_2$ (on the line $y=\frac x2-\frac32$), and similarly for the other sides. For $m\equiv1$ we get $C_1,C_2,C_3,C_4=(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1),(1,0)$, and for $m\equiv3$ we get $(0,-1),(1,0),(0,1),(-1,0)$: in both cases $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ is a square while $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ is not.

Even $m$: the property holds. Let $\varepsilon=1$ for $m\equiv0$ and $\varepsilon=-1$ for $m\equiv2$, so $C_i=\rho^{\,i-2}(1+p_i,\varepsilon)$. If $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ is a square, then necessarily $C_3-C_2=\sigma(C_2-C_1)$ and $C_4-C_3=\sigma(C_3-C_2)$, where $\sigma$ is $\rho$ or $\rho^{-1}$ depending on the orientation.

  • $\varepsilon=1$: $C_1=(1,-1-p_1)$, $C_2=(1+p_2,1)$, $C_3=(-1,1+p_3)$, $C_4=(-1-p_4,-1)$, so $C_2-C_1=(p_2,2+p_1)$, $C_3-C_2=(-2-p_2,p_3)$, $C_4-C_3=(-p_4,-2-p_3)$. With $\sigma=\rho^{-1}$, $\rho^{-1}(C_2-C_1)=(2+p_1,-p_2)$ would need $2+p_1=-2-p_2$, impossible. With $\sigma=\rho$: $(-2-p_1,p_2)=(-2-p_2,p_3)$ and $\rho(C_3-C_2)=(-p_3,-2-p_2)=(-p_4,-2-p_3)$, so $p_1=p_2=p_3=p_4$.

  • $\varepsilon=-1$: $C_1=(-1,-1-p_1)$, $C_2=(1+p_2,-1)$, $C_3=(1,1+p_3)$, $C_4=(-1-p_4,1)$, so $C_2-C_1=(2+p_2,p_1)$, $C_3-C_2=(-p_2,2+p_3)$, $C_4-C_3=(-2-p_4,-p_3)$. With $\sigma=\rho^{-1}$: $(p_1,-2-p_2)=(-p_2,2+p_3)$ is impossible. With $\sigma=\rho$: $(-p_1,2+p_2)=(-p_2,2+p_3)$ and $(-2-p_3,-p_2)=(-2-p_4,-p_3)$, so again all $p_i$ are equal.

So $p_i=p$ for all $i$, and $(\ast)$ becomes $(1-q_i)(1+q_{i+1})=p^2$, i.e. $q_{i+1}=T(q_i)$ with $T(t)=\frac{p^2}{1-t}-1$, a strictly increasing function on $(-1,1)$. If $q_1<q_2$, applying $T$ repeatedly gives $q_1<q_2<q_3<q_4<q_5=q_1$, a contradiction; similarly $q_1>q_2$ is impossible. So $q_2=q_1$, and then $q_3=T(q_2)=T(q_1)=q_2$, and so on: all $q_i$ are equal. By the set-up, $B_1B_2B_3B_4$ is a square.

Therefore the required integers are exactly $\boxed{\text{the even integers } m}$.

Remark (a synthetic view of the case $m\equiv0$). Since $s_i\perp s_{i-1}$ at $A_i$, the foot $C_i$ lies on line $s_i$ beyond $A_i$, at distance $p_i$ (the distance from $B_i$ to $s_{i-1}$). So $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ is the “pinwheel” quadrilateral obtained by extending the sides of the square by $p_1,\dots,p_4$, and it is cut into the square and four right triangles with legs $2+p_{i-1}$ and $p_i$. If $C_1C_2C_3C_4$ is a square these four triangles are congruent (equal hypotenuses, equal angles), which again forces all $p_i$ equal; the rest of the argument is the same.

Problem 8

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Find all triples of positive integers $(a,b,n)$ where $n\ge 2$, and both $a^n+b$ and $b^n+a$ are powers of $2$.

Show the answer

$(a,b,n)=(1,1,n)$ for every $n\ge2$, and $(3,5,3)$, $(5,3,3)$

Show the solution

Solution (Divisibility between the two powers of $2$)

Let $a^n+b=2^x$ and $b^n+a=2^y$. The problem is symmetric in $a$ and $b$.

Step 1: $a$ and $b$ are odd. Both sums are at least $2$, hence even, so $a\equiv a^n\equiv b\pmod2$. Suppose both are even, with $v_2(a)=\alpha\le v_2(b)=\beta$ (by symmetry). Then $v_2(b^n)=n\beta\ge2\alpha>\alpha$, so $v_2(b^n+a)=\alpha$; as $b^n+a$ is a power of $2$, this means $b^n+a=2^\alpha\le a$, which is false.

Step 2: $a=b$. Then $a(a^{n-1}+1)$ is a power of $2$ with $a$ odd, so $a=1$; and $(1,1,n)$ works for every $n\ge2$ ($1+1=2$).

Step 3: $a<b$. Then $b^n-a^n=(b-a)(b^{n-1}+\dots+a^{n-1})\ge(b-a)b>b-a$, so $b^n+a>a^n+b$, and the power of two $a^n+b$ divides $b^n+a$. Modulo $a^n+b$ we have $b\equiv-a^n$, hence \[ 0\equiv b^n+a\equiv(-1)^na^{n^2}+a=a\big((-1)^na^{n^2-1}+1\big), \] and since $a$ is odd, $2^x\mid (-1)^na^{n^2-1}+1$.

$n$ even. Then $2^x\mid a^{N}+1$ with $N=n^2-1$ odd. Now $a^N+1=(a+1)(a^{N-1}-a^{N-2}+\dots+1)$ and the second factor is a sum of $N$ odd numbers, so it is odd. Hence $2^x\le 2^{v_2(a+1)}\le a+1<a^n+b=2^x$, a contradiction.

$n$ odd, $a=1$. Then $b\ge3$ and $b^n+1=(b+1)(b^{n-1}-b^{n-2}+\dots+1)$, where the second factor is odd and greater than $1$ (as $b^n+1>b+1$). So $b^n+1$ is not a power of $2$: no solution.

$n$ odd, $a\ge3$. Now $2^x\mid a^N-1$ with $N=n^2-1$ even. By the lifting-the-exponent lemma for $p=2$, $v_2(a^N-1)=v_2(a-1)+v_2(a+1)+v_2(N)-1$, so \[ a^n<a^n+b=2^x\le 2^{v_2(a^2-1)}\cdot 2^{v_2(N)-1}\le (a^2-1)\cdot\frac{n^2-1}{2}<a^2\cdot\frac{n^2-1}2, \] i.e. $a^{n-2}<\frac{n^2-1}2$. For $n\ge5$ this is false, since $3^{n-2}\ge\frac{n^2-1}2$ at $n=5$ ($27\ge12$), and when $n$ increases by $2$ the left side is multiplied by $9$ while the right side is multiplied by $\frac{n+3}{n-1}\le2$. So $n=3$ and $a^1<4$, i.e. $a=3$. Then $2^x\mid 3^8-1=6560=2^5\cdot205$, so $2^x\le32$, while $2^x=27+b\ge 27+5=32$ ($b>3$ is odd). Hence $b=5$, and indeed $3^3+5=32$ and $5^3+3=128$.

Adding the symmetric triple, all solutions are \[ \boxed{(a,b,n)=(1,1,n)\ (n\ge2),\quad (3,5,3),\quad (5,3,3)} . \]
