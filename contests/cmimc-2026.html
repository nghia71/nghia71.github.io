Algebra and Number Theory Round

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 11: Divisors and Arithmetic Functions of a Number · Counting Divisors

Let $k$ be the sum of the first $2^{89}-1$ positive integers. It is a fact that $k$ has $178$ factors. If $n$ is the sum of the first $2^{89}-1$ positive cubes, how many factors does $n$ have?

Show the answer

$531$

Show the solution

Solution (Read off the prime factorization)

Put $N=2^{89}-1$. Then \[k=1+2+\dots+N=\frac{N(N+1)}{2}=2^{88}N .\] Since $N$ is odd, the divisors of $k$ are the products of a divisor of $2^{88}$ and a divisor of $N$, so $d(k)=89\cdot d(N)$, where $d$ counts divisors. From $d(k)=178$ we get $d(N)=2$, so $N$ is a prime $q$.

The sum of the first $N$ cubes is the square of the sum of the first $N$ integers: \[n=1^3+2^3+\dots+N^3=\left(\frac{N(N+1)}{2}\right)^2=k^2=2^{176}q^2 .\] Hence $d(n)=(176+1)(2+1)=\boxed{531}$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Telescoping Sums and Products

Consider the arithmetic sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{43}$ with common difference $d>0$ and $a_{22}=1$. Given that \[\frac{1}{a_1\cdot a_2\cdot a_3}+\frac{1}{a_2\cdot a_3\cdot a_4}+\dots+\frac{1}{a_{41}\cdot a_{42}\cdot a_{43}}=41,\] compute $d$.

Show the answer

$\frac{29}{420}$

Show the solution

Solution (Telescoping)

Since $a_{i+2}-a_i=2d$, \[\frac{1}{a_ia_{i+1}}-\frac{1}{a_{i+1}a_{i+2}}=\frac{a_{i+2}-a_i}{a_ia_{i+1}a_{i+2}}=\frac{2d}{a_ia_{i+1}a_{i+2}} .\] Summing over $i=1,\dots,41$, the right-hand sides telescope: \[\sum_{i=1}^{41}\frac{1}{a_ia_{i+1}a_{i+2}}=\frac{1}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{a_1a_2}-\frac{1}{a_{42}a_{43}}\right).\] Because $a_{22}=1$, we have $a_1=1-21d$, $a_2=1-20d$, $a_{42}=1+20d$, $a_{43}=1+21d$. Then \begin{align*} \frac{1}{(1-21d)(1-20d)}-\frac{1}{(1+20d)(1+21d)}&=\frac{(1+20d)(1+21d)-(1-20d)(1-21d)}{(1-400d^2)(1-441d^2)}\\ &=\frac{82d}{(1-400d^2)(1-441d^2)} . \end{align*} So the sum equals $\dfrac{41}{(1-400d^2)(1-441d^2)}$, and the condition becomes $(1-400d^2)(1-441d^2)=1$, that is, \[d^2\left(176400\,d^2-841\right)=0 .\] As $d>0$, $d^2=\frac{841}{176400}$ and $d=\frac{29}{420}$. With this $d$ no term $a_i=1+(i-22)\frac{29}{420}$ is zero (that would need $420\mid 29(22-i)$ with $|22-i|\le 21$), so every fraction is defined. The answer is $\boxed{\dfrac{29}{420}}$.

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

How many multiples of $3$ are in the $2026$th row of Pascal's Triangle, i.e. the row with $2027$ entries? (We number the rows starting from $0$.)

Show the answer

$1991$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Lucas's theorem)

In base $3$, \[2026=2\cdot 729+2\cdot 243+1\cdot 81+0\cdot 27+0\cdot 9+0\cdot 3+1=(2210001)_3 .\] By Lucas's theorem, if $j=(j_6j_5\dots j_0)_3$, then \[\binom{2026}{j}\equiv\binom{2}{j_6}\binom{2}{j_5}\binom{1}{j_4}\binom{0}{j_3}\binom{0}{j_2}\binom{0}{j_1}\binom{1}{j_0}\pmod 3 ,\] and this is nonzero modulo $3$ exactly when every digit of $j$ is at most the corresponding digit of $2026$ (each factor is then $1$ or $2$). The number of such $j$ is $3\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot2=36$. So $36$ entries of the row are not multiples of $3$, and $2027-36=\boxed{1991}$ are.

Solution 2 (Frobenius in polynomials mod 3)

Work with polynomials whose coefficients are taken modulo $3$. Since $3\mid\binom{3}{1},\binom{3}{2}$, we have $(1+x)^3\equiv 1+x^3$, and by repeating, $(1+x)^{3^t}\equiv 1+x^{3^t}$. As $2026=2\cdot 3^6+2\cdot 3^5+3^4+1$, \[(1+x)^{2026}\equiv(1+x^{729})^2(1+x^{243})^2(1+x^{81})(1+x)\pmod 3 .\] Expanding the right side, we choose a term $x^{729c_6}$ with $c_6\in\{0,1,2\}$ (coefficient $1,2,1$), a term $x^{243c_5}$ with $c_5\in\{0,1,2\}$, a term $x^{81c_4}$ with $c_4\in\{0,1\}$ and a term $x^{c_0}$ with $c_0\in\{0,1\}$. Different choices give different exponents $729c_6+243c_5+81c_4+c_0$ (these are base-$3$ expansions), so no terms combine, and each coefficient is a product of $1$s and $2$s, nonzero mod $3$. Hence $(1+x)^{2026}$ has exactly $3\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 2=36$ coefficients not divisible by $3$, and the remaining $2027-36=\boxed{1991}$ binomial coefficients are multiples of $3$.

Problem 7

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Quadratic Residues

Consider the solutions to \[3x^2+4xy+y^2+2x+4y\equiv 1\pmod{83},\] where $x,y$ are nonnegative integers less than $83$. For how many values of $x$ does there exist a solution for $y$?

Show the answer

$42$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Completing the square and a character sum)

Work modulo the prime $p=83$. As a quadratic in $y$ the congruence reads $y^2+4(x+1)y+(3x^2+2x-1)\equiv 0$, and completing the square gives \[(y+2x+2)^2\equiv(2x+2)^2-(3x^2+2x-1)=x^2+6x+5=(x+1)(x+5).\] Since $y\mapsto y+2x+2$ is a bijection of residues, a solution $y$ exists exactly when $(x+1)(x+5)$ is a square modulo $83$ (possibly $0$).

Let $\left(\frac{\cdot}{p}\right)$ be the Legendre symbol. We claim $\sum_{x}\left(\frac{(x+1)(x+5)}{p}\right)=-1$, the sum over all residues $x$. For $x\not\equiv-5$, \[\left(\frac{(x+1)(x+5)}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{(x+1)(x+5)^{-1}}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{1-4(x+5)^{-1}}{p}\right),\] because multiplying by the square $(x+5)^{-2}$ does not change the symbol. As $x$ runs over the residues other than $-5$, $1-4(x+5)^{-1}$ runs over all residues except $1$. The sum of the symbol over all residues is $0$, so the sum over residues $\ne 1$ is $-1$; the term $x=-5$ adds $0$. This proves the claim.

The value $(x+1)(x+5)$ is $0$ for exactly two residues ($x\equiv-1,-5$). If $N_+$ and $N_-$ count the other $x$ with symbol $+1$ and $-1$, then $N_++N_-=81$ and $N_+-N_-=-1$, so $N_+=40$. The number of good $x$ is $40+2=\boxed{42}$.

Solution 2 (A parametrization of the conic)

Completing the square in $y$ gives \[(y+2x+2)^2\equiv(2x+2)^2-(3x^2+2x-1)=(x+1)(x+5)\pmod{83},\] so $x$ works exactly when $(x+1)(x+5)$ is a square modulo $83$. Put $u=x+3$; then $(x+1)(x+5)=u^2-4$, and $x$ works iff $u^2-t^2\equiv 4$ for some $t$, i.e. $(u-t)(u+t)\equiv 4$.

If such $t$ exists, $a=u-t$ is nonzero and $u+t\equiv 4a^{-1}$, so $u\equiv\frac12\left(a+4a^{-1}\right)$. Conversely, for any nonzero $a$, the values $u=\frac12(a+4a^{-1})$ and $t=\frac12(4a^{-1}-a)$ satisfy $(u-t)(u+t)=a\cdot 4a^{-1}=4$. So the good values of $u$ are exactly the values of $g(a)=\frac12(a+4a^{-1})$ for the $82$ nonzero residues $a$.

Now $g(a)=g(b)$ means $(a-b)\left(1-\frac{4}{ab}\right)\equiv 0$, i.e. $b=a$ or $b=4a^{-1}$. The pairing $a\leftrightarrow 4a^{-1}$ has fixed points only when $a^2\equiv 4$, i.e. $a\equiv\pm 2$. So $g$ takes $2+\frac{82-2}{2}=42$ distinct values, and since $x=u-3$ determines $x$, there are $\boxed{42}$ good values of $x$.

Problem 8

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Power Sums and Newton-Type Relations

Compute $\tan^4(20^\circ)+\tan^4(40^\circ)+\tan^4(60^\circ)+\tan^4(80^\circ)$.

Show the answer

$1044$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A cubic for the tangents and Newton's sums)

If $t=\tan\theta$, then $\tan 3\theta=\dfrac{3t-t^3}{1-3t^2}$. The angles $\theta=20^\circ,80^\circ,140^\circ$ satisfy $\tan3\theta=\tan60^\circ=\sqrt3$, so $t=\tan20^\circ,\tan80^\circ,\tan140^\circ$ are roots of \[3t-t^3=\sqrt3(1-3t^2)\iff t^3-3\sqrt3\,t^2-3t+\sqrt3=0 .\] These three values are distinct, so they are all the roots. Note $\tan140^\circ=-\tan40^\circ$, which has the same fourth power. The elementary symmetric functions are $e_1=3\sqrt3$, $e_2=-3$, $e_3=-\sqrt3$. With $p_k$ the sum of the $k$th powers of the roots, Newton's identities give \begin{align*} p_1&=e_1=3\sqrt3,\qquad p_2=e_1p_1-2e_2=27+6=33,\\ p_3&=e_1p_2-e_2p_1+3e_3=99\sqrt3+9\sqrt3-3\sqrt3=105\sqrt3,\\ p_4&=e_1p_3-e_2p_2+e_3p_1=945+99-9=1035 . \end{align*} Adding $\tan^460^\circ=(\sqrt3)^4=9$, the answer is $1035+9=\boxed{1044}$.

Solution 2 (A cubic for the squared tangents)

Let $u=\tan^2\theta$ and $t=\tan\theta$. For $\theta\in\{20^\circ,40^\circ,80^\circ\}$ we have $\tan^2 3\theta=3$ (as $3\theta$ is $60^\circ$, $120^\circ$ or $240^\circ$). From $\tan3\theta=\frac{3t-t^3}{1-3t^2}$ we get $t^2(3-t^2)^2=3(1-3t^2)^2$, that is, \[u(3-u)^2=3(1-3u)^2\iff u^3-33u^2+27u-3=0 .\] The three numbers $\tan^220^\circ<\tan^240^\circ<\tan^280^\circ$ are distinct roots of this cubic, hence all its roots. By Vieta, their sum is $33$ and the sum of their pairwise products is $27$, so \[\tan^420^\circ+\tan^440^\circ+\tan^480^\circ=33^2-2\cdot 27=1035 .\] With $\tan^460^\circ=9$ the total is $\boxed{1044}$.

Solution 3 (A cosecant identity and a differential equation)

Work in radians and put $u(x)=\csc^2x=\dfrac1{\sin^2x}$, so that $\cot^2x=u(x)-1$.

Step 1: a product formula. From $\sin3x=3\sin x-4\sin^3x=\sin x\,(3-4\sin^2x)$ and \[\sin\Bigl(x+\frac\pi3\Bigr)\sin\Bigl(x-\frac\pi3\Bigr)=\sin^2x\cos^2\frac\pi3-\cos^2x\sin^2\frac\pi3=\sin^2x-\frac34,\] we get $\sin3x=-4\sin x\,\sin\bigl(x+\frac\pi3\bigr)\sin\bigl(x-\frac\pi3\bigr)$.

Step 2: a sum of cosecants. On an interval where none of the four sines vanishes, take logarithms of absolute values and differentiate: \[3\cot3x=\cot x+\cot\Bigl(x+\frac\pi3\Bigr)+\cot\Bigl(x-\frac\pi3\Bigr).\] Differentiating again, using $(\cot x)'=-\csc^2x$, and writing $u_j=u\bigl(x+\frac{j\pi}3\bigr)$ for $j=-1,0,1$ and $U=u(3x)$, \[u_{-1}+u_0+u_1=9U. \tag{1}\]

Step 3: squares. Since $u'=-2\csc^2x\cot x$, we get $u''=4\csc^2x\cot^2x+2\csc^4x=4u(u-1)+2u^2=6u^2-4u$. Differentiate (1) twice; the right side becomes $81u''(3x)$, so \[\sum_j\left(6u_j^2-4u_j\right)=81\left(6U^2-4U\right).\] Using (1) for $\sum_j u_j$, this gives $6\sum_j u_j^2=486U^2-324U+36U$, that is, \[u_{-1}^2+u_0^2+u_1^2=81U^2-48U. \tag{2}\]

Step 4: evaluate at $x=\frac\pi{18}$ ($10^\circ$). Then $U=\csc^230^\circ=4$, so (1) and (2) give $\sum u_j=36$ and $\sum u_j^2=81\cdot16-48\cdot4=1104$. The three angles $x+\frac{j\pi}3$ are $10^\circ$, $70^\circ$ and $-50^\circ$, and the numbers $u_j-1=\cot^2\bigl(x+\frac{j\pi}3\bigr)$ are $\cot^210^\circ=\tan^280^\circ$, $\cot^270^\circ=\tan^220^\circ$ and $\cot^250^\circ=\tan^240^\circ$. Hence \begin{align*}\tan^420^\circ+\tan^440^\circ+\tan^480^\circ&=\sum_j(u_j-1)^2=\sum_ju_j^2-2\sum_ju_j+3\\&=1104-72+3=1035.\end{align*} Adding $\tan^460^\circ=9$, the answer is $\boxed{1044}$.

Combinatorics Round

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Contradiction and Elimination

Alice wrote $6$ numbers in a ring formation. (Rotations and reflections are deemed distinct.) Each number is either $1$ or $0$. A jester secretly flips $2$ of these numbers at random (either $1\to 0$ or $0\to 1$). Alice hires Bob the investigator to help her.

Bob has a tool that checks $3$ neighboring numbers and ONLY returns how many numbers are flipped by the jester. He can use this tool as much as he wants.

What is the probability that Bob can figure out where the defects are?

Show the answer

$\frac45$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (List the readings)

Number the positions $0,1,\dots,5$ around the ring (indices mod $6$) and let $W_i=\{i,i+1,i+2\}$. The tool tells Bob how many flipped positions lie in $W_i$; using it on all six windows, Bob learns the vector $(c_0,\dots,c_5)$ of these counts, and nothing more. (Alice's numbers play no role.) Bob succeeds exactly when the flipped pair is the only pair giving its vector. There are $\binom62=15$ equally likely pairs.

  • Adjacent pair $\{i,i+1\}$ (6 pairs): $W_{i-1},W_i$ contain both, $W_{i-2},W_{i+1}$ contain one, $W_{i+2},W_{i+3}$ contain none. The vector has two entries $2$, in positions $i-1,i$.

  • Pair at distance two $\{i,i+2\}$ (6 pairs): only $W_i$ contains both; $W_{i+3}=\{i+3,i+4,i+5\}$ contains none; the other four contain one. The vector has exactly one entry $2$, in position $i$.

  • Opposite pair $\{i,i+3\}$ (3 pairs): every window of three consecutive positions contains exactly one of them, so the vector is $(1,1,1,1,1,1)$.

The three kinds give different numbers of $2$s, and within the first two kinds the positions of the $2$s recover $i$. So the $12$ non-opposite pairs are identified, while the $3$ opposite pairs all give the same reading. The probability is $\frac{12}{15}=\boxed{\dfrac45}$.

Solution 2 (Which differences are invisible)

Number the positions $0,\dots,5$ mod $6$. Bob can read the number of flipped positions in each window $\{i,i+1,i+2\}$, and this is all he can learn. Write a flip pattern as a $0/1$ vector $f$ with two ones.

Suppose two patterns $f\neq g$ give the same six readings, and put $h=f-g$, with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Then $h_i+h_{i+1}+h_{i+2}=0$ for every $i$; subtracting consecutive equations gives $h_{i+3}=h_i$. So $h=(x,y,z,x,y,z)$ with $x+y+z=0$, and since $h\ne 0$, the numbers $x,y,z$ are $1,-1,0$ in some order. The pattern $f$ has a $1$ wherever $h=1$; these are two opposite positions, and as $f$ has only two ones, $f$ is an opposite pair. Likewise $g$ is an opposite pair.

So a non-opposite pair is never confused with anything, while the three opposite pairs $\{0,3\},\{1,4\},\{2,5\}$ all give the reading $1$ in every window and cannot be told apart. Out of $\binom62=15$ equally likely pairs, Bob succeeds for $12$: the probability is $\boxed{\dfrac45}$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

I'm going to write $100$ numbers $a_1,\dots,a_{100}$. For $a_1$ through $a_6$, I independently assign each of them a random integer between $0$ and $6$ inclusive. For $i>6$, I write $a_i=\operatorname{MEX}(\{a_{i-1},\dots,a_{i-6}\})$, where $\operatorname{MEX}(S)$ denotes the smallest nonnegative integer which is not in the set $S$. The probability that $a_{100}=6$ can be written as $\frac{m}{n}$ where $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Find $m+n$.

Show the answer

$18607$

Show the solution

Solution (The sequence becomes periodic at once)

Step 1: from $a_7$ on, the sequence has period $7$. For $i\ge7$, $a_i$ is not among $a_{i-6},\dots,a_{i-1}$, and $a_i\le 6$ (a set of six numbers misses one of $0,\dots,6$). Hence for $j\ge 7$, the seven terms $a_j,\dots,a_{j+6}$ are pairwise distinct (each one differs from the six before it), so they are $0,1,\dots,6$ in some order. Then $a_{j+7}$, the MEX of $a_{j+1},\dots,a_{j+6}$, is the only value of $\{0,\dots,6\}$ missing from them, namely $a_j$. So $a_{i+7}=a_i$ for $i\ge7$, and $a_{100}=a_{9}$ because $100=9+7\cdot 13$.

Step 2: when is $a_9=6$? The MEX of six numbers is $6$ exactly when they are $0,1,\dots,5$ in some order. So we need $\{a_3,\dots,a_8\}=\{0,\dots,5\}$. Then $a_3,a_4,a_5,a_6$ are distinct elements of $\{0,\dots,5\}$; let $u<v$ be the two values of $\{0,\dots,5\}$ they miss. We need $\{a_7,a_8\}=\{u,v\}$.

Since $0,\dots,5$ other than $u,v$ already occur among $a_3,\dots,a_6$, the value $a_7=\operatorname{MEX}(a_1,\dots,a_6)$ is the smallest of $u,v,6$ not in $\{a_1,a_2\}$, and similarly $a_8$ is the smallest of $u,v,6$ not in $\{a_2,a_7\}$.

  • $a_7=u$ and $a_8=v$: need $u\notin\{a_1,a_2\}$ and $v\ne a_2$.

  • $a_7=v$ and $a_8=u$: need $u\in\{a_1,a_2\}$, $v\notin\{a_1,a_2\}$ and $u\ne a_2$, i.e. $a_1=u$ and $a_2\notin\{u,v\}$.

Together: $a_2\notin\{u,v\}$ (5 choices), and $a_1$ is arbitrary ($a_1\ne u$ in the first case, $a_1=u$ in the second: 7 choices). The ordered quadruple $(a_3,\dots,a_6)$ has $6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3=360$ choices. So the number of good starts is $360\cdot 7\cdot 5=12600$ out of $7^6$, and \[P(a_{100}=6)=\frac{12600}{7^6}=\frac{1800}{7^5}=\frac{1800}{16807}.\] Since $\gcd(1800,7)=1$, $m+n=1800+16807=\boxed{18607}$.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Summation from Combinatorial Interpretation

What is the average value of the product $abc$ over all ordered triples of non-negative integers $(a,b,c)$ where $a+b+c=100$?

Show the answer

$16170$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Stars, bars and marked stars)

The number of ordered triples is $\binom{102}{2}$ (stars and bars: $100$ stars and $2$ bars in $102$ slots).

For the sum of $abc$, note that $abc$ counts the ways to mark one star in each of the three groups. Given $5$ slots $q_1<q_2<q_3<q_4<q_5$ among the $102$, put the two bars at $q_2,q_4$ and stars elsewhere, and mark the stars at $q_1,q_3,q_5$: this gives a triple with one marked star in each group, and every such marked triple arises from exactly one $5$-set (the marked stars and the bars, read left to right, alternate star, bar, star, bar, star). Hence \[\sum_{a+b+c=100}abc=\binom{102}{5}.\] The average is \[\frac{\binom{102}{5}}{\binom{102}{2}}=\frac{2!\,100!}{5!\,97!}=\frac{100\cdot 99\cdot 98}{60}=\boxed{16170}.\]

Solution 2 (Generating functions)

We have \[\sum_{a,b,c\ge0}abc\,x^{a+b+c}=\left(\sum_{a\ge0}ax^a\right)^3=\left(\frac{x}{(1-x)^2}\right)^3=\frac{x^3}{(1-x)^6}.\] Since $\frac{1}{(1-x)^6}=\sum_{j\ge0}\binom{j+5}{5}x^j$, the coefficient of $x^{100}$ is $\binom{97+5}{5}=\binom{102}{5}$; this is $\sum_{a+b+c=100}abc$. Similarly $\frac{1}{(1-x)^3}=\sum_j\binom{j+2}{2}x^j$ shows that there are $\binom{102}{2}$ triples. The average is \[\frac{\binom{102}{5}}{\binom{102}{2}}=\frac{100\cdot99\cdot98}{3\cdot4\cdot5}=\boxed{16170}.\]

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

Three airlines operate flights that fly between $2026$ cities. Each flight is operated by one airline and connects two cities in both ways. The three airlines want to make sure that if any one of the airlines stops operating, each city can still be reached from any other city via some path of flights operated by the other two airlines. Compute the minimum number of total flights needed across all three airlines for this to be possible.

Show the answer

$3038$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting edges of connected graphs, and a windmill)

Let the airlines operate $x$, $y$, $z$ flights. A connected network on $2026$ cities needs at least $2025$ flights (each flight can join at most two components into one). If the third airline stops, the other two must connect all cities, so $x+y\ge 2025$; likewise $y+z\ge2025$ and $z+x\ge2025$. Adding, $2(x+y+z)\ge 6075$, so $x+y+z\ge 3037.5$, i.e. at least $3038$ flights.

Construction with $3038$ flights. Call the airlines $A,B,C$. Take a hub $H$ and $1012$ pairs of cities $X_j,Y_j$ ($j=1,\dots,1012$): $1+2024=2025$ cities. For each $j$, let $A$ fly $H X_j$, $B$ fly $X_jY_j$, and $C$ fly $Y_jH$ (a triangle with one flight of each airline). Add the last city $Z$ with flights $ZX_1$ by $A$ and $ZY_1$ by $B$. The total is $3\cdot1012+2=3038$.

  • Without $A$: $Y_j$ reaches $H$ by $C$, $X_j$ reaches $Y_j$ by $B$, $Z$ reaches $Y_1$ by $B$.

  • Without $B$: $X_j$ and $Y_j$ reach $H$ by $A$ and $C$; $Z$ reaches $X_1$ by $A$.

  • Without $C$: $X_j$ reaches $H$ by $A$, $Y_j$ reaches $X_j$ by $B$, $Z$ reaches $X_1$ by $A$.

In each case all cities are connected to $H$. The minimum is $\boxed{3038}$.

Problem 7

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

For an undirected simple graph $G$, consider the following triangle toggling operation: select distinct vertices $u,v,w$, and toggle each of $(u,v)$, $(v,w)$, $(w,u)$ (here, toggling means that if the edge exists, remove it, otherwise add it). What is the maximum integer $E$ such that given any undirected simple graph $G$ on $2025$ vertices, one can apply the triangle toggling operation a finite number of times to create a graph with at least $E$ edges?

Show the answer

$2048288$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Degree parities and the cycle space)

Let $n=2025$, so $\binom n2=2025\cdot1012=2049300$, and every vertex of $K_n$ has even degree $2024$.

Invariant. A toggle on $u,v,w$ changes the degree of each of $u,v,w$ by $\pm1\pm1$, an even number, and leaves other degrees alone. So the parity of every degree is invariant.

Upper bound. Take $G$ to be a matching of $1012$ edges (one vertex unused). Its $2024$ matched vertices have odd degree forever. In any reachable graph each of them has odd degree, hence degree at most $2023$, so each misses at least one edge; a missing edge covers at most two such vertices, so at least $1012$ edges are missing. Thus $E\le 2049300-1012$.

Every graph reaches $2049300-1012$ edges. Toggling is adding (mod $2$) the edge set of a triangle. Every graph $C$ in which all degrees are even is a union of edge-disjoint cycles, and a cycle $v_1v_2\dots v_k$ is the mod-$2$ sum of the triangles $v_1v_iv_{i+1}$ ($2\le i\le k-1$), since every chord $v_1v_i$ appears twice. So from $G$ we can reach $G\oplus C$ for every even-degree graph $C$.

Let $T$ be the set of odd-degree vertices of $G$; $|T|$ is even and at most $2024$. Let $M$ be a perfect matching of $T$ and $G'=K_n\setminus M$. Then $\deg_{G'}(v)=2024-[v\in T]\equiv\deg_G(v)\pmod 2$, so $G\oplus G'$ has all degrees even and $G'$ is reachable. It has $\binom n2-\frac{|T|}2\ge 2049300-1012$ edges. Hence $E=\boxed{2048288}$.

Solution 2 (A greedy process on missing edges)

Let $n=2025$, $\binom n2=2049300$, and call an edge of $K_n$ not in the current graph a gap.

Every graph reaches $2049300-1012$ edges. While two gaps share a vertex, say $uv$ and $vw$ are gaps, toggle the triangle $uvw$: $uv$ and $vw$ become edges and $uw$ changes, so the number of edges grows by $1$ or $3$. The number of edges is bounded, so this stops, and then no two gaps share a vertex: the gaps form a matching, with at most $\lfloor 2025/2\rfloor=1012$ edges. The final graph has at least $2049300-1012$ edges.

Some graph cannot do better. A toggle changes the degree of each of its three vertices by $-2$, $0$ or $2$, so every degree keeps its parity. Start from a matching of $1012$ edges. Its $2024$ matched vertices always have odd degree, so at most $2023<2024$; each lies on a gap, and a gap covers at most two of them, so there are always at least $1012$ gaps. Hence $E=2049300-1012=\boxed{2048288}$.

$67$

Show the 2 solutions

The altitudes are inversely proportional to the sides, so the median altitude is the altitude $h$ to the side $n+1$. The triangle is non-degenerate iff $n+(n+1)>n+2$, i.e. $n\ge2$. With $s=\frac{3(n+1)}2$, Heron's formula gives \[[ABC]^2=\frac{3(n+1)}2\cdot\frac{n+3}2\cdot\frac{n+1}2\cdot\frac{n-1}2=\frac{3(n+1)^2\left((n+1)^2-4\right)}{16},\] and $h=\frac{2[ABC]}{n+1}$, so $4h^2=3\left((n+1)^2-4\right)$.

$1792$

Show the solution

Solution (Squarefree parts)

Write $Q(N)$ for the squarefree part of a positive integer $N$ (the product of the primes appearing to an odd power), so that $N_1/N_2$ is a perfect square iff $Q(N_1)=Q(N_2)$ (when $N_2\mid N_1$).

Problem 7

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure · Expectation Arguments

An $8$-sided die is crafted, and the probability $p_i$ of the $i$-th face being rolled is generated as follows: $p_1$ is a uniformly random number from $0$ to $1$, and $p_k$ is a uniformly random number from $0$ to $1-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i$ for all $k$ from $2$ to $7$, with the $p_k$ generated iteratively in order. The $8$th side has all the leftover probability: $p_8=1-\sum_{i=1}^{7}p_i$. This die is then rolled $8$ times. Find the probability that in these $8$ rolls, each face from $1$ to $8$ shows up exactly once.

Show the answer

$\frac{1}{181440}$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Independent uniform fractions)

Reformulation. Let $r_k=1-(p_1+\dots+p_k)$ be the probability left after $k$ steps ($r_0=1$). Choosing $p_k$ uniformly in $[0,r_{k-1}]$ is the same as choosing $U_k$ uniform in $[0,1]$, independent of the past, and putting $p_k=U_kr_{k-1}$. Then $r_k=(1-U_k)r_{k-1}$, so with independent uniform $U_1,\dots,U_7$, \[p_k=U_k\prod_{j<k}(1-U_j)\ (k\le7),\qquad p_8=\prod_{j\le7}(1-U_j).\]

The probability. For fixed $p_1,\dots,p_8$, the probability that $8$ rolls show each face once is $8!\,p_1p_2\cdots p_8$. Collecting the factors $U_k$ and $1-U_k$ ($1-U_k$ appears in $p_{k+1},\dots,p_8$, that is $8-k$ times), \[p_1\cdots p_8=\prod_{k=1}^{7}U_k(1-U_k)^{8-k}.\] By independence the expectation is $\prod_{k=1}^7E\left[U(1-U)^{8-k}\right]$, and \[E\left[U(1-U)^m\right]=\int_0^1u(1-u)^m\,du=\int_0^1(1-v)v^m\,dv=\frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)} .\] For $m=1,\dots,7$ the product of the $(m+1)$ is $8!$ and the product of the $(m+2)$ is $\frac{9!}{2}$. So the answer is \[8!\cdot\frac{2}{8!\cdot 9!}=\frac{2}{9!}=\boxed{\frac{1}{181440}} .\]

Solution 2 (Ranking random points)

Choosing $p_k$ uniformly in what is left is the same as taking a uniform fraction $U_k$ of what is left, so the construction is equivalent to $p_k=U_k\prod_{j<k}(1-U_j)$ for $k\le 7$ and $p_8=\prod_{j\le7}(1-U_j)$ with independent uniform $U_1,\dots,U_7$. For fixed $p_1,\dots,p_8$, the $8$ rolls show each face once with probability $8!\,p_1\cdots p_8$, and $p_1\cdots p_8=\prod_{k=1}^{7}U_k(1-U_k)^{8-k}$ (the factor $1-U_k$ occurs in $p_{k+1},\dots,p_8$). By independence the required probability is $8!\,E[p_1\cdots p_8]=8!\prod_{k=1}^{7}E\left[U_k(1-U_k)^{8-k}\right]$.

To evaluate $E[U(1-U)^m]$ without integrals, take $m+2$ independent uniform points $V_0,V_1,\dots,V_{m+1}$ in $[0,1]$. Given $V_0=u$, the probability that $V_1<u$ and $V_2,\dots,V_{m+1}>u$ is $u(1-u)^m$; so $E[U(1-U)^m]$ is the probability that, among the $m+2$ points, $V_1$ is the smallest and $V_0$ the second smallest. All $(m+2)!$ orders are equally likely, and this event fixes the first two places, so its probability is $\frac{m!}{(m+2)!}=\frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)}$.

Hence \[8!\prod_{m=1}^{7}\frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)}=8!\cdot\frac{1}{8!}\cdot\frac{2}{9!}=\boxed{\frac1{181440}} .\]

Solution 3 (Recursion on the number of faces)

Generalize: for $r\ge1$, build an $r$-sided die by choosing $p_1$ uniformly in $[0,1]$, then each $p_k$ ($k\le r-1$) uniformly in $[0,1-p_1-\dots-p_{k-1}]$, and $p_r=1-p_1-\dots-p_{r-1}$. Let $P_r$ be the probability that $r$ rolls show every face exactly once. Then $P_1=1$, and we want $P_8$.

Conditioning on the first face. Let $p_1=u$, uniform in $[0,1]$. Given $u$, the chance that face $1$ appears in exactly one of the $r$ rolls is $ru(1-u)^{r-1}$. Given that, and given which roll shows face $1$, the other $r-1$ rolls are independent and each shows face $k\ge2$ with probability $q_k=\frac{p_k}{1-u}$.

The renormalized die. Write $p_k=U_k\,(1-p_1-\dots-p_{k-1})$ with independent uniform $U_k\in[0,1]$; this is exactly the rule “uniform in what is left”. Dividing by $1-u$, the numbers $q_2,\dots,q_r$ satisfy $q_k=U_k\,(1-q_2-\dots-q_{k-1})$ for $k\le r-1$ and $q_r=1-q_2-\dots-q_{r-1}$. So $(q_2,\dots,q_r)$ is built by the same rule for $r-1$ faces, from $U_2,\dots,U_{r-1}$, which are independent of $u=U_1$. Therefore the probability that the remaining $r-1$ rolls show faces $2,\dots,r$ once each is $P_{r-1}$, whatever $u$ is.

The recursion. Hence \[P_r=P_{r-1}\int_0^1ru(1-u)^{r-1}\,du=P_{r-1}\cdot r\cdot\frac{1}{r(r+1)}=\frac{P_{r-1}}{r+1},\] where $\int_0^1u(1-u)^{r-1}du=\int_0^1(1-v)v^{r-1}dv=\frac1r-\frac1{r+1}=\frac1{r(r+1)}$. Starting from $P_1=1$, \[P_8=\frac{1}{3\cdot4\cdot5\cdot6\cdot7\cdot8\cdot9}=\frac{2}{9!}=\boxed{\frac1{181440}}.\]

Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Finite Difference and Discrete Polynomial Behavior

Suppose $P(x)$ is a polynomial of degree $2024$ such that for all $0\le n\le 2024$, we have $P(n)=F_n$. (Here, $F_n$ is the Fibonacci sequence given by $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, and $F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}$ for $n\ge 2$.) Find $P(2025)$.

Show the answer

$0$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Finite differences of the Fibonacci sequence)

Let $\Delta g(n)=g(n+1)-g(n)$, and extend the Fibonacci numbers to negative indices by $F_{n}=F_{n+2}-F_{n+1}$; then $F_{-k}=(-1)^{k+1}F_k$ (e.g. $F_{-1}=1$, $F_{-2}=-1$). Since $F_{n+1}-F_n=F_{n-1}$, we get $\Delta^kF(n)=F_{n-k}$, so $\Delta^kF(0)=F_{-k}$.

For any function $g$ and $N\ge0$, $g(N)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom Nk\Delta^kg(0)$ (expand $g(N)=(1+\Delta)^N g(0)$, where $1$ is the identity and $(1+\Delta)g(n)=g(n+1)$). Put $M=2025$. Since $P$ and $F$ agree at $0,1,\dots,2024$, their differences $\Delta^kP(0)$ and $\Delta^kF(0)$ agree for $k\le 2024$ (each depends only on these values), and $\Delta^{2025}P(0)=0$ because $\deg P=2024$. Therefore \[P(2025)=\sum_{k=0}^{2024}\binom{2025}{k}F_{-k}=F_{2025}-\binom{2025}{2025}F_{-2025}=F_{2025}-(-1)^{2026}F_{2025}=\boxed{0}.\]

Solution 2 (Binet's formula and an alternating sum)

For a polynomial of degree at most $2024$, the $2025$th finite difference vanishes: \[\sum_{k=0}^{2025}(-1)^{2025-k}\binom{2025}{k}P(k)=0 .\] (Indeed $\sum_k(-1)^{M-k}\binom Mk k^j=0$ for $j<M$, which follows by applying $\left(x\frac{d}{dx}\right)^j$ to $(x-1)^M$ and setting $x=1$.) So \[P(2025)=-\sum_{k=0}^{2024}(-1)^{2025-k}\binom{2025}{k}F_k=F_{2025}-S,\qquad S=\sum_{k=0}^{2025}(-1)^{2025-k}\binom{2025}kF_k .\] With $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$, $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$ and $F_k=\frac{\varphi^k-\psi^k}{\sqrt5}$, the binomial theorem gives \[S=\frac{(\varphi-1)^{2025}-(\psi-1)^{2025}}{\sqrt5}.\] Since $\varphi+\psi=1$, we have $\varphi-1=-\psi$ and $\psi-1=-\varphi$, so $S=\frac{-\psi^{2025}+\varphi^{2025}}{\sqrt5}=F_{2025}$. Therefore $P(2025)=F_{2025}-F_{2025}=\boxed{0}$.
