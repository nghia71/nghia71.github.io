Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

Each cell of the following $1\times 8$ grid is labeled with a single-digit positive integer such that any two adjacent cells differ by either $3$ or $5$. Compute the sum of all eight numbers in the grid.

Show the answer

$38$

Show the solution

Solution (Forced cells)

Number the cells $1$ to $8$ from left to right, so cells $1,3,6,8$ hold $6,8,7,5$. Every cell holds an integer from $1$ to $9$, and the neighbours of a value $v$ must lie in $\{v-5,\,v-3,\,v+3,\,v+5\}$.

Cell 2 is adjacent to $6$ and to $8$. The admissible neighbours of $6$ are $1,3,9$ and those of $8$ are $3,5$, so cell $2$ holds $3$.

Cell 7 is adjacent to $7$ and to $5$. The admissible neighbours of $7$ are $2,4$ and those of $5$ are $2,8$, so cell $7$ holds $2$.

Cells 4 and 5. Cell $4$ is adjacent to $8$, so it holds $3$ or $5$; cell $5$ is adjacent to $7$, so it holds $2$ or $4$. They must differ by $3$ or $5$. The pairs $(3,2)$, $(3,4)$, $(5,4)$ differ by $1$, and only $(5,2)$ works. So cell $4$ holds $5$ and cell $5$ holds $2$.

The grid is $6,3,8,5,2,7,2,5$; every adjacent difference is $3$ or $5$, and the sum is \[6+3+8+5+2+7+2+5=\boxed{38}.\]

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

Suppose $m$ and $b$ are positive real numbers such that the line $y=mx+b$ intersects the parabola $y=x^2$ at two points whose $x$-coordinates differ by $4$ and whose $y$-coordinates differ by $12$. Compute $b$.

Show the answer

$\frac{7}{4}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vieta's formulas)

The intersection points have $x$-coordinates $x_1<x_2$, the roots of $x^2=mx+b$, that is, of $x^2-mx-b=0$. By Vieta's formulas, $x_1+x_2=m$ and $x_1x_2=-b$.

Both points lie on the line, so their $y$-coordinates differ by $m(x_2-x_1)=4m$. Since $m>0$, this gives $4m=12$, so $m=3$.

Also \[(x_2-x_1)^2=(x_1+x_2)^2-4x_1x_2=m^2+4b,\] so $16=9+4b$ and $b=\boxed{\frac{7}{4}}$.

Solution 2 (Using the parabola)

Let the two intersection points be $(t,t^2)$ and $(t+4,(t+4)^2)$. Their $y$-coordinates differ by \[(t+4)^2-t^2=8t+16.\] The line through them has positive slope $m$, so the right-hand point is higher and $8t+16=12$, giving $t=-\frac12$. The points are $\left(-\frac12,\frac14\right)$ and $\left(\frac72,\frac{49}{4}\right)$, and the slope of the line is $\frac{12}{4}=3$. Its $y$-intercept is \[b=\frac14-3\cdot\left(-\frac12\right)=\frac14+\frac32=\boxed{\frac{7}{4}}.\]

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Constraint-Based Enumeration

Eric the monkey has no bananas. Every morning, Eric may optionally go to the supermarket to buy a single bunch of exactly $5$ bananas. Eric may only go to the supermarket $3$ times. Every night, Eric must eat $1$ banana or else he dies. Compute the number of ways Eric may survive for $15$ nights.

Show the answer

$35$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Deadlines for the three trips)

Eric eats $15$ bananas in $15$ nights, and each trip gives exactly $5$ bananas, so he must make all $3$ trips, on mornings $d_1<d_2<d_3$ among mornings $1$ to $15$. A way of surviving is exactly such a choice of three mornings for which he never runs out.

Before night $n$ he has eaten $n-1$ bananas and must eat one more, so the bananas bought on mornings $1,\dots,n$ must number at least $n$. With $5$ bananas per trip this says:

  • at least one trip by morning $1$, so $d_1=1$;

  • at least two trips by morning $6$, so $d_2\le 6$;

  • at least three trips by morning $11$, so $d_3\le 11$.

Conversely, if $d_1=1$, $d_2\le 6$ and $d_3\le 11$, then by night $n$ he has bought at least $5$ bananas for $n\le 5$, at least $10$ for $n\le 10$ and $15$ for $n\le 15$, so he survives.

For each $d_2\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}$ the number of choices of $d_3$ with $d_2<d_3\le 11$ is $11-d_2$, so the count is \[9+8+7+6+5=\boxed{35}.\]

Solution 2 (Complementary counting)

Eric needs $15$ bananas and each of his at most $3$ trips brings $5$, so he makes exactly $3$ trips during mornings $1$ to $15$. On night $1$ he must already have a banana, so the first trip is on morning $1$. The other two trips are on mornings $d_2<d_3$ chosen from $2,\dots,15$, which can be done in $\binom{14}{2}=91$ ways.

Eric starves exactly when he runs out before a trip. After the first trip he has bananas for nights $1$ to $5$, so he starves if $d_2\ge 7$; if $d_2\le 6$, then after two trips he has bananas for nights $1$ to $10$, and he starves exactly when $d_3\ge 12$.

  • $d_2\ge 7$: both mornings come from $7,\dots,15$, giving $\binom{9}{2}=36$ choices.

  • $d_2\le 6$ and $d_3\ge 12$: $5\cdot 4=20$ choices.

These cases do not overlap, so the number of ways to survive is $91-36-20=\boxed{35}$.

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Distance from Perpendicular Structure

Square $AXYZ$ with area $13$ is placed along hypotenuse $\overline{AC}$ of right triangle $ABC$. Given that the distances from $X$ and $Y$ to $\overline{BC}$ are $10$ and $8$ respectively, compute $BC$.

Show the answer

$\frac{21}{2}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Trigonometry of the tilted square)

Let $\theta=\angle ACB$ and $s=\sqrt{13}$, the side of the square. Measure heights above line $BC$.

The side $XY$ is parallel to $AZ$, which lies along $AC$, so $XY$ makes the angle $\theta$ with $BC$; going from $X$ to $Y$ we move away from $A$ along the direction of $AC$, so the height drops by $s\sin\theta$. Thus $s\sin\theta=10-8=2$, so $\sin\theta=\frac{2}{\sqrt{13}}$ and $\cos\theta=\frac{3}{\sqrt{13}}$.

The side $AX$ is perpendicular to $AC$ and points away from the triangle, so going from $A$ to $X$ the height rises by $s\cos\theta=3$. Hence $AB$, the height of $A$, equals $10-3=7$.

Finally, in right triangle $ABC$, \[BC=\frac{AB}{\tan\theta}=7\cdot\frac{3}{2}=\boxed{\frac{21}{2}}.\]

Solution 2 (Congruent right triangles)

Let $P$ be the point with $XP$ horizontal and $PY$ vertical (so $P$ is directly above $Y$ at the height of $X$), and let $Q$ be the point with $AQ$ horizontal and $QX$ vertical. Here horizontal means parallel to $BC$.

The segments $XY$ and $AX$ are perpendicular and have the same length $\sqrt{13}$. A rotation by $90^\circ$ carries one onto the other (up to direction) and turns horizontal segments into vertical ones and vice versa, so the right triangles $XPY$ and $AQX$ are congruent with the legs swapped: \[AQ=PY=10-8=2,\qquad QX=XP=\sqrt{XY^2-PY^2}=\sqrt{13-4}=3.\] So $X$ is $3$ higher than $A$, and $AB=10-3=7$.

The side $AZ$ is parallel to $XY$ and lies along $AC$. Along $XY$ the height drops by $2$ while the horizontal distance grows by $3$, so along $AC$ the height also drops by $2$ for every $3$ across. From $A$ (height $7$) to $C$ (height $0$) the drop is $7$, so \[BC=7\cdot\frac32=\boxed{\frac{21}{2}}.\]

Problem 5

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Integer and Rational Root Structure

Let $\alpha$ be the unique real number such that both $\alpha+\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{12}}$ and $\alpha^3+\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{12}}$ are positive and rational. Compute $\alpha^3+\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{12}}$.

Show the answer

$\frac{25}{54}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Comparing irrational parts)

Let $s=\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{12}}=\frac{\sqrt{33}}{6}$. Since $33$ is not a perfect square, $s$ is irrational, so if $u+vs=u'+v's$ with rational $u,v,u',v'$, then $u=u'$ and $v=v'$.

Let $p=\alpha+s$, which is rational, so $\alpha=p-s$. Using $s^2=\frac{11}{12}$ and $s^3=\frac{11}{12}s$, \[\alpha^3=(p-s)^3=p^3-3p^2s+3ps^2-s^3=\left(p^3+\frac{11}{4}p\right)-\left(3p^2+\frac{11}{12}\right)s,\] so \[\alpha^3+s=\left(p^3+\frac{11}{4}p\right)+\left(\frac{1}{12}-3p^2\right)s.\] This is rational exactly when $3p^2=\frac{1}{12}$, that is, $p=\pm\frac16$; positivity of $\alpha+s=p$ forces $p=\frac16$. Then \[\alpha^3+s=\frac{1}{216}+\frac{11}{24}=\frac{1+99}{216}=\boxed{\frac{25}{54}},\] which is indeed positive.

Solution 2 (A quadratic factor of a cubic)

Let $s=\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{12}}$, which is irrational because $\tfrac{11}{12}=\tfrac{33}{36}$ and $33$ is not a perfect square. Let $p=\alpha+s$ and $q=\alpha^3+s$, both rational. Then $\alpha=p-s$ is irrational, and it is a root of \[g(x)=(x-p)^2-\tfrac{11}{12}\qquad\text{and of}\qquad h(x)=x^3-x-(q-p),\] both with rational coefficients. Divide $h$ by the monic quadratic $g$: $h(x)=g(x)(x-t)+R(x)$ with $t$ rational and $R(x)=ux+v$ rational of degree at most $1$. Then $R(\alpha)=h(\alpha)-g(\alpha)(\alpha-t)=0$; if $u\ne0$ this would make $\alpha=-\frac vu$ rational, so $u=0$ and then $v=0$. Hence $h(x)=g(x)(x-t)$, and the roots of $h$ are $p+s$, $p-s$ and $t$.

By Vieta's formulas for $h(x)=x^3+0x^2-x-(q-p)$: the roots sum to $0$, so $t=-2p$; the sum of their pairwise products is $-1$, so \[(p+s)(p-s)+t\bigl((p+s)+(p-s)\bigr)=p^2-\tfrac{11}{12}-4p^2=-1,\] which gives $3p^2=\frac{1}{12}$ and $p=\frac16$ (as $p>0$); and the product of the roots is $q-p$, so \[q-p=\left(p^2-\tfrac{11}{12}\right)(-2p)=\left(\tfrac1{36}-\tfrac{33}{36}\right)\left(-\tfrac13\right)=\tfrac{32}{108}=\tfrac{8}{27}.\] Therefore $q=\frac16+\frac{8}{27}=\frac{9+16}{54}=\boxed{\frac{25}{54}}$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Homothety Centers and Image Tracking

Let $O$ be the circumcenter of an acute triangle $ABC$, and let $D$ and $E$ be points on side $BC$ such that $\overline{OD}$ is parallel to $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{OE}$ is parallel to $\overline{AC}$. Given that $BD=7$, $DE=6$, and $EC=5$, compute $AO$.

Show the answer

$\sqrt{105}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A homothety of ratio one third)

The points lie on side $BC$ in the order $B,D,E,C$: if $E$ came before $D$, then $D$ would lie on segment $EC$ and $DE\le EC=5$, contradicting $DE=6$. So $BC=7+6+5=18$. Put $B$ at $0$ on line $BC$, so $D$, $E$, $C$ are at $7$, $13$, $18$. Since the triangle is acute, $O$ lies inside it, on the same side of $BC$ as $A$.

Triangles $DOE$ and $BAC$ have $DO\parallel BA$, $OE\parallel AC$ and $DE\parallel BC$ (both on line $BC$), and $O$, $A$ lie on the same side of $BC$. Hence there is a homothety with positive ratio $\frac{DE}{BC}=\frac{6}{18}=\frac13$ taking $B\mapsto D$, $C\mapsto E$, $A\mapsto O$. It maps line $BC$ to itself, so its centre $X$ lies on $BC$, and $D=X+\frac13(B-X)$ gives $7=\frac23X$, so $X=\frac{21}{2}$ (check: $\frac{21}{2}+\frac13\left(18-\frac{21}{2}\right)=13$, the position of $E$).

Let $F$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ and $M$ the midpoint of $BC$, at $9$; since $O$ is the circumcentre, $OM\perp BC$. The homothety maps the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$ onto the perpendicular from $O$ to $BC$, so it maps $F$ to $M$: \[9=X+\tfrac13(F-X)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F=X+3(9-X)=6.\] Let $h=AF$; then $OM=\frac h3$. Now $OA=OB$ gives \[(9-6)^2+\left(h-\tfrac h3\right)^2=9^2+\left(\tfrac h3\right)^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 9+\tfrac{4h^2}{9}=81+\tfrac{h^2}{9}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h^2=216.\] Therefore $AO^2=OB^2=81+\frac{216}{9}=105$, and $AO=\boxed{\sqrt{105}}$. (Indeed $AB^2=252$, $AC^2=360$, $BC^2=324$, so the triangle is acute.)

Solution 2 (Tangents of the angles)

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and $d=OM$; since $O$ is the circumcentre of an acute triangle, $OM\perp BC$ and $O$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $A$. The points lie on side $BC$ in the order $B,D,E,C$ (if $E$ came before $D$, then $D$ would lie on segment $EC$ and $DE\le EC=5<6$), so $BC=7+6+5=18$ and $BM=MC=9$, so $DM=9-7=2$ with $D$ between $B$ and $M$, and $ME=9-5=4$ with $E$ between $M$ and $C$.

Since $DO\parallel BA$ and both rays $DO$ and $BA$ point from line $BC$ into the same half-plane, $\angle ODM=\angle ABC$; in right triangle $ODM$ this gives $\tan B=\frac d2$. In the same way $OE\parallel CA$ gives $\angle OEM=\angle ACB$ and $\tan C=\frac d4$. Also the central angle $\angle BOC$ equals $2A$, and $OM$ bisects it, so $\angle BOM=A$ and $\tan A=\frac{BM}{OM}=\frac9d$.

For the angles of a triangle (none of them right), $\tan A+\tan B+\tan C=\tan A\tan B\tan C$, because $\tan A=-\tan(B+C)=-\frac{\tan B+\tan C}{1-\tan B\tan C}$. So \[\frac9d+\frac d2+\frac d4=\frac9d\cdot\frac d2\cdot\frac d4=\frac{9d}{8}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac9d=\frac{3d}{8}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d^2=24.\] Therefore $AO=OB=\sqrt{BM^2+OM^2}=\sqrt{81+24}=\boxed{\sqrt{105}}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Encoding and Representation

In an $11\times 11$ grid of unit squares, rows and columns are indexed consecutively with the integers from $1$ to $11$. Then, every cell with an even row and column index is deleted. Compute the number of ways to partition the resulting shape into rectangles with integer side lengths.

Show the answer

$3^4\cdot 35^{16}$

Show the solution

Solution (Independent choices at the junction cells)

Call a cell with odd row and odd column a junction; there are $6\cdot 6=36$ of them. Every other remaining cell has exactly one even index; call it a link.

All rectangles are strips. A rectangle with at least two rows and two columns contains two consecutive rows and two consecutive columns, one of each even, hence a deleted cell. So every rectangle is a $1\times k$ or $k\times 1$ strip.

Encoding. For a partition, call two side-by-side cells joined if they lie in the same rectangle. The partition is determined by its set of joined pairs. Conversely, a set of joined pairs comes from a partition into strips exactly when no cell is joined both horizontally and vertically: then every class of cells connected by joins is a straight run of consecutive cells, that is, a strip, and in a strip all neighbouring pairs are joined. (If some cell were joined both ways, its rectangle would contain cells in two rows and two columns.)

Each adjacent pair contains exactly one junction. A link in an even row lies in an odd column, so the cells to its left and right have two even indices and are deleted; its only neighbours are the junctions above and below it. Similarly a link in an even column has only the junctions to its left and right as neighbours. Two links are never adjacent and neither are two junctions. So every adjacent pair is a junction together with one of its link neighbours, and the condition “not joined both ways” only concerns junctions.

A junction has $h$ horizontal neighbours and $v$ vertical neighbours, where $h=1$ if it is in column $1$ or $11$ and $h=2$ otherwise, and likewise $v$ with rows. The pairs at different junctions are different, so the choices are independent: at a junction we may join a subset of the horizontal pairs and none of the vertical ones, or the reverse, giving $2^h+2^v-1$ choices (the empty choice is counted once).

  • $4$ corner junctions: $h=v=1$, giving $2+2-1=3$ choices each;

  • $16$ other junctions on the border rows and columns: $\{h,v\}=\{1,2\}$, giving $2+4-1=5$ each;

  • $16$ inner junctions: $h=v=2$, giving $4+4-1=7$ each.

The number of partitions is \[3^4\cdot 5^{16}\cdot 7^{16}=\boxed{3^4\cdot 35^{16}}=410746364767102203369140625.\]

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Dot Product and Metric Recovery

In the diagram, six circles of radius $3$ are arranged so that each is tangent to two others. Their tangency points form the vertices of a cyclic hexagon with circumradius $5$. Compute the area of this hexagon.

Show the answer

$\frac{147\sqrt3}{4}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors from the centre)

Let $O$ be the centre of the hexagon's circumcircle, and number the circles' centres $C_1,\dots,C_6$ in order around $O$ (as in the diagram, the ring of circles surrounds $O$), with circle $i$ tangent to circles $i\pm1$ (indices mod $6$). Write $\mathbf c_i$ for the vector $\overrightarrow{OC_i}$ and $r_i=|\mathbf c_i|$.

Two tangent circles of radius $3$ have centres $6$ apart and touch at the midpoint of their centres, so the tangency point of circles $i$ and $i+1$ is $T_i=\frac12(\mathbf c_i+\mathbf c_{i+1})$, with \[|\mathbf c_i-\mathbf c_{i+1}|=6,\qquad |\mathbf c_i+\mathbf c_{i+1}|=2\cdot OT_i=10.\] Subtracting and adding the squares of these gives \[\mathbf c_i\cdot\mathbf c_{i+1}=\frac{100-36}{4}=16,\qquad r_i^2+r_{i+1}^2=\frac{100+36}{2}=68.\] The second relation shows $r_1=r_3=r_5=p$ and $r_2=r_4=r_6=q$ for some $p,q$ with $p^2+q^2=68$. Then every angle $\angle C_iOC_{i+1}$ has cosine $\frac{16}{pq}$, so all six are equal; going once around $O$ they add up to $360^\circ$, so each is $60^\circ$ and $pq=32$.

The hexagon $T_1\cdots T_6$ goes around $O$, so its area is $\sum_i[OT_iT_{i+1}]=\frac12\sum_i \mathbf T_i\times\mathbf T_{i+1}$, where $\times$ is the planar cross product (all terms positive for the counterclockwise order). Since $\mathbf c_{i+1}\times\mathbf c_{i+1}=0$, \[\mathbf T_i\times\mathbf T_{i+1}=\tfrac14\left(\mathbf c_i\times\mathbf c_{i+1}+\mathbf c_i\times\mathbf c_{i+2}+\mathbf c_{i+1}\times\mathbf c_{i+2}\right).\] Here $\mathbf c_i\times\mathbf c_{i+1}=pq\sin60^\circ=16\sqrt3$, and $\mathbf c_i$, $\mathbf c_{i+2}$ have equal length $r_i$ and make an angle of $120^\circ$, so $\mathbf c_i\times\mathbf c_{i+2}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}r_i^2$. Summing over $i=1,\dots,6$ (three values of $i$ have $r_i=p$ and three have $r_i=q$): \[\text{Area}=\frac18\left(6\cdot 32\sqrt3+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 3\left(p^2+q^2\right)\right)=\frac18\left(192\sqrt3+102\sqrt3\right)=\boxed{\frac{147\sqrt3}{4}}.\]

Solution 2 (An equiangular hexagon)

Let $O$ be the centre of the circumcircle of the hexagon and $C_1,\dots,C_6$ the centres of the circles in order around $O$ (the ring surrounds $O$, as in the diagram), circle $i$ tangent to circles $i\pm1$, indices mod $6$. Equal circles touch at the midpoint of their centres, so the tangency point $T_i$ of circles $i$ and $i+1$ is the midpoint of $C_iC_{i+1}$, and $C_iC_{i+1}=6$. Let $r_i=OC_i$.

Distances to the centres. In triangle $OC_iC_{i+1}$ the median $OT_i$ has length $5$, so by Apollonius' theorem \[r_i^2+r_{i+1}^2=2\cdot 5^2+2\cdot 3^2=68.\] Hence $r_1=r_3=r_5=p$ and $r_2=r_4=r_6=q$ with $p^2+q^2=68$. All six triangles $OC_iC_{i+1}$ have sides $p,q,6$, so they are congruent and their angles at $O$ are equal; these angles fill the full turn, so each is $60^\circ$. The law of cosines gives $36=p^2+q^2-pq$, so $pq=32$.

Sides of the hexagon. In triangle $C_iC_{i+1}C_{i+2}$ the segment $T_iT_{i+1}$ joins midpoints, so $T_iT_{i+1}=\frac12C_iC_{i+2}$. In isosceles triangle $OC_iC_{i+2}$ the legs equal $r_i$ and the apex angle is $120^\circ$, so $C_iC_{i+2}=\sqrt3\,r_i$. Thus the hexagon's sides alternate between $a=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}p$ and $b=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}q$.

Angles of the hexagon. Equal chords cut equal arcs, so the arcs cut by the sides alternate between two values $\alpha,\beta$ with $3\alpha+3\beta=360^\circ$. The angle of the hexagon at a vertex is an inscribed angle standing on the arc made of the four sides not at that vertex, which is $2\alpha+2\beta=240^\circ$; so every angle of the hexagon is $120^\circ$.

Area. Extending the three sides of length $a$ of an equiangular hexagon with sides $a,b,a,b,a,b$ produces an equilateral triangle of side $a+2b$, from which three equilateral triangles of side $b$ have been cut off. Hence the area is \[\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\left((a+2b)^2-3b^2\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\left(a^2+4ab+b^2\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot\frac34\left(p^2+q^2+4pq\right)=\frac{3\sqrt3}{16}\cdot 196=\boxed{\frac{147\sqrt3}{4}}.\]

Problem 9

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Closed Forms and Hidden Structure

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots$ be a sequence of positive integers such that $a_1=a_2=\dots=a_7=1$ and \[a_n=a_{n-1}+10a_{n-7}\quad\text{for all } n\geq 8.\] Compute the remainder when $a_{2026}$ is divided by $10000$.

Show the answer

$2991$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Weighted tilings)

For $m\ge0$ let $f(m)$ be the sum, over all ways of writing $m$ as an ordered sum of $1$s and $7$s, of $10^{(\text{number of }7\text{s})}$; set $f(m)=0$ for $m<0$. Splitting by the last part gives $f(m)=f(m-1)+10f(m-7)$ for $m\ge 7$, and $f(0)=\dots=f(6)=1$ since only $1$s fit. Thus $a_n=f(n-1)$: both sequences start with seven $1$s and obey the same recurrence.

An ordered sum with $k$ sevens and $m-7k$ ones has $m-6k$ parts, and the positions of the sevens can be chosen in $\binom{m-6k}{k}$ ways. Hence \[a_{2026}=f(2025)=\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2025-6k}{k}10^k.\] Modulo $10000$ only $k\le3$ matter:

  • $k=0$: $1$;

  • $k=1$: $10\cdot 2019=20190\equiv 190$;

  • $k=2$: $100\binom{2013}{2}=100\cdot 2013\cdot 1006=100\cdot 2025078\equiv 100\cdot 78=7800$;

  • $k=3$: $1000\binom{2007}{3}=1000\cdot 669\cdot 1003\cdot 2005$, and $669\cdot1003\cdot2005\equiv 9\cdot3\cdot5\equiv 5\pmod{10}$, so this term is $\equiv 5000$.

The sum is $1+190+7800+5000=12991$, so the remainder is $\boxed{2991}$.

Solution 2 (Generating function)

Let $A(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_nx^{n}$. For $n\ge8$ the recurrence says that the coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1-x-10x^7)A(x)$ is $a_n-a_{n-1}-10a_{n-7}=0$. For $2\le n\le7$ that coefficient is $a_n-a_{n-1}=0$, and for $n=1$ it is $a_1=1$. Hence $(1-x-10x^7)A(x)=x$, and \[A(x)=\frac{x}{1-(x+10x^7)}=\sum_{j\ge0}x\,(x+10x^7)^j=\sum_{j\ge0}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom jk10^k\,x^{1+j+6k}.\] The coefficient of $x^{2026}$ collects the terms with $j+6k=2025$, so \[a_{2026}=\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2025-6k}{k}10^k\equiv 1+10\binom{2019}{1}+100\binom{2013}{2}+1000\binom{2007}{3}\pmod{10^4}.\] Now $10\cdot2019=20190$; $\binom{2013}{2}=2013\cdot1006=2025078$, which ends in $78$; and $\binom{2007}{3}=669\cdot1003\cdot2005$ ends in $5$ (its last digit is that of $9\cdot3\cdot5=135$). (A connected component is a maximal nonempty set of cities such that every city in the set is reachable from every other city in the set by traveling through built tunnels.)

Show the answer

$\frac{4950}{197}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting edges in a forest)

Think of cities as vertices and built tunnels as edges. Call a tunnel mutual if both of its cities fund it. We show that every component contains exactly one mutual tunnel.

No cycles. Suppose the built edges contain a cycle $v_1v_2\cdots v_k$ with $k\ge3$. Each of its $k$ edges is funded by one of its two ends, and each of the $k$ vertices funds only one edge, so each vertex funds exactly one cycle edge and no cycle edge is funded twice. Going around, this means (after possibly reversing the direction) that $v_1$ funds $v_1v_2$, $v_2$ funds $v_2v_3$, and so on. Since $v_2$ funds $v_2v_3$ rather than $v_1v_2$, the cost of $v_2v_3$ is less than that of $v_1v_2$; continuing, the costs strictly decrease around the cycle and return to the start, a contradiction.

One mutual edge per component. So the built graph is a forest. A component with $k$ vertices is a tree with $k-1$ edges, and each of its $k$ vertices funds an edge of this component. Counting fundings, $k=(k-1)+(\text{number of mutual edges})$, so the component has exactly one mutual edge.

Expectation. Hence the number of components equals the number of mutual tunnels. A tunnel $uv$ is mutual exactly when it is the cheapest among the $1+2\cdot98=197$ tunnels at $u$ or $v$; by symmetry this has probability $\frac1{197}$. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of components is \[\binom{100}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{197}=\boxed{\frac{4950}{197}}.\]

Solution 2 (Following cheaper and cheaper tunnels)

For a city $v$ let $f(v)$ be the other end of the tunnel that $v$ funds, and $w(v)$ the cost of that tunnel; the built tunnels are exactly the tunnels $v\,f(v)$.

Descending walks. City $f(v)$ funds its cheapest tunnel, and the tunnel to $v$ is one of its tunnels, so $w(f(v))\le w(v)$, with equality exactly when $f(v)$ also funds the tunnel $v\,f(v)$, that is, when $f(f(v))=v$. Starting at any city and applying $f$ repeatedly, the costs never increase and are strictly decreasing until we reach a pair $\{u,f(u)\}$ with $f(f(u))=u$: a tunnel funded by both ends, which we call mutual. Since there are finitely many cities and the costs cannot decrease forever, every walk ends in exactly one mutual pair, where it then alternates.

Components. Each step of the walk travels along a built tunnel, so every city lies in the same component as the mutual pair its walk reaches. Conversely, if $v$ and $f(v)$ are joined by a built tunnel, the walk from $v$ passes through $f(v)$, so they reach the same mutual pair. Thus the relation “reaches the same mutual pair” is constant along built tunnels, and the components are exactly the sets of cities reaching a given mutual pair. Therefore the number of components equals the number of mutual tunnels.

Expectation. A tunnel $uv$ is mutual exactly when its cost is the smallest among the $197$ tunnels incident to $u$ or $v$ (the tunnel itself and $98$ more at each end). All orders of these costs are equally likely, so this has probability $\frac1{197}$. By linearity of expectation over the $\binom{100}{2}=4950$ tunnels, the answer is $\boxed{\frac{4950}{197}}$.

Both are internal common tangents, so they pass through the internal centre of similitude, which divides $PK$ in the ratio $3:r$: \[I_1=\frac{rP+3K}{r+3}=\left(3,\ \frac{90}{r+3}\right).\] Likewise $x=2r+3$ and $\ell$ are internal common tangents of the right wheel and the pumpkin, through \[I_2=\frac{rQ+4K}{r+4}=\left(\frac{2r^2+11r+12}{r+4},\ \frac{120}{r+4}\right)=\left(2r+3,\ \frac{120}{r+4}\right).\] So the slope of $\ell$ is \[\frac{\frac{120}{r+4}-\frac{90}{r+3}}{2r}=\frac{30r}{2r(r+3)(r+4)}=\frac{15}{(r+3)(r+4)}=\frac{60}{d^2-1},\] using $(2r+6)(2r+8)=(d-1)(d+1)$.

External centre. The ground and $\ell$ both touch the two wheels with both wheels on the same side, so they meet at the external centre of similitude $E$ of the wheels, on line $PQ$ with $EP:EQ=3:4$; this gives $E=4P-3Q=(-3d,0)$. The line $EP$ bisects the angle between the two tangents from $E$ to the left wheel, and it makes an angle $\alpha$ with the ground with $\tan\alpha=\frac{3}{3d}=\frac1d$. So $\ell$ rises at the angle $2\alpha$, with slope \[\tan2\alpha=\frac{2/d}{1-1/d^2}=\frac{2d}{d^2-1}.\] Comparing the two slopes, $2d=60$, so $d=30$ and $r=\frac{d-7}{2}=\boxed{\frac{23}{2}}$.

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

Compute the unique triple of positive integers $(b,e,\ell)$ satisfying $be\ell+\ell+e=6886$ and $be+\ell+\ell e=586$.

Show the answer

$(19,10,36)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Adding the equations)

Adding the two equations and $2$, \[be\ell+be+2\ell+\ell e+e+2=7474.\] The left side factors: $(\ell+1)(be+e+2)=be\ell+e\ell+2\ell+be+e+2$. So \[(\ell+1)\bigl(e(b+1)+2\bigr)=7474=2\cdot37\cdot101.\] Also the second equation says $e(b+\ell)+\ell=586$, and subtracting $e(b+1)$ from $e(b+\ell)$ gives $(\ell-1)e=586-\ell-e(b+1)$. Go through the divisors $\ell+1\ge2$ of $7474$ with $e(b+1)=\frac{7474}{\ell+1}-2\ge 2$:

  • $\ell=1$: $e(b+1)=3735$, but the second equation gives $e(b+1)=585$. Impossible.

  • $\ell=36$: $e(b+1)=200$, and $35e=586-36-200=350$, so $e=10$ and $b=19$.

  • $\ell=73$: $e(b+1)=99$, and $72e=586-73-99=414$, not a multiple of $72$.

  • $\ell=100$: $e(b+1)=72$, and $99e=586-100-72=414$, not a multiple of $99$.

  • $\ell=201$: $e(b+1)=35$, and $200e=586-201-35=350$, not a multiple of $200$.

  • $\ell+1\in\{3737,7474\}$: $e(b+1)\le 0$. Impossible.

So $(b,e,\ell)=\boxed{(19,10,36)}$. Check: $19\cdot10\cdot36+36+10=6886$ and $190+36+360=586$.

Solution 2 (Eliminating b)

Multiply the second equation by $\ell$ and subtract the first; the terms $be\ell$ cancel: \[\ell^2+\ell^2e-\ell-e=586\ell-6886\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e(\ell^2-1)=-\ell^2+587\ell-6886.\] So $\ell\ge2$ (for $\ell=1$ the left side is $0$ but the right side is $-6300$), and $\ell^2-1$ divides $g(\ell)=-\ell^2+587\ell-6886$. In particular $\ell-1\mid g(\ell)$ and $\ell+1\mid g(\ell)$. Since $\ell\equiv1\pmod{\ell-1}$ and $\ell\equiv-1\pmod{\ell+1}$, \[\ell-1\mid g(1)=-6300,\qquad \ell+1\mid g(-1)=-7474=-2\cdot37\cdot101.\] Moreover $g(\ell)=e(\ell^2-1)>0$. The divisors $\ell+1\ge3$ of $7474$ give $\ell\in\{36,73,100,201,3736,7473\}$; the last two make $g(\ell)<0$. Of the rest, $\ell-1=35$ divides $6300$, while $72$, $99$, $200$ do not (none of $\frac{6300}{72}$, $\frac{6300}{99}$, $\frac{6300}{200}$ is an integer). So $\ell=36$, and \[e=\frac{-1296+21132-6886}{1295}=\frac{12950}{1295}=10.\] The second equation gives $10b+36+360=586$, so $b=19$. The triple is $\boxed{(19,10,36)}$.

Problem 10

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Addition and Balance Relations

Merida paints an equilateral triangle $ABC$ with side length $4$ units on her castle wall. She attempts to shoot an arrow at each vertex of this triangle. However, her arrows strike the wall at points $D$, $E$, and $F$, which are $1$, $2$, and $3$ units away from $A$, $B$, and $C$, respectively. Furthermore, the centroid of triangle $DEF$ coincides with the centroid of triangle $ABC$. Compute the maximum possible area of triangle $DEF$.

Show the answer

$4\sqrt3+2\sqrt{21}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors and the cross product)

Let $\mathbf u=\overrightarrow{AD}$, $\mathbf v=\overrightarrow{BE}$, $\mathbf w=\overrightarrow{CF}$, so $|\mathbf u|=1$, $|\mathbf v|=2$, $|\mathbf w|=3$. The centroids are $\frac13(A+B+C)$ and $\frac13(D+E+F)$, so they coincide exactly when $\mathbf u+\mathbf v+\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$.

The arrows are parallel. Then $|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|=|\mathbf w|=3=|\mathbf u|+|\mathbf v|$, which is the equality case of the triangle inequality, so $\mathbf v$ points the same way as $\mathbf u$. Hence $\mathbf u=\mathbf e$, $\mathbf v=2\mathbf e$, $\mathbf w=-3\mathbf e$ for a unit vector $\mathbf e$, and every unit vector $\mathbf e$ gives a valid configuration.

Area. Write $\mathbf b=\overrightarrow{AB}$, $\mathbf c=\overrightarrow{AC}$ and let $\times$ be the planar cross product, with $ABC$ labelled counterclockwise so that $\mathbf b\times\mathbf c=2[ABC]=8\sqrt3$. Now $\overrightarrow{DE}=\mathbf b+\mathbf e$ and $\overrightarrow{DF}=\mathbf c-4\mathbf e$, so, as $\mathbf e\times\mathbf e=0$, \[\overrightarrow{DE}\times\overrightarrow{DF}=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c-4\,\mathbf b\times\mathbf e+\mathbf e\times\mathbf c=8\sqrt3-\mathbf m\times\mathbf e,\qquad \mathbf m=4\mathbf b+\mathbf c.\] As $\mathbf e$ runs over all unit vectors, $\mathbf m\times\mathbf e$ takes every value in $[-|\mathbf m|,|\mathbf m|]$. Using $|\mathbf b|=|\mathbf c|=4$ and $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c=16\cos60^\circ=8$, \[|\mathbf m|^2=16\cdot16+16+8\cdot8=336,\qquad |\mathbf m|=4\sqrt{21}.\] So $2[DEF]=\left|8\sqrt3-\mathbf m\times\mathbf e\right|$ is at most $8\sqrt3+4\sqrt{21}$, with equality when $\mathbf m\times\mathbf e=-4\sqrt{21}$. The maximum area is $\boxed{4\sqrt3+2\sqrt{21}}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates and one angle)

Place $A=(0,0)$, $B=(4,0)$, $C=(2,2\sqrt3)$, and let $\mathbf u=D-A$, $\mathbf v=E-B$, $\mathbf w=F-C$, of lengths $1,2,3$. The centroid condition $\frac{A+B+C}{3}=\frac{D+E+F}{3}$ means $\mathbf u+\mathbf v+\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$. Then $|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|=3=|\mathbf u|+|\mathbf v|$, which forces $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ to point the same way, so for some angle $\theta$ \[\mathbf u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad\mathbf v=2\mathbf u,\qquad\mathbf w=-3\mathbf u,\] and every $\theta$ is allowed. Thus \[D=(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta),\qquad E=(4+2\cos\theta,\ 2\sin\theta),\qquad F=(2-3\cos\theta,\ 2\sqrt3-3\sin\theta).\] With $E-D=(4+\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)$ and $F-D=(2-4\cos\theta,\ 2\sqrt3-4\sin\theta)$, the shoelace formula gives \[2[DEF]=\left|(4+\cos\theta)(2\sqrt3-4\sin\theta)-\sin\theta\,(2-4\cos\theta)\right|=\left|8\sqrt3+2\sqrt3\cos\theta-18\sin\theta\right|,\] since the terms $\mp4\sin\theta\cos\theta$ cancel. The expression $2\sqrt3\cos\theta-18\sin\theta$ ranges over $[-R,R]$ with $R=\sqrt{12+324}=\sqrt{336}=4\sqrt{21}$. Hence $2[DEF]\le 8\sqrt3+4\sqrt{21}$, with equality for a suitable $\theta$, and the maximum area is $\boxed{4\sqrt3+2\sqrt{21}}$.
