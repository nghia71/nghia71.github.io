Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Let $x,y,z$ be positive real numbers such that $x^2+y^2+z^2=75$. Suppose that at least two of the three sums $x+y$, $y+z$ and $z+x$ are greater than or equal to $10$. Determine the minimum and maximum possible values of the remaining sum.

Show the answer

minimum $\tfrac{10}{3}$, maximum $10$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two direct estimates)

By symmetry let $x+y\ge 10$ and $y+z\ge 10$; we find the range of $x+z$.

Maximum. Since $xy+yz+zx\le x^2+y^2+z^2$ (equivalent to $\tfrac12\sum(x-y)^2\ge 0$), \[ (x+y)^2+(y+z)^2+(z+x)^2=2(x^2+y^2+z^2)+2(xy+yz+zx)\le 4(x^2+y^2+z^2)=300 . \] Hence $(x+z)^2\le 300-100-100=100$ and $x+z\le 10$, with equality for $x=y=z=5$ (then $x^2+y^2+z^2=75$ and all three sums are $10$).

Minimum. $y^2<75<100$, so $y<10$, and $x\ge 10-y>0$, $z\ge 10-y>0$. Therefore \[ 75-y^2=x^2+z^2\ge 2(10-y)^2\iff 3y^2-40y+125\le 0\iff (y-5)(3y-25)\le 0 , \] so $y\le \tfrac{25}{3}$ and \[ x+z\ge 2(10-y)\ge 2\Bigl(10-\tfrac{25}3\Bigr)=\tfrac{10}{3}. \] Equality holds for $x=z=\tfrac53$, $y=\tfrac{25}{3}$: indeed $\tfrac{25+625+25}{9}=75$ and $x+y=y+z=10$.

So the remaining sum has minimum $\boxed{\tfrac{10}{3}}$ and maximum $\boxed{10}$.

Solution 2 (A quadratic equation for the third sum)

Let $p=x+y\ge 10$, $q=y+z\ge 10$ and $s=z+x$. Then $x=\frac{p-q+s}2$, $y=\frac{p+q-s}2$, $z=\frac{q+s-p}2$, and expanding gives the identity \[ 4(x^2+y^2+z^2)=3(p^2+q^2+s^2)-2(pq+qs+sp). \] So $s$ is a root of $f(t)=3t^2-2(p+q)t+3p^2+3q^2-2pq-300$. Put $p=10+u$, $q=10+v$ with $u,v\ge 0$. A direct expansion gives \[ f(10)=2u^2+2v^2+(u-v)^2+20(u+v)\ge 0,\qquad f\!\left(\tfrac{10}3\right)=2u^2+2v^2+(u-v)^2+\tfrac{100}{3}(u+v)\ge 0 . \] The vertex of the parabola is at $t_0=\frac{p+q}3\ge\frac{20}3>\frac{10}3$, and $t_0<10$ because, by Cauchy–Schwarz, $p+q=x+2y+z\le\sqrt{1+4+1}\,\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}=\sqrt{450}<30$. On $(-\infty,t_0]$ the function $f$ is strictly decreasing, so for $t<\frac{10}3$ we get $f(t)>f(\frac{10}3)\ge 0$; similarly $f(t)>f(10)\ge 0$ for $t>10$. Hence every root of $f$, in particular $s$, lies in $[\frac{10}3,10]$.

Both ends occur: $(x,y,z)=(\frac53,\frac{25}3,\frac53)$ gives $s=\frac{10}3$ and $(5,5,5)$ gives $s=10$. The minimum is $\boxed{\tfrac{10}{3}}$ and the maximum is $\boxed{10}$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $T$ be the centroid of an acute triangle $ABC$. Let $P$ be a point on the minor arc $BC$ of the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Let $P'$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to line segment $BC$. Let $X$ be the intersection of line $P'T$ with the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$. Finally, let $S$ be the midpoint of segment $PX$. Prove that $\angle BAP=\angle SAC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Homothety at the centroid and a mirror point)

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$, $\ell$ the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, and $Q$ the reflection of $P$ in $\ell$.

$Q$ and the angle. $\ell$ passes through the circumcentre, so the reflection in $\ell$ maps the circumcircle to itself, swaps $B$ and $C$, and maps the arc $BP$ onto the arc $CQ$. Hence $Q$ lies on the minor arc $BC$ and the inscribed angles on these equal arcs are equal: $\angle BAP=\angle QAC$. It therefore suffices to prove that $S$ is the midpoint of $AQ$; then $S$ lies on segment $AQ$ and $\angle SAC=\angle QAC=\angle BAP$.

The vector $\overrightarrow{AX}$. The homothety $h$ with centre $T$ and ratio $-\frac12$ maps $A$ to $M$ (the centroid divides the median $AM$ in the ratio $2:1$). It maps the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$ to the line through $M$ parallel to $BC$, which is $BC$ itself. The point $X$ lies on that parallel and on line $P'T$, so $h(X)$ lies on $BC$ and on line $XT=P'T$; as $T\notin BC$, this forces $h(X)=P'$. Therefore $\overrightarrow{P'M}=h(X)h(A)=-\frac12\overrightarrow{XA}$, i.e. \[ \overrightarrow{AX}=2\,\overrightarrow{P'M}. \]

The vector $\overrightarrow{PQ}$. $PQ\parallel BC$, and the feet of $P$ and $Q$ on $BC$ are $P'$ and its mirror image in $M$, so $\overrightarrow{PQ}=2\,\overrightarrow{P'M}$.

Hence $\overrightarrow{AX}=\overrightarrow{PQ}$, so $A+Q=P+X$ (as position vectors): the segments $PX$ and $AQ$ have the same midpoint. Thus $S$ is the midpoint of $AQ$, and $\angle SAC=\angle QAC=\angle BAP$. (If $P$ is the midpoint of the arc, then $Q=P$ and the same argument applies.) $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Complex numbers on the circumcircle)

Let the circumcircle be the unit circle and let $a,b,c,p$ be the points $A,B,C,P$ ($|a|=|b|=|c|=|p|=1$). The foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $BC$ is \[ p'=\tfrac12\Bigl(b+c+p-\frac{bc}{p}\Bigr), \] and the centroid is $t=\frac{a+b+c}3$. Put $x=3t-2p'=a-p+\frac{bc}{p}$. Then $x-t=-2(p'-t)$, so $x$ lies on line $P'T$; and \[ \frac{x-a}{b-c}=\frac{bc-p^2}{p(b-c)} \] equals its own conjugate $\dfrac{p^2-bc}{p(c-b)}$ (use $\bar z=1/z$ on the unit circle), so it is real and $x$ lies on the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$. Line $P'T$ is not parallel to $BC$ ($T\notin BC$), so it meets that parallel only once: $x$ is the point $X$. Hence \[ s=\frac{p+x}2=\frac{a+q}2,\qquad q=\frac{bc}{p},\ |q|=1 . \]

Consider $w=\dfrac{(p-a)(s-a)}{(b-a)(c-a)}=\dfrac{(p-a)(q-a)}{2(b-a)(c-a)}$. Using $\bar z=1/z$, \[ \bar w=\frac{(\frac1p-\frac1a)(\frac1q-\frac1a)}{2(\frac1b-\frac1a)(\frac1c-\frac1a)}=\frac{(a-p)(a-q)}{2(a-b)(a-c)}\cdot\frac{bc}{pq}=w, \] because $pq=bc$. So $w$ is real. Its argument is $\arg\frac{p-a}{b-a}-\arg\frac{c-a}{s-a}$, the directed angle from $AB$ to $AP$ minus the directed angle from $AS$ to $AC$. Both rays $AP$ and $AS$ lie inside $\angle BAC$ ($P$ and the point $Q$ with affix $q$ are on the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, and $S$ is the midpoint of $AQ$), so both directed angles lie in $(0,\angle A)$ and their difference lies in $(-\pi,\pi)$. A real number with argument in $(-\pi,\pi)$ has argument $0$, so $\angle BAP=\angle SAC$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

We say that a group of people is triple if each member of the group is acquainted with exactly $3$ other members, and the group cannot be partitioned into two nonempty subsets such that every person has all their acquaintances within their own subset. The acquaintance relation is mutual. Determine the greatest integer $k\ge 3$ for which there exists a positive integer $n$ such that, from every triple group with at least $n$ people, one can select at least $k$ people and seat them around a round table so that every two adjacent people are acquainted.

Show the answer

$k=5$

Show the solution

Solution (A longest path, and bridges)

In graph language, a triple group is a finite connected graph in which every vertex has degree $3$ (a connected cubic graph), and seating at least $k$ people around the table means finding a cycle of length at least $k$. We show that the answer is $k=5$.

$k=5$ works (with $n=5$). We prove: every connected cubic graph $G$ other than $K_4$ has a cycle of length at least $5$. A triple group with at least $5$ people is not $K_4$, so this gives a seating of at least $5$ people.

Let $v_0v_1\dots v_L$ be a longest path in $G$. All neighbours of $v_0$ lie on the path (otherwise the path could be extended), so they are $v_1,v_i,v_j$ with $1<i<j$, and $v_0v_1\dots v_jv_0$ is a cycle of length $j+1$. If $j\ge 4$ we are done. Otherwise $i=2$, $j=3$: $v_0$ is adjacent to $v_1,v_2,v_3$.

Then $v_1v_2v_0v_3v_4\dots v_L$ is also a path with $L+1$ vertices, hence a longest path, so all neighbours of its end $v_1$ lie on it. These are $v_0$, $v_2$ and a third vertex $v_t$ with $t\ge 3$. If $t\ge 4$, then $v_1v_2v_0v_3v_4\dots v_tv_1$ is a cycle with $t+1\ge 5$ vertices. If $t=3$, then $v_0,v_1,v_2$ are pairwise adjacent and all adjacent to $v_3$; this uses all three edges of each of $v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3$, so no edge leaves $\{v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3\}$, and by connectedness $G=K_4$. This proves the claim.

$k=6$ fails for every $n$. Let the gadget be $K_4$ on $a,b,c,d$ with the edge $ab$ replaced by a path $a\,w\,b$: in it $w$ has degree $2$ and $a,b,c,d$ have degree $3$; it has $5$ vertices. Take a path $u_1u_2\dots u_t$ ($t\ge2$), and join $u_1$ and $u_t$ to two new gadgets each and every $u_i$ with $1<i<t$ to one new gadget, always by an edge from $u_i$ to the vertex $w$ of the gadget (see the figure). The result is a connected graph with $6t+10$ vertices in which every vertex has degree $3$, i.e. a triple group.

Every edge outside the gadgets is a bridge (removing it disconnects the graph), so it lies on no cycle. Hence every cycle lies inside a single gadget and has at most $5$ vertices. As $t$ is arbitrary, for every $n$ there is a triple group with at least $n$ people in which no $6$ people can be seated as required. So no $k\ge 6$ has the property, and the greatest $k$ is \[ \boxed{k=5}. \]

Remark (another construction, the one in the official solution, in our words). Instead of hanging gadgets on a spine, build a chain of cells joined by single bridge edges. The two end cells are copies of the gadget above (its vertex $w$ takes the bridge). Each middle cell is $K_4$ minus one edge: its two vertices of degree $2$ take the bridges to the previous and the next cell. With $m\ge0$ middle cells the graph is cubic and connected with $4m+10$ vertices, every cycle stays inside one cell, and so it has at most $5$ vertices.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Let $a$ and $b$ be distinct positive integers such that the numbers $a^2+1$ and $ab+1$ have the same sets of prime divisors. Prove that $a^2+b^2+2$ is divisible by the square of some prime.

Show the solution

Solution (Primes of $a^2+1$ divide $a-b$; then a size argument)

Let $N=a^2+b^2+2=(a^2+1)+(b^2+1)$.

Step 1. Let $p$ be a prime divisor of $a^2+1$ ($p=2$ is allowed). By hypothesis $p\mid ab+1$, and $p\nmid a$ (since $p\mid a^2+1$). From $a(a-b)=(a^2+1)-(ab+1)\equiv 0\pmod p$ we get $p\mid a-b$. Then $b\equiv a$, so $p\mid b^2+1$ as well.

Step 2: a size argument. Let $R$ be the product of the distinct primes dividing $a^2+1$; $R>1$ because $a^2+1\ge 2$. By Step 1 each of these primes divides $a-b$, so $R\mid a-b$, and since $a\ne b$, $|a-b|\ge R$.

Suppose $ab+1$ were squarefree. As its prime divisors are exactly those of $a^2+1$, we would have $ab+1=R\le|a-b|$. But $|a-b|<ab+1$: if $a\ge b$ then $a-b\le a-1<ab+1$, and if $b>a$ then $b-a<b\le ab<ab+1$. This contradiction shows that $p^2\mid ab+1$ for some prime $p$, and $p\mid a^2+1$ (same primes). By Step 1, $p\mid a-b$, so $p^2\mid (a-b)^2$. Finally \[ N=a^2+b^2+2=(a-b)^2+2(ab+1) \] is divisible by $p^2$, the square of a prime. $\blacksquare$

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $\mathcal{P}=(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{2026})$ be an arbitrary permutation of the numbers $1,2,\ldots,2026$. We say that an index $i\in\{1,2,\ldots,2026\}$ is good if each of the following numbers is nonnegative: \[\begin{array}{l} a_i\\ a_i-a_{i+1}\\ a_i-a_{i+1}+a_{i+2}\\ a_i-a_{i+1}+a_{i+2}-a_{i+3}\\ \quad\vdots\\ a_i-a_{i+1}+\cdots-a_{i+2023}+a_{i+2024}\\ a_i-a_{i+1}+\cdots-a_{i+2023}+a_{i+2024}-a_{i+2025} \end{array}\] where indices are taken modulo $2026$, that is, $a_{j+2026}=a_j$ for all integers $j$. Let $n(\mathcal{P})$ denote the number of good indices of the permutation $\mathcal{P}$. Determine the minimum and maximum possible values of $n(\mathcal{P})$.

Show the answer

minimum $1$, maximum $1013$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Prefix sums and the position of $2026$)

Indices are taken modulo $2026$. For an index $i$ and $0\le k\le 2025$ let \[ A_i(k)=a_i-a_{i+1}+a_{i+2}-\dots+(-1)^k a_{i+k}; \] $i$ is good iff $A_i(k)\ge 0$ for all $k$. Splitting off the first term gives \[ A_i(k+1)=a_i-A_{i+1}(k)\qquad(0\le k\le 2024). \tag{1} \]

At least one good index. Let $c_j=(-1)^ja_j$ (well defined modulo $2026$, which is even). Then $A_i(k)=(-1)^i(c_i+c_{i+1}+\dots+c_{i+k})$. Let $Q_0=0$, $Q_m=c_1+\dots+c_m$ for $m\ge1$; then $Q_{m+2026}=Q_m+T$ with $T=Q_{2026}$. Suppose $T\ge 0$ and choose $j\in\{0,\dots,2025\}$ with $Q_j$ minimal among $Q_0,\dots,Q_{2025}$; let $i=j+1$. For $0\le k\le 2025$, \[ c_i+\dots+c_{i+k}=Q_{j+k+1}-Q_j\ge 0, \] since either $j+k+1\le 2025$ (minimality) or $Q_{j+k+1}=Q_{j+k+1-2026}+T\ge Q_j$. For $k=0$ this gives $c_i\ge0$, so $c_i>0$, $i$ is even and $A_i(k)=c_i+\dots+c_{i+k}\ge 0$ for all $k$: $i$ is good. If $T<0$, take $Q_j$ maximal instead; the same argument gives $c_i+\dots+c_{i+k}\le 0$ for all $k$, $c_i<0$, $i$ odd, and again $A_i(k)\ge 0$. So $n(\mathcal P)\ge 1$.

For the identity permutation $(1,2,\dots,2026)$ a good index needs $A_i(1)=a_i-a_{i+1}\ge 0$, which holds only for $i=2026$. Since some index is good, exactly one is: the minimum is $\boxed{1}$.

At most $1013$ good indices. If $i$ and $i+1$ are both good, then $a_i=2026$. Indeed, by (1), $A_i(k+1)=a_i-A_{i+1}(k)\le a_i$, and $A_i(0)=a_i$; so all numbers $A_i(0),\dots,A_i(2025)$ lie in $[0,a_i]$. For $1\le k\le 2025$, $a_{i+k}=|A_i(k)-A_i(k-1)|\le a_i$. So $a_i$ is the largest number, $2026$.

Let $i_0$ be the position of $2026$. Split the cycle of indices into the $1013$ pairs $\{i_0+2r-1,\,i_0+2r\}$, $r=1,\dots,1013$, of cyclically consecutive indices. A pair contains two good indices only if its first index carries $2026$, i.e. equals $i_0$; but $i_0+2r-1\not\equiv i_0$. So each pair contains at most one good index, and $n(\mathcal P)\le 1013$.

$1013$ is attained by $(2026,1,2025,2,\dots,1014,1013)$, i.e. $a_{2r-1}=2027-r$, $a_{2r}=r$. Starting from an odd index, the alternating sum splits into the brackets $(a_{2r-1}-a_{2r})=2027-2r\ge 1$ (the brackets stay aligned after wrapping around, since $2026$ is even). After an even number of terms the partial sum is a sum of positive brackets; after an odd number it is such a sum plus a positive term. So all $1013$ odd indices are good, and the maximum is $\boxed{1013}$.

Solution 2 (Parity of the full alternating sum)

Indices are taken modulo $2026$. For an index $i$ and $0\le k\le 2025$ let $A_i(k)=a_i-a_{i+1}+\dots+(-1)^ka_{i+k}$, so $i$ is good iff $A_i(k)\ge0$ for all $k$, and let $F_i=A_i(2025)$ be the full alternating sum starting at $i$.

Two facts about $F_i$. Every number $a_j$ occurs in $F_i$ exactly once, with sign $\pm$, so \[ F_i\equiv a_1+a_2+\dots+a_{2026}=\frac{2026\cdot 2027}{2}=1013\cdot 2027\equiv 1\pmod 2 , \] and $F_i\ne 0$. Next, $F_{i+1}=-F_i$: the full sum starting at $i+1$ uses the same $2026$ numbers, and since $2026$ is even, every number $a_j$ gets the opposite sign (its distance from the start changes by one). Hence $F_i>0$ for all indices $i$ of one parity and $F_i<0$ for all indices of the other parity.

At most $1013$ good indices. A good index has $F_i\ge 0$, so all good indices have the same parity, and there are at most $1013$ of them.

At least one good index. Choose $s$ with $F_s>0$ and group the terms in pairs starting from $s$: let $b_r=a_{s+2r}-a_{s+2r+1}$ for $r=0,1,\dots,1012$ (indices of $b$ modulo $1013$; the pairs stay aligned after wrapping around, since $2026$ is even). Then $b_0+b_1+\dots+b_{1012}=F_s>0$. Let $P_0=0$ and $P_m=b_0+\dots+b_{m-1}$, and choose $j\in\{0,\dots,1012\}$ with $P_j$ minimal among $P_0,\dots,P_{1012}$. We claim that $i=s+2j$ is good. A sum of $m$ consecutive brackets starting with $b_j$ ($0\le m\le 1013$) equals $P_{j+m}-P_j\ge0$ if $j+m\le 1012$, and $F_s+P_{j+m-1013}-P_j>0$ otherwise. So every partial sum $A_i(k)$ with $k$ odd (a whole number of brackets) is $\ge0$, and every $A_i(k)$ with $k$ even is such a sum plus one more positive term $a_{i+k}$, hence positive. So $i$ is good and $n(\mathcal P)\ge 1$.

Examples. For $(2026,1,2,\dots,2025)$ every index $i\ne1$ has $A_i(1)=a_i-a_{i+1}<0$ ($i-1<i$ for $2\le i\le 2025$, and $2025<2026$ for $i=2026$), so exactly one index is good. For $(2026,2025,\dots,1)$, i.e. $a_j=2027-j$, start at an odd index: the terms split into brackets $a_{2r-1}-a_{2r}=1$ (aligned after wrapping, as $2026$ is even), so the partial sums are $\ge0$ after an even number of terms and positive after an odd number. All $1013$ odd indices are good.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{1}$ and the maximum is $\boxed{1013}$.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $ABCDEF$ be a hexagon inscribed in a circle with center $O$, such that each pair of opposite sides is parallel. The lines $AB$, $CD$ and $EF$ form a triangle $\Delta_1$, and the lines $BC$, $DE$ and $FA$ form a triangle $\Delta_2$. Prove that the circumcenters of triangles $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ are symmetric with respect to point $O$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Projections onto the parallel sides and two parallelograms)

Let $O_1$, $O_2$ be the circumcentres of $\Delta_1$ (lines $AB,CD,EF$) and $\Delta_2$ (lines $BC,DE,FA$). Name the intersection points \[ R_1=AB\cap CD,\quad Q_1=AB\cap EF,\quad Q_2=DE\cap BC,\quad R_2=DE\cap FA , \] so that $Q_1R_1$ is the side of $\Delta_1$ on line $AB$ and $Q_2R_2$ is the side of $\Delta_2$ on line $DE$.

Let $v$ be a unit vector along $AB$ and $\pi(X)=X\cdot v$ (a coordinate along $v$). Since $DE\parallel AB$, the orthogonal projection of a point onto line $AB$ or onto line $DE$ has the same $\pi$-value as the point itself.

  • The circumcentre of a triangle projects onto each side line at the midpoint of that side, so $\pi(O_1)=\frac12\bigl(\pi(Q_1)+\pi(R_1)\bigr)$ and $\pi(O_2)=\frac12\bigl(\pi(Q_2)+\pi(R_2)\bigr)$.

  • $O$ projects onto the chords $AB$ and $DE$ at their midpoints: $2\pi(O)=\pi(A)+\pi(B)=\pi(D)+\pi(E)$.

  • The lines $AB\parallel DE$ and $CD\parallel FA$ form the parallelogram $AR_1DR_2$ (vertices $FA\cap AB$, $AB\cap CD$, $CD\cap DE$, $DE\cap FA$), so $\overrightarrow{AR_1}=\overrightarrow{R_2D}$ and $\pi(R_1)-\pi(A)=\pi(D)-\pi(R_2)$.

  • The lines $AB\parallel DE$ and $BC\parallel EF$ form the parallelogram $Q_1BQ_2E$, so $\overrightarrow{Q_1B}=\overrightarrow{EQ_2}$ and $\pi(B)-\pi(Q_1)=\pi(Q_2)-\pi(E)$.

Therefore \[ 2\pi(O_1)+2\pi(O_2)-4\pi(O)=\bigl[\pi(R_1)-\pi(A)+\pi(R_2)-\pi(D)\bigr]+\bigl[\pi(Q_1)-\pi(B)+\pi(Q_2)-\pi(E)\bigr]=0, \] that is, $(O_1+O_2-2O)\cdot v=0$.

The hexagon $CDEFAB$ satisfies the same hypotheses and gives the same two triangles, so the same argument with the pair of parallel sides $CD\parallel FA$ gives $(O_1+O_2-2O)\cdot w=0$ for a unit vector $w$ along $CD$. The vectors $v,w$ are not parallel (otherwise $AB\parallel CD$ and $\Delta_1$ would not exist), so $O_1+O_2=2O$: the circumcentres are symmetric with respect to $O$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Distances from the centre and the angles of the triangles)

Take $O$ as the origin and radius $1$. Let the arcs $AB,BC,CD,DE,EF,FA$ (each not containing other vertices) measure $2\theta_1,\dots,2\theta_6$, so $\theta_1+\dots+\theta_6=180^\circ$.

Arcs. Two parallel chords cut off equal arcs between them, so $AB\parallel DE$, $BC\parallel EF$, $CD\parallel FA$ give $\theta_2+\theta_3=\theta_5+\theta_6$, $\theta_3+\theta_4=\theta_6+\theta_1$, $\theta_4+\theta_5=\theta_1+\theta_2$. From the first and third equations, $\theta_1-\theta_4=\theta_3-\theta_6=\theta_5-\theta_2=:\sigma$.

Lines. Let $u_1$ be the unit vector from $O$ to the midpoint of arc $AB$. The chord $AB$ is the line $X\cdot u_1=\cos\theta_1$. The midpoints of the arcs $AB$ and $DE$ are the two ends of the diameter perpendicular to these parallel chords (the arcs are disjoint), so $DE$ is the line $X\cdot(-u_1)=\cos\theta_4$. Similarly with $u_2$ for $CD$ ($\cos\theta_3$) and $FA$ ($-u_2$, $\cos\theta_6$), and $u_3$ for $EF$ ($\cos\theta_5$) and $BC$ ($-u_3$, $\cos\theta_2$). The convex hexagon lies on the side $X\cdot u\le h$ of each of these six lines. The arc midpoints occur around the circle in the order $u_1,-u_3,u_2,-u_1,u_3,-u_2$, so each of the three arcs between consecutive vectors $u_i$ lies strictly inside a half-circle from some $u_i$ to $-u_i$; hence no closed half-plane through $O$ contains $u_1,u_2,u_3$ (nor $-u_1,-u_2,-u_3$). Therefore the intersection of the three half-planes $X\cdot u_i\le h_i$ is bounded, and it is the triangle $\Delta_1$, with outward unit normals $u_1,u_2,u_3$; likewise $\Delta_2$ has outward unit normals $-u_1,-u_2,-u_3$. The sides of $\Delta_2$ are parallel to those of $\Delta_1$, so the two triangles have equal angles; let $\alpha_i$ be the angle opposite the side with normal $\pm u_i$.

Circumcentres. The signed distance from the circumcentre of a triangle to a side, measured towards the interior, is $R\cos$ of the opposite angle. Hence, with $R_1,R_2$ the circumradii, \[ O_1\cdot u_i=h_i-R_1\cos\alpha_i,\qquad O_2\cdot(-u_i)=k_i-R_2\cos\alpha_i, \] where $(h_1,h_2,h_3)=(\cos\theta_1,\cos\theta_3,\cos\theta_5)$ and $(k_1,k_2,k_3)=(\cos\theta_4,\cos\theta_6,\cos\theta_2)$. So \[ (O_1+O_2)\cdot u_i=(h_i-k_i)-(R_1-R_2)\cos\alpha_i. \tag{1} \]

The angles. The vertex of $\Delta_1$ opposite $AB$ is $CD\cap EF$; it forms a triangle with $D$ and $E$ whose angles at $D$, $E$ are the exterior angles of the hexagon there, $\theta_3+\theta_4$ and $\theta_4+\theta_5$ (an inscribed angle of the hexagon at $D$ is $180^\circ-\theta_3-\theta_4$). With $\theta_3=\theta_6+\sigma$, $\theta_5=\theta_2+\sigma$ and $2(\theta_2+\theta_4+\theta_6)+3\sigma=180^\circ$, \[ \alpha_1=180^\circ-(\theta_3+2\theta_4+\theta_5)=90^\circ-\theta_4-\tfrac{\sigma}{2}=90^\circ-\tfrac{\theta_1+\theta_4}{2}, \] and $h_1-k_1=\cos\theta_1-\cos\theta_4=-2\sin\frac{\theta_1+\theta_4}2\sin\frac{\sigma}{2}=\lambda\cos\alpha_1$ with $\lambda=-2\sin\frac{\sigma}2$. The same computation for the other pairs (cyclic relabelling) gives $h_i-k_i=\lambda\cos\alpha_i$ for $i=1,2,3$, with the same $\lambda$. By (1), $(O_1+O_2)\cdot u_i=\mu\cos\alpha_i$ with $\mu=\lambda-(R_1-R_2)$.

Conclusion. The sides of $\Delta_1$ have lengths $2R_1\sin\alpha_i$; rotating the closed polygon of side vectors by $90^\circ$ gives $\sum_i \sin\alpha_i\,u_i=0$. Hence \[ 0=\sum_i\sin\alpha_i\,(O_1+O_2)\cdot u_i=\mu\sum_i\sin\alpha_i\cos\alpha_i=\tfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_i\sin 2\alpha_i =2\mu\sin\alpha_1\sin\alpha_2\sin\alpha_3 , \] so $\mu=0$. Then $(O_1+O_2)\cdot u_1=(O_1+O_2)\cdot u_2=0$ with $u_1,u_2$ independent, so $O_1+O_2=0=2O$. $\blacksquare$
