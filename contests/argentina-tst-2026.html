Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Each face of a $2\times2\times2$ cube is divided into $4$ square cells (so the cube has $24$ cells in total). We define a ring as a sequence of $8$ cells such that each face of the cube contains either $0$ or $2$ of these cells, and each of the $8$ cells shares a side with the previous one and a side with the next one, forming a closed loop around the cube. (So a ring runs straight around the cube: its $8$ cells lie in one layer of unit thickness, on the four faces parallel to one edge direction.)

Ana and Beto take turns writing a distinct integer from $1$ to $24$ inclusive in an unused cell of the cube. Ana moves first. After all $24$ numbers have been written, all possible sums of the $8$ numbers in each ring are computed (there are $6$ rings). If all $6$ sums are equal, Beto wins; otherwise, Ana wins. Determine who has a winning strategy, and describe it.

Show the answer

Beto wins

Show the solution

Solution (Pairing cells on opposite faces)

Beto has a winning strategy.

Read literally, the local description of a ring is also met by some bent loops, but the statement says there are $6$ rings: these are the six belts described below, and this is the reading we use.

Place the cube as $[0,2]^3$. A cell lies on a face perpendicular to one coordinate axis, and it is a unit square described by the unit intervals it occupies in the other two coordinates. The six rings are the “belts” of the cube: for an axis, say the $z$-axis, and a unit layer $k\le z\le k+1$ ($k\in\{0,1\}$), the ring consists of the $8$ cells of the four faces parallel to the $z$-axis that lie in this layer. Each cell belongs to exactly two rings, one for each axis parallel to its face, and these rings are determined by the two unit intervals of the cell.

Call two cells partners if they lie on opposite faces and occupy the same unit intervals in the other two coordinates (for example the cell $[0,1]\times[1,2]$ of the bottom face $z=0$ and the cell $[0,1]\times[1,2]$ of the top face $z=2$). The $24$ cells split into $12$ pairs of partners. Partners belong to exactly the same two rings, so each ring is the union of $4$ pairs of partners.

Beto's strategy. Whenever Ana writes a number $k$ in a cell, Beto writes $25-k$ in its partner.

This is always possible. Before each move of Ana the filled cells form complete pairs of partners and the used numbers form complete pairs $\{j,25-j\}$ (note $j\ne 25-j$ because $25$ is odd). Ana's cell is empty, so its partner is empty too, and her number $k$ is unused, so $25-k$ is unused too.

At the end every pair of partners carries two numbers with sum $25$, so every ring has sum $4\cdot 25=100$. All six sums are equal and Beto wins.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

Let $n\ge3$ be a positive integer and let $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n$ be real numbers greater than or equal to $0$ and less than or equal to $2$, not necessarily distinct, such that the sum of the $n$ numbers is equal to $5$: \[x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n=5.\] Prove that the sum of the squares of the $n$ numbers is less than or equal to $9$: \[(x_1)^2+(x_2)^2+\dots+(x_n)^2\le9,\] and determine all values of $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n$ for which equality holds.

Show the answer

$\sum x_i^2\le 9$, with equality exactly when two of the numbers are $2$, one is $1$ and the others are $0$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Pushing numbers to the ends of the interval)

Suppose two of the numbers, say $x_i\ge x_j$, lie strictly between $0$ and $2$. Let $t=\min(2-x_i,\,x_j)>0$ and replace $x_i,x_j$ by $x_i+t$ and $x_j-t$. The sum stays $5$, all numbers stay in $[0,2]$, and the sum of squares changes by \[ (x_i+t)^2+(x_j-t)^2-x_i^2-x_j^2=2t(x_i-x_j)+2t^2>0 . \] After the move one more number equals $0$ or $2$, so after at most $n$ such moves at most one number lies strictly between $0$ and $2$.

Now look at such a final tuple. If every number were $0$ or $2$, the sum would be even, not $5$. So exactly one number $r$ lies in $(0,2)$ and the others are $0$ or $2$; if $j$ of them equal $2$, then $2j+r=5$ with $0<r<2$, which forces $j=2$, $r=1$. The final tuple therefore consists of two $2$'s, one $1$ and zeros, and its sum of squares is $4+4+1=9$.

Every move increased the sum of squares, so the original sum of squares is at most $9$, and it is strictly less than $9$ if at least one move was made. Thus equality holds exactly when no move is possible, that is, when the numbers are two $2$'s, one $1$ and $n-3$ zeros (in any order). Such tuples exist because $n\ge3$, and they give $\sum x_i^2=9$.

Solution 2 (A parity bound for the distance to the endpoints)

Since $\sum x_i=5$, \[ \sum x_i^2=2\sum x_i-\sum x_i(2-x_i)=10-\sum x_i(2-x_i), \] so we must show $\sum x_i(2-x_i)\ge1$.

For $x\in[0,2]$ let $m(x)=\min(x,2-x)$, the distance from $x$ to the nearer of $0$ and $2$. Then $x(2-x)=m(x)\bigl(2-m(x)\bigr)\ge m(x)$, because $m(x)\le 1$; equality holds iff $m(x)\in\{0,1\}$, i.e. iff $x\in\{0,1,2\}$.

Let $e_i\in\{0,2\}$ be an endpoint nearest to $x_i$, so $|x_i-e_i|=m(x_i)$. The sum $\sum e_i$ is even and $5$ is odd, hence \[ 1\le\Bigl|5-\sum e_i\Bigr|=\Bigl|\sum (x_i-e_i)\Bigr|\le\sum m(x_i)\le \sum x_i(2-x_i). \] Therefore $\sum x_i^2\le 9$.

Equality requires $x_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ for all $i$ and $\sum m(x_i)=1$, so exactly one $x_i$ equals $1$ and the others are $0$ or $2$; as the sum is $5$, exactly two of them equal $2$. Conversely two $2$'s, one $1$ and $n-3$ zeros give $4+4+1=9$. So equality holds exactly for these tuples.

Solution 3 (Splitting at the third largest number)

Order the numbers so that $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_n$ and let $t=x_3$, so $0\le t\le 2$. For $i\ge3$ we have $0\le x_i\le t$, hence $x_i^2\le t\,x_i$, with equality iff $x_i\in\{0,t\}$. Since $x_3+\dots+x_n=5-x_1-x_2$, \[ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\le x_1^2+x_2^2+t(5-x_1-x_2)=5t+x_1(x_1-t)+x_2(x_2-t). \tag{1} \] Here $t\le x_2\le x_1\le 2$.

Case $t\le1$. For $t\le x<y$ we have $y(y-t)-x(x-t)=(y-x)(x+y-t)>0$, since $x+y-t\ge y>0$. So $x(x-t)$ is strictly increasing on $[t,2]$ and $x_k(x_k-t)\le 2(2-t)$ for $k=1,2$. By (1), \[ \sum x_i^2\le 5t+4(2-t)=8+t\le 9 . \] Equality forces $t=1$, $x_1=x_2=2$, and equality in $x_i^2\le t x_i$, i.e. $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ for $i\ge3$. Then $x_3+\dots+x_n=1$ with $x_3=1$, so $x_4=\dots=x_n=0$.

Case $t>1$. Put $u=2-x_1\ge0$ and $v=2-x_2\ge0$. From $x_1+x_2+x_3\le5$ we get $4-u-v+t\le5$, i.e. $u+v\ge t-1$. For $k=1,2$, \[ 2(2-t)-x_k(x_k-t)=(2-x_k)(2+x_k-t)\ge 2(2-x_k), \] because $x_k\ge t$. Hence $x_1(x_1-t)+x_2(x_2-t)\le 4(2-t)-2(u+v)\le 4(2-t)-2(t-1)$, and by (1) \[ \sum x_i^2\le 5t+8-4t-2(t-1)=10-t<9 . \] So equality is impossible in this case.

Therefore $\sum x_i^2\le 9$, and equality holds exactly when two of the numbers equal $2$, one equals $1$ and the remaining $n-3$ equal $0$; these tuples (which exist since $n\ge3$) indeed give $4+4+1=9$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $a$ and $b$ be integers, and consider the expression \[f(x)=x^2+ax+b.\] It is known that for every positive prime number $p$, there exists an integer $n$ such that both $f(n)$ and $f(n+1)$ are divisible by $p$. Prove that there exists an integer $m$ such that $f(m)=f(m+1)=0$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Completing the square)

Let $\Delta=a^2-4b$. Note that $4f(x)=(2x+a)^2-\Delta$ and $f(x+1)-f(x)=2x+a+1$.

Let $p$ be any prime and $n$ an integer with $p\mid f(n)$ and $p\mid f(n+1)$. Then $p\mid f(n+1)-f(n)=2n+a+1$, so $2n+a\equiv-1\pmod p$ and \[ 0\equiv 4f(n)=(2n+a)^2-\Delta\equiv 1-\Delta\pmod p . \] Thus every prime divides the integer $1-\Delta$, so $1-\Delta=0$, i.e. $a^2-4b=1$.

Then $a$ is odd, and the roots of $x^2+ax+b$ are $\frac{-a\pm1}{2}$, two consecutive integers. With $m=\frac{-a-1}{2}$ we get $f(x)=(x-m)(x-m-1)$, hence $f(m)=f(m+1)=0$.

Solution 2 (Two roots modulo every prime)

Fix a prime $p$ and an integer $n$ with $p\mid f(n)$ and $p\mid f(n+1)$. Dividing $f(x)$ by $x-n$ gives the identity $f(x)=(x-n)(x-n')+f(n)$ with $n'=-a-n$. Hence, modulo $p$, $f(x)\equiv(x-n)(x-n')$ for every integer $x$. Taking $x=n+1$: $0\equiv f(n+1)\equiv 1\cdot(n+1-n')$, so $n'\equiv n+1\pmod p$.

Comparing with the coefficients of $f$: $a=-(n+n')\equiv-(2n+1)$ and $b=f(0)=nn'+f(n)\equiv n(n+1)\pmod p$. Therefore \[ a^2-4b\equiv(2n+1)^2-4n(n+1)=1\pmod p . \] So the integer $a^2-4b-1$ is divisible by every prime, hence equals $0$.

Now $a^2=4b+1$ is odd, so $a$ is odd and $m=\frac{-a-1}{2}$ is an integer. Then $m+(m+1)=-a$ and $m(m+1)=\frac{a^2-1}{4}=b$, so by Vieta $f(x)=(x-m)(x-m-1)$ and $f(m)=f(m+1)=0$.

Solution 3 (Fixing the candidate root first)

Note that $f(x+1)-f(x)=2x+a+1$.

The prime $2$. Take $n$ with $2\mid f(n)$ and $2\mid f(n+1)$. Then $2$ divides $f(n+1)-f(n)=2n+a+1$, so $a$ is odd. Hence $m=\frac{-a-1}{2}$ is an integer, and $2m=-a-1$ gives \[ f(x+1)-f(x)=2x-2m=2(x-m). \tag{1} \]

Odd primes. Let $p$ be an odd prime and $n$ an integer with $p\mid f(n)$ and $p\mid f(n+1)$. By (1), $p\mid 2(n-m)$, and since $p$ is odd, $n\equiv m\pmod p$. As $f$ has integer coefficients, $f(m)\equiv f(n)\equiv0\pmod p$.

So the integer $f(m)$ is divisible by every odd prime; it has infinitely many prime divisors, so $f(m)=0$. Finally (1) with $x=m$ gives $f(m+1)=f(m)+0=0$. Thus $f(m)=f(m+1)=0$ for the integer $m=\frac{-a-1}{2}$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $n>4$ be an integer. Consider a set $S$ of $n$ distinct integers with the following property:

If all sums $x+y$ with $x\ne y$ in $S$ are computed, exactly $2n-3$ distinct values are obtained.

Prove that, when the elements of $S$ are arranged in increasing order, they form an arithmetic progression.

Note: An arithmetic progression is a sequence of numbers in which each term is obtained from the previous one by adding a fixed constant. For example, $(3,6,9,12)$ is an arithmetic progression of $4$ terms.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Many increasing chains of sums)

Write $S=\{a_1<a_2<\dots<a_n\}$ and let $\Sigma$ be the set of sums $a_i+a_j$ with $i<j$, so $|\Sigma|=2n-3$.

For $3\le k\le n$ consider the chain \[ a_1+a_2<a_1+a_3<\dots<a_1+a_k<a_2+a_k<a_2+a_{k+1}<\dots<a_2+a_n<a_3+a_n<\dots<a_{n-1}+a_n . \] It is strictly increasing and has $(k-1)+(n-k+1)+(n-3)=2n-3$ terms, all in $\Sigma$. Since $|\Sigma|=2n-3$, each such chain lists all of $\Sigma$ in increasing order; in particular all these chains coincide term by term.

Let $3\le k\le n-1$. The $k$-th term of the chain for $k$ is $a_2+a_k$, and the $k$-th term of the chain for $k+1$ is $a_1+a_{k+1}$. Hence $a_2+a_k=a_1+a_{k+1}$, that is, \[ a_{k+1}-a_k=a_2-a_1\qquad(3\le k\le n-1). \tag{1} \] The set $-S=\{-a_n<\dots<-a_1\}$ also has exactly $2n-3$ pairwise sums. Applying (1) to it (its $i$-th smallest element is $-a_{n+1-i}$) gives $a_{n+1-k}-a_{n-k}=a_n-a_{n-1}$ for $3\le k\le n-1$. Taking $k=n-2$, which is allowed because $n\ge5$, we get $a_3-a_2=a_n-a_{n-1}$.

Let $d=a_2-a_1$. By (1), $a_4-a_3=\dots=a_n-a_{n-1}=d$, and then $a_3-a_2=a_n-a_{n-1}=d$. So all consecutive differences equal $d$, and $S$ in increasing order is an arithmetic progression.

Solution 2 (Induction by removing the largest element)

For any set $T=\{t_1<\dots<t_m\}$ of integers ($m\ge2$), the chain $t_1+t_2<t_1+t_3<\dots<t_1+t_m<t_2+t_m<\dots<t_{m-1}+t_m$ shows that $T$ has at least $2m-3$ distinct pairwise sums. We prove by induction on $n\ge5$ that a set $S=\{a_1<\dots<a_n\}$ with exactly $2n-3$ pairwise sums is an arithmetic progression.

Base case $n=5$. The two chains \[ a_1+a_2<a_1+a_3<a_1+a_4<a_1+a_5<a_2+a_5<a_3+a_5<a_4+a_5, \] \[ a_1+a_2<a_1+a_3<a_2+a_3<a_2+a_4<a_3+a_4<a_3+a_5<a_4+a_5 \] both have $7=2\cdot5-3$ terms, so both list all the sums in increasing order and agree term by term. The 3rd, 4th and 5th terms give $a_2+a_3=a_1+a_4$, $a_2+a_4=a_1+a_5$ and $a_3+a_4=a_2+a_5$. With $d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i$ these say $d_1=d_3$ (first), $d_3=d_4$ (second minus first) and $d_2=d_4$ (third). Hence $d_1=d_2=d_3=d_4$.

Inductive step. Let $n\ge6$ and $S'=S\setminus\{a_n\}$. Every pairwise sum of $S'$ is at most $a_{n-2}+a_{n-1}<a_{n-2}+a_n<a_{n-1}+a_n$, so the sums of $S$ include the sums of $S'$ and two more values. Hence $S'$ has at most $2n-5=2(n-1)-3$ sums, and therefore exactly that many. By the induction hypothesis $S'$ is an arithmetic progression: $a_i=a_1+(i-1)d$ for $i\le n-1$, and its sums are $2a_1+jd$ for $1\le j\le 2n-5$ (here $j=(i-1)+(k-1)$ for $1\le i<k\le n-1$).

Since $S$ has only two sums besides those of $S'$, namely $a_{n-2}+a_n$ and $a_{n-1}+a_n$, the smaller sum $a_{n-3}+a_n$ is a sum of $S'$: $a_{n-3}+a_n=2a_1+jd$ with $j\le 2n-5$. With $a_{n-3}=a_1+(n-4)d$ this gives $a_n=a_1+(j-n+4)d\le a_1+(n-1)d$. As $a_n>a_{n-1}=a_1+(n-2)d$ and $a_n-a_1$ is a multiple of $d$, we get $a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$. So $S$ is an arithmetic progression, which completes the induction.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle and let $S$ be a point on side $AB$. Let $P$ be the center of the circle passing through $A,S,C$ and let $Q$ be the center of the circle passing through $B,S,C$.

Determine the position of point $S$ on side $AB$ for which the area of triangle $PQS$ is minimized.

Show the answer

$S$ is the foot of the altitude from $C$ (the minimum area is $\frac14[ABC]$)

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Triangle $PQS$ is similar to triangle $ABC$)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles of triangle $ABC$, let $H$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$ and $h=CH$. We take $S$ strictly between $A$ and $B$ (for $S=A$ or $S=B$ one of the circles is not defined).

Both $P$ and $Q$ are equidistant from $S$ and $C$, so they lie on the perpendicular bisector of $SC$; let $M$ be the midpoint of $SC$. Since $\angle SAC=\alpha<90^\circ$, the centre $P$ lies on the same side of line $SC$ as $A$ (an inscribed angle on the chord $SC$ is acute exactly when its vertex and the centre are on the same side of the chord), and the central angle is $\angle SPC=2\angle SAC=2\alpha$. As $PS=PC$, the line $PM$ bisects this angle, so $\angle SPM=\alpha$. In the same way $Q$ lies on the same side of $SC$ as $B$ and $\angle SQM=\beta$. Because $S$ lies between $A$ and $B$, the points $A$ and $B$, hence $P$ and $Q$, are on opposite sides of line $SC$, so $M$ lies on segment $PQ$. Therefore triangle $PQS$ has $\angle SPQ=\alpha$, $\angle SQP=\beta$ and $\angle PSQ=\gamma$: it is similar to triangle $ABC$, with $P,Q,S$ corresponding to $A,B,C$.

The ratio of similarity is $\frac{PS}{AC}$. By the law of sines in triangle $ASC$, $PS=\frac{SC}{2\sin\alpha}$, and $AC\sin\alpha=h$. So the ratio is $\frac{SC}{2h}$ and \[ [PQS]=\frac{SC^2}{4h^2}\,[ABC]. \] This is smallest exactly when $SC$ is smallest, i.e. when $S=H$; since the triangle is acute, $H$ lies on side $AB$. The minimum area is $\frac14[ABC]$, attained only when $S$ is the foot of the altitude from $C$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Since triangle $ABC$ is acute, the foot $H$ of the altitude from $C$ lies strictly between $A$ and $B$. Take $H$ as the origin and $AB$ as the $x$-axis: $A=(-p,0)$, $B=(q,0)$, $C=(0,h)$ with $p,q,h>0$, and $S=(s,0)$ with $-p<s<q$.

The centre $P$ of circle $ASC$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AS$, so $x_P=\frac{s-p}{2}$. Being equidistant from $S$ and $C$, it satisfies $(x-s)^2+y^2=x^2+(y-h)^2$, i.e. $y=\frac{h^2-s^2+2sx}{2h}$; hence $y_P=\frac{h^2-sp}{2h}$. In the same way the centre of circle $BSC$ is $Q=\bigl(\frac{s+q}{2},\frac{h^2+sq}{2h}\bigr)$.

With $\overrightarrow{SP}=\bigl(-\frac{s+p}{2},\,y_P\bigr)$ and $\overrightarrow{SQ}=\bigl(\frac{q-s}{2},\,y_Q\bigr)$, \[ [PQS]=\frac12\left|-\frac{s+p}{2}\cdot\frac{h^2+sq}{2h}-\frac{q-s}{2}\cdot\frac{h^2-sp}{2h}\right| =\frac{(s+p)(h^2+sq)+(q-s)(h^2-sp)}{8h}=\frac{(p+q)(h^2+s^2)}{8h}, \] because the terms $\pm sh^2$ and $\pm spq$ cancel. For fixed $p,q,h$ this is smallest exactly when $s=0$. So the area is minimal when $S$ is the foot of the altitude from $C$, and the minimum is $\frac{(p+q)h}{8}=\frac14[ABC]$.

Solution 3 (Projecting $PQ$ onto $AB$)

Take $S$ strictly between $A$ and $B$, let $H$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$ and $h=CH>0$.

Both $P$ and $Q$ are equidistant from $S$ and $C$, so the line $PQ$ is the perpendicular bisector of $SC$ (we check below that $P\ne Q$). In particular the distance from $S$ to the line $PQ$ is $\frac{SC}{2}$, and \[ [PQS]=\frac12\cdot PQ\cdot\frac{SC}{2}=\frac{PQ\cdot SC}{4}. \]

Now compute $PQ$ by projecting onto line $AB$. The centre $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AS$, so its orthogonal projection onto $AB$ is the midpoint of $AS$; likewise the projection of $Q$ is the midpoint of $SB$. These midpoints are $\frac{AS}{2}+\frac{SB}{2}=\frac{AB}{2}$ apart (so $P\ne Q$). On the other hand, let $\theta$ be the angle between the lines $SC$ and $AB$, so that $\sin\theta=\frac{CH}{SC}=\frac{h}{SC}$ (with $\theta=90^\circ$ when $S=H$). The line $PQ$ is perpendicular to $SC$, so it makes the angle $90^\circ-\theta$ with $AB$, and the projection of the segment $PQ$ onto $AB$ has length $PQ\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=PQ\cdot\frac{h}{SC}$. Hence \[ PQ\cdot\frac{h}{SC}=\frac{AB}{2},\qquad PQ=\frac{AB\cdot SC}{2h},\qquad [PQS]=\frac{AB\cdot SC^2}{8h}. \] With $AB$ and $h$ fixed, the area is smallest exactly when $SC$ is smallest, i.e. when $S=H$, which lies on side $AB$ because the triangle is acute. So the area is minimal when $S$ is the foot of the altitude from $C$, and the minimum is $\frac{AB\cdot h}{8}=\frac14[ABC]$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $1000$ numbers equal to $2$ and $2000$ numbers equal to $-1$ be arranged around a circle in some order. Bruno counts the number of groups of two or more consecutive numbers whose sum is equal to $0$.

  • (a) Determine the minimum possible value that Bruno can obtain.

  • (b) Determine the maximum possible value that Bruno can obtain.

Note: Each number on the circle may belong to one or more groups, or to none. (So a group is a block of consecutive numbers that is not the whole circle.)

Show the answer

(a) $1998$; (b) $2997000$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Prefix sums and repeated values)

Number the positions $1,2,\dots,3000$ clockwise, let $v_i\in\{2,-1\}$ be the number at position $i$, and put $s_0=0$, $s_i=v_1+\dots+v_i$. The total is $1000\cdot2-2000=0$, so $s_{3000}=0=s_0$.

Reformulation. For distinct $i,j\in\{0,1,\dots,2999\}$ let $g(i,j)$ be the group of consecutive positions $i+1,i+2,\dots,j$ going clockwise (indices modulo $3000$). Every group of length between $1$ and $2999$ is $g(i,j)$ for exactly one ordered pair $(i,j)$, and its sum is $s_j-s_i$ (if it passes position $3000$, the sum is $s_{3000}-s_i+s_j=s_j-s_i$). By the statement the whole circle is not a group, so the groups are exactly the $g(i,j)$ of length at least $2$, and groups of length $1$ never have sum $0$ anyway. Let $c_v$ be the number of indices $i\in\{0,\dots,2999\}$ with $s_i=v$. Bruno's number is \[ N=\#\{(i,j):\ i\ne j,\ s_i=s_j\}=\sum_v c_v(c_v-1),\qquad \sum_v c_v=3000 . \]

(a) Minimum. Let $s$ attain its minimum $\mu$ at index $i_0$ and its maximum $M$ at $i_1$. Walking clockwise from $i_0$ to $i_1$, the value of $s$ increases by at most $2$ for each $2$ passed, so $M-\mu\le 2\cdot1000$. Hence at most $2001$ values $v$ have $c_v\ge1$. Using $c(c-1)\ge2(c-1)$ for $c\ge1$, \[ \sum_v c_v(c_v-1)\ge 2\sum_{c_v\ge1}(c_v-1)\ge 2(3000-2001)=1998, \] so $N\ge1998$. Equality occurs when the $1000$ twos are consecutive: then $s_0,\dots,s_{1000}$ are $0,2,4,\dots,2000$ and $s_{1001},\dots,s_{2999}$ are $1999,1998,\dots,1$. The even values $2,4,\dots,1998$ occur twice and all other values once, so $\sum c_v(c_v-1)=999\cdot2$ and $N=1998$.

(b) Maximum. Fix a value $v$ with $c=c_v\ge1$, and let $i_1<i_2<\dots<i_c$ be the indices with $s_i=v$. The blocks of consecutive positions from $i_t+1$ to $i_{t+1}$ ($t=1,\dots,c$, with $i_{c+1}=i_1$ going once around; for $c=1$ the single block is the whole circle) are nonempty, disjoint, cover the circle, and have sum $0$. A nonempty block with sum $0$ contains a $2$ (a block of $-1$'s has negative sum), so these $c$ blocks contain at least $c$ twos: $c_v\le1000$. Hence \[ \sum_v c_v(c_v-1)\le 999\sum_v c_v=999\cdot3000, \] and $N\le 2997000$. Equality occurs for the pattern $2,-1,-1$ repeated $1000$ times: the prefix sums run through $0,2,1,0,2,1,\dots$, so $c_0=c_1=c_2=1000$, $\sum c_v(c_v-1)=3\cdot1000\cdot999$ and $N=2997000$.

Therefore \[ \boxed{\text{(a) } 1998,\qquad \text{(b) } 2997000.} \] (If the whole circle, whose sum is $0$, were also counted as a group, both answers would increase by $1$.)

Solution 2 (A second proof of (b): counting by starting position)

Here we prove only part (b) again, without prefix sums. A group is a block of at least two and at most $2999$ consecutive numbers; it is determined by its first position and its length. The total of all $3000$ numbers is $1000\cdot2-2000=0$.

Fix a starting position $i$ and let $\ell_1<\ell_2<\dots<\ell_r$ be the lengths of the groups with sum $0$ that start at $i$ (all $\ell_j\le2999$). If $r=0$ there is nothing to prove, so let $r\ge1$. Cut the circle, starting at $i$, into consecutive blocks of lengths $\ell_1,\ \ell_2-\ell_1,\ \dots,\ \ell_r-\ell_{r-1},\ 3000-\ell_r$. These $r+1$ blocks are nonempty, disjoint and cover the circle. Each has sum $0$: the first $j$ blocks together form the group of length $\ell_j$, with sum $0$, so each block is a difference of two zero sums, and the last block has sum $0-0=0$ because the total is $0$. A nonempty block with sum $0$ contains a $2$ (a block of $-1$'s has negative sum), and there are only $1000$ twos. Hence $r+1\le1000$, i.e. at most $999$ zero-sum groups start at each position.

Every group has exactly one starting position, so Bruno counts at most $3000\cdot999=2997000$ groups. For the arrangement $2,-1,-1$ repeated $1000$ times, from any starting position the running sums repeat with period $3$ ($2,1,0$ or $-1,-2,0$ or $-1,1,0$), so the zero-sum groups starting there have lengths $3,6,\dots,2997$, which is $999$ groups. So the maximum is $\boxed{2997000}$.

Solution 3 (A third proof of (b): zero-sum groups have length divisible by 3)

Here we prove only part (b) again. A group is a block of at least two and at most $2999$ consecutive numbers; it is determined by its first position (one of $3000$) and its length (from $2$ to $2999$), and different pairs (first position, length) give different groups.

If a group contains $p$ twos and $q$ minus ones, its sum is $2p-q$, which is $0$ exactly when $q=2p$; then its length is $3p$. So every zero-sum group has length $3,6,\dots,$ or $2997$: only $999$ possible lengths. Hence there are at most $999$ zero-sum groups with a given first position, and Bruno counts at most $3000\cdot999=2997000$ groups.

For the arrangement $2,-1,-1$ repeated $1000$ times, any $3$ consecutive numbers are $2,-1,-1$ in some cyclic order, so every block of length $3p$ ($1\le p\le 999$) consists of $p$ such triples and has sum $0$. All $3000\cdot999$ pairs then give zero-sum groups, so the maximum is $\boxed{2997000}$.
