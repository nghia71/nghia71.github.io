Problem 9

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Geometric Sequences

A rabbit starts at point $A$ and hops $1$ meter north, then $\frac12$ meter west, then $\frac14$ meter south, then $\frac18$ meter east, then $\frac1{16}$ meter north, then $\frac1{32}$ meter west, etc. The rabbit approaches the point $B$. What is the distance between points $A$ and $B$?

(A) $\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}$ (B) $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ (C) $\frac{\sqrt5}{2}$ (D) $\frac{\sqrt6}{3}$ (E) $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$

Show the answer

(A) $\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two geometric series)

Put $A$ at the origin, with east the positive $x$-direction and north the positive $y$-direction. The hops of lengths $1,\frac14,\frac1{16},\dots$ go alternately north and south, and the hops of lengths $\frac12,\frac18,\frac1{32},\dots$ go alternately west and east. Hence \[y_B=1-\frac14+\frac1{16}-\cdots=\frac{1}{1+\frac14}=\frac45,\qquad x_B=-\frac12\left(1-\frac14+\frac1{16}-\cdots\right)=-\frac12\cdot\frac45=-\frac25.\] So $AB=\sqrt{\frac{16}{25}+\frac{4}{25}}=\frac{\sqrt{20}}{5}=\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}$, and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ \frac{2\sqrt5}{5}}$.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers)

Identify the plane with the complex numbers, with $A=0$, east $=1$ and north $=i$. Turning a vector by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise (north $\to$ west $\to$ south $\to$ east) is multiplication by $i$, and each hop is half as long as the previous one. So the $k$th hop ($k=0,1,2,\dots$) is $i\left(\frac i2\right)^k$, and \[B=\sum_{k\ge0} i\left(\frac i2\right)^k=\frac{i}{1-\frac i2},\qquad |AB|=\frac{|i|}{\left|1-\frac i2\right|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac14}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt5}=\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}.\] The series converges because $\left|\frac i2\right|<1$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ \frac{2\sqrt5}{5}}$.

Problem 11

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 23: Powers, Cycles, and Units Digits · Periodic Powers Modulo \(m\)

Let $D(n)$ denote the sum of the digits of the decimal representation of a positive integer $n$. Define $n_0=7^{7^7}$, and $n_{k+1}=D(n_k)$ for $k\ge0$. Eventually this sequence reaches a single digit and stays there forever. What is that digit?

(A) $1$ (B) $3$ (C) $5$ (D) $7$ (E) $9$

Show the answer

(D) $7$

Show the solution

Solution (Digit sums keep the remainder mod 9)

Since $10\equiv1\pmod 9$, every positive integer is congruent to the sum of its digits modulo $9$: $D(n)\equiv n\pmod 9$. Also $D(n)<n$ whenever $n\ge10$, so the sequence strictly decreases until it reaches a single digit $d\in\{1,\dots,9\}$, and then $D(d)=d$. Therefore $d\equiv n_0\pmod 9$.

Now compute $n_0=7^{7^7}$ modulo $9$. The powers of $7$ modulo $9$ are $7,\ 49\equiv4,\ 28\equiv1$, so they repeat with period $3$. The exponent satisfies $7^7\equiv1^7=1\pmod 3$, so $7^{7^7}\equiv7^1=7\pmod 9$. The only digit from $1$ to $9$ that is $\equiv7\pmod9$ is $7$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 7}$.

Problem 12

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · Bounded Positive Solutions

You have stamps worth $6$ cents, $9$ cents, and $10$ cents. Clearly you cannot put $11$ cents on an envelope using these stamps, but you can put $15$ cents using a $6$-cent stamp and a $9$-cent stamp. Let $y$ be the largest amount that you cannot put on an envelope using only these stamps. Which of the following is true? (Assume you have an unlimited supply of stamps of each kind.)

(A) $y<10$ (B) $10<y<15$ (C) $15<y<20$ (D) $20<y<25$ (E) $25<y$

Show the answer

(D) $20<y<25$ (in fact $y=23$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Find the gap, then a run of six)

Using only $6$- and $9$-cent stamps we can make exactly $0$ and the multiples of $3$ that are at least $6$ (start from $6$, $9$ and $12=6+6$ and add $6$s); in particular $3$ cannot be made.

$23$ cannot be made. With $c$ ten-cent stamps, $23-10c\in\{23,13,3\}$ must be made from $6$s and $9$s. But $23$ and $13$ are not multiples of $3$, and $3$ cannot be made.

Every amount above $23$ can be made. Indeed \[24=6\cdot4,\quad 25=6+9+10,\quad 26=6+10+10,\quad 27=9\cdot3,\quad 28=9+9+10,\quad 29=9+10+10.\] These are six consecutive amounts; adding $6$-cent stamps to them gives every larger amount.

So $y=23$ and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 20<y<25}$.

Solution 2 (Sort the amounts by remainder mod 3)

Write an amount as $n=6a+9b+10c$ with $a,b,c\ge0$. Since $6a+9b\equiv0$ and $10\equiv1\pmod3$, we need $c\equiv n\pmod 3$. The $6$s and $9$s together make exactly $0$ and the multiples of $3$ that are at least $6$ (from $6,9,12$ add $6$s); so the only multiple of $3$ they miss among positive amounts is $3$.

Take the least possible $c$, namely $c_0\in\{0,1,2\}$ with $c_0\equiv n\pmod 3$. Then $n$ can be made if and only if $n-10c_0$ is $0$ or a multiple of $3$ that is at least $6$; using $c_0+3$ or more ten-cent stamps only makes the rest $30$ smaller, which never helps. So the amounts that cannot be made are:

  • $n\equiv0$: $n=3$;

  • $n\equiv1$: $n-10<0$ or $n-10=3$, i.e. $n\in\{1,4,7,13\}$;

  • $n\equiv2$: $n-20<0$ or $n-20=3$, i.e. $n\in\{2,5,8,11,14,17,23\}$.

The largest is $y=23$, and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 20<y<25}$.

Problem 13

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · Blocked and Restricted Paths

A fair coin is flipped $6$ times. What is the probability that at no point during the $6$ flips the number of heads flipped exceeds the number of tails flipped?

(A) $\frac5{16}$ (B) $\frac{11}{32}$ (C) $\frac{13}{64}$ (D) $\frac7{32}$ (E) $\frac{15}{64}$

Show the answer

(A) $\frac5{16}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count step by step)

Track the lead $\ell=(\text{tails})-(\text{heads})$ after each flip; a sequence is good when $\ell\ge0$ throughout. A tail raises $\ell$ by $1$, a head lowers it by $1$, and a head is forbidden when $\ell=0$. Let us record, after $k$ flips, how many good sequences have each value of $\ell$ (each count is the sum of the counts for $\ell-1$ and $\ell+1$ one row above): \[\begin{array}{c|ccccccc} k & \ell=0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6\\\hline 1 & & 1 & & & & &\\ 2 & 1 & & 1 & & & &\\ 3 & & 2 & & 1 & & &\\ 4 & 2 & & 3 & & 1 & &\\ 5 & & 5 & & 4 & & 1 &\\ 6 & 5 & & 9 & & 5 & & 1 \end{array}\] After $6$ flips there are $5+9+5+1=20$ good sequences out of $2^6=64$, so the probability is $\frac{20}{64}=\frac5{16}$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ \frac5{16}}$.

Solution 2 (Reflection)

Let $h_k$ be (heads) $-$ (tails) after $k$ flips. A sequence is bad if $h_k=1$ for some $k$ (the first time heads exceed tails, the difference is exactly $1$). The final value $h_6$ is even.

Every sequence with $h_6\ge2$ is bad. For a bad sequence with $h_6\le0$, switch heads and tails in all flips after the first moment when $h_k=1$. This changes the final value from $h_6$ to $2-h_6\ge2$, and it is reversible (a sequence ending at $2$ or more must reach $1$, and switching back after its first visit to $1$ recovers the original). So bad sequences with $h_6\le0$ correspond one-to-one to sequences with $h_6\ge2$.

The sequences with $h_6\ge2$ are those with $4$, $5$ or $6$ heads: $\binom64+\binom65+\binom66=15+6+1=22$. Hence there are $22+22=44$ bad sequences, $64-44=20$ good ones, and the probability is $\frac{20}{64}=\frac5{16}$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ \frac5{16}}$.

Problem 14

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

The number $n=99\cdots9$ has $250$ digits all equal to $9$. The sum of the digits of $n^2$ is

(A) $2241$ (B) $2250$ (C) $2259$ (D) $2268$ (E) $2277$

Show the answer

(B) $2250$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Expand $(10^{250}-1)^2$)

We have $n=10^{250}-1$, so \[n^2=10^{500}-2\cdot10^{250}+1=10^{250}\left(10^{250}-2\right)+1.\] The number $10^{250}-2$ is $\underbrace{99\cdots9}_{249}8$. Multiplying by $10^{250}$ appends $250$ zeros, and adding $1$ turns the last zero into $1$: \[n^2=\underbrace{99\cdots9}_{249}8\underbrace{00\cdots0}_{249}1.\] The digit sum is $9\cdot249+8+1=2250$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 2250}$.

Solution 2 (Write $n^2=n\cdot10^{250}-n$)

Since $n^2=n(n+1)-n=n\cdot10^{250}-n$, we can rewrite \[n^2=(n-1)\cdot10^{250}+\left(10^{250}-n\right)=(n-1)\cdot10^{250}+1.\] Here $n-1=\underbrace{99\cdots9}_{249}8$ has $250$ digits, so $n^2$ consists of the digits of $n-1$, followed by $249$ zeros and a final $1$; there are no carries. The digit sum is $(9\cdot250-1)+1=2250$ (check with $99^2=9801$: $9+8+0+1=18=9\cdot2$). The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 2250}$.

Problem 15

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Angle Expressions and Trigonometric Identities

Consider the following numbers: \[A=\cos(0^\circ)+\cos(1^\circ)+\cdots+\cos(180^\circ),\] \[B=\sin(90^\circ)+\sin(91^\circ)+\cdots+\sin(270^\circ),\] \[C=\sin(A^\circ)+\cos(B^\circ).\] Which of the following is true?

(A) $A<B<C$ (B) $C<A=B$ (C) $A=B=C$ (D) $A=B<C$ (E) $B<A<C$

Show the answer

(D) $A=B<C$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pair the terms)

Since $\cos(180^\circ-k^\circ)=-\cos k^\circ$, the terms of $A$ for $k$ and $180-k$ ($k=0,\dots,89$) cancel, and the middle term is $\cos90^\circ=0$. So $A=0$.

Since $\sin(180^\circ+k^\circ)=-\sin k^\circ=-\sin(180^\circ-k^\circ)$, the terms of $B$ for $180-k$ and $180+k$ ($k=1,\dots,90$) cancel, and the middle term is $\sin180^\circ=0$. So $B=0$.

Then $C=\sin0^\circ+\cos0^\circ=0+1=1$. Thus $A=B=0<1=C$, and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ A=B<C}$.

Solution 2 (Symmetric points on the unit circle)

The point $(\cos\theta^\circ,\sin\theta^\circ)$ lies on the unit circle. As $\theta$ runs over $0,1,\dots,180$, the points form a set that is symmetric in the $y$-axis (the reflection sends angle $\theta$ to $180-\theta$, which is again in the set). Reflection in the $y$-axis changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate, so the sum of the $x$-coordinates, which is $A$, equals its own negative: $A=0$.

As $\theta$ runs over $90,91,\dots,270$, the points are symmetric in the $x$-axis (angle $\theta$ goes to $360-\theta$, again between $90$ and $270$). This reflection changes the sign of the $y$-coordinate, so the sum of the $y$-coordinates, which is $B$, is $0$.

Hence $C=\sin0^\circ+\cos0^\circ=1$ and $A=B<C$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ A=B<C}$.

Problem 16

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations · Composite Angle Chases

Suppose in an isosceles triangle $ABC$ we know $AB=AC$ and $\angle BAC=40^\circ$. Point $D$ lies on side $AB$ so that $BD=BC$. What is the measure of $\angle ACD$ in degrees?

(A) $10$ (B) $15$ (C) $20$ (D) $25$ (E) $30$

Show the answer

(B) $15$

Show the solution

Solution (Two isosceles triangles)

Since $AB=AC$ and $\angle BAC=40^\circ$, the base angles are $\angle ABC=\angle ACB=\frac{180^\circ-40^\circ}{2}=70^\circ$.

In triangle $DBC$ we have $BD=BC$ and $\angle DBC=\angle ABC=70^\circ$, so its base angles are $\angle BCD=\angle BDC=\frac{180^\circ-70^\circ}{2}=55^\circ$. Since $D$ lies on side $AB$, the ray $CD$ lies inside $\angle ACB$, and \[\angle ACD=\angle ACB-\angle BCD=70^\circ-55^\circ=15^\circ.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 15}$.

Problem 17

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

Alice has eleven cards with integers $0,1,\dots,10$ written on them, one distinct integer on each card. She wants to form a three-tier tower with six different cards. The bottom row has three cards, the middle row has two and the top has one. Each card in the middle and top must be the sum of the two cards directly beneath it. One such example is the following: bottom row $2,3,1$; middle row $5,4$; top $9$.

In how many ways can she choose and arrange the cards to form such a tower?

(A) $14$ (B) $16$ (C) $18$ (D) $20$ (E) $22$

Show the answer

(A) $14$

Show the solution

Solution (Casework on the middle bottom card)

Let the bottom row be $a,b,c$ (left to right). Then the middle row is $a+b,\ b+c$ and the top is $a+2b+c$.

No card is $0$: if $a=0$ then $a+b=b$; if $b=0$ then $a+b=a$; if $c=0$ then $b+c=b$; each repeats a card. So $a,b,c\ge1$, and then the top $a+2b+c$ is the largest card, so we need $a+2b+c\le10$. The six cards are different exactly when $a,b,c$ are different, $a+b\ne c$ and $b+c\ne a$ (the other coincidences are impossible: $a+b\ne b+c$ since $a\ne c$, and $a+b>a,b$, $b+c>b,c$).

Reversing the bottom row gives another valid tower, and $a\ne c$, so we count bottom rows with $a<c$ and double. Then $b+c>a$ automatically, so only $a+b\ne c$ remains.

  • $b=1$: $a,c\ge2$, $a+c\le8$, $c\ne a+1$: $(2,4),(2,5),(2,6),(3,5)$ ($(2,3)$, $(3,4)$ fail).

  • $b=2$: $a,c\ne2$, $a+c\le6$, $c\ne a+2$: $(1,4),(1,5)$ ($(1,3)$ fails).

  • $b=3$: $a+c\le4$, $a,c\ne3$: $(1,2)$.

  • $b\ge4$: $a+c\le2$ is impossible for $a\ne c$.

This gives $7$ bottom rows with $a<c$, hence $14$ towers. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 14}$.

Problem 18

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 11: Divisors and Arithmetic Functions of a Number · Reconstructing from Divisor Data

How many digits does the smallest positive integer with exactly $100$ positive integer factors have?

(A) $4$ (B) $5$ (C) $6$ (D) $7$ (E) $8$

Show the answer

(B) $5$

Show the solution

Solution (List the exponent patterns)

If $N=p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then $N$ has $(e_1+1)\cdots(e_k+1)$ divisors. For a given multiset of exponents, $N$ is smallest when the exponents are in decreasing order on the primes $2,3,5,7,\dots$. The ways to write $100$ as a product of factors $\ge2$ (each factor is some $e_i+1$) and the smallest $N$ for each are: \[\begin{array}{ll} 100: & 2^{99}\\ 50\cdot2: & 2^{49}\cdot3\\ 25\cdot4: & 2^{24}\cdot3^3\\ 25\cdot2\cdot2: & 2^{24}\cdot3\cdot5\\ 20\cdot5: & 2^{19}\cdot3^4\\ 10\cdot10: & 2^9\cdot3^9\\ 10\cdot5\cdot2: & 2^9\cdot3^4\cdot5=207360\\ 5\cdot5\cdot4: & 2^4\cdot3^4\cdot5^3=162000\\ 5\cdot5\cdot2\cdot2: & 2^4\cdot3^4\cdot5\cdot7=45360 \end{array}\] The first six are larger than $2^{19}>10^5$ ($2^9\cdot3^9>2^{19}$ because $3^9>2^{10}$). So the smallest number with $100$ divisors is $45360$, which has $5$ digits. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 5}$.

Problem 19

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 47: Combinatorial Number Theory · Extremal Integer Configurations

What is the largest possible size of a subset $S$ of $\{1,2,3,\dots,100\}$ such that no element of $S$ is three times another element of $S$?

(A) $70$ (B) $73$ (C) $76$ (D) $79$ (E) $82$

Show the answer

(C) $76$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Chains $x,3x,9x,\dots$)

Every $n\le100$ is uniquely $n=3^jx$ with $3\nmid x$. So $\{1,\dots,100\}$ splits into chains $x,3x,9x,\dots$ (all terms $\le100$), one for each $x\le100$ not divisible by $3$. Two numbers with one three times the other are consecutive terms of the same chain. Hence $S$ is allowed exactly when it never contains two consecutive terms of a chain, and from a chain of length $L$ we can take at most $\lceil L/2\rceil$ terms, attained by the $1$st, $3$rd, $5$th, … terms.

The chain of $x$ has length at least $j$ exactly when $3^{j-1}x\le100$. Among $1,\dots,m$ there are $m-\lfloor m/3\rfloor$ numbers not divisible by $3$, so the numbers of chains of length $\ge1,\ge2,\ge3,\ge4,\ge5$ are $67,22,8,2,1$ (take $m=100,33,11,3,1$), and none is longer ($3^5>100$). Since $\lceil L/2\rceil$ counts the levels $1,3,5$ that the chain reaches, the maximum is \[67+8+1=76.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(C)}\ 76}$.

Solution 2 (Parity of the exponent of 3)

Let $v(n)$ be the exponent of $3$ in $n$. If $m=3k$ then $v(m)=v(k)+1$, so $m$ and $k$ have exponents of different parity.

Construction. Let $S$ be the set of $n\le100$ with $v(n)$ even. By the remark, no element of $S$ is three times another. Its size: $v(n)=0$ for $100-33=67$ numbers; $v(n)=2$ for $n=9k$ with $k\le11$, $3\nmid k$: $11-3=8$ numbers; $v(n)=4$ only for $n=81$. Total $76$.

Bound. The numbers with odd $v(n)$ are the $n=3k$ with $v(k)$ even; there are $(33-11)+2=24$ of them ($v=1$: $n=3k$, $k\le33$, $3\nmid k$; $v=3$: $27,54$). Pair each such $n$ with $n/3$. The $24$ pairs $\{n/3,n\}$ are disjoint (the larger element has odd $v$, the smaller even $v$, and $n$ determines $n/3$), and any allowed set contains at most one element of each pair. The remaining $100-48=52$ numbers are singletons. So any allowed set has at most $24+52=76$ elements.

The answer is $\boxed{\text{(C)}\ 76}$.

Problem 20

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Angle Bisectors and Incenter

The incircle of a right triangle $ABC$ divides the hypotenuse $BC$ into two parts with lengths $a$ and $b$. The area of $ABC$ is equal to:

(A) $ab$ (B) $a^2+b^2-ab$ (C) $2a^2+2b^2-3ab$ (D) $3ab-a^2-b^2$ (E) $\frac{(a+b)^2}{4}$

Show the answer

(A) $ab$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangent lengths and Pythagoras)

The right angle is at $A$ (the hypotenuse is $BC$). Let the incircle, with centre $I$ and radius $r$, touch $BC$ at $T$, with $BT=a$ and $TC=b$. Tangent segments from a point are equal, so the tangent lengths from $B$, $C$ are $a$, $b$; the tangent points on $AB$ and $AC$ together with $A$ and $I$ form a square of side $r$, so the tangent length from $A$ is $r$. Thus $AB=r+a$, $AC=r+b$, $BC=a+b$. By Pythagoras, \[(r+a)^2+(r+b)^2=(a+b)^2\iff 2r^2+2r(a+b)=2ab\iff r^2+r(a+b)=ab.\] Therefore \[[ABC]=\tfrac12(r+a)(r+b)=\tfrac12\bigl(r^2+r(a+b)+ab\bigr)=\tfrac12(ab+ab)=ab.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ ab}$.

Solution 2 (Two area formulas)

Let the right angle be at $A$, let the incircle have radius $r$ and touch $BC$ at $T$ with $BT=a$, $TC=b$. Equal tangents give $AB=r+a$, $AC=r+b$ (the tangent length from the right-angle vertex is $r$, because the two tangent points, $A$ and the incentre form a square), so the semiperimeter is $s=a+b+r$.

The area is $[ABC]=rs=r(a+b+r)$ (split the triangle into three triangles with apex at the incentre) and also $[ABC]=\frac12(r+a)(r+b)$. Equating, \[2r^2+2r(a+b)=r^2+r(a+b)+ab,\qquad\text{so}\qquad r^2+r(a+b)=ab,\] and hence $[ABC]=r(a+b+r)=ab$, without using Pythagoras. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ ab}$.

Solution 3 (Tangent lengths through the semiperimeter)

Let the right angle be at $A$, and write $u=AB$, $v=AC$, $w=BC$ and $s=\frac{u+v+w}{2}$. Let the incircle touch $BC$ at $T$, with $BT=a$ and $TC=b$.

Tangent lengths. The two tangent segments from a vertex to the incircle are equal; call their lengths $x$ (from $B$), $y$ (from $C$) and $z$ (from $A$). Then $x+z=u$, $y+z=v$, $x+y=w$, and adding, $x+y+z=s$. Hence $x=s-v$ and $y=s-u$, so \[a=BT=s-v,\qquad b=TC=s-u .\]

The product. Using $u+v=2s-w$, \[ab=(s-u)(s-v)=s^2-s(u+v)+uv=s^2-s(2s-w)+uv=uv-s(s-w).\] By Pythagoras, $u^2+v^2=w^2$, so \[s(s-w)=\frac{(u+v+w)(u+v-w)}{4}=\frac{(u+v)^2-w^2}{4}=\frac{2uv}{4}=\frac{uv}{2}.\] Therefore $ab=uv-\frac{uv}2=\frac{uv}{2}$, which is the area of the right triangle $ABC$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ ab}$.

Problem 22

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Suppose $x\ge1$, $y\ge2$, $z\ge3$ are real numbers satisfying \[2\sqrt{x-1}+4\sqrt{y-2}+6\sqrt{z-3}=x+y+z+8.\] What is the smallest possible value of $x+2y+3z$?

(A) $40$ (B) $42$ (C) $46$ (D) $48$ (E) $50$

Show the answer

(E) $50$

Show the solution

Solution (A sum of squares)

Put $u=\sqrt{x-1}$, $v=\sqrt{y-2}$, $w=\sqrt{z-3}$, all $\ge0$; then $x=u^2+1$, $y=v^2+2$, $z=w^2+3$. The condition becomes \[2u+4v+6w=u^2+v^2+w^2+14,\] that is, \[(u-1)^2+(v-2)^2+(w-3)^2=0.\] A sum of squares is $0$ only if each square is $0$, so $u=1$, $v=2$, $w=3$, i.e. $x=2$, $y=6$, $z=12$ (and these do satisfy the condition: $2+8+18=28=2+6+12+8$). So $x+2y+3z=2+12+36=50$ is the only possible value, and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 50}$.

Problem 23

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Winning and Losing Positions

Two players play a game with a pile of $n$ stones. They alternate turns, and on each turn a player must remove $1$, $4$, or $5$ stones. The player who removes the last stone wins. Both players play as well as possible (that is, each player, on every turn, makes whatever move gives them the best chance to win). For how many positive integers $n\le2026$ does the second player win the game?

(A) $253$ (B) $500$ (C) $505$ (D) $506$ (E) $507$

Show the answer

(E) $507$

Show the solution

Solution (Losing positions are $0,2 \pmod 8$)

Call a pile size losing if the player to move loses with best play (pile $0$ is losing: the previous player took the last stone). The second player wins exactly when $n$ is losing. Computing the first few: $0$ L, $1$ W, $2$ L, $3$ W ($\to2$), $4$ W ($\to0$), $5$ W ($\to0$), $6$ W ($\to2$), $7$ W ($\to2$), $8$ L, … suggests:

Claim. $n$ is losing if and only if $n\equiv0$ or $2\pmod 8$.

We check the two properties that characterise losing positions, by strong induction on $n$.

  • From $n\equiv0$: removing $1,4,5$ gives residues $7,4,3$; from $n\equiv2$: residues $1,6,5$. None is $0$ or $2$, so every move from a claimed losing position goes to a claimed winning one.

  • From the other residues there is a move to residue $0$ or $2$: $1\to0$ (remove $1$), $3\to2$ (remove $1$), $4\to0$ (remove $4$), $5\to0$ (remove $5$), $6\to2$ (remove $4$), $7\to2$ (remove $5$). The number removed never exceeds the residue, so the move is always legal.

Now count $n\le2026$: $n\equiv0$: $8,16,\dots,2024$, that is $253$ numbers; $n\equiv2$: $2,10,\dots,2026$, that is $254$ numbers. Total $507$, and the answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 507}$.

Problem 24

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Angle Expressions and Trigonometric Identities

Consider the product: \[P=\left(1+\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4052}\right)\right)\left(1+\tan\left(\frac{2\pi}{4052}\right)\right)\cdots\left(1+\tan\left(\frac{1012\pi}{4052}\right)\right)\left(1+\tan\left(\frac{1013\pi}{4052}\right)\right).\] All angles are in radians. $P$ can be written as $m^n$, where $m,n$ are positive integers and $m$ is as small as possible. What is $m+n$?

(A) $509$ (B) $510$ (C) $512$ (D) $514$ (E) $518$

Show the answer

(A) $509$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pairs of angles adding to $\pi/4$)

Note that $4052=4\cdot1013$, so $\frac{1013\pi}{4052}=\frac\pi4$. Write $x_k=\frac{k\pi}{4052}$.

Lemma. If $x+y=\frac\pi4$ (with $x,y\in(0,\frac\pi4)$), then $(1+\tan x)(1+\tan y)=2$. Indeed $1=\tan(x+y)=\frac{\tan x+\tan y}{1-\tan x\tan y}$ gives $\tan x+\tan y+\tan x\tan y=1$, so $(1+\tan x)(1+\tan y)=1+1=2$.

Pair $k$ with $1013-k$ for $k=1,\dots,506$; since $x_k+x_{1013-k}=\frac\pi4$, this splits the factors $k=1,\dots,1012$ into $506$ pairs with product $2$ each. The last factor is $1+\tan\frac\pi4=2$. So $P=2^{507}$.

The smallest $m\ge2$ with $P=m^n$ is $m=2$ ($m=1$ is impossible because $P\ne1$), and then $n=507$. Thus $m+n=509$; the answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 509}$.

Solution 2 (Cancelling cosines)

For any $x$ with $\cos x\ne0$, \[1+\tan x=\frac{\cos x+\sin x}{\cos x}=\frac{\sqrt2\cos\left(\frac\pi4-x\right)}{\cos x}.\] Let $x_k=\frac{k\pi}{4052}$; as $4052=4\cdot1013$, we have $\frac\pi4-x_k=x_{1013-k}$. Hence \[\prod_{k=1}^{1012}(1+\tan x_k)=(\sqrt2)^{1012}\,\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{1012}\cos x_{1013-k}}{\prod_{k=1}^{1012}\cos x_k}=2^{506},\] because as $k$ runs over $1,\dots,1012$ so does $1013-k$, and all these cosines are positive. The last factor is $1+\tan x_{1013}=1+\tan\frac\pi4=2$, so $P=2^{507}$.

Since $P\ne1$, the smallest base is $m=2$, with $n=507$, and $m+n=509$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 509}$.

Problem 25

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Equation-Based Figure Analysis

Points $A$, $C$, $B$ lie on a line, with $C$ between $A$ and $B$, where $AC=12$ and $CB=20$. Semicircles are drawn on diameters $CB$ and $AB$, on the same side of line $AB$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $C$ perpendicular to $AB$. A circle $\omega$ is tangent to $\ell$, tangent internally to the semicircle with diameter $AB$, and tangent externally to the semicircle with diameter $CB$. Find the radius of $\omega$.

(A) $\frac{10}{3}$ (B) $\frac72$ (C) $\frac{15}{4}$ (D) $4$ (E) $\frac{25}{6}$

Show the answer

(C) $\frac{15}{4}$

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates)

Put $C=(0,0)$, $A=(-12,0)$, $B=(20,0)$, with the semicircles in the upper half-plane. The semicircle on $AB$ has centre $O_1=(4,0)$ and radius $16$; the one on $CB$ has centre $O_2=(10,0)$ and radius $10$. Let $\omega$ have centre $P=(p,h)$, $h>0$, and radius $r$. Tangency to $\ell$ (the $y$-axis) means $|p|=r$.

If $p=-r$, external tangency to the small semicircle gives $(r+10)^2+h^2=(r+10)^2$, so $h=0$, impossible. Hence $p=r$: $\omega$ lies on the side of $\ell$ towards $B$, as in the figure. Internal tangency to the large circle and external tangency to the small one give \[(r-4)^2+h^2=(16-r)^2,\qquad (r-10)^2+h^2=(10+r)^2,\] that is, $h^2=240-24r$ and $h^2=40r$. Hence $64r=240$ and $r=\frac{15}{4}$ (with $h^2=150$; the tangency points lie on the upper arcs, since $h>0$). The answer is $\boxed{\text{(C)}\ \frac{15}{4}}$.
