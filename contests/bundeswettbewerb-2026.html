Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Romeo and Juliet meet at a romantic place. She writes her $10$-digit phone number on a piece of paper for him.

Later, Romeo finds the note again, but he can no longer read one of the digits; he only knows that it was not zero. Therefore, he asks Juliet for the missing digit.

Juliet enjoys playing with numbers and says to Romeo: If I count how many digits of my phone number are smaller than $1$, then how many digits are smaller than $2$, and so on, and finally how many digits are smaller than $10$, and then add these ten numbers together, the result is divisible by $9$.

How can Romeo determine the missing digit using this information?

Show the answer

The missing digit is the unique $x\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ with $x\equiv 1-s\pmod 9$, where $s$ is the sum of the nine digits Romeo can read.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count each digit separately)

Let the digits of the phone number be $d_1,\dots,d_{10}$ and let $c_k$ be the number of digits smaller than $k$ ($k=1,\dots,10$). Juliet's sum is $c_1+c_2+\dots+c_{10}$, the number of pairs $(i,k)$ with $d_i<k$. Counting these pairs digit by digit instead: the digit $d_i$ is smaller than exactly the $10-d_i$ numbers $k=d_i+1,\dots,10$. Hence \[c_1+c_2+\dots+c_{10}=\sum_{i=1}^{10}(10-d_i)=100-S,\qquad S=d_1+\dots+d_{10}.\] So Juliet tells Romeo that $9\mid 100-S$, that is, $S\equiv 100\equiv 1\pmod 9$.

Let $s$ be the sum of the nine digits Romeo can read and $x$ the missing digit. Then $s+x\equiv1\pmod 9$, i.e. $x\equiv 1-s\pmod 9$. Since $x\ne0$, we have $x\in\{1,2,\dots,9\}$, and these nine numbers lie in nine different residue classes modulo $9$. So exactly one of them satisfies the congruence, and Romeo finds the missing digit as the unique $x\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ with $x\equiv 1-s\pmod 9$ (in particular $x=9$ when $s\equiv1\pmod 9$).

For example, if the readable digits are $0,1,7,6,5,4,3,2,1$, then $s=29\equiv2$, so $x\equiv -1\equiv 8\pmod 9$ and the missing digit is $8$.

Solution 2 (Raise the digits one step at a time)

For a string of ten digits let $T$ be Juliet's quantity: the number of digits smaller than $1$, plus the number smaller than $2$, and so on up to $10$.

For the string $0000000000$ every digit is smaller than each of $1,\dots,10$, so $T=10\cdot10=100$.

Now raise one digit from $d$ to $d+1$ ($0\le d\le 8$). For $k\le d$ the digit was and stays $\ge k$; for $k\ge d+2$ it was and stays $<k$. Only for $k=d+1$ does it change from “smaller than $k$” to “not smaller than $k$”. So $T$ decreases by exactly $1$.

Any phone number with digit sum $S$ is reached from $0000000000$ by exactly $S$ such single raises, so $T=100-S$. Juliet's information is therefore $9\mid 100-S$, i.e. $S\equiv1\pmod 9$.

If Romeo reads nine digits with sum $s$, the missing digit $x\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ must satisfy $x\equiv1-s\pmod 9$. The numbers $1,\dots,9$ represent each residue class modulo $9$ exactly once, so this determines $x$ uniquely: Romeo computes $1-s$ modulo $9$ and takes the representative in $\{1,\dots,9\}$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Determine all triples of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ that satisfy the following properties:

  • (1) The sum $a+b+c$ is a prime number.

  • (2) The number $ab+bc+ca$ is a divisor of the number $a^2+b^2+c^2$.

Show the answer

$(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)$ only

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The divisor must be the prime itself)

Let $p=a+b+c$ (a prime) and $s=ab+bc+ca$. Since $a,b,c\ge1$ we have $p\ge3$ and $s\ge3$.

$s$ divides $p^2$. From $p^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2s$ we get $a^2+b^2+c^2=p^2-2s$. As $s$ divides $a^2+b^2+c^2$ and $2s$, it divides $p^2$. The divisors of $p^2$ are $1,p,p^2$.

$s=p$. We have $s\ne1$. Also $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca$ (this is $\tfrac12[(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2]\ge0$), so $p^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2s\ge 3s$ and $s\le p^2/3<p^2$. Hence $s=p$, that is, \[ab+bc+ca=a+b+c.\]

Conclusion. Rewrite this as \[0=(ab+bc+ca)-(a+b+c)=a(b-1)+b(c-1)+c(a-1).\] All three terms are $\ge0$, so all are $0$: $b=c=a=1$. Conversely, for $(1,1,1)$ the sum $3$ is prime and $ab+bc+ca=3$ divides $a^2+b^2+c^2=3$. So the only triple is $\boxed{(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)}$.

Solution 2 (The quotient and shifted variables)

Let $p=a+b+c$, $s=ab+bc+ca$ and let $k=\dfrac{a^2+b^2+c^2}{s}$, a positive integer by condition (2). Then \[p^2=(a^2+b^2+c^2)+2s=(k+2)\,s.\] Here $k+2\ge3$ and $s\ge3$, so neither factor is $1$. The only factorisation of $p^2$ ($p$ prime) into two factors larger than $1$ is $p\cdot p$, hence $s=p$ (and $k=p-2$).

Now put $x=a-1$, $y=b-1$, $z=c-1$, all $\ge0$. Expanding, \[xy+yz+zx=(ab+bc+ca)-2(a+b+c)+3=s-2p+3=3-p .\] The left side is $\ge0$, so $p\le3$. As $p=a+b+c\ge3$, we get $p=3$ and $a=b=c=1$, which indeed works ($3$ is prime and $3\mid3$). The answer is $\boxed{(1,1,1)}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

We consider a tetrahedron $ABCD$, which is not necessarily regular.

A line in space is called a twofold symmetry axis of this tetrahedron if a rotation of $180^\circ$ around this line maps the tetrahedron onto itself.

Prove that the following two properties are equivalent:

  • (1) All three lines connecting the midpoints of opposite edges of the tetrahedron are twofold symmetry axes.

  • (2) All faces of the tetrahedron have the same perimeter.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Half-turns and congruent opposite edges)

Write $AB=a$, $CD=a'$, $AC=b$, $BD=b'$, $AD=c$, $BC=c'$ (so $a,a'$; $b,b'$; $c,c'$ are the pairs of opposite edges), and let $M,N$ be the midpoints of $AB$ and $CD$. Recall that a half-turn (rotation by $180^\circ$) about a line $\ell$ maps a point $X$ to the point $X'$ such that the foot of the perpendicular from $X$ to $\ell$ is the midpoint of $XX'$; its fixed points are exactly the points of $\ell$.

Step 0: (2) means that opposite edges are equal. The perimeters of $ABC,ABD,ACD,BCD$ are $a+b+c'$, $a+c+b'$, $b+c+a'$, $a'+b'+c'$. If they are equal, then from the first two $b-b'=c-c'$, and from the last two $b-b'=c'-c$. Hence $c-c'=c'-c$, so $c=c'$ and then $b=b'$; finally $a+b+c'=a'+b'+c'$ gives $a=a'$. Conversely, if $a=a'$, $b=b'$, $c=c'$, every face has one edge from each pair and perimeter $a+b+c$. So (2) is equivalent to \[AB=CD,\qquad AC=BD,\qquad AD=BC. \tag{$*$}\]

(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2). Let $\rho$ be the half-turn about the line $MN$; by (1) it maps the tetrahedron onto itself, so it permutes the vertices and maps edges to edges. It fixes $M$, and it maps midpoints to midpoints, so it maps $AB$ to an edge whose midpoint is $M$. The six edge midpoints are distinct: two edges with a common vertex have different midpoints, and if $AB$ and $CD$ had the same midpoint, $ACBD$ would be a parallelogram and the four vertices would be coplanar. So $\rho$ maps the pair $\{A,B\}$ to itself, and likewise $\{C,D\}$ to itself.

If $\rho$ fixed $A$, it would also fix $B$, so $A,B$ would lie on the axis and the axis would be the line $AB$; then $N$ would lie on line $AB$ and the line $CD$ would meet line $AB$, making $A,B,C,D$ coplanar. Hence $\rho(A)=B$, $\rho(B)=A$, and in the same way $\rho(C)=D$, $\rho(D)=C$. Since $\rho$ preserves lengths, \[AC=\rho(A)\rho(C)=BD,\qquad AD=\rho(A)\rho(D)=BC.\] The same argument for the half-turn about the line through the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$ gives $AB=CD$. So $(*)$ holds, and by Step 0 all faces have the same perimeter.

(2) $\Rightarrow$ (1). Assume $(*)$. Triangles $ACD$ and $BDC$ are congruent (SSS: $AC=BD$, $AD=BC$, $CD$ common), and $AN$, $BN$ are corresponding medians to the common side $CD$, so $NA=NB$. Thus $N$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$; since $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$ and $N\ne M$, the median $NM$ of the isosceles triangle $NAB$ is perpendicular to $AB$. In the same way (triangles $CAB$ and $DBA$ are congruent) $MC=MD$ and $MN\perp CD$.

Now the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to the line $MN$ is $M$ (as $AM\perp MN$), and $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$; so the half-turn about $MN$ maps $A$ to $B$ and $B$ to $A$. Likewise it maps $C$ to $D$ and $D$ to $C$. Thus it maps the tetrahedron onto itself, and $MN$ is a twofold symmetry axis. The same argument applies to the other two lines joining midpoints of opposite edges, so (1) holds.

Solution 2 (Vectors from the centroid)

Put the origin at the centroid $G$ of the vertices and denote the position vectors of $A,B,C,D$ by $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf d$, so $\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c+\mathbf d=\mathbf 0$. The midpoint of $AB$ is $\mathbf m=\tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b)$ and that of $CD$ is $-\mathbf m$; so the line through them passes through $G$. Here $\mathbf m\ne\mathbf 0$, since otherwise $AB$ and $CD$ would have a common midpoint and the vertices would be coplanar. The half-turn about this line is \[\rho(\mathbf x)=2\,\frac{\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf m}{|\mathbf m|^2}\,\mathbf m-\mathbf x .\]

Key computation. Since $\mathbf a+\mathbf b=2\mathbf m$, \[\rho(\mathbf a)=\mathbf b\iff 2\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf m}{|\mathbf m|^2}\mathbf m=2\mathbf m\iff \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf a+\mathbf b)=\tfrac12|\mathbf a+\mathbf b|^2 \iff |\mathbf a|^2=|\mathbf b|^2. \tag{1}\] Similarly, as $\mathbf c+\mathbf d=-2\mathbf m$, $\rho(\mathbf c)=\mathbf d\iff|\mathbf c|=|\mathbf d|$. Also, by the parallelogram law, \[AB^2-CD^2=\bigl(2|\mathbf a|^2+2|\mathbf b|^2-|\mathbf a+\mathbf b|^2\bigr)-\bigl(2|\mathbf c|^2+2|\mathbf d|^2-|\mathbf c+\mathbf d|^2\bigr) =2\bigl(|\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2-|\mathbf c|^2-|\mathbf d|^2\bigr), \tag{2}\] because $|\mathbf a+\mathbf b|=|\mathbf c+\mathbf d|$; and analogously for the pairs $AC,BD$ and $AD,BC$.

(2) $\Leftrightarrow$ opposite edges equal. If the four faces have equal perimeters, comparing $ABC$ with $ABD$ and $ACD$ with $BCD$ gives $AC-BD=AD-BC$ and $AC-BD=BC-AD$, so $AD=BC$, $AC=BD$, and then $AB=CD$. The converse is clear (each face uses one edge of each opposite pair).

(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2). The half-turn $\rho$ about the line through $\pm\mathbf m$ permutes the vertices and fixes $\mathbf m$, the midpoint of $AB$; the edge midpoints are pairwise distinct (as noted, equal midpoints of $AB,CD$ would make the vertices coplanar), so $\rho$ maps $\{A,B\}$ onto itself. It cannot fix $A$: otherwise $A$ and then $B=2\mathbf m-\mathbf a$ lie on the axis, the axis is the line $AB$, and it contains $-\mathbf m$, the midpoint of $CD$ – coplanarity again. So $\rho(\mathbf a)=\mathbf b$, and by (1) $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf b|$; likewise $|\mathbf c|=|\mathbf d|$. Using the axis through the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$ we get $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf c|$ and $|\mathbf b|=|\mathbf d|$. Thus $GA=GB=GC=GD$, and by (2) and its analogues all pairs of opposite edges are equal; hence all faces have the same perimeter.

(2) $\Rightarrow$ (1). Now opposite edges are equal, so by (2) and its analogues \[|\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf b|^2=|\mathbf c|^2+|\mathbf d|^2,\quad |\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf c|^2=|\mathbf b|^2+|\mathbf d|^2,\quad |\mathbf a|^2+|\mathbf d|^2=|\mathbf b|^2+|\mathbf c|^2 .\] Adding the first two gives $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf d|$; adding the first and third gives $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf c|$; adding the last two gives $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf b|$. So $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf b|=|\mathbf c|=|\mathbf d|$, and by (1) the half-turn about the line through the midpoints of $AB$ and $CD$ swaps $A\leftrightarrow B$ and $C\leftrightarrow D$, i.e. maps the tetrahedron onto itself. The same holds for the other two lines, so all three are twofold symmetry axes.

Solution 3 (A congruence that swaps the vertices)

(2) means equal opposite edges. The faces $ABC,ABD,ACD,BCD$ have perimeters $AB+AC+BC$, $AB+AD+BD$, $AC+AD+CD$, $BC+BD+CD$. If they are equal, the first two give $AC-BD=AD-BC$ and the last two give $AC-BD=BC-AD$; hence $AD=BC$, then $AC=BD$, and the first and the last give $AB=CD$. Conversely, under these equalities every face has perimeter $AB+AC+AD$. So (2) is equivalent to \[AB=CD,\qquad AC=BD,\qquad AD=BC. \tag{$*$}\]

(1) $\Rightarrow$ $(*)$. The six edge midpoints are distinct: edges with a common vertex have different midpoints, and if $AB$ and $CD$ had a common midpoint, $ACBD$ would be a parallelogram and the vertices would be coplanar. Let $M,N$ be the midpoints of $AB$ and $CD$, and $\rho$ the half-turn about $MN$. By (1) it permutes the vertices; it fixes $M$, so it maps the edge $AB$ to the edge with midpoint $M$, which is $AB$, and likewise $CD$ to $CD$. If $\rho$ fixed $A$, it would fix $B$, the axis would be the line $AB$, and $N\in AB$ would make the vertices coplanar. Hence $\rho$ swaps $A,B$ and likewise $C,D$, so $AC=BD$ and $AD=BC$. The axis through the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$ gives $AB=CD$ in the same way.

$(*)\Rightarrow$ (1). We build the symmetry directly. As shown above, $M\ne N$.

A congruence. Put $\mathbf x_1=B-A$, $\mathbf x_2=C-A$, $\mathbf x_3=D-A$ and $\mathbf y_1=A-B$, $\mathbf y_2=D-B$, $\mathbf y_3=C-B$. By $(*)$, \[|\mathbf x_1|=|\mathbf y_1|=AB,\quad |\mathbf x_2|=AC=BD=|\mathbf y_2|,\quad |\mathbf x_3|=AD=BC=|\mathbf y_3|,\] \[|\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_1|=BC=AD=|\mathbf y_2-\mathbf y_1|,\quad |\mathbf x_3-\mathbf x_1|=BD=AC=|\mathbf y_3-\mathbf y_1|,\quad |\mathbf x_3-\mathbf x_2|=CD=|\mathbf y_3-\mathbf y_2|.\] Since $\mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf x_j=\frac12\bigl(|\mathbf x_i|^2+|\mathbf x_j|^2-|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j|^2\bigr)$, we get $\mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf x_j=\mathbf y_i\cdot\mathbf y_j$ for all $i,j$. The vectors $\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2,\mathbf x_3$ are linearly independent (the tetrahedron is not flat), so there is a linear map $T$ with $T\mathbf x_i=\mathbf y_i$; it preserves the dot products of basis vectors, hence of all vectors, so $T$ is orthogonal. The map $\varphi(X)=B+T(X-A)$ is therefore an isometry, and \[\varphi(A)=B,\qquad \varphi(B)=B+\mathbf y_1=A,\qquad \varphi(C)=B+\mathbf y_2=D,\qquad \varphi(D)=B+\mathbf y_3=C .\]

It is a half-turn about $MN$.

  • $\varphi\circ\varphi$ is an affine map fixing $A,B,C,D$, which are not coplanar; so $\varphi\circ\varphi$ is the identity.

  • An affine map preserves midpoints, so $\varphi(M)=M$ and $\varphi(N)=N$, and $T(N-M)=\varphi(N)-\varphi(M)=N-M$.

  • $\det T=1$: $\det(\mathbf y_1,\mathbf y_2,\mathbf y_3)$ and $\det(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2,\mathbf x_3)$ are $6$ times the signed volumes of the ordered tetrahedra $BADC$ and $ABCD$; exchanging two vertices changes the sign, and $BADC$ arises from $ABCD$ by two exchanges. So the two determinants are equal.

Take $M$ as origin and an orthonormal basis $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$ with $\mathbf e_1$ along $MN$. Then $T\mathbf e_1=\mathbf e_1$, and the orthogonal map $T$ maps the plane spanned by $\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$ onto itself, with determinant $1$ there; so it acts on this plane as a rotation by some angle $\theta$, and $\varphi$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about the line $MN$. As $\varphi\circ\varphi$ is the identity, $2\theta$ is a multiple of $360^\circ$, and as $\varphi(A)=B\ne A$, $\theta\ne0^\circ$. Hence $\theta=180^\circ$: the half-turn about $MN$ maps the tetrahedron onto itself.

The same argument for the pairs $AC,BD$ and $AD,BC$ (rename the vertices) shows that all three lines joining midpoints of opposite edges are twofold symmetry axes, which is (1).

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Luna and Marie play the following game:

A number is written on the board. On each turn, a player adds one of its divisors (but not the number itself) to the number on the board and replaces the number on the board with the resulting sum.

The player who is forced to write a number greater than $2026$ loses the game.

Initially, the number $2$ is written on the board, and Luna starts. Who can force a win?

Show the answer

Luna can force a win.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A parity strategy with one exception)

Moves are always available: $1$ is a proper divisor of every number $N\ge2$, so the move $N\to N+1$ exists, and a player is forced to exceed $2026$ only when even $N+1>2026$, i.e. when $N\ge2026$. The numbers strictly increase, so the game ends.

Observation. An odd number has only odd divisors, so from an odd $N$ every move leads to the even number $N+d$.

Luna's strategy. Whenever the board shows an even number $E$, Luna writes $E+1$ if $E\le2022$, and $E+2$ (allowed, since $2\mid E$) if $E=2024$.

We claim that with this strategy Luna always faces an even number $E\le2024$, and Marie always faces an odd number $O\le2023$. At the start Luna faces $2$. If Luna faces an even $E\le2022$, she writes the odd number $E+1\le2023$. If Marie faces an odd $O\le2023$, she must write $O+d$ with $d$ an odd proper divisor of $O$; this number is even. It is not $2026$: from $O+d=2026$ we would get $d\mid2026=2\cdot1013$, where $1013$ is prime (it has no prime divisor up to $31$, and $32^2>1013$), and $d$ is odd, so $d\in\{1,1013\}$; $d=1$ gives $O=2025>2023$, and $d=1013$ gives $O=1013=d$, not a proper divisor. Hence Marie writes an even number at most $2024$ (or a number larger than $2026$, losing at once). If Luna faces $2024$, she writes $2026$, and Marie, facing $2026$, is forced to write a number larger than $2026$ and loses.

Luna is never forced to exceed $2026$ (she always has the move described, which stays $\le2026$). Since the game is finite, it ends with Marie forced to exceed $2026$. So Luna can force a win.

Solution 2 (Classifying all positions backwards)

Call a number $N$ (with $2\le N\le2026$) a win if the player who must move from $N$ can force a win, and a loss otherwise. From $N$ the move $N+1$ is always available while $N\le2025$; a player at $2026$ has no move staying $\le2026$.

Claim. The losses are exactly the odd numbers $N\le2023$ together with $N=2026$; all other numbers (the even numbers $\le2024$, and $2025$) are wins.

We prove this by downward induction on $N$.

  • $N=2026$: every move exceeds $2026$, so it is a loss.

  • $N=2025$: the move $+1$ gives $2026$, a loss for the opponent; so $2025$ is a win.

  • $N=2024$: the move $+2$ gives $2026$; a win.

  • $N\le2023$ odd: there is a move ($N+1\le2024$). Every proper divisor $d$ of $N$ is odd, so every move gives an even number $N+d$. It is not $2026$: that would force $d\mid2026=2\cdot1013$ with $1013$ prime (no prime divisor up to $31$, and $32^2>1013$) and $d$ odd, so $d=1$ and $N=2025$, or $d=1013=N$, both impossible. So each move gives either an even number $\le2024$, which is a win for the opponent by induction, or a number above $2026$, which loses at once. Hence $N$ is a loss.

  • $N\le2022$ even: the move $+1$ gives an odd number $\le2023$, a loss for the opponent; so $N$ is a win.

Luna starts at $2$, an even number at most $2024$, i.e. a win. So Luna wins; her winning moves are: add $1$ from an even number $\le2022$, add $2$ from $2024$.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Prove that the representation of $2026^{2026}$ in base $3$ ends with the base $3$ representation of $2026$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Lifting the exponent)

Since $3^6=729\le2026<2187=3^7$, the number $2026$ has exactly $7$ digits in base $3$ (namely $2026=2210001_3$). The base-$3$ representation of $2026^{2026}$ (which certainly has more than $7$ digits) ends with these $7$ digits if and only if \[2026^{2026}\equiv2026\pmod{3^7},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad 3^7\mid 2026\,\bigl(2026^{2025}-1\bigr).\] As $3\nmid2026$, this means $3^7\mid2026^{2025}-1$.

Now $2026-1=2025=3^4\cdot25$, so $3\mid 2026-1$, and the lifting-the-exponent lemma for the odd prime $3$ gives \[v_3\bigl(2026^{2025}-1^{2025}\bigr)=v_3(2026-1)+v_3(2025)=4+4=8\ge7 .\] (Proof of this special case: for $u=2026$, $u^{25}-1=(u-1)(1+u+\dots+u^{24})$ and the second factor is $\equiv25\not\equiv0\pmod3$, so $v_3(u^{25}-1)=4$. If $w\equiv1\pmod3$, say $w=1+3h$, then $w^3-1=(w-1)(w^2+w+1)$ with $w^2+w+1=3(1+3h+3h^2)$, so $v_3(w^3-1)=v_3(w-1)+1$. Since $2025=25\cdot3^4$, cubing $u^{25}$ four times gives $v_3(u^{2025}-1)=4+4=8$.) Hence $3^7\mid2026^{2025}-1$, and the base-$3$ representation of $2026^{2026}$ ends with $2210001$, the representation of $2026$. (In fact $3^8$ divides the difference, so even the last eight digits are $02210001$.)

Solution 2 (The binomial theorem)

As $3^6\le2026<3^7$, the base-$3$ representation of $2026$ has $7$ digits, and the claim is equivalent to $2026^{2026}\equiv2026\pmod{3^7}$; since $\gcd(2026,3)=1$, it suffices to show $2026^{2025}\equiv1\pmod{3^7}$.

Write $2026=1+2025$ with $2025=3^4\cdot25$. By the binomial theorem, \[2026^{2025}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{2025}\binom{2025}{k}2025^{k}.\] For $k=1$ the term is $2025\cdot2025=3^8\cdot625$. For $k\ge2$ the term is a multiple of $2025^k$, hence of $3^{4k}$ with $4k\ge8$. So every term of the sum is divisible by $3^8$, and $2026^{2025}\equiv1\pmod{3^8}$. In particular $2026^{2026}\equiv2026\pmod{3^7}$, which says exactly that the last seven base-$3$ digits of $2026^{2026}$ are those of $2026$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $k,m,n$ be positive integers. Prove that: for a set with $n$ elements, there exist $m$ subsets of it, no two of which are equal, such that any two of them have fewer than $k$ common elements, if and only if \[m\le\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n}{j}.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Shrink every large set to $k$ elements)

The $m$ subsets are distinct by hypothesis. Let $S$ be the $n$-element set and $\mathcal F_k$ the family of all subsets of $S$ with at most $k$ elements; it has $\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom nj$ members (terms with $j>n$ are $0$).

If $m\le\sum_{j=0}^k\binom nj$, the subsets exist. Two distinct members $X,Y$ of $\mathcal F_k$ have fewer than $k$ common elements: if $|X|<k$ or $|Y|<k$, then $|X\cap Y|\le\min(|X|,|Y|)<k$, and if $|X|=|Y|=k$ then $X\cap Y$ is a proper subset of $X$, so $|X\cap Y|\le k-1$. Any $m$ members of $\mathcal F_k$ therefore work.

If the subsets exist, then $m\le\sum_{j=0}^k\binom nj$. Let $X_1,\dots,X_m$ be distinct subsets, any two with fewer than $k$ common elements. Define $\varphi(X)=X$ if $|X|\le k$, and otherwise let $\varphi(X)$ be any $k$-element subset of $X$. Then $\varphi(X_i)\in\mathcal F_k$ and $\varphi(X_i)\subseteq X_i$. The map is injective: if $\varphi(X_i)=\varphi(X_j)=:Z$ with $i\ne j$, then $Z\subseteq X_i\cap X_j$, so $|Z|<k$; but $|Z|<k$ happens only when $Z=\varphi(X_i)=X_i$ and $Z=\varphi(X_j)=X_j$, contradicting $X_i\ne X_j$. Hence $m\le|\mathcal F_k|=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom nj$.

Both directions together prove the equivalence.

Solution 2 (Induction with Pascal's rule)

Write $T(n,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom nj$, with $\binom nj=0$ for $j>n$ or $j<0$.

Existence for $m\le T(n,k)$. The subsets with at most $k$ elements are $T(n,k)$ in number, and two distinct ones share at most $k-1$ elements (a set with fewer than $k$ elements meets anything in fewer than $k$ elements; two different $k$-sets share at most $k-1$). Choose any $m$ of them.

Bound. We prove, for all integers $n\ge0$ and $k\ge0$: a family $\mathcal F$ of distinct subsets of an $n$-set in which any two members have fewer than $k$ common elements has at most $T(n,k)$ members. Induction on $n$.

  • If $k=0$, no two members can exist (an intersection cannot have fewer than $0$ elements), so $|\mathcal F|\le1=T(n,0)$. If $n=0$, the only subset is $\varnothing$, so $|\mathcal F|\le1\le T(0,k)$.

  • Let $n\ge1$, $k\ge1$ and fix an element $x$. Let $\mathcal F_0$ be the members not containing $x$ and $\mathcal G=\{X\setminus\{x\}: x\in X\in\mathcal F\}$. $\mathcal F_0$ is a family on the $(n-1)$-set $S\setminus\{x\}$ with intersections smaller than $k$, so $|\mathcal F_0|\le T(n-1,k)$. The members of $\mathcal G$ are distinct and, for $X,Y\ni x$, $(X\setminus\{x\})\cap(Y\setminus\{x\})=(X\cap Y)\setminus\{x\}$ has fewer than $k-1$ elements; so $|\mathcal G|\le T(n-1,k-1)$.

Hence, by Pascal's rule $\binom{n-1}{j}+\binom{n-1}{j-1}=\binom nj$, \[|\mathcal F|=|\mathcal F_0|+|\mathcal G|\le\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n-1}{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\Bigl[\binom{n-1}{j}+\binom{n-1}{j-1}\Bigr]=T(n,k).\] This completes the induction and the proof of the equivalence.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement

One considers an equilateral triangle with area $1$ and a real number $0<q<1$. A point $P$ that is part of the triangle is colored green if and only if $P$ lies in every triangle of area $q$ whose vertices are part of the given triangle. Calculate the area of the green-colored region.

Note: “Part of the triangle” means the inner part of the triangle and the sides of the triangle.

Show the answer

$0$ if $q\le\frac12$, and $\dfrac{2(2q-1)^2}{(1+q)(2-q)}$ if $\frac12<q<1$

Show the solution

Solution (The largest triangle that misses a point)

Let the given triangle be $T=ABC$ (area $1$). For a point $P$ inside $T$ let $\alpha=[PBC]$, $\beta=[PCA]$, $\gamma=[PAB]$, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$ (areal coordinates; $P=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C$), and let $M=\max(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, $m=\min(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$. Points of the sides of $T$ form a set of area $0$ and can be ignored. All triangles below have their vertices in $T$.

Step 1. Every triangle $\Delta$ not containing $P$ has area at most $\dfrac M{M+m}$. A closed triangle not containing $P$ lies strictly on one side of some line through $P$ (take a line separating $P$ from the convex set $\Delta$ and move it parallel until it passes through $P$). So $\Delta\subseteq K$, where $K$ is one of the two pieces into which a line $\ell$ through $P$ cuts $T$. For fixed $Y,Z$ the area $[XYZ]$ is the absolute value of an affine function of $X$, hence a convex function of $X$; on a convex polygon it is largest at a vertex. Replacing $X$, then $Y$, then $Z$ by a suitable vertex without decreasing the area, we see that a largest triangle inside a convex polygon can be taken with its vertices at vertices of the polygon. We check all such triangles.

If $\ell$ passes through a vertex, say $A$, and meets $BC$ at $D$, the pieces are $ABD$ and $ADC$, of areas $\frac{\gamma}{\beta+\gamma}$ and $\frac{\beta}{\beta+\gamma}$ (as $BD:DC=\gamma:\beta$), both at most $\frac M{M+m}$, because $\frac{x}{x+y}=\frac1{1+y/x}\le\frac1{1+m/M}=\frac{M}{M+m}$ whenever $0<x\le M$ and $y\ge m$.

Otherwise $\ell$ meets two sides, say $AB$ at $X$ and $AC$ at $Y$; write $AX=x\cdot AB$, $AY=y\cdot AC$ ($0<x,y\le1$) and $P=(1-t)X+tY$ with $0<t<1$. Then $\beta=(1-t)x$, $\gamma=ty$ and $\alpha=(1-t)(1-x)+t(1-y)$.

  • The piece $XBCY$ (a quadrilateral, or a triangle if $X=B$ or $Y=C$): its vertex triangles have areas $[XBC]=1-x$, $[YBC]=1-y$, $[XBY]=y(1-x)\le1-x$, $[XCY]=x(1-y)\le1-y$. Moreover \[1-x\le\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\iff(1-x)\bigl[(1-t)+t(1-y)\bigr]\le(1-t)(1-x)+t(1-y)\iff t(1-y)(1-x)\le t(1-y),\] which is true; so $1-x\le\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}\le\frac M{M+m}$, and symmetrically $1-y\le\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\gamma}\le\frac M{M+m}$.

  • The piece $AXY$ has area $xy$. In the affine coordinates with origin $A$ and unit vectors $\vec{AB},\vec{AC}$ we have $P=(\beta,\gamma)$ and the condition $P\in XY$ reads $\frac\beta x+\frac\gamma y=1$, i.e. $y=\frac{\gamma x}{x-\beta}$. So $xy=\frac{\gamma x^2}{x-\beta}$, whose derivative $\frac{\gamma x(x-2\beta)}{(x-\beta)^2}$ changes sign only once, from $-$ to $+$; hence $xy$ is largest at an end of the admissible range $\frac\beta{1-\gamma}\le x\le1$ (here $x>\beta$, and $y\le1$ means $x\ge\frac\beta{1-\gamma}$), that is, when $x=1$ ($X=B$) or $y=1$ ($Y=C$). These give $xy=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha+\gamma}$ and $xy=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}$, both $\le\frac M{M+m}$.

Step 2. Areas just below $\frac M{M+m}$ do occur. Say $\alpha=M$, $\gamma=m$. The line $BP$ meets $AC$ at $E$ with $AE:EC=\gamma:\alpha$, so $[BEC]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\gamma}=\frac M{M+m}$ and $P$ lies on the side $BE$. Moving $E$ slightly towards $C$ gives triangles that miss $P$ with areas arbitrarily close to $\frac M{M+m}$; shrinking a triangle by a homothety centred at one of its vertices keeps it inside itself, so every smaller positive area occurs too. Consequently: if $\frac M{M+m}>q$, some triangle of area $q$ misses $P$; if $\frac M{M+m}<q$, every triangle of area $q$ contains $P$. The points with $\frac M{M+m}=q$ lie on finitely many segments and have area $0$. So the green area equals the area of \[G_q=\Bigl\{P:\ \frac{M}{M+m}\le q\Bigr\}=\{P:\ M\le\lambda m\},\qquad \lambda=\frac q{1-q}.\]

Step 3. The area of $G_q$. If $q<\frac12$ then $\lambda<1$ and $M\le\lambda m<m$ is impossible: $G_q=\varnothing$. If $q=\frac12$, $G_q$ is the centroid alone. Let $q>\frac12$, so $\lambda>1$. By symmetry $G_q$ consists of six congruent parts, one in each region $\alpha\ge\beta\ge\gamma$ etc. The region $\alpha\ge\beta\ge\gamma$ is the triangle with vertices $G=(\frac13,\frac13,\frac13)$, $A=(1,0,0)$ and the midpoint $(\frac12,\frac12,0)$ of $AB$; there the condition is $\alpha\le\lambda\gamma$. The line $\alpha=\lambda\gamma$ meets the side $GA$ (where $\beta=\gamma$) at $U=\frac{(\lambda,1,1)}{\lambda+2}$ and the side from $G$ to the midpoint (where $\alpha=\beta$) at $W=\frac{(\lambda,\lambda,1)}{2\lambda+1}$, and the part containing $G$ is the triangle $GUW$. Since $[ABC]=1$, the area of a triangle equals the absolute value of the determinant of the areal coordinates of its vertices: \[[GUW]=\frac{1}{3(\lambda+2)(2\lambda+1)}\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\ \lambda&1&1\\ \lambda&\lambda&1\end{pmatrix}\right|=\frac{(\lambda-1)^2}{3(\lambda+2)(2\lambda+1)}.\] Hence the green area is $\frac{2(\lambda-1)^2}{(\lambda+2)(2\lambda+1)}$. With $\lambda=\frac q{1-q}$: $\lambda-1=\frac{2q-1}{1-q}$, $\lambda+2=\frac{2-q}{1-q}$, $2\lambda+1=\frac{1+q}{1-q}$, so the green area is \[\boxed{\dfrac{2(2q-1)^2}{(1+q)(2-q)}}\quad\text{for }q>\tfrac12,\qquad\text{and }0\text{ for }q\le\tfrac12 .\] (Check: it tends to $1$ as $q\to1$ and to $0$ as $q\to\frac12$.) In the figure the green hexagon is $G_q$ for $q=\frac34$; the grey triangle $BCZ$ has area $q$ and one side of the hexagon lies on $CZ$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 43: Locus, Transformation, and Construction Geometry

A mapping $f$ of the set of points of the plane into itself has the following two properties:

  • (1) There are no two distinct points $P$ and $Q$ with $f(P)=f(Q)$.

  • (2) The mapping $f$ maps every rectangle $R$ of the plane onto a rectangle $R'$ of the plane, i.e., the set of image points $f(P)$ of all points $P$ of the rectangle $R$ is the set of all points of $R'$.

Determine whether $f$ then also maps every square onto a square, and whether every point of the plane is an image point $f(P)$ of a point $P$ of the plane.

Note: Every rectangle has positive area and consists of its sides and its inner points.

Show the answer

Yes (squares go to squares) and yes (every point is an image point).

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Segments, then squares, then midpoints)

By the note in the statement, every rectangle has positive area and is closed (sides included). We use only injectivity and property (2).

Step 1: $f$ maps every segment $[P,Q]$ onto the segment $[f(P),f(Q)]$. Let $P\ne Q$. The two rectangles $R_1,R_2$ with common side $PQ$ lying on opposite sides of line $PQ$ (say squares) satisfy $R_1\cap R_2=[P,Q]$. As $f$ is injective, $f([P,Q])=f(R_1)\cap f(R_2)$, an intersection of two closed rectangles; so $C(P,Q):=f([P,Q])$ is a compact convex set.

Let $X$ be a point strictly between $P$ and $Q$ and put $A=C(P,X)$, $B=C(X,Q)$, $z=f(X)$. Then $A\cup B=C(P,Q)$ is convex and $A\cap B=\{z\}$ (injectivity). For $a\in A$, $b\in B$ the segment $[a,b]$ lies in $A\cup B$ and is the union of the two closed sets $[a,b]\cap A\ni a$ and $[a,b]\cap B\ni b$; a segment is connected, so these sets meet, and their common point is $z$. Thus $z\in[a,b]$ for all $a\in A$, $b\in B$. Taking $a=f(P)$, $b=f(Q)$ gives $f(X)\in[f(P),f(Q)]$. Hence $f([P,Q])\subseteq[f(P),f(Q)]$, and since $f([P,Q])$ is convex and contains $f(P),f(Q)$, equality holds.

Step 2: rectangles go to rectangles vertex to vertex. For a triangle, $f(\triangle ABC)=\bigcup_{X\in[B,C]}[f(A),f(X)]=\triangle f(A)f(B)f(C)$ by Step 1. For a rectangle $R=ABCD=\triangle ABC\cup\triangle ACD$ we get $f(R)=\operatorname{conv}\{f(A),f(B),f(C),f(D)\}$. This is the rectangle $R'$ of (2), and the corners of $R'$, being extreme points of this convex hull, are among $f(A),\dots,f(D)$; so these four points are exactly the corners. The centre $O$ of $R$ lies on both diagonals, so $f(O)$ lies on $[f(A),f(C)]$ and on $[f(B),f(D)]$ and is not a corner. Two segments joining corners of a rectangle meet in a non-corner point only if they are the two diagonals. So $f(A)f(B)f(C)f(D)$ is the rectangle $R'$ with its vertices in this order. In particular, $f$ preserves right angles: if $\angle XYZ=90^\circ$, complete $XYZ$ to a rectangle; then $\angle f(X)f(Y)f(Z)=90^\circ$.

Step 3: squares go to squares, and midpoints to midpoints. Let $APBP'$ be a square with centre $M$, so its diagonals $AB$ and $PP'$ meet at $M$ at a right angle. By Step 2, $f(A)f(P)f(B)f(P')$ is a rectangle; its diagonals $[f(A),f(B)]$ and $[f(P),f(P')]$ meet at $f(M)$, and $\angle f(A)f(M)f(P)=90^\circ$ because $\angle AMP=90^\circ$. A rectangle with perpendicular diagonals is a square. So every square is mapped onto a square, and since the diagonals of a square bisect each other, $f(M)$ is the midpoint of $f(A)f(B)$. Every segment is a diagonal of a square, so $f$ maps the midpoint of any segment $XY$ to the midpoint of $f(X)f(Y)$.

Step 4: $f$ is onto. Fix a point $O$ and let $Q_k$ be the square with centre $O$ and corners $O+2^k\mathbf v_i$ ($i=1,2,3,4$) for a fixed square with corners $O+\mathbf v_i$. Since $O+2^k\mathbf v_i$ is the midpoint of $O$ and $O+2^{k+1}\mathbf v_i$, Step 3 gives $f(O+2^{k+1}\mathbf v_i)-f(O)=2\bigl(f(O+2^k\mathbf v_i)-f(O)\bigr)$. Hence $f(Q_k)$ is the image of the square $f(Q_0)$ under the homothety with centre $f(O)$ and ratio $2^k$; and $f(O)$ is the centre of $f(Q_0)$ ($O$ is the midpoint of a diagonal). So $f(Q_0)$ contains a disc of some radius $r>0$ around $f(O)$, $f(Q_k)$ contains the disc of radius $2^kr$, and these discs exhaust the plane. Hence every point of the plane is an image point.

Remark. Step 3 also gives $f\bigl(\frac{X+Y}2\bigr)=\frac{f(X)+f(Y)}2$; with Step 1 (betweenness on lines) this forces $f$ to be affine, and an affine map that preserves right angles is a similarity.

Solution 2 (Midpoints make $f$ a similarity)

Segments and rectangles. Let $P\ne Q$. The two squares with side $PQ$ on opposite sides of line $PQ$ meet exactly in $[P,Q]$, so by injectivity $f([P,Q])$ is the intersection of two rectangles, a compact convex set. Let $X$ lie strictly between $P$ and $Q$. As $f([P,Q])=f([P,X])\cup f([X,Q])$ is convex, the segment $[f(P),f(Q)]$ is the union of its parts in $f([P,X])$ and in $f([X,Q])$; these are closed and non-empty (they contain $f(P)$ and $f(Q)$), so, as a segment is connected, they meet. By injectivity the only common point of $f([P,X])$ and $f([X,Q])$ is $f(X)$, so $f(X)\in[f(P),f(Q)]$. Hence $f$ maps $[P,Q]$ onto $[f(P),f(Q)]$. Consequently a triangle, the union of the segments from one vertex to the points of the opposite side, is mapped onto the triangle of the images of its vertices, and a rectangle $ABCD=\triangle ABC\cup\triangle ACD$ onto the convex hull of $f(A),f(B),f(C),f(D)$. By (2) this hull is a rectangle; its corners are extreme points of the hull, so they are exactly $f(A),\dots,f(D)$. The images of the diagonals $[A,C]$ and $[B,D]$ meet at the image of the centre, which is not a corner (injectivity), and two segments joining corners of a rectangle meet at a non-corner point only if they are its diagonals. So $f(A)f(B)f(C)f(D)$ is a rectangle with the vertices in this order. In particular $f$ preserves right angles: if $\angle XYZ=90^\circ$, complete $X,Y,Z$ to a rectangle; then $\angle f(X)f(Y)f(Z)=90^\circ$.

Step A: $f$ preserves midpoints. Let $X\ne Y$ and let $XUYW$ be a square with diagonal $XY$; its centre $O$ is the midpoint of $XY$. Then $f(X)f(U)f(Y)f(W)$ is a rectangle, and $f(O)$ lies on both of its diagonals $[f(X),f(Y)]$ and $[f(U),f(W)]$. So $f(O)$ is the centre of this rectangle, the midpoint of $f(X)f(Y)$: \[f\Bigl(\frac{X+Y}2\Bigr)=\frac{f(X)+f(Y)}2\qquad\text{for all points }X,Y\] (trivially also for $X=Y$). Here points are identified with their position vectors from a fixed origin $0$.

Step B: $f$ is affine. Let $g(X)=f(X)-f(0)$; then $g(0)=0$ and $g$ also preserves midpoints. With $Y=0$ this gives $g\bigl(\frac X2\bigr)=\frac{g(X)}2$, hence $g(X+Y)=2g\bigl(\frac{X+Y}2\bigr)=g(X)+g(Y)$. From additivity, $g(kX)=kg(X)$ for integers $k$ and then $g(qX)=qg(X)$ for rationals $q$. The square $Q_0$ with vertices $(\pm1,\pm1)$ is mapped onto a rectangle, so $|g(X)|\le c$ on $Q_0$ for some constant $c$. If $X\in\frac1kQ_0$ for a positive integer $k$, then $kX\in Q_0$ and $|g(X)|=\frac1k|g(kX)|\le\frac ck$. So $g$ is continuous at $0$, and by $g(X)-g(Y)=g(X-Y)$ it is continuous everywhere. For real $\lambda$ choose rationals $q_n\to\lambda$; then $g(\lambda X)=\lim g(q_nX)=\lim q_ng(X)=\lambda g(X)$. Thus $g$ is linear.

As $f$ is injective, so is the linear map $g$; an injective linear map of the plane is invertible, so $g$ and hence $f$ is onto: every point of the plane is an image point.

Step C: $f$ is a similarity. Let $\mathbf e_1=(1,0)$, $\mathbf e_2=(0,1)$ and $\mathbf u=g(\mathbf e_1)$, $\mathbf w=g(\mathbf e_2)$; both are non-zero. The angle $\mathbf e_1,0,\mathbf e_2$ is right, so the angle $f(\mathbf e_1),f(0),f(\mathbf e_2)$ is right: $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w=0$. The vectors $\mathbf e_1+\mathbf e_2$ and $\mathbf e_1-\mathbf e_2$ are also perpendicular, so $(\mathbf u+\mathbf w)\cdot(\mathbf u-\mathbf w)=0$, i.e. $|\mathbf u|=|\mathbf w|=:\kappa$. Hence $|g(x\mathbf e_1+y\mathbf e_2)|^2=|x\mathbf u+y\mathbf w|^2=\kappa^2(x^2+y^2)$, so $|f(X)-f(Y)|=|g(X-Y)|=\kappa|X-Y|$ for all $X,Y$: $f$ is a similarity with ratio $\kappa$. A square $ABCD$ with side $a$ is mapped onto the rectangle $f(A)f(B)f(C)f(D)$ (see the first paragraph), whose sides all have length $\kappa a$; so it is a square, and every square is mapped onto a square.
