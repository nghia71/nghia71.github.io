Problem 8.1

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change

The “Red Brick” collective farm and the “Green Rostock” farm employ a total of $100$ workers. After tractor driver Petr transferred from Red Brick to Green Rostock, the average salary at both enterprises increased by $1$ ruble, although Petr's salary remained the same. How much higher is the average salary at Red Brick after Petr left than the average salary at Green Rostock before he arrived?

Show the answer

$101$ rubles

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Petr's salary from both sides)

Before the transfer let Red Brick have $r$ workers with average salary $A$, and Green Rostock $g$ workers with average salary $B$, so $r+g=100$; let $p$ be Petr's salary. (Red Brick still has workers after Petr leaves, since its new average exists, so $r\ge 2$.)

At Red Brick the remaining $r-1$ workers have total salary $rA-p$ and average $A+1$: \[rA-p=(r-1)(A+1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=A-r+1=(A+1)-r.\] At Green Rostock the $g+1$ workers have total salary $gB+p$ and average $B+1$: \[gB+p=(g+1)(B+1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=B+g+1.\] Equating the two expressions for $p$ gives $(A+1)-B=r+g+1=101$.

The average salary at Red Brick after Petr left, $A+1$, exceeds the average at Green Rostock before he arrived, $B$, by $\boxed{101}$ rubles.

Solution 2 (The total payroll does not change)

Before the transfer let Red Brick have $r$ workers with average salary $A$ and Green Rostock $g$ workers with average salary $B$, so $r+g=100$. Petr's salary does not change, so the total salary of all $100$ workers is the same before and after the transfer: \[rA+gB=(r-1)(A+1)+(g+1)(B+1).\] Expanding the right-hand side gives $rA+r-A-1+gB+g+B+1$, so $0=r+g-A+B$, that is, $A-B=r+g=100$.

The required difference is $(A+1)-B=\boxed{101}$ rubles. (Petr's salary is not even needed.)

Problem 8.2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Given a convex hexagon $ABCDEF$ such that $AB\parallel CF$, $CD\parallel BE$ and $EF\parallel AD$. Diagonals $AD$ and $BE$ intersect at $X$, $BE$ and $CF$ at $Y$, $CF$ and $AD$ at $Z$. It turned out that the midpoints of segments $XY$ and $BE$ coincide, and the midpoints of segments $YZ$ and $CF$ coincide. Prove that the midpoints of segments $XZ$ and $AD$ coincide.

Show the solution

Solution (Three homotheties)

We write $\vec{U}$ for the position vector of a point $U$.

The degenerate case. If two of $X,Y,Z$ coincide, say $X=Y$, then this point lies on $AD$ and on $CF$, so $Z=X$ as well (the lines $AD$ and $CF$ are different). We treat the case $X=Y=Z=:O$ at the end.

The main case. Let $X,Y,Z$ be distinct. They are not collinear (otherwise the lines $BE$ and $CF$ would both be the line $XYZ$), so $XYZ$ is a triangle, and $A,D$ lie on line $XZ$, $B,E$ on line $XY$, $C,F$ on line $YZ$.

Since $A\in XZ$, $B\in XY$ and $AB\parallel YZ$, triangle $XAB$ is the image of triangle $XZY$ under a homothety with centre $X$: there is a real number $\alpha$ with \[\vec A=\vec X+\alpha(\vec Z-\vec X),\qquad \vec B=\vec X+\alpha(\vec Y-\vec X).\] In the same way $CD\parallel BE=XY$ (centre $Z$) and $EF\parallel AD=XZ$ (centre $Y$) give real numbers $\gamma,\varepsilon$ with \[\vec C=\vec Z+\gamma(\vec Y-\vec Z),\quad \vec D=\vec Z+\gamma(\vec X-\vec Z),\qquad \vec E=\vec Y+\varepsilon(\vec X-\vec Y),\quad \vec F=\vec Y+\varepsilon(\vec Z-\vec Y).\] Two segments have the same midpoint exactly when the sums of the position vectors of their endpoints are equal. Now \[\vec B+\vec E=\vec X+\vec Y+(\alpha-\varepsilon)(\vec Y-\vec X),\qquad \vec C+\vec F=\vec Y+\vec Z+(\gamma-\varepsilon)(\vec Y-\vec Z).\] The midpoints of $XY$ and $BE$ coincide, and $\vec Y\neq\vec X$, so $\alpha=\varepsilon$; the midpoints of $YZ$ and $CF$ coincide, so $\gamma=\varepsilon$. Hence $\alpha=\gamma$ and \[\vec A+\vec D=\vec X+\vec Z+(\alpha-\gamma)(\vec Z-\vec X)=\vec X+\vec Z,\] so the midpoints of $XZ$ and $AD$ coincide.

The case $X=Y=Z=O$. Put $O$ at the origin and write $a,b,\dots,f$ for the position vectors of $A,\dots,F$. The hypotheses say that $O$ is the midpoint of $BE$ and of $CF$: $e=-b$, $f=-c$; the vectors $b,c$ are linearly independent because $BE$ and $CF$ are different lines. As $A,O,D$ are collinear, $a=\lambda d$ for some real $\lambda$. From $CD\parallel BE$: $c-d=\mu b$, so $d=c-\mu b$. From $EF\parallel AD$: $f-e=b-c$ is parallel to $d$, so $c-\mu b=\nu(b-c)$ for some $\nu$; comparing coefficients, $\nu=-1$, $\mu=1$, so $d=c-b$. From $AB\parallel CF$: $a-b=\kappa c$, so $b+\kappa c=\lambda(c-b)$, giving $\lambda=-1$. Thus $a=-d$, and $O$ is the midpoint of $AD$ (and of $XZ$, which is the single point $O$).

In all cases the midpoints of $XZ$ and $AD$ coincide. (The computation shows more: any two of the three midpoint conditions imply the third.)

Problem 8.3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Vertices of a regular $n$-gon are colored in white and black. In one move it is allowed to invert the color of any three vertices which form an isosceles triangle (white becomes black, black becomes white). Find all $n\ge 3$ such that starting from any coloring of the $n$-gon it is possible to make all vertices white in finitely many moves.

Show the answer

all $n\ge 4$ except $n=6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Flipping one vertex with three moves)

Number the vertices $0,1,\dots,n-1$ around the polygon and read indices modulo $n$. Any three distinct vertices of a regular polygon form a triangle, and $\{x-d,\,x,\,x+d\}$ (three distinct vertices) is an isosceles triangle with apex $x$.

The order of the moves does not matter: a vertex ends up with its original colour or the opposite one according to the parity of the number of moves that contain it. So a sequence of moves changes exactly the colours of the symmetric difference of the triangles used. We must decide when every set of vertices can be obtained in this way (the set of black vertices is then turned white).

$n=3$ fails. There is only one triangle. Starting from one black vertex, after any number of moves we have either the starting colouring or its complement; neither is all white.

$n=6$ fails. Three vertices of a regular hexagon cut the boundary into arcs of $1,1,4$, of $2,2,2$ or of $1,2,3$ sides. The last triangle has sides of three different lengths (a side, a short diagonal and a long diagonal), so the isosceles triangles are the six triangles $\{x-1,x,x+1\}$ and the two triangles $\{0,2,4\}$, $\{1,3,5\}$. Each of them contains exactly one of the opposite vertices $2$ and $5$, hence exactly two of the vertices $0,1,3,4$. So every move changes the colours of exactly two of $0,1,3,4$, and the parity of the number of black vertices among $0,1,3,4$ never changes. Starting with only vertex $0$ black, we can never make all vertices white.

$n\ge4$, $n\ne6$, works. Fix a vertex $x$. Since $n\ge4$, the vertices $x-3,x-2,x-1,x$ are distinct, and \[\{x-3,x-2,x-1\}\ \triangle\ \{x-2,x-1,x\}=\{x-3,x\}.\] Since $3\not\equiv0$ and $6\not\equiv0\pmod n$, the vertices $x-3,x,x+3$ are distinct, so $\{x-3,x,x+3\}$ is an isosceles triangle, and \[\{x-3,x\}\ \triangle\ \{x-3,x,x+3\}=\{x+3\}.\] Thus three moves change the colour of the single vertex $x+3$ and nothing else. As $x$ is arbitrary, we can turn each black vertex white separately.

The answer is: all $n\ge4$ with $n\ne6$.

Solution 2 (Generating sets modulo 2)

Call a set $S$ of vertices achievable if some sequence of moves changes the colours of exactly the vertices of $S$. A vertex changes colour exactly when it lies in an odd number of the triangles used, so achievable sets are closed under symmetric difference, every isosceles triangle is achievable, and the answer is “yes” exactly when every set is achievable. For $n=3$ the only achievable sets are $\varnothing$ and the whole triangle. For $n=6$ the isosceles triangles are $\{x-1,x,x+1\}$, $\{0,2,4\}$ and $\{1,3,5\}$, and each meets $\{0,1,3,4\}$ in exactly two vertices, so every achievable set meets $\{0,1,3,4\}$ in an even number of vertices. In both cases $\{0\}$ is not achievable. Let $n\ge4$, $n\ne6$.

Pairs at distance $3$. Since $\{y-1,y,y+1\}\triangle\{y,y+1,y+2\}=\{y-1,y+2\}$, so every pair $\{x,x+3\}$ is achievable.

Case $3\nmid n$. Then $3$ is invertible modulo $n$, so for any two vertices $u\ne v$ we can write $v=u+3m$ with $1\le m<n$, and \[\{u,u+3\}\triangle\{u+3,u+6\}\triangle\dots\triangle\{u+3(m-1),u+3m\}=\{u,v\}.\] So every pair is achievable, and hence every set with an even number of vertices (split it into pairs). If $|S|$ is odd, then $S\triangle\{0,1,2\}$ is even, hence achievable, and $S=(S\triangle\{0,1,2\})\triangle\{0,1,2\}$ is achievable.

Case $3\mid n$, so $n\ge9$. Split the vertices into the three residue classes modulo $3$; each class is the cycle $c,c+3,c+6,\dots$, so, as above, every pair inside one class is achievable, and therefore every set that meets each class in an even number of vertices. Since $n\ge 9$, $\{c-3,c,c+3\}$ consists of three distinct vertices of the class of $c$, so it is an isosceles triangle lying inside one class with an odd number of vertices there. Given any $S$, take the symmetric difference of $S$ with $\{c-3,c,c+3\}$ for every class $c$ that meets $S$ in an odd number of vertices; the result meets every class evenly, so it is achievable, and hence $S$ is achievable.

So the answer is all $n\ge4$ with $n\ne6$.

Problem 8.4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

A positive integer $n$, which can be written as $ab+cd$ for some positive integers $a,b,c,d$, can be replaced by $a+b^2+c^2+d^2$. Is it true that for any $x,y\ge 20$ starting from $x$ it is possible to get $y$ after finitely many such operations?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Two ways up and one way down)

We use three particular operations.

  • (i) For $n\ge2$: $n=(n-1)\cdot1+1\cdot1$ is replaced by $(n-1)+1+1+1=n+2$.

  • (ii) For $n\ge5$: $n=(n-4)\cdot1+2\cdot2$ is replaced by $(n-4)+1+4+4=n+5$.

  • (iii) For odd $n\ge3$: $n=\frac{n-1}2\cdot2+1\cdot1$ is replaced by $\frac{n-1}2+4+1+1=\frac{n+11}2$.

Going up. Every $s\in\{2,4,5,6,7,\dots\}$ has the form $2a+5b$ with $a,b\ge0$ (even $s=2a$; odd $s\ge5$ is $5+2a$). Hence from any $m\ge5$ we can reach $m+s$ for every such $s$, using (i) $a$ times and (ii) $b$ times (the numbers only grow, so (ii) stays allowed).

Going down. Start from $x\ge20$ and repeat the following while the current number $v$ is greater than $16$:

  • if $v$ is odd, apply (iii): $v\mapsto\frac{v+11}2$, which is less than $v$ (as $v>11$) and at least $\frac{17+11}2=14$;

  • if $v$ is even (so $v\ge18$), apply (ii) and then (iii): $v\mapsto v+5\mapsto\frac{v+16}2$, which is less than $v$ (as $v>16$) and at least $17$.

The number strictly decreases at each round, so after finitely many rounds it is at most $16$. The round that brought it to at most $16$ was an odd round, so the final number $m$ satisfies $14\le m\le16$ (and at least one operation was made, since $x>16$).

Finish. For $y\ge20$ we have $y-m\ge4$, so $y$ is reachable from $m$ by going up. Hence $y$ can be obtained from $x$, and the answer is yes.

Problem 8.5

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization

Kolya wants to insert a digit between two adjacent digits of $20252026$ such that the new number is divisible by $33$. Find all digits that he can insert in this way.

Show the answer

only the digit $5$ (the number $202525026=33\cdot6137122$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tests for 3 and for 11)

The new number must be divisible by $3$ and by $11$.

Divisibility by $3$. The digit sum of $20252026$ is $19$, so the inserted digit $d$ must satisfy $3\mid 19+d$: $d\in\{2,5,8\}$.

Divisibility by $11$. Insert $d$ after the first $j$ digits ($1\le j\le7$). Let $A$ be the number formed by the first $j$ digits and $B$ the number formed by the last $k=8-j$ digits. The new number is $A\cdot10^{k+1}+d\cdot10^{k}+B$, and since $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$ it is congruent to $(-1)^{k+1}A+(-1)^kd+B$. This gives the following conditions modulo $11$: \[\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c} j&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\\hline A\bmod 11&2&9&4&1&1&10&3\\ B\bmod 11&5&5&7&2&4&4&6\\ k&7&6&5&4&3&2&1\\\hline d\equiv&7&4&0&10&5&6&9 \end{array}\] (for example, for $j=5$: $A=20252$, $B=26$, $k=3$, and $A-d+B\equiv1-d+4\equiv0$ gives $d\equiv5$). The case $j=4$ needs $d\equiv10$, which is not a digit. The only value in $\{2,5,8\}$ is $d=5$, for $j=5$.

So the only digit is $\boxed{5}$: $202525026=33\cdot6137122$.

Solution 2 (Two-digit blocks modulo 33)

Since $100=3\cdot33+1$, we have $100\equiv1\pmod{33}$, so $10^{2i}\equiv1$ and $10^{2i+1}\equiv10\pmod{33}$.

Insert the digit $d$ after the first $j$ digits and let $A$ be the number formed by the first $j$ digits and $B$ the number formed by the last $k=8-j$ digits: the new number is $N=A\cdot10^{k+1}+d\cdot10^k+B\equiv r_j+c_jd\pmod{33}$, where $c_j\equiv10^k$ is $1$ or $10$, and $r_j$ is the residue of the number obtained by inserting the digit $0$; by $100\equiv1$, a number is congruent modulo $33$ to the sum of its two-digit blocks taken from the right (for $j=5$: $202520026\equiv2+02+52+00+26=82\equiv16$). This gives \[\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c} j&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\\hline r_j&7&7&22&1&16&16&31\\ c_j&10&1&10&1&10&1&10 \end{array}\] Now check $d=0,\dots,9$. For $c_j=1$ ($j=2,4,6$) we need $d\equiv-r_j$, i.e. $d\equiv26,32,17$: no digit. For $c_j=10$ we need $10d\equiv-r_j$; as $10\cdot10=100\equiv1$, this means $d\equiv-10r_j\pmod{33}$, i.e. $d\equiv-70\equiv29$, $-220\equiv11$, $-160\equiv5$, $-310\equiv20$ for $j=1,3,5,7$. Only $j=5$, $d=5$ gives a digit.

Hence the only digit is $\boxed{5}$, giving $202525026$.

Problem 8.6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Given a board $33\times 83$, all cells of which are initially white. In one move we can choose a $1\times 3$ or $3\times 1$ rectangle, all cells of which are white, and recolor its middle cell to black. What is the biggest number of black cells we can get?

Show the answer

$2025$

Show the solution

Solution (A partition into blocks with a white cell)

Number the rows $1,\dots,33$ and the columns $1,\dots,83$, and call the cell in row $i$, column $j$ odd if $i$ and $j$ are both odd. There are $17\cdot42=714$ odd cells and $33\cdot83=2739$ cells in all. We show that the maximum is $2739-714=\boxed{2025}$.

Construction. We blacken all cells except the odd ones, in three rounds.

  • 1. Cells with $i,j$ both even: the cell $(i,j)$ is the middle of the horizontal rectangle $(i,j-1),(i,j),(i,j+1)$, whose end cells have $j\pm1$ odd, $i$ even, so they are still white.

  • 2. Cells with $i$ odd, $j$ even: use the horizontal rectangle $(i,j\pm1)$; its end cells are odd cells, which are never blackened.

  • 3. Cells with $i$ even, $j$ odd: use the vertical rectangle $(i\pm1,j)$; again its end cells are odd cells.

All rectangles fit in the board because an even index is at least $2$ and at most $32$ (rows) or $82$ (columns). This gives $2025$ black cells.

Bound. We need two observations about the final colouring.

  • Every $2\times2$ square contains a white cell. Suppose all four of its cells are black and look at the one blackened last. Its rectangle contains its horizontal neighbour or its vertical neighbour on both sides, and one of these neighbours is in the square; that neighbour was already black, but all three cells of the rectangle had to be white. Contradiction.

  • Two adjacent cells of row $33$ are not both black, and similarly for column $83$. A cell of row $33$ can only be the middle of a horizontal rectangle. If two adjacent cells of row $33$ were black, the rectangle of the later one contains the earlier one, which was already black. The corner $(33,83)$ is never the middle of a rectangle, so it stays white.

Now split the board into blocks: the $16\cdot41$ squares formed by rows $2a-1,2a$ and columns $2b-1,2b$ ($a\le16$, $b\le41$), the $41$ dominoes in row $33$ formed by columns $2b-1,2b$, the $16$ dominoes in column $83$ formed by rows $2a-1,2a$, and the corner cell. That is $656+41+16+1=714$ blocks, and each contains a white cell. So at least $714$ cells are white and at most $2739-714=2025$ are black.

Problem 8.7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation

Given a sequence of at least three irreducible fractions, where every next fraction is larger than the previous one. The first fraction is $\frac{1}{3}$, while the last is $\frac{1}{2}$. The difference of any two consecutive fractions equals to $1$ divided by the product of their denominators. Prove that the sequence contains the fraction $\frac{2}{5}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A determinant identity)

Write every fraction in lowest terms with positive denominator. For consecutive fractions $\frac pq<\frac{p'}{q'}$ the condition $\frac{p'}{q'}-\frac pq=\frac1{qq'}$ means \[p'q-pq'=1.\] The fractions increase from $\frac13$ to $\frac12$, so all of them lie in $[\frac13,\frac12]$.

Suppose $\frac25$ is not in the sequence. Since $\frac13<\frac25<\frac12$, there are consecutive terms $\frac ab<\frac25<\frac cd$. Then $2b-5a$ and $5c-2d$ are positive integers, so both are at least $1$, and \[b(5c-2d)+d(2b-5a)=5(bc-ad)=5.\] Hence $b+d\le5$. A fraction in $[\frac13,\frac25)$ cannot have denominator $1$ or $2$ (the only candidate, $\frac12$, is too large), so $b\ge3$ and $d\le2$. The only fraction in $(\frac25,\frac12]$ with denominator at most $2$ is $\frac12$, so $\frac cd=\frac12$, $d=2$, $b=3$ and $\frac ab=\frac13$. Thus $\frac13$ and $\frac12$ are consecutive terms; since $\frac13$ is the first term and $\frac12$ the last, the sequence is just $\frac13,\frac12$, contradicting that it has at least three terms. Therefore $\frac25$ belongs to the sequence.

Solution 2 (Removing the largest denominator)

Let the sequence be $\frac{p_0}{q_0}=\frac13,\ \frac{p_1}{q_1},\dots,\frac{p_k}{q_k}=\frac12$ with $k\ge2$, in lowest terms with positive denominators. Since $\frac{p_{i+1}}{q_{i+1}}-\frac{p_i}{q_i}=\frac{p_{i+1}q_i-p_iq_{i+1}}{q_iq_{i+1}}$, the condition says $p_{i+1}q_i-p_iq_{i+1}=1$ for all $i$. Call such a sequence a chain.

Every term strictly between $\frac13$ and $\frac12$ has denominator at least $5$ (denominators $1,2,3,4$ give no such fraction). So the largest denominator in a chain with $k\ge2$ occurs at an inner index $j$ ($0<j<k$). Subtracting the relations for the pairs $(j-1,j)$ and $(j,j+1)$, \[p_jq_{j-1}-p_{j-1}q_j=p_{j+1}q_j-p_jq_{j+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_j(q_{j-1}+q_{j+1})=q_j(p_{j-1}+p_{j+1}).\] As $\gcd(p_j,q_j)=1$, $q_j$ divides $q_{j-1}+q_{j+1}$; write $q_{j-1}+q_{j+1}=tq_j$, so also $p_{j-1}+p_{j+1}=tp_j$, with $t\ge1$ an integer. Since $q_{j\pm1}\le q_j$, we have $t\le2$. If $t=2$, then $q_{j-1}=q_j$ and $1=p_jq_{j-1}-p_{j-1}q_j=q_j(p_j-p_{j-1})$ forces $q_j=1$, which is false. So $t=1$: \[p_j=p_{j-1}+p_{j+1},\qquad q_j=q_{j-1}+q_{j+1},\] the term $\frac{p_j}{q_j}$ is the mediant of its neighbours, and \[p_{j+1}q_{j-1}-p_{j-1}q_{j+1}=(p_j-p_{j-1})q_{j-1}-p_{j-1}(q_j-q_{j-1})=p_jq_{j-1}-p_{j-1}q_j=1.\] So deleting the term $\frac{p_j}{q_j}$ leaves a chain again.

Repeat this deletion as long as the chain has at least three terms. Each step deletes a term of the original sequence and the chain gets shorter, so we end with the chain $\frac13,\frac12$. The term deleted in the last step was the mediant of $\frac13$ and $\frac12$, namely $\frac{1+1}{3+2}=\frac25$. Hence $\frac25$ was a term of the original sequence.

Problem 8.8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $M,N,K$ be the midpoints of sides $BC,CA,AB$ respectively of an equilateral triangle $ABC$. Point $D$ lies in the same halfplane as $C$ with respect to $AM$ such that triangle $AMD$ is equilateral. Segment $BD$ intersects side $AC$ at $P$, and the circumcircle of $APD$ intersects the segment $KN$ at $Q$. Line $CQ$ intersects the side $AB$ at $R$. Determine $\angle PMR$.

Show the answer

$\angle PMR=60^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A rectangle, a power of a point and similar triangles)

Let $a$ be the side of the triangle.

The point $D$. The median $AM$ bisects $\angle BAC$, so $\angle BAM=\angle MAC=30^\circ$. The ray $AD$ makes $60^\circ$ with $AM$ on the side of $C$, so $\angle BAD=90^\circ$ and $AD=AM=\frac{\sqrt3}2a$. The altitude $CK$ also has length $\frac{\sqrt3}2a$ and is perpendicular to $AB$, on the same side as $D$. Hence $ADCK$ is a rectangle: $DC\parallel AB$, $DC=AK=\frac a2$, and its diagonals $AC$ and $DK$ (both of length $a$) bisect each other at $N$. In particular $N$ lies on $DK$, so the line $KN$ is the line $DK$.

The point $P$. As $DC\parallel AB$, triangles $PCD$ and $PAB$ are similar with ratio $\frac{DC}{AB}=\frac12$, so $CP=\frac a3$ and $AP=\frac{2a}3$.

The point $Q$. Since $AN=\frac a2<AP$, the point $N$ lies inside the chord $AP$ of the circle $(APD)$, hence inside the circle. The line $DK$ through $N$ meets the circle at $D$ and at a second point on the other side of $N$, at distance $NQ$ with \[NA\cdot NP=ND\cdot NQ:\qquad \frac a2\cdot\frac a6=\frac a2\cdot NQ,\qquad NQ=\frac a6.\] This point lies on segment $NK$ (as $NK=\frac a2$), so it is $Q$, and $NQ:QK=1:2$.

The point $R$. Apply Menelaus's theorem to triangle $AKN$ and the line $CQR$ ($C$ lies on line $AN$ with $NC=AN$): \[\frac{AR}{RK}\cdot\frac{KQ}{QN}\cdot\frac{NC}{CA}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{AR}{RK}\cdot2\cdot\frac12=1,\] so $R$ is the midpoint of $AK$ and $BR=\frac{3a}4$.

The angle. Now $CP\cdot BR=\frac a3\cdot\frac{3a}4=\frac{a^2}4=CM\cdot BM$, so $\frac{CP}{CM}=\frac{BM}{BR}$. Together with $\angle PCM=\angle MBR=60^\circ$ this shows that triangles $PCM$ and $MBR$ are similar, so $\angle CMP=\angle BRM$. Since $P,R$ lie on the sides $CA$, $AB$ and $M$ on $BC$, \[\angle PMR=180^\circ-\angle CMP-\angle BMR=180^\circ-\angle BRM-\angle BMR=\angle MBR=60^\circ.\] So $\angle PMR=\boxed{60^\circ}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Problem 9.1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities

Prove that for any real $x$, different from $0$ and $\pm 1$, at least one of $x^2+\frac{1}{x}$ and $x+\frac{1}{x^2}$ is not an integer.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The ratio of the two numbers is x)

Suppose that $u=x^2+\frac1x$ and $v=x+\frac1{x^2}$ are both integers. Then \[xu=x^3+1=x^2v.\] If $v=0$ then $x^3=-1$, so $x=-1$, which is excluded. Hence $v\ne0$, and dividing $xu=x^2v$ by $x\ne0$ gives $u=xv$, so $x=\frac uv$ is rational.

Write $x=\frac pq$ with $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $q>0$. Then $u=\frac{p^2}{q^2}+\frac qp$, that is, \[up\,q^2=p^3+q^3.\] So $p\mid q^3$, and since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $p=\pm1$; also $q^2\mid p^3=\pm1$, so $q=1$. Thus $x=\pm1$, a contradiction. Hence at least one of the two numbers is not an integer.

Solution 2 (Through s = x + 1/x)

Suppose $u=x^2+\frac1x$ and $v=x+\frac1{x^2}$ are integers and put $s=x+\frac1x$. As $x$ is real, $|s|\ge2$, with $s=\pm2$ only for $x=\pm1$; so $|s|>2$. We have \[u+v=\Bigl(x^2+\frac1{x^2}\Bigr)+\Bigl(x+\frac1x\Bigr)=s^2+s-2,\qquad uv=x^3+\frac1{x^3}+2=s^3-3s+2.\] So $m=s^2+s=u+v+2$ is an integer, and $s^3=s(m-s)=ms-(m-s)=(m+1)s-m$, which gives $uv=(m-2)(s-1)$.

If $m=2$, then $s^2+s-2=0$, so $s\in\{1,-2\}$, impossible as $|s|>2$. Hence $m\ne2$ and $s=1+\frac{uv}{m-2}$ is rational. But $s$ is a root of the monic integer polynomial $t^2+t-m$, so the rational number $s$ is an integer.

Now $x^2=sx-1$ and $\frac1x=s-x$, so \[u=x^2+\frac1x=(sx-1)+(s-x)=(s-1)(x+1).\] As $s\ne1$, $x=\frac u{s-1}-1$ is rational; it is a root of the monic integer polynomial $t^2-st+1$, so $x$ is an integer, and so is $\frac1x=s-x$. Hence $x=\pm1$, a contradiction.

Problem 9.2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Katya has $10$ cards and a machine. For each $i$ from $1$ to $10$ the $i$-th card contains two numbers: $2i-6$ and $i-6$. Katya can insert two cards into the machine to get one new card instead. If a card contains numbers $a$ and $b$, and another card contains $x$ and $y$, then the new card contains $ax+by$ and $ay+bx$. After several such operations Katya had only one card left. Which numbers can be written on it?

Show the answer

$1814400$ and $-1814400$ (that is, $\pm\frac{10!}2$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sums and differences multiply)

For a card with numbers $a,b$ consider $a+b$ and $a-b$ (the sign of $a-b$ depends on the order in which we read the card). If cards $(a,b)$ and $(x,y)$ give the card $(ax+by,\ ay+bx)$, then \[(ax+by)+(ay+bx)=(a+b)(x+y),\qquad (ax+by)-(ay+bx)=(a-b)(x-y).\] So, whatever the order of the operations, the final card $(u,v)$ satisfies \[u+v=\prod_{i=1}^{10}\bigl((2i-6)+(i-6)\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{10}(3i-12)=0\] (the factor $i=4$ vanishes) and \[|u-v|=\prod_{i=1}^{10}\bigl|(2i-6)-(i-6)\bigr|=\prod_{i=1}^{10}i=10!.\] Hence $\{u,v\}=\{\frac{10!}2,-\frac{10!}2\}$, and the last card contains the numbers $\boxed{1814400}$ and $\boxed{-1814400}$. (Since the result does not depend on the choices, these are the only possible numbers, and they do occur.)

Solution 2 (Following the card that contains card 4)

Card $4$ is $(2,-2)$, a card of the form $(w,-w)$. Combining a card $(w,-w)$ with any card $(x,y)$ gives \[(wx-wy,\ wy-wx)=\bigl(w(x-y),\,-w(x-y)\bigr),\] again of the form $(w',-w')$ with $|w'|=|w|\cdot|x-y|$. The final card arises from card $4$ by such steps, so it is $(W,-W)$, where $|W|$ equals $2$ times the product of the values $|x-y|$ of the cards that were merged into it.

Each merged card is itself the result of merging some of the original cards other than card $4$, and by the identity $(ax+by)-(ay+bx)=(a-b)(x-y)$ its value $|x-y|$ is the product of $|a_i-b_i|=i$ over its original cards. Every original card except card $4$ ends up in exactly one of these merged cards, so \[|W|=2\prod_{i\ne4}i=2\cdot\frac{10!}4=\frac{10!}2=1814400.\] So the last card contains $\boxed{1814400}$ and $\boxed{-1814400}$.

Problem 9.3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

The incircle of triangle $ABC$ with incentre $I$ is tangent to $BC$ at $D$. Segment $AL$ is the angle bisector of triangle $ABC$. Ray $ID$ intersects the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of triangle $ABC$ at $F$. It turned out that $IA=IF$. Prove that $FL$ passes through the centre of $\Gamma$.

  • (F1) $MB=MC=MI$ (incentre–excentre lemma), and $MB=2R\sin\frac A2$;

  • (F2) $AH$ and $AO$ are symmetric with respect to $AI$ (if $B\le90^\circ$ then $\angle BAH=90^\circ-B=\angle CAO$; if $B>90^\circ$ then $H$ lies beyond $B$ and $\angle BAH=B-90^\circ$ on the other side of $AB$, with the same conclusion);

  • (F3) $IA=4R\sin\frac B2\sin\frac C2$;

  • (F4) triangles $MBL$ and $MAB$ are similar (common angle at $M$, $\angle MBL=\angle MBC=\frac A2=\angle MAB$), so $MB^2=ML\cdot MA$.

Case $B=C$. Then $D=N$, and the line $ID$ is the axis of symmetry of the triangle; it contains $A,I,L,O$ and meets $\Gamma$ again at $F=M$. So the line $FL$ is this axis and passes through $O$.

Step 1: $\angle A=60^\circ$. The rays $IF$ and $AH$ have the same direction (both are perpendicular to $BC$ and point towards $BC$). Hence $F$ and $H$ lie on the same side of line $AI$, and $\angle AIF=180^\circ-\angle IAH=180^\circ-\delta$. In the isosceles triangle $AIF$ ($IA=IF$) the base angles are $\angle IAF=\angle IFA=\frac\delta2$, and \[AF=2\,IA\cos\frac\delta2.\] As $O$ and $F$ are on different sides of $AI$, $\angle OAF=\angle OAI+\angle IAF=\frac{3\delta}2$. Triangle $OAF$ is isosceles ($OA=OF=R$) with base angle $\angle OAF$, so $AF=2R\cos\frac{3\delta}2$. By (F1), $MI=MB=2R\sin30^\circ=R$.

Step 2: a homothety. The point $L$ lies on $BC$ between $I$ (inside the triangle) and $M$ (on the other side of $BC$). Let $h$ be the homothety with centre $L$ and negative ratio $-\frac{LM}{LI}$; it maps $I$ to $M$. The lines $ID$ and $MN$ are both perpendicular to $BC$, so $h$ maps line $ID$ to line $MN$, which contains $O$. Let $F'=h(F)$. Hence $F'=O$. A homothety maps each point to a point on the line through the centre, so $L$, $F$ and $h(F)=O$ are collinear: the line $FL$ passes through the centre of $\Gamma$.

Solution 2 (A reflection in OI, then a homothety at L)

If $AB=AC$, the line $ID$ is the axis of symmetry, so $F=M$ and the line $FL$ is the axis, which contains $O$. Let $AB\ne AC$. Then $O\notin AI$: otherwise line $AI$ would be the line $OM$, the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, and $AB=AC$. In particular $O\ne I$.

From $IA=IF$ and $OA=OF$, the line $OI$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AF$. The reflection in $OI$ maps $A$ to $F$ and fixes $I$ and $O$, so it maps the ray $IA$ to the ray $IF$. So $L$, $F$ and $h(F)=O$ are collinear: the line $FL$ passes through the centre of $\Gamma$.

Problem 9.4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Does there exist a set of positive integers $M$ such that any power of two can be represented as $a-b$, where $a,b\in M$, in exactly one way, while any other positive integer can be represented in this form in at least two ways?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Adding far-away pairs one at a time)

List the requirements as a sequence $d_1,d_2,d_3,\dots$ in which every power of two appears once and every other positive integer appears twice, for example $1,2,3,3,4,5,5,6,6,7,7,8,9,9,\dots$. We build $M$ in steps; at step $k$ we add two numbers $x_k$ and $x_k+d_k$.

Step $k$. Let $T$ be the largest number chosen so far ($T=0$ at the start). Choose $N$ with $2^N>T+d_k$ and put $x_k=2^N+T+1$. The new differences are the intended one, $(x_k+d_k)-x_k=d_k$, and the differences $x_k-m$ and $x_k+d_k-m$ with old elements $1\le m\le T$. The latter lie in $[2^N+1,\ 2^N+T+d_k]$, strictly between $2^N$ and $2^{N+1}$, so none of them is a power of two.

Let $M$ be the set of all numbers chosen. Every representation $a-b$ ($a>b$, $a,b\in M$) comes from the step $k$ at which $a$ was added: either $\{a,b\}=\{x_k+d_k,x_k\}$, giving the intended difference $d_k$, or $b$ was chosen earlier, giving a difference that is not a power of two. Different steps give different pairs. Hence a power of two $2^j$ is represented exactly once (it occurs once in the list), and every other positive integer is represented at least twice (it occurs twice in the list). So such a set exists: yes.

Problem 9.5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Does there exist an infinite non-constant sequence $m_1,m_2,m_3,\dots$ of positive integers such that for all positive integers $k$ the following equality holds \[m_{k+2}=(k+1)m_k-km_{k+1}.\]

Show the answer

No

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Explicit differences)

Suppose such a sequence exists and let $d_k=m_{k+1}-m_k$. The recurrence gives \[m_{k+2}-m_{k+1}=(k+1)m_k-(k+1)m_{k+1},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad d_{k+1}=-(k+1)d_k,\] so $d_k=(-1)^{k-1}k!\,d_1$. If $d_1=0$ all differences vanish and the sequence is constant; hence $d_1\ne0$. Now \[m_{k+1}=m_1+d_1S_k,\qquad S_k=\sum_{j=1}^k(-1)^{j-1}j!.\] For $k\ge2$, $\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}j!\le(k-1)\cdot(k-1)!=k!-(k-1)!$, so $|S_{k-1}|\le k!-(k-1)!$ and $S_k=(-1)^{k-1}k!+S_{k-1}$ has the sign of $(-1)^{k-1}$ with $|S_k|\ge(k-1)!$. Choose $k$ with $(k-1)!>m_1$ and with $(-1)^{k-1}$ of sign opposite to $d_1$. Then $d_1S_k\le-(k-1)!$ and $m_{k+1}\le m_1-(k-1)!<0$, a contradiction. So the answer is no.

Solution 2 (Differences grow faster than the terms)

Suppose such a sequence exists; let $d_k=m_{k+1}-m_k$. The recurrence gives $m_{k+2}-m_{k+1}=(k+1)(m_k-m_{k+1})$, i.e. $d_{k+1}=-(k+1)d_k$, and $d_1\ne0$ (otherwise every $d_k=0$ and the sequence is constant), so $|d_k|=k!\,|d_1|\ge k!$.

We claim $|d_k|<m_k$ for every $k$. If $d_k<0$, then $1\le m_{k+1}=m_k+d_k$ gives $|d_k|<m_k$. If $d_k>0$, then $m_{k+2}=m_{k+1}+d_{k+1}=m_k+d_k-(k+1)d_k=m_k-kd_k\ge1$, so $d_k<\frac{m_k}k\le m_k$.

Consequently $m_{k+1}=m_k+d_k<2m_k$, so $m_k\le2^{k-1}m_1$, and $k!\le|d_k|<m_k\le2^{k-1}m_1$ for every $k$. But $\frac{k!}{2^{k-1}}$ grows without bound (it is multiplied by $\frac{k+1}2\ge\frac32$ at each step from $k=2$ on), a contradiction. So the answer is no.

Solution 3 (Every other term decreases)

Suppose such a sequence exists and let $d_k=m_{k+1}-m_k$. The recurrence can be written as $m_{k+2}-m_{k+1}=(k+1)(m_k-m_{k+1})$, that is, \[d_{k+1}=-(k+1)\,d_k.\] If $d_1=0$, then every $d_k$ is $0$ and the sequence is constant. So $d_1\ne0$; then every $d_k$ is non-zero and any two consecutive differences have opposite signs. Hence there is $k_0\in\{1,2\}$ such that $d_k>0$ for every $k\equiv k_0\pmod2$. For each such $k$, \[m_{k+2}=m_k+d_k+d_{k+1}=m_k+d_k-(k+1)d_k=m_k-k\,d_k<m_k.\] So $m_{k_0}>m_{k_0+2}>m_{k_0+4}>\cdots$ is an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, which is impossible. So the answer is no.

Problem 9.6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Inside a regular $2n$-gon $A_1A_2\dots A_{2n}$ points $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_{2n}$ are chosen such that $A_1$ is the farthest vertex of the $2n$-gon from $B_1$, $A_2$ is the farthest vertex of the $2n$-gon from $B_2$, $\dots$, $A_{2n}$ is the farthest vertex of the $2n$-gon from $B_{2n}$. What is the maximal number of points out of $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_{2n}$ that can be collinear?

Show the answer

$n+1$

Show the solution

Solution (Farthest-vertex regions are sectors)

Let $O$ be the centre and $R$ the circumradius ($n\ge2$). For a point $P$, \[PA_j^2=OP^2+R^2-2\,\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\overrightarrow{OA_j},\] so the farthest vertices from $P$ are those for which $\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\overrightarrow{OA_j}$ is smallest. If $P=O$ all vertices are equally far, so $B_i\ne O$. For $P\ne O$, $\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\overrightarrow{OA_j}=OP\cdot R\cos\theta_j$, where $\theta_j$ is the angle between the ray $OP$ and the ray $OA_j$, and the smallest value belongs to the vertex whose direction is closest to the ray opposite to $OP$. The directions of the vertices are spaced $\frac\pi n$ apart, so $A_i$ is the unique farthest vertex from $P$ exactly when the ray opposite to $OP$ makes an angle less than $\frac\pi{2n}$ with $OA_i$, that is, when $P$ lies in the open sector $\Sigma_i$ of angle $\frac\pi n$ with vertex $O$ centred on the ray $OA_{i+n}$ (indices mod $2n$). The $2n$ sectors $\Sigma_i$ are disjoint and $B_i\in\Sigma_i$.

At most $n+1$. Let a line $\ell$ contain some of the $B_i$. If $\ell$ passes through $O$, its points other than $O$ lie on two opposite rays from $O$, and each ray lies in at most one sector, so $\ell$ contains at most $2\le n+1$ of the points. Otherwise, the directions from $O$ to the points of $\ell$ form an open half-turn (an open arc of length $\pi$). The $2n$ boundary rays of the sectors are spaced $\frac\pi n$ apart, so at most $n$ of them fall inside this open arc, and $\ell$ meets at most $n+1$ sectors. Different points $B_i$ lie in different sectors, so $\ell$ contains at most $n+1$ of them.

Example with $n+1$. Place $O$ at the origin with $A_k=R\bigl(\cos\frac{(k-1)\pi}n,\sin\frac{(k-1)\pi}n\bigr)$, take $R=1$, let $\rho=\cos\frac\pi{2n}$ be the inradius and $\varepsilon=\frac\rho2\sin\frac\pi{4n}$. Problem 9.7

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is such that for any real numbers $x,y$ the following equality holds \[f(x+f(y))=f(x)+yf(f(x)).\]

  • (a) Find all possible values of $f(0)$.

  • (b) Does there exist such a non-constant $f$?

Show the answer

(a) $f(0)=0$; (b) Yes, for example $f(x)=0$ for rational $x$ and $f(x)=1$ for irrational $x$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Injectivity against periodicity)

Let $c=f(0)$.

(a) Putting $y=0$: $f(x+c)=f(x)$ for all $x$ (1). Putting $x=0$: $f(f(y))=c+y\,f(c)$, and $f(c)=f(0+c)=f(0)=c$ by (1), so \[f(f(y))=c+cy\quad\text{for all }y.\qquad(2)\] If $c\ne0$, the right side of (2) is an injective function of $y$, so $f$ is injective. But (1) with $x=0$ gives $f(c)=f(0)$ with $c\ne0$, a contradiction. Hence $f(0)=0$. This value occurs: $f\equiv0$ satisfies the equation ($0=0+y\cdot0$). So the only possible value is $\boxed{0}$.

(b) Let $f(x)=0$ for rational $x$ and $f(x)=1$ for irrational $x$. Every value of $f$ is $0$ or $1$, which are rational, so $f(f(x))=0$; and adding a rational number does not change whether a number is rational, so $f(x+f(y))=f(x)$. Hence $f(x+f(y))=f(x)=f(x)+y\,f(f(x))$ for all $x,y$, and $f$ is not constant. So the answer is yes.

Solution 2 (Comparing two values of f(f(y)))

Let $c=f(0)$. As in Solution 1, $y=0$ gives $f(x+c)=f(x)$, and $x=0$ together with $f(c)=f(0)=c$ gives $f(f(y))=c+cy$ for all $y$.

(a) Since $f(y+c)=f(y)$, we have $f(f(y+c))=f(f(y))$, so $c+c(y+c)=c+cy$, i.e. $c^2=0$. Hence $f(0)=\boxed{0}$ (and $f\equiv0$ shows that this value occurs).

(b) With $c=0$ we get $f(f(y))=0$ for all $y$, and the equation becomes $f(x+f(y))=f(x)$: every value of $f$ is a period of $f$. Conversely, any $f$ with these two properties satisfies the equation, since then $f(x+f(y))=f(x)=f(x)+y\cdot0=f(x)+yf(f(x))$. For example, let $f(x)=0$ if $x$ is an integer and $f(x)=1$ otherwise: its values $0,1$ are periods (adding $1$ does not change whether a number is an integer) and $f(0)=f(1)=0$. This $f$ is not constant, so the answer is yes.

Problem 9.8

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Given a positive integer $n$. Vika wants for the chosen by Sasha real numbers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ the inequality $|ja_i-ia_j|<j-i$ to hold for all $1\le i<j\le n$. To achieve it, she can choose some of these inequalities and ask Sasha to choose his numbers in any way so that they hold. For each positive integer $n$ determine the least possible number of inequalities Vika needs to choose to achieve the desired.

Show the answer

$n-1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Telescoping and connectivity)

Dividing by $ij$, the inequality for $i<j$ reads \[\Bigl|\frac{a_i}i-\frac{a_j}j\Bigr|<\frac1i-\frac1j.\] Put $b_i=\frac{a_i}i$.

$n-1$ inequalities suffice. Let Vika choose the $n-1$ inequalities for the pairs $(k,k+1)$, $1\le k\le n-1$. Then for $i<j$, by the triangle inequality, \[|b_i-b_j|\le\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}|b_k-b_{k+1}|<\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Bigl(\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}\Bigr)=\frac1i-\frac1j,\] so all inequalities hold.

Fewer do not suffice. Suppose Vika chooses fewer than $n-1$ inequalities. Consider the graph on the vertices $1,\dots,n$ whose edges are the chosen pairs. A graph with $n$ vertices and fewer than $n-1$ edges is not connected; let $K$ be the vertex set of one connected component, so $K\ne\{1,\dots,n\}$. Sasha takes $a_i=i$ for $i\in K$ and $a_i=0$ otherwise, so $b_i=1$ on $K$ and $b_i=0$ elsewhere. Every chosen inequality joins two indices of the same component, so it reads $0<\frac1i-\frac1j$ and holds. But for $i\in K$ and $j\notin K$ we get $|b_i-b_j|=1>\bigl|\frac1i-\frac1j\bigr|$, so the corresponding inequality fails.

Hence the least number is $\boxed{n-1}$ (which is $0$ for $n=1$).

Solution 2 (A witness for every consecutive pair)

Dividing by $ij$, the inequality for $i<j$ reads \[|b_i-b_j|<t_i-t_j,\qquad\text{where } b_i=\frac{a_i}i,\ t_i=\frac1i.\] Sasha may choose the $b_i$ freely (then $a_i=ib_i$), and $t_1>t_2>\dots>t_n$.

The $n-1$ consecutive inequalities suffice. If $|b_k-b_{k+1}|<t_k-t_{k+1}$ for $1\le k\le n-1$, then for $i<j$ \[|b_i-b_j|\le\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}|b_k-b_{k+1}|<\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}(t_k-t_{k+1})=t_i-t_j.\]

Every consecutive inequality must be chosen. Fix $k$ with $1\le k\le n-1$ and suppose Vika does not choose the inequality for the pair $(k,k+1)$. Let $\delta=t_k-t_{k+1}>0$, and let Sasha take $b_i=0$ for $i\le k$ and $b_i=\delta$ for $i>k$. For a pair $i<j$ on the same side of $k$ the inequality reads $0<t_i-t_j$ and holds. For a pair $i\le k<j$ other than $(k,k+1)$ we have $t_i\ge t_k$ and $t_j\le t_{k+1}$, with at least one of them strict, so $t_i-t_j>\delta=|b_i-b_j|$ and the inequality holds. So every inequality except the one for $(k,k+1)$ holds; in particular all the chosen ones do. But for $(k,k+1)$ we get $|b_k-b_{k+1}|=\delta=t_k-t_{k+1}$, and the strict inequality fails.

Hence any good choice contains the $n-1$ consecutive inequalities, and these alone are enough. The least number is $\boxed{n-1}$ (which is $0$ for $n=1$). In fact the consecutive pairs form the only good choice of $n-1$ inequalities.

Show the solution

Solution (A homothety centred at B)

Let $\omega$ be the circle $(BIC)$ and $M$ the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of the circumcircle not containing $A$. By the incentre–excentre lemma, $MB=MI=MC$, so $\omega$ has centre $M$, which lies on the line $AI$. Let $C'$ be the reflection of $C$ in the line $AI$.

$C'$ lies on $\omega$ and on line $AB$. The reflection in $AI$ maps $\omega$ to itself (its centre $M$ is on $AI$), so $C'\in\omega$. It also swaps the rays $AB$ and $AC$ (as $AI$ bisects $\angle BAC$), so $C'$ lies on ray $AB$ with $AC'=AC>AB$; in particular $C'\ne B$.

$DE\parallel CC'$. Both lines are perpendicular to $AI$. They are different lines: $I\in DE$, while $CC'$ meets $AI$ at the foot of the perpendicular from $C$, which is not $I$ because $\angle AIC=90^\circ+\frac B2\ne90^\circ$. (Similarly $D\ne B$ because $\angle AIB=90^\circ+\frac C2\ne90^\circ$.)

So $(BDE)$ and $(BIC)$ have the same tangent at their common point $B$, i.e. they are tangent at $B$. Problem 10.2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Find all arithmetic progressions of even length at least $4$, the terms of which are positive integers, for which there exists a permutation $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{2n}$ of its terms such that \[(x_1+2)(x_2+2)\dots(x_n+2)=x_{n+1}x_{n+2}\dots x_{2n}+7.\]

Show the answer

only the progression $1,2,3,4$ (in either order)

Show the solution

Solution (Parity, then size)

Write the progression in increasing order (a decreasing one is the same set of terms) as $t_k=a+kd$, $k=0,1,\dots,2n-1$, with $a\ge1$, $d\ge0$, $n\ge2$. Let $U=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ and $V=\{x_{n+1},\dots,x_{2n}\}$, and $\mathcal L=\prod_{x\in U}(x+2)$, $\mathcal R=\prod_{x\in V}x$, so $\mathcal L=\mathcal R+7$.

Parity. If $d$ is even, all terms have the same parity: if all are odd, $\mathcal L$ is odd and $\mathcal R+7$ is even; if all are even, $\mathcal L$ is even and $\mathcal R+7$ is odd. So $d$ is odd and the terms alternate in parity; the even-indexed terms $E=\{t_0,t_2,\dots,t_{2n-2}\}$ have one parity and the odd-indexed terms $O=\{t_1,\dots,t_{2n-1}\}$ the other. If $U$ contains an even number, $\mathcal L$ is even, so $\mathcal R$ is odd and $V$ consists of odd numbers; otherwise $U$ consists of odd numbers. In both cases one of $U,V$ is the set of odd terms and the other the set of even terms, so $\{U,V\}=\{E,O\}$.

A simple estimate. If $x_k\ge y_k>0$ for $k=1,\dots,n$, then \[\prod x_k-\prod y_k=(x_1-y_1)\,x_2\cdots x_n+y_1\Bigl(x_2\cdots x_n-y_2\cdots y_n\Bigr)\ge(x_1-y_1)\,x_2\cdots x_n.\qquad(*)\]

Case $U=O$, $V=E$. Pair $t_{2j+1}+2=t_{2j}+d+2$ with $t_{2j}$. By $(*)$, $\mathcal L-\mathcal R\ge(d+2)(t_3+2)\ge3\cdot(1+3+2)=18>7$. Impossible.

Case $U=E$, $V=O$. Pair $t_{2j}+2$ with $t_{2j+1}=t_{2j}+d$. If $d\ge3$, each factor of $\mathcal L$ is smaller than the corresponding factor of $\mathcal R$, so $\mathcal L<\mathcal R$, impossible. So $d=1$, $\mathcal L=\prod_{j=1}^n(a+2j)$, $\mathcal R=\prod_{j=1}^n(a+2j-1)$. For $n\ge3$, $(*)$ gives $\mathcal L-\mathcal R\ge(a+4)(a+6)\ge35>7$. For $n=2$, \[\mathcal L-\mathcal R=(a+2)(a+4)-(a+1)(a+3)=2a+5=7\iff a=1.\]

So the progression is $1,2,3,4$, and it works: $(1+2)(3+2)=15=2\cdot4+7$. The answer is $\boxed{1,2,3,4}$.

Problem 10.3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 38: Inequalities: Convexity and Functional Methods

Prove that for any positive integers $m,n$ and real $x>0$ the following inequality holds \[\left(x^{m+n-1}+x^{m+n-2}+\dots+x+1\right)^2\le\frac{(m+n)^2}{4mn}\left(x^{2m-1}+\dots+x+1\right)\left(x^{2n-1}+\dots+x+1\right).\]

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Discrete log-convexity)

Let $S_k=1+x+\dots+x^{k-1}$ and $g_k=\frac{S_k}k$ for $k\ge1$; all are positive. The inequality is \[g_{m+n}^2\le g_{2m}\,g_{2n}.\qquad(1)\]

Step 1: $g_k^2\le g_{k-1}g_{k+1}$ for $k\ge2$. For $x\ne1$, $S_k=\frac{x^k-1}{x-1}$ and \[(x^{k-1}-1)(x^{k+1}-1)-(x^k-1)^2=-x^{k-1}(x-1)^2,\] so $S_{k-1}S_{k+1}=S_k^2-x^{k-1}$; this also holds for $x=1$ ($(k-1)(k+1)=k^2-1$). By AM–GM applied to $1,x,\dots,x^{k-1}$, $S_k\ge k\,x^{(k-1)/2}$, i.e. $x^{k-1}\le\frac{S_k^2}{k^2}$. Hence \[k^2S_{k-1}S_{k+1}=k^2S_k^2-k^2x^{k-1}\ge(k^2-1)S_k^2,\] which is $g_k^2\le g_{k-1}g_{k+1}$ after dividing by $k^2(k^2-1)$.

Step 2: (1). Let $\lambda_k=\ln g_{k+1}-\ln g_k$. Step 1 says $\lambda_{k-1}\le\lambda_k$: the sequence $\lambda_k$ is non-decreasing. By symmetry let $m\le n$. Then \[\ln g_{m+n}-\ln g_{2m}=\sum_{k=2m}^{m+n-1}\lambda_k\le(n-m)\lambda_{m+n-1}\le\sum_{k=m+n}^{2n-1}\lambda_k=\ln g_{2n}-\ln g_{m+n},\] because each sum has $n-m$ terms, those of the first are at most $\lambda_{m+n-1}$ and those of the second at least $\lambda_{m+n}\ge\lambda_{m+n-1}$. So $2\ln g_{m+n}\le\ln g_{2m}+\ln g_{2n}$, which is (1), i.e. the required inequality.

Solution 2 (Cauchy–Schwarz for integrals)

If $x=1$ both sides equal $(m+n)^2$. Let $x\ne1$ and $u=\ln x\ne0$. For $k\ge1$, \[I_k:=\int_0^1e^{kus}\,ds=\frac{e^{ku}-1}{ku}=\frac{x^k-1}{ku}=\frac{x-1}{u}\cdot\frac{S_k}k,\] where $S_k=1+x+\dots+x^{k-1}$, and $\frac{x-1}u>0$ (since $x-1$ and $\ln x$ have the same sign). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for integrals, \[I_{m+n}^2=\Bigl(\int_0^1e^{mus}e^{nus}\,ds\Bigr)^2\le\int_0^1e^{2mus}\,ds\int_0^1e^{2nus}\,ds=I_{2m}I_{2n}.\] Dividing by $\bigl(\frac{x-1}u\bigr)^2$ gives $\frac{S_{m+n}^2}{(m+n)^2}\le\frac{S_{2m}S_{2n}}{4mn}$, which is the claim.

Solution 3 (Pairs of powers symmetric about the centre)

If $x=1$, both sides equal $(m+n)^2$; if $m=n$, both sides are equal. So let $x\ne1$ and, by symmetry, $m>n$. Write $S_k=1+x+\dots+x^{k-1}=\frac{x^k-1}{x-1}$.

Reduction. Put $P=x^{m+n}-1$ and $Q=x^m-x^n$. Then \[(x^{2m}-1)(x^{2n}-1)=x^{2m+2n}-x^{2m}-x^{2n}+1=P^2-Q^2.\] Multiplying the inequality by $4mn(x-1)^2>0$, it becomes $4mnP^2\le(m+n)^2(P^2-Q^2)$, that is, $(m+n)^2Q^2\le(m-n)^2P^2$, or $(m+n)|Q|\le(m-n)|P|$. Dividing by $|x-1|$, and using $\frac{|Q|}{|x-1|}=x^n\Bigl|\frac{x^{m-n}-1}{x-1}\Bigr|=x^n+x^{n+1}+\dots+x^{m-1}$ and $\frac{|P|}{|x-1|}=S_{m+n}$, we must show \[\frac{x^n+x^{n+1}+\dots+x^{m-1}}{m-n}\le\frac{1+x+\dots+x^{m+n-1}}{m+n}.\qquad(*)\] So the average of $x^e$ over the inner exponents $E_0=\{n,\dots,m-1\}$ must be at most the average over all exponents $E=\{0,\dots,m+n-1\}$.

Symmetric pairs. Both sets are symmetric about $c=\frac{m+n-1}2$: the map $e\mapsto2c-e$ maps each of them onto itself. Hence the sum of $x^e$ over either set equals the sum of $g(e)=\frac12\bigl(x^e+x^{2c-e}\bigr)$ over it. Let $y=\max(x,\frac1x)>1$. Then $\{x^e,x^{2c-e}\}=\{x^cy^r,x^cy^{-r}\}$ with $r=|e-c|$, so \[g(e)=h(|e-c|),\qquad h(r)=\frac{x^c}2\bigl(y^r+y^{-r}\bigr).\] The function $h$ is strictly increasing for $r\ge0$: for $0\le r<s$, \[(y^s+y^{-s})-(y^r+y^{-r})=(y^{s-r}-1)(y^r-y^{-s})>0.\] Every $e\in E_0$ has $|e-c|\le\frac{m-n-1}2$, and every $e$ in $E\setminus E_0=\{0,\dots,n-1\}\cup\{m,\dots,m+n-1\}$ has $|e-c|\ge c-(n-1)=\frac{m-n+1}2$. So every value of $g$ on the non-empty set $E\setminus E_0$ is larger than every value of $g$ on $E_0$, and the average $\beta$ of $g$ over $E\setminus E_0$ is larger than the average $\alpha$ over $E_0$. The average over $E$ is a weighted average of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ with positive weights, hence larger than $\alpha$. This is $(*)$, even with strict inequality.

So the inequality holds, with equality exactly when $x=1$ or $m=n$.

Problem 10.4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Let a pyramid of height $n$ be a checkered figure shown in the picture (the bottom row consists of $n$ squares). The cells of a pyramid of height $n$ are filled with integers such that for any cell, which is not in the bottom row, the number in it equals to the sum of numbers directly below it. For every positive integer $n\ge 2$ find the biggest possible number of odd numbers in a pyramid of height $n$.

Show the answer

$\left\lceil\frac{n(n+1)}3\right\rceil$

Show the solution

Solution (Three rows at a time)

Only parities matter: a cell is odd exactly when one of the two cells below it is odd. Write $0$ for even and $1$ for odd; then each cell is the sum modulo $2$ of the two cells below it. Let $T=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ be the number of cells.

Lemma. Let a row have parities $a_1,\dots,a_k$ ($k\ge3$), the row above $b_i=a_i+a_{i+1}$ ($i\le k-1$), and the next one $c_i=b_i+b_{i+1}=a_i+a_{i+2}$ ($i\le k-2$), all modulo $2$. These three rows contain at least $k-1$ zeros.

Proof. The number of zeros is $Z=z+E_1+E_2$, where $z$ is the number of zeros among the $a_i$, $E_1$ the number of $i$ with $a_i=a_{i+1}$, and $E_2$ the number of $i$ with $a_i=a_{i+2}$. Cut $a_1\dots a_k$ into $t$ maximal runs of equal digits, of lengths $r_1,\dots,r_t$; runs of zeros and runs of ones alternate. Then $E_1=\sum(r_j-1)=k-t$. Pairs $a_i=a_{i+2}$ arise inside a run of length $r\ge3$ ($r-2$ pairs) and around every inner run of length $1$ (its two neighbours belong to runs of the same digit). Hence \[Z-(k-1)=z+E_2-t+1\ge1-t+\sum_{\text{zero runs}}w_j,\] where for a zero run of length $r$ we put $w_j=r+\max(r-2,0)+[\,r=1\text{ and the run is inner}\,]$ (we dropped the non-negative contributions of the runs of ones to $E_2$). Every zero run has $w_j\ge2$, except an end run of length $1$, which has $w_j=1$. Let $t_0$ be the number of zero runs. If both end runs are runs of ones, then $t=2t_0+1$ and all zero runs are inner, so $\sum w_j\ge2t_0=t-1$. If both end runs are zero runs, then $t=2t_0-1$ and $\sum w_j\ge2t_0-2=t-1$. Otherwise $t=2t_0$ and at most one zero run is an end run, so $\sum w_j\ge2t_0-1=t-1$. In all cases $Z\ge k-1$. $\square$

Upper bound. Let $f(n)$ be the largest possible number of odd entries. Clearly $f(1)=1$, and $f(2)\le2$ because the three numbers $x,y,x+y$ are not all odd. For $n=3$ the Lemma (with $k=3$) gives at least $2$ even entries among $6$, so $f(3)\le4$. For $n\ge4$, the Lemma applied to the bottom three rows ($k=n$) gives at least $n-1$ even entries among their $3n-3$ cells, and the remaining top part is a pyramid of height $n-3$ with the same rule. Hence \[f(n)\le(3n-3)-(n-1)+f(n-3)=2n-2+f(n-3).\] By induction (with $\lceil\frac{n(n+1)}3\rceil=1,2,4$ for $n=1,2,3$) we get $f(n)\le2n-2+\bigl\lceil\frac{(n-3)(n-2)}3\bigr\rceil=\bigl\lceil\frac{(n-3)(n-2)+6n-6}3\bigr\rceil=\bigl\lceil\frac{n(n+1)}3\bigr\rceil$.

Construction. Number the cells of each row from the left starting with $0$. In the bottom row put an even number (say $0$) in the cells with index $i\equiv2-n\pmod3$ and an odd number (say $1$) in the others.

Claim: in the row of length $L$ the even cells are exactly those with $i\equiv2-L\pmod3$. This holds for the bottom row. If it holds for a row of length $L$ with even cells at $i\equiv s$, then cell $i$ of the row above is odd exactly when exactly one of the cells $i,i+1$ below is even, i.e. when $i\equiv s$ or $i\equiv s-1$; so its even cells are those with $i\equiv s+1=2-(L-1)$.

The number of $i\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}$ with $i\equiv2-L\pmod 3$ is $\frac L3,\ \frac{L-1}3,\ \frac{L+1}3$ for $L\equiv0,1,2\pmod 3$, that is, $\lfloor\frac{L+1}3\rfloor$ in all cases. For three consecutive lengths $3j+1,3j+2,3j+3$ these counts are $j,j+1,j+1$, a total of $3j+2$, exactly one third of the $9j+6$ cells. Summing over $L=1,\dots,n$: for $n=3J$ the number of even cells is $\frac T3$; for $n=3J+1$ the extra row $L=3J+1$ adds $J$ even cells among $3J+1$, giving $\lfloor\frac T3\rfloor$; for $n=3J+2$ the extra rows add $J+(J+1)$ among $6J+3$, giving $\frac T3$. So there are exactly $\lfloor\frac T3\rfloor$ even entries and $T-\lfloor\frac T3\rfloor=\lceil\frac{2T}3\rceil=\lceil\frac{n(n+1)}3\rceil$ odd entries.

Hence the answer is $\boxed{\left\lceil\frac{n(n+1)}3\right\rceil}$. The figure shows the construction for $n=6$ ($14$ odd entries).

Three pairwise distinct real numbers $x,y,z$ satisfy \[x^3-3x^2-yz=y^3-3y^2-zx=z^3-3z^2-xy.\] Find all possible values of the expression $x^2-4x-yz$.

$-4$

Subtract the first two expressions and factor: \[(x^3-y^3)-3(x^2-y^2)+z(x-y)=(x-y)\bigl(x^2+xy+y^2-3x-3y+z\bigr)=0.\] As $x\ne y$, \[x^2+xy+y^2-3(x+y)+z=0,\qquad(1)\] and in the same way (from the second and third, and from the third and first expressions) \[y^2+yz+z^2-3(y+z)+x=0,\qquad z^2+zx+x^2-3(z+x)+y=0.\qquad(2)\] Subtracting the first equation of (2) from (1): $(x^2-z^2)+y(x-z)-3(x-z)-(x-z)=(x-z)(x+y+z-4)=0$, so $x+y+z=4$.

Now $x^2+xy+y^2=(x+y)^2-xy=(4-z)^2-xy$ and $x+y=4-z$, so (1) becomes $(4-z)^2-xy-3(4-z)+z=0$, i.e. $xy=(z-2)^2$. Likewise $yz=(x-2)^2$ and $zx=(y-2)^2$. Adding, with $q=xy+yz+zx$ and $x^2+y^2+z^2=16-2q$: \[q=(x^2+y^2+z^2)-4(x+y+z)+12=16-2q-16+12,\qquad q=4.\] Therefore \[x^2-4x-yz=x^2-(x+y+z)x-yz=-(xy+yz+zx)=-4.\]

The value is attained. The roots of $t^3-4t^2+4t-1=(t-1)(t^2-3t+1)$ are $x=1$, $y,z=\frac{3\pm\sqrt5}2$, distinct reals. Here $yz=1$ and $y+z=3$, so $x^3-3x^2-yz=-3$; and $y^2=3y-1$, $y^3=8y-3$ give $y^3-3y^2-zx=8y-3-9y+3-z=-(y+z)=-3$, and the same for $z$. So all three expressions equal $-3$, and $x^2-4x-yz=1-4-1=-4$. The answer is $\boxed{-4}$.

Solution 2 (Vieta's formulas for a quartic)

Let $k$ be the common value and $e_3=xyz$. Multiplying $x^3-3x^2-yz=k$ by $x$ gives $x^4-3x^3-kx-e_3=0$, and similarly for $y$ and $z$. So $x,y,z$ are three distinct roots of \[P(t)=t^4-3t^3+0\cdot t^2-kt-e_3,\] and $P(t)=(t-x)(t-y)(t-z)(t-w)$ for some real $w$. Let $s=x+y+z$, $q=xy+yz+zx$. Vieta's formulas give \[s+w=3,\qquad q+ws=0,\qquad xyz\,w=-e_3.\] If $e_3\ne0$: the last relation gives $w=-1$. If $e_3=0$: one of the numbers, say $x$, is $0$ (the others are then non-zero), and $P(t)=t(t^3-3t^2-k)$, so $y,z,w$ are the roots of $t^3-3t^2-k$ and $yzw=k$. The first given expression is $0-0-yz=k$, so $yzw=-yz$ and again $w=-1$.

In both cases $w=-1$, so $s=4$ and $q=-ws=4$. Then $x^2-4x-yz=x^2-sx-yz=-q=\boxed{-4}$. (In the case $e_3=0$ this would force $y+z=4$, $yz=q-x(y+z)=4$, so $y=z=2$, which is excluded; so in fact $e_3\ne0$.) The value is attained: for $x=1$, $y,z=\frac{3\pm\sqrt5}2$ (the roots of $(t-1)(t^2-3t+1)$) all three expressions equal $-3$.

Problem 10.6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

We say a list of non-negative integers is tabular if there exists a rectangular table, each cell of which contains one of “$+$” and “$-$”, and the list of amounts of pluses in columns and amounts of minuses in rows coincides with the given list (numbers in the list are not necessarily distinct). For every positive integer $n$ determine the least amount of numbers that can be added to the list \[0,2,4,\dots,2n-2,2n\] to make it a tabular list.

Show the answer

$\left\lfloor\frac{5n}3\right\rfloor$

Show the solution

Solution (Large even numbers must be rows)

Consider a table with $r$ rows and $c$ columns whose list (column plus-counts and row minus-counts) contains $0,2,\dots,2n$. The list has $r+c$ numbers, so $r+c-(n+1)$ numbers were added; we must find the least possible $r+c$. Every column value is at most $r$ and every row value at most $c$. Transposing the table and changing every sign turns column plus-counts into row minus-counts and vice versa, so the list stays the same while $r$ and $c$ are exchanged; hence we may assume $r\le c$.

Lower bound. All values are at most $c$, so $c\ge2n$. If $r\ge2n$, then $r+c\ge4n\ge n+1+\lfloor\frac{5n}3\rfloor$. So let $r<2n$. The even numbers $2j$ with $r<2j\le2n$ cannot be column values, so they are values of different rows; there are $n-\lfloor\frac r2\rfloor$ of them, so $r+\lfloor\frac r2\rfloor\ge n$. The quantity $r+\lfloor\frac r2\rfloor$ increases with $r$ and equals $3t$, $3t+1$, $3t+3$ for $r=2t$, $2t+1$, $2t+2$; hence \[r\ge2t\ (n=3t),\qquad r\ge2t+1\ (n=3t+1),\qquad r\ge2t+2\ (n=3t+2).\] With $c\ge2n$ this gives $r+c\ge8t$, $8t+3$, $8t+6$ respectively, i.e. at least $5t-1$, $5t+1$, $5t+3$ added numbers.

For $n=3t$ ($t\ge1$) the value $8t$ is impossible. Equality would need $r=2t$ and $c=6t$. Then the $2t$ even numbers $2t+2,\dots,6t$ fill all the rows, so $0,2,\dots,2t$ are column values. A column with value $2t=r$ consists of pluses only, and a row with value $6t=c$ consists of minuses only; their common cell cannot be both. So $r+c\ge8t+1$ and at least $5t$ numbers are added.

In all cases at least $\lfloor\frac{5n}3\rfloor$ numbers must be added ($5t$, $5t+1$, $5t+3$ for $n=3t,3t+1,3t+2$).

Constructions. Choose the numbers of pluses $R_1\ge R_2\ge\dots\ge R_r$ in the rows and put the pluses of row $i$ in the first $R_i$ columns (a staircase). Row $i$ then has $c-R_i$ minuses and column $j$ has $p_j=\#\{i:R_i\ge j\}$ pluses.

  • $n=3t+1$ ($t\ge0$): $r=2t+1$, $c=6t+2$, $R_i\in\{0,2,\dots,4t\}$. Row values: $2t+2,2t+4,\dots,6t+2$. Column values: for $j\le4t$, $p_j=2t+1-\lceil\frac j2\rceil$, which takes each value $1,\dots,2t$ twice; the other $2t+2$ columns have $0$. So the list contains all even numbers $0,\dots,2n$, and $r+c=8t+3$.

  • $n=3t+2$ ($t\ge0$): $r=2t+2$, $c=6t+4$, $R_i\in\{0,2,\dots,4t+2\}$. Row values: $2t+2,\dots,6t+4$. Columns: for $j\le4t+2$, $p_j=2t+2-\lceil\frac j2\rceil\in\{1,\dots,2t+1\}$ (each twice), the remaining $2t+2$ columns have $0$. Again all of $0,2,\dots,2n$ occur, and $r+c=8t+6$.

  • $n=3t$ ($t\ge1$): $r=2t$, $c=6t+1$, $R_i\in\{1,3,\dots,4t-1\}$. Row values: $2t+2,\dots,6t$. Columns: $p_j=2t+1-\lceil\frac{j+1}2\rceil$ for $j\le4t-1$ (the values $2t,2t-1,2t-1,\dots,1,1$), and $0$ for the last $2t+2$ columns. All of $0,2,\dots,2n$ occur, and $r+c=8t+1$.

In each case $r+c-(n+1)=\lfloor\frac{5n}3\rfloor$. Hence the least number of added numbers is $\boxed{\lfloor\frac{5n}3\rfloor}$.

Let $AB=c$, $AC=b$ and let $t$ be the side of the rhombus. Write $\varphi(u)=\bigl\|\bigl(u-\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2\bigr)\bigr\|$. By the triangle inequality for vectors, $\varphi(\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v)\le\lambda\varphi(u)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(v)$ for $\lambda\in[0,1]$, so $\varphi$ is convex; also $\varphi$ is increasing for $u\ge\frac12$. From $(b+c)\bigl(\frac1b+\frac1c\bigr)\ge4$ we get $b+c\ge4$. Hence \[\varphi(b)+\varphi(c)\ge2\varphi\Bigl(\frac{b+c}2\Bigr)\ge2\varphi(2)=2\sqrt3=2\,AD.\]

Problem 10.8

Let $a$ be an odd positive integer greater than $1$. Show the solution

Solution (Blocks whose binary digits do not overlap)

We need a finite set $S$ of exponents and the number $N=\sum_{e\in S}a^e$ such that $n\mid|S|$, $N$ is even, and the number of ones in the binary expansion of $N$ is divisible by $n$. Then the blue weights $2^j$ for the positions $j$ of the ones of $N$ (all $j\ge1$, as $N$ is even) balance the red weights $a^e$, $e\in S$, and both pans carry a multiple of $n$ weights. Let $s(N)$ denote the number of ones in the binary expansion of $N$.

Two facts. (i) For odd $a$ and $L\ge1$: $a^{2^{L-1}}\equiv1\pmod{2^L}$ (Euler's theorem, as $\varphi(2^L)=2^{L-1}$), so $a^e\equiv1\pmod{2^L}$ whenever $2^{L-1}\mid e$. (ii) If $0\le N'<2^L$ and $2^L\mid Y$, then the binary digits of $N'$ and $Y$ occupy disjoint positions, so $s(N'+Y)=s(N')+s(Y)$.

Blocks. We build numbers $Y_1,Y_2,\dots$ one after another. Having built $Y_1,\dots,Y_{k-1}$, choose $L_k\ge1$ with $2^{L_k}>Y_1+\dots+Y_{k-1}$ and let $Y_k$ be the sum of $a^e$ over $n\cdot2^{L_k}$ distinct exponents $e$, all positive multiples of $2^{L_k-1}$ and larger than every exponent used before. By (i), $Y_k\equiv n2^{L_k}\equiv0\pmod{2^{L_k}}$. The blocks use disjoint sets of exponents, and each uses a multiple of $n$ of them.

Choosing $n$ blocks. The residues of $s(Y_k)$ modulo $n$ take finitely many values, so some residue $\varrho$ occurs for infinitely many $k$; pick $k_1<k_2<\dots<k_n$ with $s(Y_{k_i})\equiv\varrho\pmod n$, and let $N=Y_{k_1}+\dots+Y_{k_n}$. For each $i$, the sum of the earlier chosen blocks is at most $Y_1+\dots+Y_{k_i-1}<2^{L_{k_i}}$, while $2^{L_{k_i}}\mid Y_{k_i}$; by (ii), adding the blocks one by one, \[s(N)=s(Y_{k_1})+\dots+s(Y_{k_n})\equiv n\varrho\equiv0\pmod n.\] The number of red weights is a sum of multiples of $n$, and $N$ is even (each block is divisible by $2^{L}\ge2$). Putting the red weights $a^e$ of the chosen blocks on the right pan and the blue weights $2^j$, for the positions $j$ of the binary ones of $N$, on the left pan balances the scales with a multiple of $n$ weights on each pan.

Find all triples of positive integers $(m,n,k)$ such that there exists an infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ of integers which satisfies for each $i\ge 1$ \[ma_{i+1}-na_i=k.\]

Show the answer

exactly the triples with $m\mid n$ and $m\mid k$, together with the triples with $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Distance from the fixed point)

These triples work. If $m\mid n$ and $m\mid k$, then $a_{i+1}=\frac nm a_i+\frac km$ is an integer whenever $a_i$ is, so any integer $a_1$ starts a valid sequence. If $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$, the constant sequence $a_i=\frac k{m-n}$ works: $m\frac k{m-n}-n\frac k{m-n}=k$.

No other triples work. Suppose a sequence exists.

If $m=n$, then $m(a_{i+1}-a_i)=k$, so $m\mid k$ (and $m\mid n$).

If $m\ne n$, let $g=\gcd(m,n)$, $m=gm'$, $n=gn'$ with $\gcd(m',n')=1$, and put $X_i=(m-n)a_i-k$, an integer. Multiplying $ma_{i+1}=na_i+k$ by $m-n$ and rearranging, \[mX_{i+1}=m(m-n)a_{i+1}-mk=n(m-n)a_i+(m-n)k-mk=nX_i,\] so $m'X_{i+1}=n'X_i$ and, by induction, $m'^{\,i}X_{i+1}=n'^{\,i}X_1$. Hence $m'^{\,i}\mid n'^{\,i}X_1$, and since $\gcd(m',n')=1$, $m'^{\,i}\mid X_1$ for every $i$. If $m'>1$, this forces $X_1=0$, i.e. $(m-n)a_1=k$, so $(m-n)\mid k$. If $m'=1$, then $m=g\mid n$, and $ma_2=na_1+k$ gives $m\mid k$.

So the answer is: all triples with $m\mid n$ and $m\mid k$, and all triples with $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$.

Solution 2 (Differences and p-adic valuations)

If $m\mid n$ and $m\mid k$, any integer $a_1$ works, since $a_{i+1}=\frac nma_i+\frac km$ stays an integer; if $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$, the constant sequence $a_i=\frac k{m-n}$ works. Conversely, suppose a sequence exists and let $d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i$. Subtracting the relations for $i$ and $i+1$ gives $md_{i+1}=nd_i$.

If $d_1=0$, then all $d_i=0$, the sequence is constant, $a_i=a$, and $(m-n)a=k$. As $k>0$, $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$.

If $d_1\ne0$, then all $d_i\ne0$. Suppose $m\nmid n$. Then some prime $p$ has $v_p(m)>v_p(n)$, where $v_p$ is the exponent of $p$. From $md_{i+1}=nd_i$, \[v_p(d_{i+1})=v_p(d_i)+v_p(n)-v_p(m)\le v_p(d_i)-1,\] so $v_p(d_i)\le v_p(d_1)-(i-1)$, which is negative for large $i$, impossible for a non-zero integer. Hence $m\mid n$, and then $ma_2=na_1+k$ gives $m\mid k$.

So the triples are exactly those with $m\mid n$ and $m\mid k$, or $m\ne n$ and $(m-n)\mid k$.

Solution 1 (The tangent at A cuts off a segment equal to ED)

By the law of sines in triangles $ABM$ and $ACM$, $\frac{BM}{\sin\angle BAM}=\frac{AM}{\sin B}$ and $\frac{CM}{\sin\angle CAM}=\frac{AM}{\sin C}$; as $BM=CM$, $\frac{\sin\angle BAM}{\sin\angle CAM}=\frac{\sin B}{\sin C}$. Hence, by the law of sines in triangle $AED$, \[ED=AE\cdot\frac{\sin\angle EAD}{\sin\angle ADE}=AE\cdot\frac{\sin B}{\sin C}.\]

The point $T$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $A$. On the side of line $AC$ away from $B$, the line $t$ makes with $AC$ the angle $B$ (tangent–chord angle). The ray from $E$ beyond $E$ (direction from $B$ to $A$) makes with ray $EA$ the angle $A$. Since $A+B<180^\circ$, these two rays meet at a point $T$, and in triangle $AET$ we have $\angle AET=A$, $\angle EAT=B$, $\angle ATE=C$. By the law of sines \[ET=AE\cdot\frac{\sin B}{\sin C}=ED.\]

Conclusion. $E$ is inside $\Gamma$ (an interior point of the chord $AC$), and $T$ is outside $\Gamma$ (it lies on the tangent $t$ and $T\ne A$). So the segment $ET$ crosses $\Gamma$ at exactly one point, and this is the first point of the ray from $E$ (away from $D$) on $\Gamma$, namely $F$. Hence $EF<ET=ED$.

The power of a point $(x,y)$ is the left-hand side.

Let $E=\gamma C$ with $0<\gamma<1$. In triangle $AED$, $\angle EAD=\angle BAM$ and $\angle ADE=\angle DAB=\angle CAM$ (alternate angles); the law of sines in triangles $ABM$ and $ACM$ with $BM=CM$ gives $\frac{\sin\angle BAM}{\sin\angle CAM}=\frac{\sin B}{\sin C}=\frac bc$, so by the law of sines in $AED$, $ED=AE\cdot\frac bc=\gamma b\cdot\frac bc$; the ray from $E$ through $D$ points in the direction of the positive $x$-axis. Let $D^*$ be the reflection of $D$ in $E$: $D^*=\bigl(\gamma b\cos A-\frac{\gamma b^2}c,\ \gamma b\sin A\bigr)$. Solution 1 (Reciprocal polynomials)

For a polynomial $f$ of degree at most $n$ put $f^*(x)=x^nf(\frac1x)$ (the coefficients in reverse order). For $x\ne0$ the equation is equivalent, after multiplying by $x^n$, to \[P(x):=p(x)q^*(x)-p^*(x)q(x)=0.\] ($x=0$ is never a solution, since $\frac10$ is undefined.)

Which finite numbers can occur. The numbers $x=1$ and $x=-1$ are always solutions (then $\frac1x=x$). If $x\ne0$ is a solution, so is $\frac1x$ (the equation is symmetric under $x\leftrightarrow\frac1x$). If $P$ is not the zero polynomial, it has degree at most $2n$, so there are at most $2n$ solutions; the solutions other than $\pm1$ split into pairs $\{x,\frac1x\}$ with $x\ne\frac1x$. Hence the number of distinct real solutions is even, between $2$ and $2n$.

Every even number $2k$, $1\le k\le n$, occurs. Choose distinct numbers $r_1,\dots,r_{k-1}>1$ and let \[\Phi(x)=(x^2-1)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(x^2-\bigl(r_j+\tfrac1{r_j}\bigr)x+1\Bigr)\cdot(x^2+1)^{n-k}=\sum_{i=0}^{2n}c_ix^i.\] Each quadratic factor $g$ other than $x^2-1$ satisfies $x^2g(\frac1x)=g(x)$, while $x^2-1$ changes sign, so $x^{2n}\Phi(\frac1x)=-\Phi(x)$, i.e. $c_{2n-i}=-c_i$; in particular $c_n=0$, and $c_0=-1$. Its real roots are $\pm1$, $r_j$, $\frac1{r_j}$: exactly $2k$ distinct non-zero numbers. Take \[q(x)=x^n,\qquad p(x)=x^n+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_ix^i.\] Both have degree $n$; $q$ has only the root $0$ and $p(0)=-1\ne0$, so there are no common real roots. Here $q^*=1$ and $p^*(x)=1+\sum_{i<n}c_ix^{n-i}$, so \[P(x)=p(x)-x^np^*(x)=\sum_{i<n}c_ix^i-\sum_{i<n}c_ix^{2n-i}=\sum_{i<n}c_ix^i+\sum_{i<n}c_{2n-i}x^{2n-i}=\Phi(x).\] So the equation has exactly $2k$ real solutions.

Infinitely many solutions: possible exactly for even $n$. This happens exactly when $P\equiv0$.

Even $n$. Let $p(x)=(x^2+1)^{n/2}$ and $q(x)=(x^2+3x+1)(x^2+1)^{n/2-1}$. Every factor $g$ satisfies $x^2g(\frac1x)=g(x)$, so $p^*=p$, $q^*=q$ and $P=pq-pq=0$: every $x\ne0$ is a solution. The polynomial $p$ has no real roots, so there are no common real roots.

Odd $n$. Suppose $pq^*=p^*q$. (i) At $x=0$: $p(0)\cdot(\text{leading coefficient of }q)=(\text{leading coefficient of }p)\cdot q(0)$, so $p(0)=0$ would give $q(0)=0$, a common root. Hence $p(0),q(0)\ne0$, and the roots of $p^*$ are the reciprocals of the roots of $p$, with the same multiplicities; the same holds for $q$. Write $\mu_\alpha(f)$ for the multiplicity of $\alpha$ as a root of $f$.

(ii) Let $\alpha\ne\pm1$ be a real root of $p$; then $q(\alpha)\ne0$. Comparing multiplicities of $\alpha$ in $pq^*=p^*q$: $\mu_\alpha(p)+\mu_{1/\alpha}(q)=\mu_{1/\alpha}(p)$. So $\frac1\alpha$ is a root of $p$, hence not of $q$, so $\mu_{1/\alpha}(q)=0$ and $\mu_{1/\alpha}(p)=\mu_\alpha(p)$.

(iii) Let $\varepsilon=\pm1$ be a root of $p$ of multiplicity $\mu$: $p=(x-\varepsilon)^\mu u$ with $u(\varepsilon)\ne0$. Since $1-\varepsilon x=-\varepsilon(x-\varepsilon)$, we get $p^*=(-\varepsilon)^\mu(x-\varepsilon)^\mu u^*$ with $u^*(x)=x^{n-\mu}u(\frac1x)$. Cancelling $(x-\varepsilon)^\mu$ in $pq^*=p^*q$ and putting $x=\varepsilon$, using $q^*(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^nq(\varepsilon)$ and $u^*(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{n-\mu}u(\varepsilon)$: \[u(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^nq(\varepsilon)=(-\varepsilon)^\mu\varepsilon^{n-\mu}u(\varepsilon)q(\varepsilon)=(-1)^\mu\varepsilon^nu(\varepsilon)q(\varepsilon).\] As $q(\varepsilon)\ne0$ (no common root) and $u(\varepsilon)\ne0$, $\mu$ is even.

(iv) By (ii) and (iii), the real roots of $p$, counted with multiplicity, are an even number. But $p$ has odd degree $n$ and its non-real roots come in conjugate pairs, so it has an odd number of real roots counted with multiplicity. This contradiction shows $P\not\equiv0$ for odd $n$.

Answer. For odd $n$: $2,4,\dots,2n$. For even $n$: $2,4,\dots,2n$, or infinitely many (all $x\ne0$).

Solution 2 (A substitution t = x + 1/x and a continuity argument)

Here $x=0$ is never a solution, since $\frac10$ is undefined.

Which numbers can occur. For $x\ne0$ the equation is equivalent to $R(x)=0$, where \[R(x)=x^n\Bigl(p(x)\,q\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)-p\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)q(x)\Bigr)\] is a polynomial of degree at most $2n$. The numbers $1$ and $-1$ are always solutions, and if $x$ is a solution then so is $\frac1x$. If $R$ is not the zero polynomial, there are at most $2n$ solutions, and those other than $\pm1$ form pairs $\{x,\frac1x\}$ with $x\ne\frac1x$; so the number of solutions is even and between $2$ and $2n$. If $R$ is the zero polynomial, every $x\ne0$ is a solution.

Every even number from $2$ to $2n$ occurs. Put $t=x+\frac1x$ and define polynomials $U_j$ in $t$ by $U_0=1$, $U_1=t$, $U_{j+1}=tU_j-U_{j-1}$; then $U_j$ has degree $j$ and leading coefficient $1$. For $k\ge1$, \[x^k-x^{-k}=\bigl(x-x^{-1}\bigr)\,U_{k-1}(t):\] this is clear for $k=1,2$, and the step from $k-1,k$ to $k+1$ follows from $x^{k+1}-x^{-(k+1)}=\bigl(x+x^{-1}\bigr)\bigl(x^k-x^{-k}\bigr)-\bigl(x^{k-1}-x^{-(k-1)}\bigr)$.

Let $0\le j\le n-1$, choose distinct numbers $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_j>2$ and put $H(t)=t^{\,n-1-j}(t-\tau_1)\cdots(t-\tau_j)$, of degree $n-1$ with leading coefficient $1$. Subtracting suitable multiples of $U_{n-1},U_{n-2},\dots,U_0$ in turn to remove the leading term, we get $H=\sum_{k=1}^nc_kU_{k-1}$ with real $c_k$ and $c_n=1$. Let \[P(x)=1+\sum_{k=1}^nc_kx^k,\qquad p(x)=x^nP\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)=x^n+\sum_{k=1}^nc_kx^{n-k},\qquad q(x)=x^n.\] Both $p$ and $q$ have degree $n$; the only root of $q$ is $0$ and $p(0)=c_n=1$, so there is no common real root. For $x\ne0$ we have $p(x)q(\frac1x)=P(\frac1x)$ and $p(\frac1x)q(x)=P(x)$, so the equation becomes \[0=P(x)-P\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^nc_k\bigl(x^k-x^{-k}\bigr)=\bigl(x-x^{-1}\bigr)\,H\bigl(x+\tfrac1x\bigr).\] The first factor gives $x=\pm1$. For real $x\ne0$, $x+\frac1x\ne0$, and $x+\frac1x=\tau_i$ holds for exactly two numbers $x$, the roots of $x^2-\tau_ix+1=0$, which are distinct (as $\tau_i^2>4$), positive and different from $1$; different $\tau_i$ give different $x$. So the equation has exactly $2+2j$ real solutions, and $j=0,1,\dots,n-1$ gives $2,4,\dots,2n$.

Infinitely many solutions for even $n$. Take $p=q=(x^2+1)^{n/2}$: there are no real roots at all, and the equation holds for every $x\ne0$.

No identity for odd $n$. Let $n$ be odd and suppose the equation holds for all $x\ne0$. As $p$ and $q$ have no common real root, $V(x)=(p(x),q(x))$ is a non-zero vector for every real $x$, and $N(x)=V(x)/|V(x)|$ is a continuous unit vector on $\mathbb R$. For $x\ne0$ the equation says that the determinant of $V(x)$ and $V(\frac1x)$ vanishes, so $N(\frac1x)=\varepsilon(x)N(x)$ with $\varepsilon(x)=N(x)\cdot N(\frac1x)\in\{1,-1\}$. The function $\varepsilon$ is continuous on each of the intervals $(0,\infty)$ and $(-\infty,0)$, so it is constant on each, and $\varepsilon(1)=\varepsilon(-1)=|N(\pm1)|^2=1$. Hence $N(\frac1x)=N(x)$ for all $x\ne0$.

Let $a,b\ne0$ be the leading coefficients of $p,q$ and $\lambda=(a,b)/|(a,b)|$. As $y\to+\infty$, $V(y)/y^n\to(a,b)$, so $N(y)\to\lambda$; as $y\to-\infty$, $V(y)/|y|^n\to-(a,b)$ because $n$ is odd, so $N(y)\to-\lambda$. Letting $x\to0^+$ in $N(x)=N(\frac1x)$ gives $N(0)=\lambda$, and letting $x\to0^-$ gives $N(0)=-\lambda$. Then $\lambda=-\lambda$, impossible for a unit vector.

Answer. For odd $n$: $2,4,\dots,2n$. For even $n$: $2,4,\dots,2n$, or infinitely many (all $x\ne0$).

Denote by $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ the set of all positive real numbers. Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ such that for any $x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ the following equality holds \[f(x)=f(x+y)\bigl(yf(x)+1\bigr).\]

$f(x)=\dfrac1{x+c}$ with a constant $c\ge0$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pass to 1/f)

Let $g(x)=\frac1{f(x)}>0$. Dividing the equation by $f(x)f(x+y)$ gives \[\frac1{f(x+y)}=y+\frac1{f(x)},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad g(x+y)=g(x)+y\quad(x,y>0).\] For $0<s<t$ take $x=s$, $y=t-s$: $g(t)-t=g(s)-s$. So $g(x)-x$ is a constant $c$, and $g(x)=x+c$. Since $g(x)>0$ for all $x>0$, letting $x\to0^+$ gives $c\ge0$; conversely $c\ge0$ makes $x+c>0$.

Check: for $f(x)=\frac1{x+c}$, $f(x+y)\bigl(yf(x)+1\bigr)=\frac1{x+y+c}\cdot\frac{x+y+c}{x+c}=f(x)$.

So the solutions are $\boxed{f(x)=\frac1{x+c}}$ with $c\ge0$.

Solution 2 (Determine f on (1, infinity) first)

Let $a=f(1)>0$ and $c=\frac1a-1>-1$. With $x=1$ the equation gives, for $y>0$, \[f(1+y)=\frac{a}{ya+1}=\frac1{y+\frac1a},\qquad\text{so}\qquad f(t)=\frac1{t+c}\ \ (t>1).\] Now let $0<x\le1$ and take $y=2-x>0$: $f(x)=f(2)\bigl((2-x)f(x)+1\bigr)$ with $f(2)=\frac1{2+c}$ ($2+c>1$). Problem 11.6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Given an acute triangle $ABC$. Point $D$ is chosen on the line $AH$ (where $AH$ is an altitude) such that $\angle BDC=90^\circ$ and $D$ lies outside the triangle $ABC$. Point $F$ lies on the ray $DB$ such that $\angle FAC>90^\circ$ and $FC$ is the angle bisector of angle $AFB$. Lines $AB$ and $CF$ intersect at $G$. Point $X$ on the ray $BF$ satisfies $GD=GX$. Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $CDF$, $FXG$ and $AHC$ have a common point.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The foot of the perpendicular from C to AF)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Two circles with diameters. Since $\angle AHC=90^\circ$, the circle $(AHC)$ has diameter $AC$. Since $F$ lies on line $DB$ and $\angle CDB=90^\circ$, the circle $(CDF)$ has diameter $CF$.

The common point. Let $P$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to the line $AF$. Then $\angle APC=\angle FPC=90^\circ$ (if $P$ coincides with $A$ or $F$, the corresponding statement is trivial), so $P$ lies on both circles. We show that $P$ lies on $(FXG)$.

Position of $F$. Let $\pi$ be the line through $A$ perpendicular to $AC$. The points $B$ and $D$ lie on the same side of $\pi$ as $C$ (the triangle is acute, so $\angle BAC<90^\circ$. The points $Z$ of line $AH$ with $\angle BZC=90^\circ$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, which meets line $AH$ at two points on opposite sides of $BC$, since $H$ is inside it. As $\angle BAC<90^\circ$, $A$ is outside this circle, so the intersection point on $A$'s side lies on segment $AH$, inside the triangle. Hence $D$ is the other point, on the ray from $A$ through $H$ beyond $H$, and this ray makes the acute angle $\angle HAC=90^\circ-C$ with $AC$.). The condition $\angle FAC>90^\circ$ says that $F$ lies on the other side of $\pi$. So $F$ lies on ray $DB$ beyond $B$; in particular $D$ and $X$ lie on different sides of $B$ on this line, so $X\ne D$.

A reflection. The line $CD$ is perpendicular to $DB=FB$, so $D$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to line $FB$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the line $FC$, the bisector of $\angle AFB$. It fixes $F$, $C$ and $G\in FC$ and maps line $FB$ to line $FA$; hence it maps $D$, the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $FB$, to the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $FA$, which is $P$. Consequently $GP=GD=GX$, and since a reflection reverses directed angles, \[\angle(PG,PF)=-\angle(DG,DF).\]

Concyclicity. The points $X,D,F$ are collinear and $X\ne D$. Triangle $GDX$ is isosceles with $GD=GX$, so its base angles satisfy $\angle(XG,XD)=-\angle(DG,DX)$. Therefore \[\angle(XG,XF)=\angle(XG,XD)=-\angle(DG,DX)=-\angle(DG,DF)=\angle(PG,PF),\] so $X,P,G,F$ are concyclic. (If $P$ coincides with $X$ or $G$, it lies on $(FXG)$ trivially. The circle $(FXG)$ exists only when $F,X,G$ form a triangle, so the statement tacitly assumes $X\ne F$, and we use this in the computation. The assumption is a real restriction: for $BH=1$, $HC=\frac45$, $AH=\frac{\sqrt5}2$ the point $F$ on ray $DB$ with $DF=2\,DB$ satisfies all the conditions, and then $X=F$.)

Hence $P$ lies on the circumcircles of $CDF$, $AHC$ and $FXG$.

The point $P$. Since $\angle AHC=90^\circ$, the circle $(AHC)$ has diameter $AC$; since $F$ lies on line $DB$ and $\angle CDB=90^\circ$, the circle $(CDF)$ has diameter $CF$. Position of $X$. As shown in Solution 1, $D$ lies on the ray from $A$ through $H$ beyond $H$, and $F$ lies on ray $DB$ beyond $B$. Since $X$ lies on ray $BF$, the points $D$ and $X$ are on different sides of $B$ on line $FB$; in particular $X\ne D$. Also $X\ne F$, because $F,X,G$ form a triangle.

The reflection in line $FC$, the bisector of $\angle AFB$, fixes $F$, $C$ and $G\in FC$ and swaps the lines $FB$ and $FA$, so it maps $D$ to the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $FA$, which is $P$. Both lie on the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $XP$, as $QQ'$ is the diameter perpendicular to $XP$. Problem 11.7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Numbers $1,2,\dots,1024$ are written in a row in some order, each number once. In one move it is allowed to choose two numbers $a$ and $b$ such that $a$ is to the left of $b$ and there is exactly one number between $a$ and $b$, erase the number $a$ and replace $b$ by $a+b$. Is it true that for any initial ordering of the numbers it is possible to make $1022$ moves so that the two remaining numbers are equal?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Which splits are reachable, then a subset sum)

Lemma. Let $N\ge3$ numbers $s_1,\dots,s_N$ stand in a row and let $X\subseteq\{1,\dots,N-3\}$ be any set of positions. Then one can make $N-2$ moves so that the two remaining numbers are: on the left, the sum of the $s_i$ with $i\in X\cup\{N-1\}$; on the right, the sum of all the other $s_i$.

Proof by induction on $N$. For $N=3$, $X=\varnothing$: move $s_1$ onto $s_3$; the row becomes $s_2,\ s_1+s_3$. Now let the statement hold for $N$ and consider $N+1$ numbers and $X\subseteq\{1,\dots,N-2\}$. The left result must contain $s_N$, and the right one $s_{N-1}$ and $s_{N+1}$.

  • If $N-2\notin X$: move $s_{N-1}$ onto $s_{N+1}$ (one number, $s_N$, lies between). The row becomes $s_1,\dots,s_{N-2},\ s_N,\ s_{N-1}+s_{N+1}$, a row of $N$ numbers whose position $N-1$ holds $s_N$, and whose positions $N-2$ and $N$ hold $s_{N-2}$ and $s_{N-1}+s_{N+1}$. Apply the induction hypothesis with the same $X\subseteq\{1,\dots,N-3\}$.

  • If $N-2\in X$: move $s_{N-2}$ onto $s_N$. The row becomes $s_1,\dots,s_{N-3},\ s_{N-1},\ s_{N-2}+s_N,\ s_{N+1}$; its position $N-1$ holds $s_{N-2}+s_N$, and positions $N-2$, $N$ hold $s_{N-1}$, $s_{N+1}$. Apply the induction hypothesis with $X\setminus\{N-2\}$.

In both cases the final split is the required one. $\square$

Choosing the split. Here $N=1024$ and the total is $\frac{1024\cdot1025}2=524800$, so we need both final numbers to be $262400$. Let $u,v,w$ be the numbers at positions $1022,1023,1024$. By the Lemma it suffices to find a set $Y$ of numbers among $\{1,\dots,1024\}\setminus\{u,v,w\}$ (they occupy positions $1,\dots,1021$) with \[\textstyle\sum Y=262400-v=255\cdot1025+v',\qquad v'=1025-v\in[1,1024].\] Use the $512$ complementary pairs $\{j,1025-j\}$, each with sum $1025$.

  • If $v'\ge513$: the pairs $\{a,v'-a\}$, $1\le a<\frac{v'}2$, are at least $256$ disjoint pairs of distinct numbers with sum $v'$; each of $u,v,w$ lies in at most one of them, so we can choose one, $\{a,b\}$, avoiding $u,v,w$. Then take $255$ complementary pairs that contain none of $u,v,w,a,b$ (at most $5$ of the $512$ pairs are excluded). Let $Y$ be $\{a,b\}$ together with these pairs: $\sum Y=v'+255\cdot1025$.

  • If $v'\le512$: the pairs $\{a,1025+v'-a\}$ with $v'+1\le a<\frac{1025+v'}2$ are at least $256$ disjoint pairs of distinct numbers in $[1,1024]$ with sum $1025+v'$; choose one, $\{a,b\}$, avoiding $u,v,w$, and $254$ complementary pairs avoiding $u,v,w,a,b$. Then $\sum Y=1025+v'+254\cdot1025=255\cdot1025+v'$.

Applying the Lemma with $X$ the set of positions of the numbers of $Y$, the left number is $\sum Y+v=262400$ and the right one is $524800-262400=262400$. So the answer is yes.

Problem 11.8

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A positive integer $l$ is $k$-starred if it is smaller than $2^k$ and for every positive integer $m<k$ the remainder of $l$ when divided by $2^m$ does not equal $\frac{2^m-1}{2^k-1}\,l$. Let $p$ be a prime number, and $n>1$ be a positive integer which is not divisible by $p$. Prove that the number of $n$-starred and $np$-starred numbers combined is divisible by $2^p$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Starred numbers are primitive binary words; Moebius inversion)

Lemma. For $1\le l<2^k$ let $w(l)$ be the binary word of length $k$ of $l$ (with leading zeros). Then $l$ is $k$-starred if and only if $w(l)$ is primitive, i.e. not of the form $v^{k/d}$ (a word $v$ of length $d$ repeated) with $d\mid k$, $d<k$.

Proof. If $w(l)=v^{k/d}$ with $d<k$, and $v$ also denotes the value of the word $v$, then $l=v(1+2^d+\dots+2^{k-d})=v\cdot\frac{2^k-1}{2^d-1}$, so $l\bmod2^d=v=\frac{2^d-1}{2^k-1}\,l$, and $l$ is not starred ($m=d$).

Conversely, let $r=l\bmod2^m$ equal $\frac{2^m-1}{2^k-1}l$ for some $m<k$. Then $r\ge1$ and \[\xi:=\frac l{2^k-1}=\frac r{2^m-1}\in(0,1].\] The binary expansion of $\frac l{2^k-1}=\sum_{j\ge1}l\,2^{-kj}$ is $0.w(l)w(l)w(l)\dots$, and that of $\frac r{2^m-1}$ is $0.uuu\dots$ with $u$ the $m$-digit word of $r$. Both expansions contain infinitely many ones, and a real number has only one binary expansion with infinitely many ones, so the two infinite sequences of digits coincide. This sequence $(s_i)$ has periods $k$ and $m$, hence period $g=\gcd(k,m)$: choose positive integers $\alpha,\beta$ with $\alpha k-\beta m=g$; then $s_{i+g}=s_{i+g+\beta m}=s_{i+\alpha k}=s_i$. So $w(l)=v^{k/g}$ with $|v|=g\le m<k$, and $w(l)$ is not primitive. $\square$

Let $P(k)$ be the number of primitive binary words of length $k$. For $k\ge2$ the zero word is not primitive, so the number of $k$-starred numbers is $P(k)$. Every word $w$ of length $k$ is $v^{k/d}$ for exactly one primitive word $v$, whose length $d$ divides $k$ (take the shortest $v_0$ with $w=v_0^{k/|v_0|}$; if also $w=v^{k/d}$ with $v$ primitive, the infinite word $www\dots$ has periods $d$ and $|v_0|$, hence period $\gcd(d,|v_0|)$, so $|v_0|\mid d$ by minimality, $v=v_0^{d/|v_0|}$, and $v=v_0$ as $v$ is primitive). Hence \[\sum_{d\mid k}P(d)=2^k,\qquad\text{and by M\"obius inversion}\qquad P(k)=\sum_{d\mid k}\mu(d)\,2^{k/d}.\] Since $p\nmid n$, the divisors of $np$ are the numbers $d$ and $dp$ with $d\mid n$, and $\mu(dp)=-\mu(d)$. Therefore \[P(n)+P(np)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)2^{n/d}+\sum_{d\mid n}\bigl(\mu(d)2^{np/d}-\mu(d)2^{n/d}\bigr)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,\bigl(2^p\bigr)^{n/d},\] and every term is divisible by $2^p$. As $n>1$ and $np>1$, $P(n)+P(np)$ is the number of $n$-starred plus $np$-starred numbers, which is therefore divisible by $2^p$.

Solution 2 (Induction without Moebius)

This solution replaces only the counting step; it uses the Lemma proved in Solution 1 (starred numbers = primitive words). By that Lemma, for $k\ge2$ the number of $k$-starred numbers equals the number $P(k)$ of primitive binary words of length $k$, and $\sum_{d\mid k}P(d)=2^k$ for every $k\ge1$ (each word is a power of a unique primitive word).

Claim: $2^p\mid P(e)+P(ep)$ for every positive integer $e$ with $p\nmid e$. We use strong induction on $e$. The divisors of $ep$ are the $d$ and $dp$ with $d\mid e$, so \[2^{ep}=\sum_{d\mid ep}P(d)=\sum_{d\mid e}\bigl(P(d)+P(dp)\bigr),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad P(e)+P(ep)=2^{ep}-\sum_{d\mid e,\ d<e}\bigl(P(d)+P(dp)\bigr).\] Every $d\mid e$ is not divisible by $p$, so by the induction hypothesis each term of the last sum is divisible by $2^p$ (for $e=1$ the sum is empty), and so is $2^{ep}$. This proves the claim.

Applying the claim with $e=n$ ($n>1$, $p\nmid n$) shows that the number of $n$-starred and $np$-starred numbers combined, $P(n)+P(np)$, is divisible by $2^p$.

Solution 3 (Adding a constant to every block)

This solution uses the Lemma of Solution 1 and the fact proved there that every binary word $w$ of length $k$ equals $v^{k/d}$ for exactly one primitive word $v$ (the root of $w$), whose length $d$ divides $k$. Let $P(k)$ be the number of primitive binary words of length $k$; for $k\ge2$ it is the number of $k$-starred numbers. For $d\mid k$, the map $v\mapsto v^{k/d}$ is a bijection from the primitive words of length $d$ onto the words of length $k$ whose root has length $d$, so there are $P(d)$ such words.

Roots and powers. If $w=v^{k/\ell}$ for a word $v$ of length $\ell$, then the root of $v$ is also a root of $w$, so its length is $d$ and $d\mid\ell$. Conversely, if $d\mid\ell\mid k$, then $w=\bigl(r^{\ell/d}\bigr)^{k/\ell}$, where $r$ is the root. So $w$ is a power of a word of length $\ell$ exactly when $d\mid\ell$.

Block words. Let $\Sigma$ be the set of the $2^p$ binary words of length $p$, identified with the residues modulo $2^p$ by their values. A binary word $w$ of length $np$ is the same as a word $W$ of length $n$ over the alphabet $\Sigma$ (cut $w$ into blocks of $p$ digits). The word $W$ is not primitive exactly when $W=V^{n/e}$ for some $e\mid n$, $e<n$, that is, when $w$ is a power of a binary word of length $ep$ with $e\mid n$, $e<n$; by the previous paragraph, exactly when $d\mid ep$ for such an $e$, where $d$ is the length of the root of $w$. Since $d\mid np$ and $p\nmid n$, we can write $d=d_1$ or $d=d_1p$ with $d_1\mid n$, and $p\nmid d_1$. In both cases $d\mid ep$ if and only if $d_1\mid e$. So $W$ is not primitive exactly when $d_1$ divides a proper divisor of $n$, that is, when $d_1<n$. Hence $W$ is primitive exactly when $d=n$ or $d=np$, and the number of primitive words of length $n$ over $\Sigma$ is \[P(n)+P(np).\]

Shifting all letters. For a residue $s$ modulo $2^p$, let $T_s$ add $s$ (modulo $2^p$) to every letter of a word over $\Sigma$. It is a bijection with inverse $T_{-s}$, and $T_s(V^r)=(T_sV)^r$, so $T_s$ and $T_{-s}$ map non-primitive words to non-primitive words; hence $T_s$ maps the primitive words onto themselves. If $s\ne0$ then $T_s$ changes every letter, so $T_sW\ne W$; consequently the $2^p$ words $T_sW$ ($s=0,1,\dots,2^p-1$) are distinct, since $T_sW=T_{s'}W$ implies $T_{s-s'}W=W$. The sets $\{T_sW\}$ split the primitive words into classes of exactly $2^p$ words each, so their number is divisible by $2^p$.

As $n>1$ and $np>1$, $P(n)+P(np)$ is the number of $n$-starred plus $np$-starred numbers, which is therefore divisible by $2^p$.
