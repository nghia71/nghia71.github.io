Problem 1

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $N=2n(n+1)$. Suppose $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N$ are nonnegative real numbers such that $x_1+x_2+\dots+x_N=N$. Find the maximal value of \[\sum_{1\le i<j\le N}\max(x_i,\,x_j,\,x_ix_j).\]

Show the answer

$2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1)$

Show the solution

Solution (Sort, then count the large entries)

Let $S$ be the sum. Relabel so that $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_N$. For $i<j$ we have $\max(x_i,x_j,x_ix_j)=x_i\max(1,x_j)$: if $x_j\ge1$ then $x_ix_j\ge x_i\ge x_j$, and if $x_j<1$ then $x_ix_j<x_i$. Hence \[S=\sum_{i<j}x_i\max(1,x_j).\] Let $k$ be the number of indices with $x_j>1$ (these are $j=1,\dots,k$), $T=x_1+\dots+x_k$ and $R=N-T=\sum_{i>k}x_i$.

The case $k=0$. Then $S=\sum_i x_i(N-i)\le(N-1)\sum x_i=N(N-1)$.

The case $k\ge1$. For $i\le k$, $\sum_{j>i}\max(1,x_j)=\sum_{i<j\le k}x_j+(N-k)$; for $i>k$ it is $N-i\le N-k-1$. Therefore \[S\le T(N-k)+\sum_{i<j\le k}x_ix_j+R(N-k-1).\] Since $\sum_{i<j\le k}x_ix_j=\frac{T^2-\sum_{i\le k}x_i^2}{2}\le\frac{T^2}2\bigl(1-\frac1k\bigr)$ (as $\sum_{i\le k}x_i^2\ge\frac{T^2}k$) and $R=N-T$, \[S\le N(N-k-1)+T+\frac{k-1}{2k}T^2\le N(N-k)+\frac{k-1}{2k}N^2=\frac32N^2-\Bigl(\frac{N^2}{2k}+kN\Bigr),\] using $T\le N$. The case $k=0$ gives the same bound as $k=1$.

Optimising over $k$. With $N=2n(n+1)$, $\frac{N^2}{2k}+kN=N\bigl(\frac{n(n+1)}{k}+k\bigr)$, and for every positive integer $k$ \[\frac{n(n+1)}{k}+k\ge2n+1\iff k^2-(2n+1)k+n(n+1)\ge0\iff(k-n)(k-n-1)\ge0,\] which holds for integers. Hence \[S\le\frac32N^2-(2n+1)N=6n^2(n+1)^2-2n(n+1)(2n+1)=2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1).\] Equality. Take $k=n$ entries equal to $\frac Nn=2(n+1)$ and the rest $0$. Pairs of large entries give $\binom n2\cdot4(n+1)^2$, pairs (large, zero) give $n(N-n)\cdot2(n+1)$, and pairs of zeros give $0$; the total is \[2n(n-1)(n+1)^2+2n(n+1)(2n^2+n)=2n(n+1)\bigl[(n^2-1)+2n^2+n\bigr]=2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1).\] (Also $k=n+1$ entries equal to $2n$ give equality.) The maximum is $\boxed{2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1)}$.

Problem 2

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with incenter $I$ and $A$-excenter $J$. Let $X$ be a point on $BC$. Define points $P$ and $Q$ such that, in directed angles, \[\measuredangle PAX=\measuredangle PJA=\measuredangle XJQ\quad\text{and}\quad\measuredangle PXA=\measuredangle PJX=\measuredangle JXQ.\] Suppose the circles $(PIJ)$ and $(QBC)$ intersect at two points $U,V$. Prove that $UVMN$ is cyclic, where $M,N$ are the midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$.

Show the answer

proof (not completed: one step verified only numerically)

Show the solution

Solution (Partial progress: (PIJ) is a fixed circle, and it would suffice that Q lies on (ABC))

Partial progress only. Let $b=CA$, $c=AB$, let $A_1$ be the antipode of $A$ on the circumcircle $(ABC)$, $K$ the reflection of $A_1$ in the bisector $AI$, and $S$ the second intersection of $AI$ with $(ABC)$. Recall $AI\cdot AJ=bc$ and that $S$ is the midpoint of $IJ$ (incenter–excenter lemma).

Reading the definitions. $\measuredangle PAX=\measuredangle PJA$ says that line $AX$ is tangent at $A$ to the circle $\omega_1=(APJ)$, and $\measuredangle PXA=\measuredangle PJX$ says that $AX$ is tangent at $X$ to $\omega_2=(XPJ)$; so $P$ is the second common point of these two circles. Also $\measuredangle XJQ=\measuredangle PJA$ and $\measuredangle JXQ=\measuredangle PXA$ say that $JQ,JP$ are isogonal in angle $XJA$ and $XQ,XP$ are isogonal in angle $JXA$: $Q$ is the isogonal conjugate of $P$ in triangle $AJX$.

Claim 1: $P$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with centre $K$ through $I$ and $J$; hence $(PIJ)=\Gamma$ does not depend on $X$. First, $\Gamma$ is well defined: $\angle ASA_1=90^\circ$, so the projection of $A_1$, and hence of $K$, on line $AI$ is $S$, the midpoint of $IJ$; thus $KI=KJ$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and power $bc$; it swaps $I$ and $J$ and maps $\Gamma$ to itself (the power of $A$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $AI\cdot AJ=bc$). Since $A\in\omega_1$ and $\omega_1$ is tangent to $AX$ at $A$, $\iota(\omega_1)$ is the line through $I$ parallel to $AX$. Since $\omega_2$ passes through $J$ and $X$ and is tangent at $X$ to the line $AX$ (which passes through $A$), $\iota(\omega_2)$ is the circle through $I$ and $X'=\iota(X)$ tangent to $AX$ at $X'$. So $P^*=\iota(P)$ is the second common point of a chord through $I$ parallel to the tangent at $X'$; hence $X'I=X'P^*$ and $P^*$ is the reflection of $I$ in the line $m$ through $X'$ perpendicular to $AX$. Now $X'$ lies on $\iota(BC)$, the circle through $A$ and the points of rays $AB$, $AC$ at distances $b$, $c$ from $A$, i.e. the reflection of $(ABC)$ in $AI$; its antipode of $A$ is $K$, so by Thales the line $m$ passes through $K$. Therefore $KP^*=KI$, i.e. $P^*\in\Gamma$, and $P=\iota(P^*)\in\iota(\Gamma)=\Gamma$.

Claim 2: the common points $U',V'$ of $\Gamma$ and $(ABC)$ are concyclic with $M,N$. Use vectors with origin $A$; let $O$ be the circumcentre. The powers of a point $Z$ are $f(Z)=|Z|^2-2Z\cdot O$ for $(ABC)$ and $g(Z)=|Z|^2-2Z\cdot K+bc$ for $\Gamma$ (centre $K$, and $g(A)=bc$). Since $|O-C|=|O|$ we have $2O\cdot C=b^2$, and since $K$ and $2O$ are reflections in $AI$, $K\cdot B=2O\cdot C'$ where $C'=\frac cbC$ is the reflection of $B$; so $K\cdot B=bc$, and likewise $K\cdot C=bc$. With $M=\frac B2$, $N=\frac C2$: \[f(M)=-\tfrac{c^2}4,\quad g(M)=\tfrac{c^2}4,\qquad f(N)=-\tfrac{b^2}4,\quad g(N)=\tfrac{b^2}4 .\] Hence $M$ and $N$ lie on the circle $f+g=0$, i.e. $2|Z|^2-2Z\cdot(O+K)+bc=0$, which also passes through $U'$ and $V'$ (where $f=g=0$). So $U',V',M,N$ are concyclic.

What is missing. By Claims 1 and 2 the statement follows once one shows that $Q$ lies on $(ABC)$ (then $(QBC)=(ABC)$ and $\{U,V\}=\{U',V'\}$). From the isogonal-conjugate description one gets $\measuredangle AQJ=\measuredangle JQX=\measuredangle JAX-\measuredangle JXA$ and $\measuredangle AQX=2\measuredangle AJX$, so $Q$ is the second intersection of the $J$-symmedian of triangle $AJX$ with the circle through $A$, $X$ and the circumcentre of $AJX$. That this point lies on $(ABC)$ is confirmed numerically (verify/t2facts.py) but was not proved.

Problem 3

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · High-Level Olympiad Synthesis

Wong has $n$ coins of distinct unknown weights and a device that, given $2025$ distinct coins, returns the coin with the median weight. After using the device finitely many times, Wong orders all coins in a row. For each $1\le i\le n$, he earns $1$ point if the $i$-th lightest coin is in the $i$-th position. Determine the maximum number of points he can guarantee.

Show the answer

for $n\ge2025$: $2\left\lfloor\frac{n-2024}{4}\right\rfloor+\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon=1$ for odd $n$ and $\varepsilon=0$ for even $n$ (that is, $\frac{n-2024}2,\frac{n-2023}2,\frac{n-2026}2,\frac{n-2025}2$ for $n\equiv0,1,2,3\pmod 4$); for $2\le n\le2024$ the answer is $0$

Show the solution

Solution (Indistinguishable orders, then pairs of mirror positions)

Put $t=1012$, so the device takes $2t+1$ coins and returns the one that has exactly $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins among them. The rank $r(c)$ of a coin $c$ is $i$ if $c$ is the $i$-th lightest. Call the $t$ lightest coins light, the $t$ heaviest heavy, and the others middle.

If $n\le2024$ the device cannot be used, and for $n\ge2$ the weights may be such that the coin in position $i$ has rank $i+1$ (and the coin in position $n$ has rank $1$): Wong scores $0$. From now on $n\ge2025$; there are $m=n-2024$ middle coins, of ranks $t+1,\dots,n-t$. Put $P=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$. We show that the answer is \[A=2\Bigl\lfloor\frac P2\Bigr\rfloor+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon=m-2P\in\{0,1\},\] which equals $2\lfloor\frac{n-2024}4\rfloor+\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon=1$ exactly for odd $n$.

Mirror positions. For a middle rank $i$ with $i<n+1-i$, the positions $i$ and $i'=n+1-i$ form a mirror pair; there are $P$ such pairs, and if $m$ is odd the middle rank $\frac{n+1}2$ is a central position.

Step 1: worlds that the device cannot tell apart. Starting from the true ranks $r$, consider (i) the reversed ranks $n+1-r(c)$; (ii) any permutation of the ranks among the light coins; (iii) the same among the heavy coins. For every set $S$ of $2t+1$ coins the device gives the same answer in all these worlds. For (i) this is clear: the median has $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins in $S$, a symmetric condition. For (ii): the answer $x$ has $t$ lighter coins in $S$, so $r(x)\ge t+1$ and $x$ is not light. Permuting ranks among light coins keeps every light coin lighter than every other coin and changes no comparison between two non-light coins, so it changes no comparison between $x$ and another coin, and $x$ is still the median. (iii) is the same. Hence Wong's questions, the answers, and his final arrangement are identical in all these worlds.

Step 2: Wong cannot guarantee more than $A$. Let coin $c$ end in position $\pi(c)$, and compare the true world $r$ with the reversed world $\bar r$. Take a mirror pair $\{i,i'\}$ and let $A,A'$ be the coins with $r(A)=i$, $r(A')=i'$. In world $r$ a point is scored at position $i$ iff $\pi(A)=i$, and at $i'$ iff $\pi(A')=i'$. In world $\bar r$ the coin of rank $i$ is $A'$ and that of rank $i'$ is $A$, so a point is scored at $i$ iff $\pi(A')=i$ and at $i'$ iff $\pi(A)=i'$. If world $r$ scores at $i$, then $\pi(A)=i$, so world $\bar r$ scores neither at $i$ (occupied by $A$) nor at $i'$ (that needs $\pi(A)=i'$); if world $r$ scores at $i'$, the same argument with $A'$ applies. So every mirror pair gives points to at most one of the two worlds, and at most $2$. Let $\alpha,\beta$ be the numbers of points at mirror positions in the worlds $r,\bar r$. Then $\alpha+\beta\le2P$. If $P$ is even, $\min(\alpha,\beta)\le P=2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. If $P$ is odd and $\alpha,\beta\ge P$, then $\alpha+\beta=2P$, so every pair gives exactly $2$ points to one world; then $\alpha,\beta$ are even, hence both at least $P+1$, a contradiction. So in all cases $\min(\alpha,\beta)\le2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. The central position gives at most one point.

Choose, among $r$ and $\bar r$, a world $w$ with at most $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$ points at mirror positions. Now permute the ranks of the light coins of $w$ so that none of them stands in the position equal to its rank. This is possible because $t\ge2$: list the light coins as $c_1,\dots,c_t$, take a bijection $\tau$ from them onto $\{1,\dots,t\}$ with $\tau(c_k)=\pi(c_k)$ whenever $\pi(c_k)\le t$ (possible, as $\pi$ is injective), and give $c_k$ the new rank $\tau(c_{k+1})$ (indices modulo $t$); this differs from $\tau(c_k)$, so $c_k$ is not in its place. Do the same with the heavy coins. By Step 1 this world is consistent with everything Wong has seen, and in it he scores nothing at the $2t$ outer positions, so at most $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor+\varepsilon=A$ points.

Step 3: what Wong can learn. Wong asks the device about all $\binom n{2t+1}$ sets.

  • The middle coins are exactly the coins returned at least once: a middle coin $x$ is the median of $x$ together with $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins (there are enough of them since $t+1\le r(x)\le n-t$), and Step 1 showed that light and heavy coins are never returned. If $m=1$ this already gives $A=1$: put the middle coin in the central position. If $m=2$ then $A=0$. Let $m\ge3$, so $n\ge2t+3$.

  • Let $x$ be middle and $y\ne z$ other coins; Wong can tell whether $y,z$ are on the same side of $x$ (both lighter or both heavier). The sets with answer $x$ consist of $x$, $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins. Call a coin forced if it lies in all of them. A side of $x$ with exactly $t$ coins consists of forced coins, a side with more coins contains none; since $n\ge2t+2$, at most one side is forced. If $y,z$ are both forced they are on the same side; if exactly one is forced they are on different sides. If neither is forced, pick a set $S$ with answer $x$ that contains $y$ but not $z$ (on each non-forced side there are at least $t+1$ coins, so we can choose $t$ of them avoiding $z$ and, on the side of $y$, including $y$). Then the set $S\setminus\{y\}\cup\{z\}$ has answer $x$ if and only if $y$ and $z$ are on the same side of $x$.

  • For middle coins $x,y,z$, the coin $x$ lies between $y$ and $z$ in weight iff $y,z$ are on different sides of $x$. So Wong knows, for every triple of middle coins, which one is between the other two. Exactly two middle coins are never between two others (the lightest and the heaviest middle coin); call one of them $e$. Then $y$ is lighter than $z$ in the order starting from $e$ iff $y=e$ or $y$ lies between $e$ and $z$. Thus Wong knows the order of the middle coins up to reversal, i.e. a labelling $\rho$ of the middle coins by $t+1,\dots,n-t$ which is either $r$ or $\bar r$ on them.

Step 4: a strategy scoring $A$. Split the mirror pairs into two groups $G_1,G_2$ of sizes $\lceil P/2\rceil$ and $\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. For $\{i,i'\}\in G_1$ put the coin with $\rho=i$ at position $i$ and the coin with $\rho=i'$ at position $i'$; for $\{i,i'\}\in G_2$ put the coin with $\rho=i$ at $i'$ and the coin with $\rho=i'$ at $i$. If $m$ is odd, put the coin with $\rho=\frac{n+1}2$ in the central position; the light and heavy coins fill the remaining positions. If $\rho=r$, every pair of $G_1$ gives $2$ points; if $\rho=\bar r$, every pair of $G_2$ gives $2$ points; the central coin scores in both cases. So Wong always gets at least $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor+\varepsilon=A$ points.

Hence the maximum is $\boxed{2\lfloor\frac{n-2024}4\rfloor+\varepsilon}$ for $n\ge2025$, with $\varepsilon=1$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$ (and $0$ for $2\le n\le2024$).

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

A sequence of positive integers $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ satisfies, for all $n\ge1$, \[a_{2n}=a_{2n-2}\,a_{2n-1},\qquad a_{2n+1}=a_{2n}+a_{2n-1}.\] Determine the maximum number of perfect squares that can appear in this sequence.

Show the answer

$4$

Show the solution

Solution (Even terms, one pair, and odd terms trapped between consecutive squares)

Write $b_k=a_{2k}$ and $c_k=a_{2k+1}$ for $k\ge0$. The recurrences read, for $k\ge1$, \[b_k=b_{k-1}c_{k-1},\qquad c_k=b_k+c_{k-1}=c_{k-1}(b_{k-1}+1).\] Four squares are possible. For $a_0=a_1=1$ the sequence begins $1,1,1,2,2,4,8,12,\dots$, with the squares $a_0,a_1,a_2,a_5$.

Step 1: $a_{2k}$ is not a square for $k\ge2$. With $m=b_{k-2}\ge1$, \[b_k=b_{k-1}c_{k-1}=b_{k-2}c_{k-2}\cdot c_{k-2}(b_{k-2}+1)=c_{k-2}^2\,m(m+1),\] and $m(m+1)$ is not a square because $m^2<m(m+1)<(m+1)^2$. So $b_k$ is not a square.

Step 2: $a_2$ and $a_3$ are not both squares. We have $b_1c_1=b_0c_0\cdot c_0(b_0+1)=c_0^2\,b_0(b_0+1)$. If $b_1=s^2$ and $c_1=r^2$, then $b_0(b_0+1)=(rs/c_0)^2$ is an integer that is the square of a rational number, hence a perfect square, which is impossible as in Step 1.

Step 3: $a_{2k+1}$ is not a square for $k\ge3$. For $k\ge2$, applying the second recurrence twice and then the first, \[c_k=c_{k-1}(b_{k-1}+1)=c_{k-2}(b_{k-2}+1)(b_{k-2}c_{k-2}+1)=y(m+1)(my+1),\] where $m=b_{k-2}$ and $y=c_{k-2}$. Multiplying out, \[4c_k=4m(m+1)y^2+4(m+1)y=\bigl((2m+1)y\bigr)^2+y\,(4m+4-y).\] Now let $k\ge3$, so $j=k-2\ge1$. Then $y=c_j=c_{j-1}(b_{j-1}+1)\ge2$, and $y=c_j=b_j+c_{j-1}\le2b_j=2m$ because $c_{j-1}\le b_{j-1}c_{j-1}=b_j$. Hence $0<y(4m+4-y)$, and \[y(4m+4-y)-\bigl(2(2m+1)y+1\bigr)=-(y-1)^2<0 .\] Therefore \[\bigl((2m+1)y\bigr)^2<4c_k<\bigl((2m+1)y+1\bigr)^2,\] so $4c_k$ lies strictly between two consecutive squares. It is not a square, so neither is $c_k$.

Conclusion. By Steps 1 and 3 every square of the sequence is among $a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,a_5$, and by Step 2 at most one of $a_2,a_3$ is a square. Hence at most $4$ terms are squares, and the example shows that $4$ occurs. The maximum is $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Given $n\ge4$ lines in general position, a panda is located at the intersection of every pair of the $n$ lines. Two pandas are friends of each other if they lie on the same line, with no other pandas between them. A panda is called friendly if it has exactly four friends.

Find all possible numbers of friendly pandas over all configurations of the $n$ lines.

Show the answer

for odd $n$: every integer from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2$; for even $n$: every integer from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Double ends: convex hull, directions of end rays, and a small rotation)

Each line carries $n-1\ge3$ pandas (its intersections with the other lines; they are distinct because no three lines are concurrent). The first and the last panda on a line are its two ends. A panda $V=\ell\cap m$ has two friends on $\ell$, or one if it is an end of $\ell$, and likewise on $m$; the friends on $\ell$ differ from those on $m$. So $V$ is friendly iff it is an end of neither of its lines. Call $V$ a double end if it is an end of both of its lines; let $e$ be the number of double ends and $e'$ the number of pandas that are an end of exactly one line. Counting the $2n$ pairs (line, end) gives $e'+2e=2n$, so the number of friendly pandas is \[F=\binom n2-e-e'=\binom n2-2n+e=\frac{n(n-5)}2+e .\] We prove that the possible values of $e$ are exactly $3,4,\dots,n$ for odd $n$ and $3,4,\dots,n-1$ for even $n$; since $\frac{n(n-5)}2+3=\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ and $\frac{n(n-5)}2+n=\frac{n(n-3)}2$, this gives the answer.

Step 1: $e\ge3$. The pandas are not all collinear (the $n-1$ pandas of $\ell_1$ lie on $\ell_1$, while $\ell_2\cap\ell_3$ does not), so their convex hull is a polygon with at least $3$ vertices, all pandas. If a vertex $V=\ell\cap m$ were not an end of $\ell$, it would lie strictly between two pandas of $\ell$ and could not be a vertex. So every vertex is a double end.

Step 2: $e\le n$. For each end $E$ of a line $\ell$, the end ray is the open ray of $\ell$ starting at $E$ that contains no panda. There are $2n$ end rays; their directions are $2n$ distinct points of the circle of directions (each line gives two opposite directions, and no two lines are parallel). These points are centrally symmetric and cut the circle into $2n$ arcs, the gaps, which form $n$ pairs of opposite gaps.

Let $V=\ell\cap m$ be a double end with end rays $\rho\subset\ell$ and $\sigma\subset m$, and let $W$ be the closed angular region bounded by $\rho$ and $\sigma$ (its angle is less than $180^\circ$). (a) No other line $k$ meets $W$: $k$ does not pass through $V$, and it meets neither $\rho$ nor $\sigma$, which contain no pandas; a line meeting $W$ would have to cross its boundary, since $W$ contains no whole line. (b) No end-ray direction $w$ lies strictly inside the angle of $W$: $w$ would be a direction of some line $k\ne\ell,m$ (the other directions of $\ell,m$ are opposite to those of $\rho,\sigma$), and for a point $K\in k$ and large $s$ the point $K+sw=V+s\bigl(w+\frac{K-V}s\bigr)$ lies in $W$, contradicting (a). So the directions of $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are neighbours on the circle, and the arc between them is a gap: the gap of $V$.

Different double ends have different gaps (a gap determines its two end directions, hence the two end rays and $V$). Two opposite gaps cannot both be gaps of double ends: the opposite gap is bounded by the opposite directions of the same two lines $\ell,m$, so it could only be the gap of $V=\ell\cap m$ again, with end rays opposite to $\rho,\sigma$; then $V$ would be the end of $\ell$ in both directions, i.e. the only panda on $\ell$. So at most one gap of each opposite pair is used, and $e\le n$.

Step 3: $e\ne n$ for even $n$. Suppose $e=n$. Then $e'=0$, so every end is a double end and every end ray is used by exactly one double end (an end ray determines its endpoint), whose gap is one of the two gaps adjacent to that ray's direction. Number the lines so that, going counterclockwise, the directions are $u_1,\dots,u_n,-u_1,\dots,-u_n$ with $u_i$ a direction of $\ell_i$. The gaps are $g_i=(u_i,u_{i+1})$ for $i<n$, $g_n=(u_n,-u_1)$, and their opposites $-g_i$. Exactly one gap of each pair $\{g_i,-g_i\}$ is used; put $\varepsilon_i=1$ if it is $g_i$ and $\varepsilon_i=-1$ if it is $-g_i$.

  • For $2\le i\le n-1$ the gaps adjacent to $u_i$ are $g_{i-1},g_i$; exactly one is used, so $\varepsilon_i=-\varepsilon_{i-1}$. Hence $\varepsilon_{n-1}=(-1)^{n-2}\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_1$.

  • The gaps adjacent to $u_1$ are $-g_n$ and $g_1$; exactly one is used, so $\varepsilon_n=\varepsilon_1$.

  • The gaps adjacent to $u_n$ are $g_{n-1}$ and $g_n$; so $\varepsilon_n=-\varepsilon_{n-1}$.

Together $\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_n=-\varepsilon_{n-1}=-\varepsilon_1$, a contradiction.

Step 4: constructions. (a) Regular configurations. For odd $k=2s+1\ge3$ take the $k$ tangents of a unit circle at the points $T_j$ with angles $\frac{2\pi j}k$. No two are parallel (no two $T_j$ are antipodal) and no three are concurrent (from an outside point there are only two tangents). The tangents at angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$, $0<|\beta-\alpha|<\pi$, meet on the tangent at $\alpha$ at signed distance $\tan\frac{\beta-\alpha}2$ from $T_\alpha$. On the tangent $j$ the other tangents $j+d$ ($d=\pm1,\dots,\pm s$) therefore appear in the order of $d$, and its ends are its intersections with the tangents $j+s$ and $j-s$. Since the ends of tangent $j+s$ are with $j+2s=j-1$ and $j$, every pair $\{j,j+s\}$ gives a double end; these are $k$ different pandas, so $e=k$ by Step 2.

(b) Two operations. Take a configuration, a line $\ell$ with ends $E_1=\ell\cap m_1$, $E_2=\ell\cap m_2$, a point $P\in\ell$ that is not a panda, and let $\ell'$ be $\ell$ rotated about $P$ through a small angle $\theta\ne0$. Let $\delta>0$ be less than half the smallest distance between two different points among the pandas and $P$. For $\theta$ small enough, $\ell'$ is parallel to no line and passes through no panda, each new panda $\ell'\cap m$ is within $\delta$ of $\ell\cap m$, and the pandas of $\ell'$ appear along $\ell'$ in the same order as the corresponding pandas along $\ell$, with $P$ in the same place (all by continuity in $\theta$). Then on every line $k\ne\ell,\ell'$ the new panda $\ell'\cap k$ is next to $\ell\cap k$. Consequently:

  • A panda on neither $\ell$ nor $\ell'$ is a double end after the operation iff it was before.

  • For $m\in\{m_1,m_2\}$ with $E=\ell\cap m$ still an end of $\ell$: the pandas $\ell\cap m$ and $\ell'\cap m$ are ends of $\ell$ and $\ell'$, and they are neighbours on $m$; if $E$ was an end of $m$, exactly one of them is now an end of $m$, otherwise neither is. So they contribute exactly what $E$ contributed before.

Operation (I): $P$ strictly between two pandas of $\ell$. Then $\ell$ and $\ell'$ keep the ends $E_1,E_2$ (resp. their copies), $P$ is not an end, and by the two bullets $e$ does not change.

Operation (II): $P$ on the end ray at $E_2$, where $E_2$ is not a double end. Now the ends of $\ell$ are $E_1,P$ and those of $\ell'$ are $\ell'\cap m_1,P$; so $P$ is a new double end, $E_1$ contributes as before, and $\ell\cap m_2,\ell'\cap m_2$ are not double ends, just as $E_2$ was not. So $e$ increases by $1$.

(c) All values occur. For odd $k$ with $3\le k\le n$: start from (a) with $k$ lines and apply (I) $n-k$ times. For even $k$ with $4\le k\le n-1$: start from (a) with $k-1$ lines, apply (I) once ($k$ lines, $e=k-1$); now $e'=2k-2e>0$, so some end is not a double end; apply (II) at it ($k+1$ lines, $e=k$), and then (I) $n-k-1$ times. With Steps 1–3 the possible values of $e$ are exactly $3,\dots,n$ for odd $n$ and $3,\dots,n-1$ for even $n$.

Hence the number of friendly pandas can be exactly every integer from $\boxed{\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2}$ to $\boxed{\frac{n(n-3)}2}$ when $n$ is odd, and from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$ when $n$ is even.

Problem 6

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Reconstruction from Functional Conditions

Given a fixed positive integer $n$, find all polynomials $P$ and $Q$ with real coefficients such that \[P\bigl(Q(x)+Q(y)\bigr)=Q\bigl(P(x)+x^nQ(y)\bigr)+Q\bigl(P(y)+y^nQ(x)\bigr).\]

Show the answer

For every $n$: $Q\equiv c$ constant and $P$ any polynomial with $P(2c)=2c$. Besides these, for $n=1$: $(P,Q)=(x^{m+1},x^m)$ with $m\ge2$, $(x^2+cx,\,x)$ with $c\in\mathbb R$, and $(\frac{x^3}9,\,-\frac{x^2}3)$; for $n=2$: $(\frac{x^3}3+cx,\,x)$ and $(-\frac{x^3}3+cx,\,-x)$ with $c\in\mathbb R$; for $n\ge3$ there are no others

Show the solution

Solution (Leading homogeneous parts, then the lower terms)

Write (E) for the identity $P(Q(x)+Q(y))=Q(P(x)+x^nQ(y))+Q(P(y)+y^nQ(x))$.

The listed pairs work. If $Q\equiv c$, (E) reads $P(2c)=2c$. For $n=1$, $Q=x^m$, $P=x^{m+1}$: $P(x)+xQ(y)=x(x^m+y^m)$, so the right side is $(x^m+y^m)^m(x^m+y^m)=P(x^m+y^m)$. For $n=1$, $Q=x$, $P=x^2+cx$: the right side is $P(x)+P(y)+2xy=(x+y)^2+c(x+y)$. For $n=1$, $Q=-\frac{x^2}3$, $P=\frac{x^3}9$: $P(x)+xQ(y)=\frac x9(x^2-3y^2)$, and the right side is $-\frac1{243}\bigl[x^2(x^2-3y^2)^2+y^2(y^2-3x^2)^2\bigr]=-\frac{(x^2+y^2)^3}{243}$, which is the left side. For $n=2$, $Q=\epsilon x$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+cx$ ($\epsilon=\pm1$): the right side is $\epsilon(P(x)+P(y))+xy(x+y)=\frac{(x+y)^3}3+\epsilon c(x+y)=P(\epsilon(x+y))$.

From now on $Q$ is not constant: $\deg Q=q\ge1$ with leading coefficient $b$.

Step 0: $P$ is not constant. If $P\equiv k$, put $y=x$: $2Q(k+x^nQ(x))=k$; as $x^nQ(x)$ takes infinitely many values, $Q$ would be constant. So $\deg P=p\ge1$; let $a$ be its leading coefficient.

Step 1: $p=n+q$. Compare the top homogeneous components of both sides. On the left it is $ab^p(x^q+y^q)^p$, of degree $pq$. Put $A=P(x)+x^nQ(y)$; its top component $A^*$ is $ax^p$, $bx^ny^q$ or $ax^p+bx^ny^q$ according as $p>n+q$, $p<n+q$ or $p=n+q$ (the two monomials are different). The right side has top component $b\bigl[A^*(x,y)^q+A^*(y,x)^q\bigr]$, which is not zero: if $p\ge n+q$, its value at $(1,0)$ is $ba^q\ne0$; if $p<n+q$ it is $b^{q+1}(x^{nq}y^{q^2}+x^{q^2}y^{nq})$. So the right side has degree $q\max(p,n+q)$, which forces $p\ge n+q$. If $p>n+q$, the top components are $ab^p(x^q+y^q)^p$ and $ba^q(x^{pq}+y^{pq})$; since $p\ge2$, the first contains $x^{q(p-1)}y^q$ with coefficient $pab^p\ne0$ and the second does not. Hence $p=n+q$, and with $u=x^q$, $v=y^q$ the top components give the polynomial identity \[ab^p(u+v)^p=b\bigl[u^n(au+bv)^q+v^n(av+bu)^q\bigr]. \tag{1}\] Step 2: the leading coefficients. Comparing the coefficients of $u^p$ gives $ab^p=ba^q$. With $\lambda=a/b$ this means $b^n=\lambda^{q-1}$, and dividing (1) by $b^{q+1}$ we get \[\lambda^q(u+v)^{n+q}=u^n(\lambda u+v)^q+v^n(\lambda v+u)^q, \tag{2}\] whose coefficients of $u^{p-k}v^k$ give (binomial coefficients with an index out of range are $0$) \[\lambda^q\binom pk=\binom qk\lambda^{q-k}+\binom q{k-n}\lambda^{k-n}. \tag{3}\] Putting $(u,v)=(-1,1)$ in (2): $0=(\lambda-1)^q\bigl((-1)^{n+q}+1\bigr)$, so $\lambda=1$ or $n+q$ is odd. Putting $(u,v)=(1,1)$: $\lambda^q2^{p}=2(\lambda+1)^q$.

  • $n\ge3$: (3) with $k=1$ gives $\lambda=\frac qp$. With $k=2$: if $q=1$ it gives $\lambda\binom p2=0$, impossible; if $q\ge2$ it gives $\lambda^2p(p-1)=q(q-1)$, i.e. $q(p-1)=p(q-1)$, so $p=q$, impossible. So there is no solution with $Q$ nonconstant.

  • $\lambda=1$: $(1,1)$ gives $2^{n+q}=2^{q+1}$, so $n=1$, and $b=\lambda^{q-1}=1=a$.

  • $\lambda\ne1$, $n=1$, $q$ even: $(1,1)$ gives $(2\lambda)^q=(\lambda+1)^q$, so $2\lambda=-(\lambda+1)$, $\lambda=-\frac13$. Then (3) with $k=1$ reads $\lambda^q(q+1)=q\lambda^{q-1}+1$, i.e. $3^q=4q+1$, so $q=2$ (for $q\ge3$, $3^q>4q+1$). Thus $b=\lambda=-\frac13$, $a=\frac19$.

  • $\lambda\ne1$, $n=2$, $q$ odd: $k=1$ gives $\lambda=\frac q{q+2}$, and $(1,1)$ becomes $2=(1+\frac1q)^q$; for $q\ge2$ the binomial theorem gives $(1+\frac1q)^q\ge2+\frac{q-1}{2q}>2$, so $q=1$, $\lambda=\frac13$, $b^2=1$, $a=\frac b3$.

Step 3: the lower terms. Four cases remain.

(i) $n=2$, $Q=\epsilon x+d$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+rx^2+sx+w$, $\epsilon=\pm1$. With $\sigma=x+y$ the left side is $\frac{\sigma^3}3+(2\epsilon d+r)\sigma^2+(\dots)\sigma+\dots$, and the right side is $\epsilon\bigl[P(x)+P(y)+\epsilon xy\sigma+d(x^2+y^2)\bigr]+2d=\frac{\sigma^3}3+\epsilon(r+d)(x^2+y^2)+\epsilon s\sigma+2\epsilon w+2d$. Comparing $xy$: $2\epsilon d+r=0$; comparing $x^2$: $r+d=0$. Hence $d(2\epsilon-1)=0$, so $d=r=0$. Then the constant terms give $w=2\epsilon w$, so $w=0$: $Q=\epsilon x$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+sx$.

(ii) $n=1$, $q=1$, $Q=x+d$, $P=x^2+sx+w$. The left side is $\sigma^2+(4d+s)\sigma+4d^2+2sd+w$ and the right side is $P(x)+P(y)+2xy+d\sigma+2d=\sigma^2+(s+d)\sigma+2w+2d$. So $d=0$, then $w=0$: $Q=x$, $P=x^2+sx$.

(iii) $n=1$, $q=2$, $Q=-\frac{x^2}3+ex+d$, $P=\frac{x^3}9+rx^2+sx+w$. Expanding (a routine computation, see the check), the homogeneous part of degree $5$ of (left side $-$ right side) is \[\tfrac{x+y}{27}\bigl[(2r+e)(x^4+y^4)+2(e-r)(x^3y+xy^3)-(4r+6e)x^2y^2\bigr],\] so $r=e=0$; then the part of degree $4$ is $\frac1{27}\bigl[(2s+4d)(x^4+y^4)-(12s+8d)x^2y^2\bigr]$, so $s=d=0$; then the part of degree $3$ is $\frac{2w}{27}(x+y)(x^2-4xy+y^2)$, so $w=0$. Thus $Q=-\frac{x^2}3$, $P=\frac{x^3}9$.

(iv) $n=1$, $q\ge2$, $Q=x^q+Q_1$, $P=x^{q+1}+P_1$ with $\deg Q_1=j<q$, $\deg P_1=i\le q$ and leading coefficients $\beta,\alpha$ (if $Q_1$ or $P_1$ is zero, ignore it below). Suppose they are not both zero, and let $\mu=\max(i,j+1)$, $D=q^2+\mu-1$, $S_0=x^q+y^q$, $S_1=Q_1(x)+Q_1(y)$. Expand: \[P(Q(x)+Q(y))=S_0^{q+1}+(q+1)S_0^qS_1+R_1,\qquad Q(P(x)+xQ(y))=(xS_0)^q+q(xS_0)^{q-1}\bigl(P_1(x)+xQ_1(y)\bigr)+R_2,\] where $R_1$ (the higher powers of $S_1$, and $P_1(S_0+S_1)$) has degree at most $\max\bigl(q(q-1)+2j,\;iq\bigr)$ and $R_2$ (the higher powers of $P_1(x)+xQ_1(y)$, and $Q_1(\dots)$) has degree at most $\max\bigl((q+1)(q-2)+2\mu,\;(q+1)j\bigr)$; all these are less than $D$ because $j<q$, $i\le q$, $\mu\le q$ and $q\ge2$. Since $S_0^{q+1}=(xS_0)^q+(yS_0)^q$, the part of degree $D$ of (left side $-$ right side) is $S_0^{q-1}K$ with \[K=\beta\bigl[(q+1)(x^{q+j}+y^{q+j})+x^qy^j+x^jy^q\bigr]-q\alpha\bigl(x^{q-1+i}+y^{q-1+i}\bigr),\] where $\beta$ is to be read as $0$ unless $j=\mu-1$, and $\alpha$ as $0$ unless $i=\mu$. As $K=0$: if only the $\beta$-term is present, the coefficient of $x^{q+j}$, which is $(q+1)\beta$ or $(q+2)\beta$, vanishes; if only the $\alpha$-term is present, $\alpha=0$; if both are present and $j\ge1$, the mixed monomial $x^qy^j$ has coefficient $\beta$. All three are impossible. The only remaining possibility is $j=0$, $i=1$, $K=\bigl((q+2)\beta-q\alpha\bigr)(x^q+y^q)$, i.e. \[Q=x^q+\beta,\quad P=x^{q+1}+\alpha x+w,\quad \alpha=\tfrac{q+2}q\beta,\quad\beta\ne0 .\] Put $x=0$ in (E): $(y^q+2\beta)^{q+1}+\alpha(y^q+2\beta)+w=\bigl(y^{q+1}+(\alpha+\beta)y+w\bigr)^q+\beta+Q(w)$. The left side contains only powers $y^{qk}$, while on the right $y^{q^2-1}$ has coefficient $qw$; so $w=0$, and the right side becomes $y^q\bigl(y^q+\alpha+\beta\bigr)^q+2\beta$. For $q\ge3$ compare $y^{q^2-q}$: $\binom{q+1}2(2\beta)^2=\binom q2(\alpha+\beta)^2$ with $\alpha+\beta=\frac{2(q+1)}q\beta$ gives $q^2=q^2-1$, impossible. For $q=2$ ($\alpha=2\beta$) compare $y^2$: $12\beta^2+2\beta=9\beta^2$, so $\beta=-\frac23$; and the constant terms: $8\beta^3+2\alpha\beta=2\beta$, i.e. $4\beta^2+2\beta=1$, false for $\beta=-\frac23$. So $P_1=Q_1=0$: $Q=x^q$, $P=x^{q+1}$.

Collecting the cases gives exactly the pairs listed in the answer.

Problem 7

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Euler Line and Nine-Point Circle Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $\angle A<\angle B<\angle C$ and circumcenter $O$. Points $X$ and $Y$ lie on segments $AB$ and $AC$ respectively such that \[\angle OXA=\angle OYA=90^\circ+\angle A.\] Show that the line $XY$ is perpendicular to the Euler line of triangle $ABC$.

Show the solution

Solution (Lengths AX, AY and a linear function)

Let $R$ be the circumradius, $H$ the orthocentre, and write $A,B,C$ also for the angles. As the triangle is acute and not equilateral, $O$ is inside it and $O\ne H$.

Step 1: $AX$ and $AY$. In triangle $AXO$ the angle at $X$ is $90^\circ+A$ and the angle at $A$ is $\angle OAB=90^\circ-C$ (triangle $OAB$ is isosceles with apex angle $2C$). Hence $\angle AOX=180^\circ-(90^\circ+A)-(90^\circ-C)=C-A>0$, and the law of sines gives \[AX=\frac{AO\sin(C-A)}{\sin(90^\circ+A)}=\frac{R\sin(C-A)}{\cos A}.\] In the same way ($\angle OAC=90^\circ-B$) we get $AY=\frac{R\sin(B-A)}{\cos A}$. Both are positive, so $X\ne A\ne Y$ and $X\ne Y$.

Step 2: a criterion. For a point $Z$ put $g(Z)=ZO^2-ZH^2$. In vectors, $g(Z)=2\,Z\cdot(H-O)+|O|^2-|H|^2$, so $g(X)=g(Y)$ if and only if $(X-Y)\cdot(H-O)=0$, i.e. $XY\perp OH$. Moving along line $AB$ with unit vector $\mathbf u$ from $A$ to $B$, $g(A+t\mathbf u)=g(A)+2t\,\mathbf u\cdot(H-O)$; similarly along $AC$ with unit vector $\mathbf v$. Thus $XY\perp OH$ iff \[AX\cdot\mathbf u\cdot(H-O)=AY\cdot\mathbf v\cdot(H-O). \tag{1}\]

Step 3: the projections of $OH$. With $O$ as origin, $H=A+B+C$ (Euler/Sylvester). Since $A$ and $B$ are symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, $(A+B)\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so $(H-O)\cdot\mathbf u=C\cdot\mathbf u$, the signed distance from the midpoint of $AB$ to the foot of the altitude from $C$, which is $b\cos A-\frac c2=\frac{b^2-a^2}{2c}$. With $a=2R\sin A$ etc. and $\sin^2B-\sin^2A=\sin(B-A)\sin(B+A)=\sin(B-A)\sin C$, \[(H-O)\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{4R^2\sin(B-A)\sin C}{4R\sin C}=R\sin(B-A),\qquad (H-O)\cdot\mathbf v=R\sin(C-A)\] (the second by the same computation with $B$ and $C$ exchanged).

Step 4. Both sides of (1) equal $\frac{R^2\sin(C-A)\sin(B-A)}{\cos A}$. Hence $XY\perp OH$: the line $XY$ is perpendicular to the Euler line.

Problem 8

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Let $\mathbb R^+$ be the set of positive real numbers. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb R^+\to\mathbb R^+$ such that $f(1)=1$ and, for every positive integer $n$, the numbers \[f(x+y)-\frac1n\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x)+f(y)-\frac1n\] are either both positive, both negative, or both zero for every $x,y\in\mathbb R^+$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x$ for $0<x\le1$, and $f(x)$ an arbitrary number greater than $1$ for each $x>1$

Show the solution

Solution (Dyadic anchors and periodicity of the error)

Call positive reals $u,v$ equivalent, $u\sim v$, if $u-\frac1n$ and $v-\frac1n$ have the same sign for every positive integer $n$. The equivalence classes are the single points $\{\frac1n\}$, the open intervals $\bigl(\frac1{n+1},\frac1n\bigr)$ and the interval $(1,\infty)$. The condition says $f(x+y)\sim f(x)+f(y)$ for all $x,y>0$; in particular, if one side equals some $\frac1n$, so does the other.

These functions work. If $x+y\le1$, then $f(x+y)=x+y=f(x)+f(y)$. If $x+y>1$, then $f(x+y)>1$, and $f(x)+f(y)>1$ too (if $x,y\le1$ it equals $x+y$; otherwise one term exceeds $1$). Also $f(1)=1$.

Necessity for $x>1$. $f(x)\sim f(x-1)+f(1)=f(x-1)+1>1$, so $f(x)>1$.

Dyadic anchors. $f(1)=1\sim2f(\frac12)$ forces $2f(\frac12)=1$; inductively $f(2^{-k})=2^{-k}\sim2f(2^{-k-1})$ forces $f(2^{-k-1})=2^{-k-1}$. So $f(s)=s$ for $s=2^{-k}$, $k\ge0$, and then for $0<a<s$: $f(s)\sim f(a)+f(s-a)$ gives \[f(a)+f(s-a)=s. \tag{1}\] In particular $f(a)<2^{-k}$ whenever $a<2^{-k}$.

Periodicity. Put $e(a)=f(a)-a$ on $(0,1]$; by (1), $e(a)=-e(s-a)$ for $s=2^{-k}>a$. If $0<a<2^{-k-1}$, apply this with $s=2^{-k-1}$ and then, to $b=2^{-k-1}-a<2^{-k}$, with $s=2^{-k}$: \[e(a)=-e(2^{-k-1}-a)=e\bigl(2^{-k}-2^{-k-1}+a\bigr)=e\bigl(a+2^{-k-1}\bigr).\] Conclusion. Fix $a\in(0,1)$. Define $r_0=a$ and, for $K\ge1$, $r_K=r_{K-1}-2^{-K}$ if $r_{K-1}>2^{-K}$ and $r_K=r_{K-1}$ otherwise. By induction $0<r_K\le2^{-K}$. If $r_K=2^{-K}$ for some $K$, take the first such $K$; then $r_K=r_{K-1}$ (otherwise $r_{K-1}=2^{-K+1}$, an earlier such index, or $a=1$), and every earlier subtraction step produced a value $r_j<2^{-j}$. By periodicity (applied at $r_j<2^{-j}$ whenever $r_j=r_{j-1}-2^{-j}$), $e(r_K)=e(r_{K-1})=\dots=e(a)$; in the case $r_K=2^{-K}$ this gives $e(a)=e(2^{-K})=0$. If $r_K<2^{-K}$, then $0<f(r_K)<2^{-K}$, so $|e(a)|=|e(r_K)|<2^{-K}$. Letting $K\to\infty$ gives $e(a)=0$, i.e. $f(a)=a$ on $(0,1]$.

So the solutions are exactly: $f(x)=x$ for $0<x\le1$ and $f(x)$ any value greater than $1$ for $x>1$.

Problem 9

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Factorization and Algebraic Cleanup

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $A$ and $B$ be distinct lattice points in the plane. Prove that there exists a point $O$ with the following properties:

  • There is a circle with center $O$ passing through $A$ and $B$ and at least $2026$ other lattice points.

  • The value $\tan\angle OAB$ is a fraction with denominator $n$ in simplest form.

Show the solution

Solution (Gaussian integers)

Identify the plane with $\mathbb C$ and lattice points with Gaussian integers; translating by $-A$ we may assume $A=0$, and put $\beta=B\ne0$ (a Gaussian integer).

The centre. For a Gaussian integer $w=n+im$ put $O=\frac{\beta w}{2n}$. Then $O=\frac\beta2+\frac{m}{2n}\,i\beta$: it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, at distance $\frac{m}{2n}|\beta|$ from the midpoint, so $\tan\angle OAB=\frac{(m/2n)|\beta|}{|\beta|/2}=\frac mn$ (for $m>0$). The circle through $A$ and $B$ has radius $|O|=\frac{|\beta||w|}{2n}$.

Lattice points on the circle. If $z$ is a Gaussian integer with $|z|=|w|$ and $z\equiv-w\pmod{2n}$ (i.e. $\frac{z+w}{2n}$ is a Gaussian integer), then $Z=\beta\,\frac{z+w}{2n}$ is a lattice point with $|Z-O|=\frac{|\beta||z|}{2n}=|O|$, so $Z$ lies on the circle; different $z$ give different $Z$.

Construction. Let $g=1+2ni$ and $K=2027$. Since $g\equiv\bar g\pmod{2n}$ (their difference is $4ni$), we have $g^k\bar g^{K-k}\equiv\bar g^K\pmod{2n}$ for $0\le k\le K$. Let $G=\bar g^K=c+di$.

  • $\gcd(c,d)=1$: a rational prime $q$ dividing $c$ and $d$ divides $\bar g^K$ in $\mathbb Z[i]$; then every Gaussian prime factor of $q$ divides $\bar g$, and so does its conjugate (if $q\equiv1\pmod4$ both factors of $q$ divide $\bar g^K$, hence $\bar g$; if $q\equiv3\pmod 4$, $q$ itself is prime; $q=2$ is impossible as $|g|^2=1+4n^2$ is odd). In all cases $q\mid\bar g=1-2ni$, which is false.

  • $|G|^2=(1+4n^2)^K$ is coprime to $n$.

Since $\gcd(c,d)=1$, the equation $cx-dy=n$ has integer solutions $x=x_0+dt$, $y=y_0+ct$ ($t\in\mathbb Z$). For $h=x+iy$ we get $\operatorname{Re}(Gh)=cx-dy=n$ and $\operatorname{Im}(Gh)=cy+dx=m_0+(c^2+d^2)t$ with $m_0=cy_0+dx_0$. As $c^2+d^2$ is invertible modulo $n$, we can choose $t$ so that $m=m_0+(c^2+d^2)t\equiv1\pmod n$ and $m>0$. Then $w=Gh=n+im$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$, so $\tan\angle OAB=\frac mn$ is in lowest terms with denominator $n$.

Now put $z_k=-h\,g^k\bar g^{K-k}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,K$. These are Gaussian integers with $|z_k|=|h||g|^K=|w|$, and $z_k\equiv-h\bar g^K=-w\pmod{2n}$. They are pairwise different: $z_k=-w\,(g/\bar g)^k$ and $g/\bar g$ is not a root of unity (the roots of unity in $\mathbb Q(i)$ are $\pm1,\pm i$, and $g=1+2ni$ is neither real, nor imaginary, nor a multiple of $1\pm i$). This gives $K+1=2028$ different lattice points on the circle; besides $A$ and $B$ at least $2026$ remain.

  • take any three consecutive integers of the form $(a,a-1,a)$ and change them to $(a,a,a)$;

  • take any three consecutive integers of the form $(a,a,a)$ and change them to $(a,a+1,a)$.

Find the maximum possible integer that can appear on the circle after several of these operations.

Show the answer

$1012$

Show the solution

Solution (Level sets consist of arcs of odd length)

Let the positions be the residues modulo $N=2025$ and let $x_j$ be the number at position $j$. Both operations raise one middle entry $x_i$ by $1$, provided $x_{i-1}=x_{i+1}$ and $x_i\in\{x_{i-1}-1,\,x_{i-1}\}$.

Neighbouring entries differ by at most $1$. This holds at the start; an operation changes $x_i$ from $a-1$ to $a$ or from $a$ to $a+1$ while both neighbours equal $a$, so it stays true.

Invariant. For $h\ge1$ let $S_h=\{j: x_j\ge h\}$. We claim: for every $h\ge1$, $S_h$ is not the whole circle and each of its maximal arcs (runs of consecutive positions) has odd length. Initially every $S_h$ is empty. An operation changes only one set:

  • Raising $x_i$ from $a$ to $a+1$ with $x_{i-1}=x_{i+1}=a$ adds $i$ to $S_{a+1}$ as a new arc $\{i\}$ of length $1$ (its neighbours are not in $S_{a+1}$).

  • Raising $x_i$ from $a-1$ to $a$ with $x_{i\pm1}=a$ adds $i$ to $S_a$, joining the arcs through $i-1$ and $i+1$. If these were one arc, it would be the whole circle except $i$, of length $N-1$, which is even, contradicting the invariant. So they are two different arcs, of odd lengths $\ell_1,\ell_2$, and they merge into one arc of odd length $\ell_1+\ell_2+1$. This arc is not the whole circle: otherwise $S_a$ minus $i$ would be the whole circle except $i$, a single arc, whereas the arcs through $i-1$ and $i+1$ are different.

Upper bound. By the invariant $S_1$ is never the whole circle: some entry is always $0$. Every position is at cyclic distance at most $\frac{N-1}2=1012$ from that zero, and entries change by at most $1$ per step along the circle, so every entry is at most $1012$.

Construction. Fix a position $c$; we reach $x_j=1012-d(j)$, where $d(j)$ is the cyclic distance from $j$ to $c$ (a tent with peak $1012$ at $c$ and two adjacent zeros at the positions $c\pm1012$). We build it level by level: after level $h-1$ is done ($h\ge1$), the entries are $\min(h-1,\,1012-d(j))$, so they equal $h-1$ on the arc $J=\{d(j)\le1013-h\}$ (for $h=1$, the whole circle), and the target arc of level $h$ is $J'=\{d(j)\le1012-h\}$, which is $J$ without its two ends ($J'=\{c\}$ when $h=1012$).

To raise $J'$ to $h$: if $J'=\{c\}$, raise $c$ (its neighbours and itself equal $h-1$). Otherwise $J'$ has odd length $2m-1$ with $m=1013-h\ge2$; pick a seed $s\in J'$ such that the distances from $s$ to the two ends of $J'$ are even (possible because the length is odd). Raise $s$ to $h$ (operation 2: $s$ and its neighbours equal $h-1$). Then repeatedly extend the arc of $h$'s by two positions on one side: if the arc ends at $e$ and $e-1,e-2$ are still inside $J'$, then $e-3\in J$, so $x_{e-3}=x_{e-2}=x_{e-1}=h-1$; raise $e-2$ to $h$ (operation 2), then $e-1$ (operation 1, between two $h$'s). Do the same on the right. Since the distances from $s$ to the ends of $J'$ are even, the arc of $h$'s becomes exactly $J'$, and entries outside $J'$ are unchanged. (For $h=1$, $J'$ omits only the two adjacent positions $c\pm1012$, which stay $0$.)

After level $1012$ the entry at $c$ is $1012$. Hence the maximum possible integer is $\boxed{1012}$.

$(p,k,t)\in\{(2,1,1),(2,2,1),(2,2,2),(2,3,4),(3,1,0),(3,1,1),(5,1,2)\}$ (and $(2,0,1)$ if $k=0$ is allowed)

Show the solution

Solution (Modular restrictions, then 2-adic and 3-adic sizes)

If $t=0$ then $F\in\{0,2\}$, so $F=2$, which happens only for $(p,k)=(3,1)$; this gives $(3,1,0)$. From now on $t\ge1$ and $F=p^t\mp1$.

Case $p=2$. $F=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k-1)$. For $k=1,2,3$: $F=1=2^1-1$; $F=3=2^2-1=2^1+1$; $F=15=2^4-1$. This gives $(2,1,1)$, $(2,2,2)$, $(2,2,1)$, $(2,3,4)$. Let $k\ge4$, so $3\cdot5\cdot7\mid F$. Modulo $7$ the powers of $2$ are $1,2,4$, so $2^t\equiv\pm1\pmod7$ forces $F=2^t-1$ and $3\mid t$; modulo $5$, $2^t\equiv1$ forces $4\mid t$. Then $3\cdot9\mid F$, so $v_3(F)\ge3$; since $t$ is even, LTE gives $v_3(2^t-1)=v_3(4^{t/2}-1)=1+v_3(t)$, so $v_3(t)\ge2$, $9\mid t$, hence $36\mid t$ and $37\mid2^{36}-1\mid F$, so $k\ge19$. For $k=19$: $3^5=243>19\log_2 38\approx99.7$, and when $k$ increases by $1$ the left side grows by the factor $3^{1/3}>1.44$ while the right side grows by less. So no solution has $k\ge19$.

Case $p$ odd. The numbers $1,\dots,pk$ not divisible by $p$ form $k$ blocks, each congruent to $1,2,\dots,p-1$ modulo $p$, so $F\equiv((p-1)!)^k\equiv(-1)^k\pmod p$ by Wilson's theorem. As $F\equiv\mp1$, we get $F=p^t+(-1)^k$.

$k\ge3$ odd. Now $F=p^t-1$ with $t$ even, so by LTE $v_2(F)=v_2(p^2-1)+v_2(t)-1$. On the other hand $v_2(F)=v_2((pk)!)-v_2(k!)\ge(pk-1-\log_2(pk))-(k-1)=(p-1)k-\log_2(pk)$, and $p^t\le F+1\le(pk)^{(p-1)k}$ gives $t\le(p-1)k\log_2(pk)$.   • $p=3$: $5\mid F$ gives $3^t\equiv1\pmod5$, $4\mid t$; $7\mid F$ gives $6\mid t$; so $12\mid t$ and $13\mid3^{12}-1\mid F$, impossible for $k=3$ ($3k=9<13$). Let $X$ lie on $\omega_2$ such that $OX$ is tangent to $\omega_2$, and let $C$ be the reflection of $B$ in line $OX$. Let $D$ be a point on $\omega_1$, and suppose $AD$ meets $\omega_2$ again at $Y$. A classroom has $2026$ students. Every second, the teacher can do the following operation:

Suppose that after some time, any two students in the classroom are friends. What is the minimum number of pairs of friends that must exist initially?

Show the answer

$5061$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting departures from each degree)

Let $n=2026$. Each operation picks four students of the same degree $k$, adds four friendships, and raises each of their degrees by $2$; other degrees do not change. At the end every degree is $n-1=2025$.

Parity. Degrees change by $2$, so every initial degree $d_v$ is odd.

Departures. Fix an odd $k\le n-3$. A student whose initial degree is at most $k$ has degree exactly $k$ at some moment (degrees go up in steps of $2$ from an odd start to $n-1>k$) and leaves the value $k$ exactly once, in an operation performed on four students of degree $k$. Each such operation removes exactly four students from degree $k$. Hence \[N_k=\#\{v: d_v\le k\}\equiv0\pmod4\quad\text{for every odd }k\le n-3 .\] For $k=n-3$: $N_{n-3}=n-\#\{v: d_v=n-1\}$, and $n\equiv2\pmod4$, so the number of students with initial degree $n-1$ is $\equiv2\pmod4$, in particular at least $2$.

Lower bound. Let $U,V$ be two students who initially are friends with everybody. Every other student is a friend of $U$ and $V$, so has degree at least $2$, hence (odd) at least $3$. The number of friendships is at least \[\frac{2(n-1)+3(n-2)}{2}=\frac{2\cdot2025+3\cdot2024}{2}=5061 .\]

Construction. Let $U,V$ be friends with everyone (and with each other), and let the other $2024=4q$ students ($q=506$) be split into $2q=1012$ pairs of friends. That is $1+2\cdot2024+1012=5061$ friendships. The degrees of $U,V$ stay $2025$, and among the others, degree $=2+{}$(number of friends among the others); so it suffices to complete the graph on the $4q$ others.

Think of the $2q$ pairs as vertices $p_1,\dots,p_{2q}$ of an auxiliary graph, initially empty. If pairs $p=\{a,b\}$ and $p'=\{c,d\}$ have no friendships between them, and all four students have the same degree, the operation on the cycle $a,c,b,d$ adds exactly the four friendships between $p$ and $p'$; we record this as the edge $pp'$. A student in pair $p$ has degree $3+2\deg(p)$. The complete graph $K_{2q}$ is the union of $2q-1$ perfect matchings (a 1-factorisation, e.g. pairs with $i+j\equiv r\pmod{2q-1}$ together with the vertex $2q$). Add the matchings one after another: during round $r$ every pair-vertex not yet treated in this round has auxiliary degree $r-1$, so each matching edge joins two pairs whose four students all have degree $3+2(r-1)$, and the operation is allowed. After all rounds, all students are friends.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{5061}$.

Problem 3

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Injectivity and Surjectivity

Find all functions $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ such that for all real numbers $x,y$, \[f\bigl(xf(y)+y+f(x)\bigr)=f\bigl(yf(x)\bigr)+x+f(y).\]

Show the answer

$f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=-x$

Show the solution

Solution (Involution, then the error term h(x)=f(x)+x)

Both functions work: for $f(x)=x$ each side is $xy+x+y$; for $f(x)=-x$ the left side is $f(-xy+y-x)=xy-y+x$ and the right side is $f(-xy)+x-y=xy+x-y$. Let $E(x,y)$ denote the equation.

Step 1: $f(0)=0$ and $f(f(x))=x$. Put $a=f(0)$. $E(x,0)$: $f(ax+f(x))=x+2a$, so $f$ is surjective. If $f(z)=0$, then $E(z,z)$ gives $f(z)=a+z$, so $z=-a$: the only zero of $f$ is $-a$. $E(x,-a)$: $f(f(x)-a)=f(-af(x))+x$, so $f(u)=f(v)$ implies $u=v$: $f$ is injective. $E(-a,y)$: $f(-af(y)+y)=f(0)-a+f(y)=f(y)$, so $-af(y)=0$ for all $y$; as $f$ is surjective, $a=0$. Then $E(x,0)$ reads $f(f(x))=x$.

Step 2: $b=f(1)=\pm1$. $E(x,1)$: $f(bx+1+f(x))=2x+b$; applying $f$, $f(2x+b)=bx+1+f(x)$. With $x=-b$: $f(-b)=-b^2+1+f(-b)$, so $b^2=1$.

Step 3: $b=1$. $E(x,1)$ gives $f(x+f(x)+1)=2x+1$, and $E(1,x)$ gives $f(f(x)+x+1)=f(x)+1+f(x)$. So $2x+1=2f(x)+1$ and $f(x)=x$.

Step 4: $b=-1$. Then $f(-1)=1$, and Step 2 gives \[f(2x-1)=f(x)-x+1. \tag{1}\] Let $h(x)=f(x)+x$. Then (1) says $h(2x-1)=h(x)$, and $f(f(x))=x$ gives $h(f(x))=h(x)$. From (1): $f(-1)=1$ gives $f(-3)=3$, so $f(3)=-3$; and $h(\frac12)=h(0)=0$, so $f(\frac12)=-\frac12$, $f(-\frac12)=\frac12$.

$h$ is even. $E(1,y)$: $f(f(y)+y-1)=f(-y)+1+f(y)$; applying $f$: $f\bigl(f(-y)+f(y)+1\bigr)=f(y)+y-1$. The left side does not change under $y\mapsto-y$, so $f(y)+y=f(-y)-y$, i.e. $h(y)=h(-y)$.

A master identity. Substituting $f=h-\mathrm{id}$ into $E(x,y)$ and simplifying gives, for all $x,y$, \[h\bigl(xf(y)+y+f(x)\bigr)=h\bigl(yf(x)\bigr)+(1+x)h(y)+(1-y)h(x). \tag{2}\] Write $s=h(y)$.

  • $x=1$ ($f(1)=-1$, $h(1)=0$): $h(s-1)=h(-y)+2s$, so $h(s-1)=3s$. (3)

  • $y=1$, then rename: $h(h(x)-2x+1)=h(f(x))=h(x)$. With $t=2x-1$, so $h(x)=h(t)$, and evenness: $h(t-h(t))=h(t)$, hence also $h(t+h(t))=h(t)$ (apply to $-t$). (4)

  • $x=-\frac12$ ($f(-\frac12)=\frac12$, $h(-\frac12)=0$): $h\bigl(\frac{3y-s+1}{2}\bigr)=h\bigl(\frac y2\bigr)+\frac s2$; by $h(u)=h(2u-1)$ this is $h(3y-s)=h(y-1)+\frac s2$. (5)

Fix $y$ and $s=h(y)$. By (3), $h(s-1)=3s$; by evenness $h(1-s)=3s$; by (4) at $t=1-s$, $h(1+2s)=3s$; and $h(1+2s)=h(2(s+1)-1)=h(s+1)$, so $h(s+1)=3s$. Apply (5) at the point $s+1$, whose $h$-value is $3s$: $h(3(s+1)-3s)=h(s)+\frac{3s}2$, i.e. $h(s)=h(3)-\frac32s=-\frac32s$. Now apply (4) in the form $h(t+h(t))=h(t)$ at $t=s$: $h\bigl(s-\frac32s\bigr)=-\frac32s$, so $h(\frac s2)=-\frac32s$ by evenness; but $h(\frac s2)=h(2\cdot\frac s2-1)=h(s-1)=3s$ by (3). Hence $3s=-\frac32s$, $s=0$. So $h\equiv0$ and $f(x)=-x$.

The solutions are $\boxed{f(x)=x}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=-x}$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

Do there exist $2026$ infinite sets of integers $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_{2026}$ such that every integer $n$ can be written as a sum \[n=a_1+a_2+\dots+a_{2026},\qquad a_i\in A_i,\] in exactly:

  • (a) $1404$ ways?

  • (b) $n^{1404}+1$ ways?

Show the answer

(a) Yes; (b) Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Negabinary digits and a greedy construction)

Base $-2$. Every integer has exactly one representation $\sum_{k\ge0}\varepsilon_k(-2)^k$ with digits $\varepsilon_k\in\{0,1\}$, finitely many nonzero. Indeed $\varepsilon_0\equiv n\pmod 2$ is forced, and then $\frac{n-\varepsilon_0}{-2}$ must be represented by the remaining digits; since $\bigl|\frac{n-\varepsilon_0}{-2}\bigr|<|n|$ for $|n|\ge2$, and $0=\emptyset$, $1=1$, $-1=1+(-2)$, induction on $|n|$ gives existence and uniqueness.

(a) Yes. Split the positions $k\ge0$ into $2026$ infinite classes: $P_1=\{2026m: m\ge1\}$, $P_2=\{k\equiv1\pmod{2026}\}\cup\{0\}$ and $P_i=\{k\equiv i-1\pmod{2026}\}$ for $3\le i\le2026$. Let $B_i$ be the set of numbers $\sum_{k\in P_i}\varepsilon_k(-2)^k$ (finite sums). Each $B_i$ is infinite, and by uniqueness of the base $-2$ representation, every integer is $b_1+\dots+b_{2026}$ ($b_i\in B_i$) in exactly one way. Every element of $B_1$ is a multiple of $2^{2026}$. Put \[A_1=\{b+j: b\in B_1,\ 0\le j\le1403\},\qquad A_i=B_i\ (i\ge2).\] The numbers $b+j$ are pairwise different (reduce modulo $2^{2026}>1403$), so each element of $A_1$ determines $b$ and $j$. Then $n=a_1+\dots+a_{2026}$ with $a_1=b+j$ means $b+a_2+\dots+a_{2026}=n-j$, which for each of the $1404$ values of $j$ has exactly one solution. So every $n$ has exactly $1404$ representations.

(b) Yes, by a greedy construction. Let $T(n)=n^{1404}+1\ge1$. Enumerate the integers as $0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots$ and build finite sets $A_1,\dots,A_{2026}$ step by step, keeping the following true after each step: integers already handled have exactly $T$ representations (among tuples from the current sets), and every other integer $m$ has at most $T(m)$.

To add one representation of a target $n$, choose a huge integer $L$ and add $y_i=L$ to $A_i$ for $i\le2025$ and $y_{2026}=n-2025L$ to $A_{2026}$. The new tuples are those using at least one new element. The tuple of all new elements sums to $n$. A tuple using the new elements exactly for $i\in S$ ($\emptyset\ne S\ne$ all) and old elements otherwise has sum $|S|L+(\text{old})$ if $2026\notin S$, and $n-(2026-|S|)L+(\text{old})$ if $2026\in S$; in both cases its absolute value is at least $L-|n|-\beta$, where $\beta$ bounds the absolute sum of old elements in any tuple. Choose $L$ so large that (i) these sums exceed in absolute value $|n|$, every handled integer and every sum created in earlier steps, and (ii) $T(m)\ge\kappa$ for all $|m|\ge L-|n|-\beta$, where $\kappa$ is the number of new tuples ($T$ grows to infinity, so this is possible). Then handled integers keep their counts, $n$ gains exactly one representation, and every other integer $m$ gains representations only in this step, at most $\kappa\le T(m)$.

When the enumeration reaches $n$, it has at most $T(n)$ representations; add the missing ones one at a time as above, then mark $n$ handled. Every step adds a new element to every set, so in the limit all $A_i$ are infinite, and every integer $n$ ends with exactly $n^{1404}+1$ representations (later steps never touch handled integers).

Problem 5

SAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Uniqueness by Degree Constraints

Find all positive real numbers $r$ such that the following statement is true:

There exists a non-constant polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that $|P(t)|<1$ for all $|t|\le r$.

Show the answer

$0<r<2$

Show the solution

Solution (Parseval for r at least 2, Minkowski for r below 2)

$r\ge2$ fails. Let $P$ be nonconstant with integer coefficients, degree $n\ge1$ and leading coefficient $c\ne0$. For $|z|=1$, $t=z+\frac1z=2\cos\theta\in[-2,2]$, and $R(z)=z^nP\bigl(z+\frac1z\bigr)$ is a polynomial of degree $2n$ whose coefficients of $z^{2n}$ and $z^0$ both equal $c$. By Parseval, the mean of $|R(z)|^2$ over the unit circle is the sum of the squares of its coefficients, at least $2c^2\ge2$. So $|P(t)|=|R(z)|\ge\sqrt2>1$ for some $t\in[-2,2]\subseteq[-r,r]$.

$r<2$ works. Let $T_k$ be the Chebyshev polynomials ($T_k(\cos\theta)=\cos k\theta$, so $|T_k|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$; $T_0=1$ and $T_k$ has leading coefficient $2^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$). Every polynomial of degree at most $n$ can be written as $P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^nb_kT_k(x/r)$, and then $|P(t)|\le\sum|b_k|$ for $|t|\le r$. The map from the coefficient vector $(c_0,\dots,c_n)$ of $P$ (in the basis $1,x,\dots,x^n$) to $(b_0,\dots,b_n)$ is linear and triangular with diagonal entries $1$ and $\frac{r^k}{2^{k-1}}$ ($k\ge1$), so its determinant is $\Delta=\frac{r^{n(n+1)/2}}{2^{n(n-1)/2}}$.

Let $\varepsilon=\Delta^{1/(n+1)}$. The set of real vectors $(c_k)$ with all $|b_k|\le\varepsilon$ is a centrally symmetric convex body of volume $\frac{(2\varepsilon)^{n+1}}{\Delta}=2^{n+1}$, so by Minkowski's theorem it contains a nonzero integer vector: a nonzero integer polynomial $P$ with $\max_{[-r,r]}|P|\le(n+1)\varepsilon$. Here \[\varepsilon=\frac{r^{n/2}}{2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2(n+1)}}}=\frac{r^{n/2}}{2^{\frac n2-\frac{n}{n+1}}}\le2\Bigl(\frac r2\Bigr)^{n/2},\] so $\max|P|\le2(n+1)(r/2)^{n/2}\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ (because $r<2$). Choose $n$ with $2(n+1)(r/2)^{n/2}<1$. Then $|P(t)|<1$ on $[-r,r]$, and $P$ is not constant (a nonzero integer constant has absolute value at least $1$).

The answer is $\boxed{0<r<2}$.
