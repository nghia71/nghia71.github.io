Problem 1

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $N=2n(n+1)$. Suppose $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N$ are nonnegative real numbers such that $x_1+x_2+\dots+x_N=N$. Find the maximal value of \[\sum_{1\le i<j\le N}\max(x_i,\,x_j,\,x_ix_j).\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
In a pair with $x_i\ge x_j$, test when the product exceeds $x_i$. Which entry in the pair determines whether that happens?
Hint 2: key idea
Sort the entries and split them at $1$. For entries above $1$, fix their count and their total, then bound their pairwise products by expressing them in terms of the sum of their squares. (LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the contributions from pairs with zero, one, or two entries above $1$, handling the case with no such entries separately. Replace the total of the large entries by its upper bound and optimize the resulting expression over the integer count by clearing its denominator and factoring. Read off from the equality conditions of every bound what an extremal list must look like.
Common trap
The count of entries above $1$ can be zero, so handle that case before dividing by the count.
Show the answer

$2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1)$

Show the solution – Sort, then count the large entries

Let $S$ be the sum. Relabel so that $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_N$. For $i<j$ we have $\max(x_i,x_j,x_ix_j)=x_i\max(1,x_j)$: if $x_j\ge1$ then $x_ix_j\ge x_i\ge x_j$, and if $x_j<1$ then $x_ix_j<x_i$. Hence \[S=\sum_{i<j}x_i\max(1,x_j).\] Let $k$ be the number of indices with $x_j>1$ (these are $j=1,\dots,k$), $T=x_1+\dots+x_k$ and $R=N-T=\sum_{i>k}x_i$.

The case $k=0$. Then $S=\sum_i x_i(N-i)\le(N-1)\sum x_i=N(N-1)$.

The case $k\ge1$. For $i\le k$, $\sum_{j>i}\max(1,x_j)=\sum_{i<j\le k}x_j+(N-k)$; for $i>k$ it is $N-i\le N-k-1$. Therefore \[S\le T(N-k)+\sum_{i<j\le k}x_ix_j+R(N-k-1).\] Since $\sum_{i<j\le k}x_ix_j=\frac{T^2-\sum_{i\le k}x_i^2}{2}\le\frac{T^2}2\bigl(1-\frac1k\bigr)$ (as $\sum_{i\le k}x_i^2\ge\frac{T^2}k$) and $R=N-T$, \[S\le N(N-k-1)+T+\frac{k-1}{2k}T^2\le N(N-k)+\frac{k-1}{2k}N^2=\frac32N^2-\Bigl(\frac{N^2}{2k}+kN\Bigr),\] using $T\le N$. The case $k=0$ gives the same bound as $k=1$.

Optimising over $k$. With $N=2n(n+1)$, $\frac{N^2}{2k}+kN=N\bigl(\frac{n(n+1)}{k}+k\bigr)$, and for every positive integer $k$ \[\frac{n(n+1)}{k}+k\ge2n+1\iff k^2-(2n+1)k+n(n+1)\ge0\iff(k-n)(k-n-1)\ge0,\] which holds for integers. Hence \[S\le\frac32N^2-(2n+1)N=6n^2(n+1)^2-2n(n+1)(2n+1)=2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1).\] Equality. Take $k=n$ entries equal to $\frac Nn=2(n+1)$ and the rest $0$. Pairs of large entries give $\binom n2\cdot4(n+1)^2$, pairs (large, zero) give $n(N-n)\cdot2(n+1)$, and pairs of zeros give $0$; the total is \[2n(n-1)(n+1)^2+2n(n+1)(2n^2+n)=2n(n+1)\bigl[(n^2-1)+2n^2+n\bigr]=2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1).\] (Also $k=n+1$ entries equal to $2n$ give equality.) The maximum is $\boxed{2n(n+1)(3n^2+n-1)}$.

Problem 2

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with incenter $I$ and $A$-excenter $J$. Let $X$ be a point on $BC$. Define points $P$ and $Q$ such that, in directed angles, \[\measuredangle PAX=\measuredangle PJA=\measuredangle XJQ\quad\text{and}\quad\measuredangle PXA=\measuredangle PJX=\measuredangle JXQ.\] Suppose the circles $(PIJ)$ and $(QBC)$ intersect at two points $U,V$. Prove that $UVMN$ is cyclic, where $M,N$ are the midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $AX$ and mark the two angles involving $P$ that point to tangencies along that line. Mark the midpoint of $AX$ and compare its distances to the two points of tangency.
Hint 2: key idea
Read $AX$ as the common tangent to $(APJ)$ at $A$ and $(XPJ)$ at $X$. Invert at $A$ with power $AB\cdot AC$ to simplify those circles and track $P$, then use the tangent powers of the midpoint of $AX$ to locate $JP$. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the reflection arising in the first inversion to place $P$ on a fixed circle through $I,J$, centered at the reflection of the antipode of $A$ in $AI$. The direction of $JP$ identifies $JQ$ as a symmedian in $AJX$, so invert at $J$ using its power with respect to $(ABC)$ and use the reflected median and a translation to locate the image of $Q$. Once the two fixed circles are identified, evaluate the sum of their powers at the two side midpoints.
Common trap
The first inversion acts at $A$ and the second at $J$, with different inversion powers.
Show the solution – A fixed circle through I and J, and Q on the circumcircle by inversion at J

We work with directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and assume the configuration is non-degenerate (all circles and lines in the statement are defined). Let $b=CA$, $c=AB$, let $R$ and $O$ be the circumradius and circumcentre, $\Omega=(ABC)$, $A_1$ the antipode of $A$ on $\Omega$, $K$ the reflection of $A_1$ in the bisector $AI$, and $S$ the second intersection of $AI$ with $\Omega$. Recall $AI\cdot AJ=bc$ and that $S$ is the midpoint of $IJ$ with $SB=SC=SI=SJ$ (incenter–excenter lemma). The points $A,I,S,J$ lie on the bisector in this order, so $J$ lies outside $\Omega$.

Reading the definitions. $\measuredangle PAX=\measuredangle PJA$ says that line $AX$ is tangent at $A$ to the circle $\omega_1=(APJ)$, and $\measuredangle PXA=\measuredangle PJX$ says that $AX$ is tangent at $X$ to $\omega_2=(XPJ)$; so $P$ is the second common point of these two circles. The point $Q$ is the intersection of the line through $J$ with $\measuredangle XJQ=\measuredangle PJA$ and the line through $X$ with $\measuredangle JXQ=\measuredangle PJX$.

Claim 1: $P$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with centre $K$ through $I$ and $J$; hence $(PIJ)=\Gamma$ does not depend on $X$. First, $\Gamma$ is well defined: $\angle ASA_1=90^\circ$, so the projection of $A_1$, and hence of $K$, on line $AI$ is $S$, the midpoint of $IJ$; thus $KI=KJ$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and power $bc$; it swaps $I$ and $J$ and maps $\Gamma$ to itself (the power of $A$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $AI\cdot AJ=bc$). Since $A\in\omega_1$ and $\omega_1$ is tangent to $AX$ at $A$, $\iota(\omega_1)$ is the line through $I$ parallel to $AX$. Since $\omega_2$ passes through $J$ and $X$ and is tangent at $X$ to the line $AX$ (which passes through $A$), $\iota(\omega_2)$ is the circle through $I$ and $X'=\iota(X)$ tangent to $AX$ at $X'$. So $P^*=\iota(P)$ is the second common point of this circle with a chord through $I$ parallel to the tangent at $X'$; hence $X'I=X'P^*$ and $P^*$ is the reflection of $I$ in the line $m$ through $X'$ perpendicular to $AX$. Now $X'$ lies on $\iota(BC)$, the circle through $A$ and the points of rays $AB$, $AC$ at distances $b$, $c$ from $A$, i.e. the reflection of $\Omega$ in $AI$; its antipode of $A$ is $K$, so by Thales the line $m$ passes through $K$. Therefore $KP^*=KI$, i.e. $P^*\in\Gamma$, and $P=\iota(P^*)\in\iota(\Gamma)=\Gamma$.

Claim 2: the common points $U',V'$ of $\Gamma$ and $\Omega$ are concyclic with $M,N$. Use vectors with origin $A$. The powers of a point $Z$ are $f(Z)=|Z|^2-2Z\cdot O$ for $\Omega$ and $g(Z)=|Z|^2-2Z\cdot K+bc$ for $\Gamma$ (centre $K$, and $g(A)=bc$). Since $|O-C|=|O|$ we have $2O\cdot C=b^2$, and since $K$ and $2O$ are reflections of each other in $AI$, $K\cdot B=2O\cdot C'$ where $C'=\frac cbC$ is the reflection of $B$; so $K\cdot B=bc$, and likewise $K\cdot C=bc$. With $M=\frac B2$, $N=\frac C2$: \[f(M)=-\tfrac{c^2}4,\quad g(M)=\tfrac{c^2}4,\qquad f(N)=-\tfrac{b^2}4,\quad g(N)=\tfrac{b^2}4 .\] Hence $M$ and $N$ lie on the circle $f+g=0$, i.e. $2|Z|^2-2Z\cdot(O+K)+bc=0$, which also passes through $U'$ and $V'$ (where $f=g=0$). So $U',V',M,N$ are concyclic.

Claim 3: $Q$ lies on $\Omega$. Let $W$ be the midpoint of $AX$. It has power $WA^2=WX^2$ with respect to both $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ (tangent to $AX$ at $A$ and at $X$), so it lies on their radical axis $PJ$: $P$ lies on the median $JW$ of triangle $AJX$. Let $\ell$ be the line $JQ$. Since $\measuredangle XJQ=\measuredangle PJA=\measuredangle WJA$, the line $\ell$ is the reflection of $JW$ in the bisector of angle $AJX$ (the $J$-symmedian of triangle $AJX$). Moreover \[\measuredangle AJQ=\measuredangle AJX+\measuredangle XJQ=\measuredangle AJX+\measuredangle PJA=\measuredangle PJX=\measuredangle JXQ,\] so by the tangent–chord angle $Q$ lies on the circle $\gamma$ through $J$ and $X$ tangent to line $JA$ at $J$.

Let $\psi$ be the inversion with centre $J$ and power $k=JA\cdot JS$, the power of $J$ with respect to $\Omega$. Then $\psi$ maps $\Omega$ to itself and swaps $A$ and $S$. Let $X'=\psi(X)$, on ray $JX$. The circle $\gamma$ passes through $J$ and is tangent there to $JA$, so $\psi(\gamma)$ is the line $m$ through $X'$ parallel to $JA$; the line $\ell$ passes through $J$, so $\psi(\ell)=\ell$. Hence $\psi(Q)$ is the common point of $\ell$ and $m$.

From $JS\cdot JA=JX'\cdot JX$ we get $JS:JX=JX':JA$, and the angles at $J$ coincide, so triangle $JSX'$ is similar to triangle $JXA$ (with $S\leftrightarrow X$, $X'\leftrightarrow A$), with opposite orientation: the similarity is the reflection in the bisector of angle $AJX$ followed by a dilation at $J$. It maps the midpoint $W$ of $XA$ to the midpoint $W'$ of $SX'$, so $JW'$ is the reflection of $JW$ in the bisector, that is, $W'\in\ell$. Let $Q''=S+X'-J$ (vectors), so that $JSQ''X'$ is a parallelogram. Its diagonal $JQ''$ passes through the midpoint $W'$ of the other diagonal, so $Q''\in\ell$; and $X'Q''\parallel JS$, so $Q''\in m$. The line $\ell$ passes through $J$ while $m$ is parallel to $JA$ and does not pass through $J$; as $\ell\ne JA$, the two lines meet in exactly one point, so $\psi(Q)=Q''$.

It remains to see that $Q''\in\Omega$. Let $r_a$ be the distance from $J$ to $BC$ (the $A$-exradius). Dropping the perpendicular from $J$ to line $AB$ gives $JA=r_a/\sin\frac A2$, and $JS=SB=2R\sin\frac A2$ (the chord $SB$ subtends the inscribed angle $\angle BAS=\frac A2$). Thus $k=2Rr_a$. Let $\mathbf n$ be the unit vector perpendicular to $BC$ pointing towards the side of $A$. The inversion $\psi$ maps line $BC$, at distance $r_a$ from $J$, to the circle $\kappa$ through $J$ whose diameter from $J$ is perpendicular to $BC$ and has length $k/r_a=2R$; so $\kappa$ has centre $J+R\mathbf n$ and radius $R$. Since $S$ is the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$, $S=O-R\mathbf n$. Translating $\kappa$ by the vector $S-J$ gives the circle with centre $J+R\mathbf n+S-J=O$ and radius $R$, which is $\Omega$. As $X\in BC$, $X'\in\kappa$, hence $Q''=X'+(S-J)\in\Omega$, and $Q=\psi(Q'')\in\psi(\Omega)=\Omega$.

Conclusion. By Claim 3, $(QBC)=\Omega$; by Claim 1, $(PIJ)=\Gamma$. So $\{U,V\}=\{U',V'\}$, and by Claim 2 the points $U,V,M,N$ are concyclic.

Problem 3

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · High-Level Olympiad Synthesis

Wong has $n$ coins of distinct unknown weights and a device that, given $2025$ distinct coins, returns the coin with the median weight. After using the device finitely many times, Wong orders all coins in a row. For each $1\le i\le n$, he earns $1$ point if the $i$-th lightest coin is in the $i$-th position. Determine the maximum number of points he can guarantee.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a coin of a given rank, ask whether a set of $2025$ coins can have that coin as its median. Also ask what the device would return if every rank were reversed.
Hint 2: key idea
Query all admissible sets and, for a returned coin $x$, compare sets whose median is $x$ before and after swapping one coin. This can reveal which coins lie on the same side of $x$, while reversal and permutations within the extreme ranks remain invisible to the device. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Separate coins forced into every set with median $x$ before using the swap test, then use the resulting betweenness information to order the middle coins up to reversal. For the upper bound, compare scores in the two indistinguishable orientations pair by pair and use permutations of the extreme ranks to remove any matches there. For the strategy, use nearly equal groups of mirror pairs to favour the two orientations and handle any central position separately.
Common trap
With too few coins for any query, handle a single coin separately from a collection whose order is unknown.
Show the answer

for $n\ge2025$: $2\left\lfloor\frac{n-2024}{4}\right\rfloor+\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon=1$ for odd $n$ and $\varepsilon=0$ for even $n$ (that is, $\frac{n-2024}2,\frac{n-2023}2,\frac{n-2026}2,\frac{n-2025}2$ for $n\equiv0,1,2,3\pmod 4$); for $2\le n\le2024$ the answer is $0$

Show the solution – Indistinguishable orders, then pairs of mirror positions

Put $t=1012$, so the device takes $2t+1$ coins and returns the one that has exactly $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins among them. The rank $r(c)$ of a coin $c$ is $i$ if $c$ is the $i$-th lightest. Call the $t$ lightest coins light, the $t$ heaviest heavy, and the others middle.

If $n\le2024$ the device cannot be used, and for $n\ge2$ the weights may be such that the coin in position $i$ has rank $i+1$ (and the coin in position $n$ has rank $1$): Wong scores $0$. From now on $n\ge2025$; there are $m=n-2024$ middle coins, of ranks $t+1,\dots,n-t$. Put $P=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$. We show that the answer is \[A=2\Bigl\lfloor\frac P2\Bigr\rfloor+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon=m-2P\in\{0,1\},\] which equals $2\lfloor\frac{n-2024}4\rfloor+\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon=1$ exactly for odd $n$.

Mirror positions. For a middle rank $i$ with $i<n+1-i$, the positions $i$ and $i'=n+1-i$ form a mirror pair; there are $P$ such pairs, and if $m$ is odd the middle rank $\frac{n+1}2$ is a central position.

Step 1: worlds that the device cannot tell apart. Starting from the true ranks $r$, consider (i) the reversed ranks $n+1-r(c)$; (ii) any permutation of the ranks among the light coins; (iii) the same among the heavy coins. For every set $S$ of $2t+1$ coins the device gives the same answer in all these worlds. For (i) this is clear: the median has $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins in $S$, a symmetric condition. For (ii): the answer $x$ has $t$ lighter coins in $S$, so $r(x)\ge t+1$ and $x$ is not light. Permuting ranks among light coins keeps every light coin lighter than every other coin and changes no comparison between two non-light coins, so it changes no comparison between $x$ and another coin, and $x$ is still the median. (iii) is the same. Hence Wong's questions, the answers, and his final arrangement are identical in all these worlds.

Step 2: Wong cannot guarantee more than $A$. Let coin $c$ end in position $\pi(c)$, and compare the true world $r$ with the reversed world $\bar r$. Take a mirror pair $\{i,i'\}$ and let $A,A'$ be the coins with $r(A)=i$, $r(A')=i'$. In world $r$ a point is scored at position $i$ iff $\pi(A)=i$, and at $i'$ iff $\pi(A')=i'$. In world $\bar r$ the coin of rank $i$ is $A'$ and that of rank $i'$ is $A$, so a point is scored at $i$ iff $\pi(A')=i$ and at $i'$ iff $\pi(A)=i'$. If world $r$ scores at $i$, then $\pi(A)=i$, so world $\bar r$ scores neither at $i$ (occupied by $A$) nor at $i'$ (that needs $\pi(A)=i'$); if world $r$ scores at $i'$, the same argument with $A'$ applies. So every mirror pair gives points to at most one of the two worlds, and at most $2$. Let $\alpha,\beta$ be the numbers of points at mirror positions in the worlds $r,\bar r$. Then $\alpha+\beta\le2P$. If $P$ is even, $\min(\alpha,\beta)\le P=2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. If $P$ is odd and $\alpha,\beta\ge P$, then $\alpha+\beta=2P$, so every pair gives exactly $2$ points to one world; then $\alpha,\beta$ are even, hence both at least $P+1$, a contradiction. So in all cases $\min(\alpha,\beta)\le2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. The central position gives at most one point.

Choose, among $r$ and $\bar r$, a world $w$ with at most $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor$ points at mirror positions. Now permute the ranks of the light coins of $w$ so that none of them stands in the position equal to its rank. This is possible because $t\ge2$: list the light coins as $c_1,\dots,c_t$, take a bijection $\tau$ from them onto $\{1,\dots,t\}$ with $\tau(c_k)=\pi(c_k)$ whenever $\pi(c_k)\le t$ (possible, as $\pi$ is injective), and give $c_k$ the new rank $\tau(c_{k+1})$ (indices modulo $t$); this differs from $\tau(c_k)$, so $c_k$ is not in its place. Do the same with the heavy coins. By Step 1 this world is consistent with everything Wong has seen, and in it he scores nothing at the $2t$ outer positions, so at most $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor+\varepsilon=A$ points.

Step 3: what Wong can learn. Wong asks the device about all $\binom n{2t+1}$ sets.

  • The middle coins are exactly the coins returned at least once: a middle coin $x$ is the median of $x$ together with $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins (there are enough of them since $t+1\le r(x)\le n-t$), and Step 1 showed that light and heavy coins are never returned. If $m=1$ this already gives $A=1$: put the middle coin in the central position. If $m=2$ then $A=0$. Let $m\ge3$, so $n\ge2t+3$.

  • Let $x$ be middle and $y\ne z$ other coins; Wong can tell whether $y,z$ are on the same side of $x$ (both lighter or both heavier). The sets with answer $x$ consist of $x$, $t$ lighter and $t$ heavier coins. Call a coin forced if it lies in all of them. A side of $x$ with exactly $t$ coins consists of forced coins, a side with more coins contains none; since $n\ge2t+2$, at most one side is forced. If $y,z$ are both forced they are on the same side; if exactly one is forced they are on different sides. If neither is forced, pick a set $S$ with answer $x$ that contains $y$ but not $z$ (on each non-forced side there are at least $t+1$ coins, so we can choose $t$ of them avoiding $z$ and, on the side of $y$, including $y$). Then the set $S\setminus\{y\}\cup\{z\}$ has answer $x$ if and only if $y$ and $z$ are on the same side of $x$.

  • For middle coins $x,y,z$, the coin $x$ lies between $y$ and $z$ in weight iff $y,z$ are on different sides of $x$. So Wong knows, for every triple of middle coins, which one is between the other two. Exactly two middle coins are never between two others (the lightest and the heaviest middle coin); call one of them $e$. Then $y$ is lighter than $z$ in the order starting from $e$ iff $y=e$ or $y$ lies between $e$ and $z$. Thus Wong knows the order of the middle coins up to reversal, i.e. a labelling $\rho$ of the middle coins by $t+1,\dots,n-t$ which is either $r$ or $\bar r$ on them.

Step 4: a strategy scoring $A$. Split the mirror pairs into two groups $G_1,G_2$ of sizes $\lceil P/2\rceil$ and $\lfloor P/2\rfloor$. For $\{i,i'\}\in G_1$ put the coin with $\rho=i$ at position $i$ and the coin with $\rho=i'$ at position $i'$; for $\{i,i'\}\in G_2$ put the coin with $\rho=i$ at $i'$ and the coin with $\rho=i'$ at $i$. If $m$ is odd, put the coin with $\rho=\frac{n+1}2$ in the central position; the light and heavy coins fill the remaining positions. If $\rho=r$, every pair of $G_1$ gives $2$ points; if $\rho=\bar r$, every pair of $G_2$ gives $2$ points; the central coin scores in both cases. So Wong always gets at least $2\lfloor P/2\rfloor+\varepsilon=A$ points.

Hence the maximum is $\boxed{2\lfloor\frac{n-2024}4\rfloor+\varepsilon}$ for $n\ge2025$, with $\varepsilon=1$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$ (and $0$ for $2\le n\le2024$).

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

A sequence of positive integers $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ satisfies, for all $n\ge1$, \[a_{2n}=a_{2n-2}\,a_{2n-1},\qquad a_{2n+1}=a_{2n}+a_{2n-1}.\] Determine the maximum number of perfect squares that can appear in this sequence.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write out the first few terms and label the even- and odd-indexed subsequences separately. Look for repeated factors in each after applying the recurrence more than once.
Hint 2: key idea
After stripping off a squared factor, a later even-indexed term reduces to a product of consecutive integers. For a later odd-indexed term, multiply by $4$ and try comparing with squares of consecutive integers. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Expand two recurrence steps to treat the even-indexed terms, then use the same consecutive-integer product to test whether $a_2$ and $a_3$ can both be squares. Write a later odd-indexed term as $y(m+1)(my+1)$, obtain bounds on $y$ from the recurrence, and compare four times the term with the squares starting at $((2m+1)y)^2$. Check the early terms separately and try small initial values for an example.
Common trap
The bounds on $y$ for the odd-indexed argument require that the earlier subsequence terms have already undergone a recurrence step.
Show the answer

$4$

Show the solution – Even terms, one pair, and odd terms trapped between consecutive squares

Write $b_k=a_{2k}$ and $c_k=a_{2k+1}$ for $k\ge0$. The recurrences read, for $k\ge1$, \[b_k=b_{k-1}c_{k-1},\qquad c_k=b_k+c_{k-1}=c_{k-1}(b_{k-1}+1).\] Four squares are possible. For $a_0=a_1=1$ the sequence begins $1,1,1,2,2,4,8,12,\dots$, with the squares $a_0,a_1,a_2,a_5$.

Step 1: $a_{2k}$ is not a square for $k\ge2$. With $m=b_{k-2}\ge1$, \[b_k=b_{k-1}c_{k-1}=b_{k-2}c_{k-2}\cdot c_{k-2}(b_{k-2}+1)=c_{k-2}^2\,m(m+1),\] and $m(m+1)$ is not a square because $m^2<m(m+1)<(m+1)^2$. So $b_k$ is not a square.

Step 2: $a_2$ and $a_3$ are not both squares. We have $b_1c_1=b_0c_0\cdot c_0(b_0+1)=c_0^2\,b_0(b_0+1)$. If $b_1=s^2$ and $c_1=r^2$, then $b_0(b_0+1)=(rs/c_0)^2$ is an integer that is the square of a rational number, hence a perfect square, which is impossible as in Step 1.

Step 3: $a_{2k+1}$ is not a square for $k\ge3$. For $k\ge2$, applying the second recurrence twice and then the first, \[c_k=c_{k-1}(b_{k-1}+1)=c_{k-2}(b_{k-2}+1)(b_{k-2}c_{k-2}+1)=y(m+1)(my+1),\] where $m=b_{k-2}$ and $y=c_{k-2}$. Multiplying out, \[4c_k=4m(m+1)y^2+4(m+1)y=\bigl((2m+1)y\bigr)^2+y\,(4m+4-y).\] Now let $k\ge3$, so $j=k-2\ge1$. Then $y=c_j=c_{j-1}(b_{j-1}+1)\ge2$, and $y=c_j=b_j+c_{j-1}\le2b_j=2m$ because $c_{j-1}\le b_{j-1}c_{j-1}=b_j$. Hence $0<y(4m+4-y)$, and \[y(4m+4-y)-\bigl(2(2m+1)y+1\bigr)=-(y-1)^2<0 .\] Therefore \[\bigl((2m+1)y\bigr)^2<4c_k<\bigl((2m+1)y+1\bigr)^2,\] so $4c_k$ lies strictly between two consecutive squares. It is not a square, so neither is $c_k$.

Conclusion. By Steps 1 and 3 every square of the sequence is among $a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,a_5$, and by Step 2 at most one of $a_2,a_3$ is a square. Hence at most $4$ terms are squares, and the example shows that $4$ occurs. The maximum is $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Given $n\ge4$ lines in general position, a panda is located at the intersection of every pair of the $n$ lines. Two pandas are friends of each other if they lie on the same line, with no other pandas between them. A panda is called friendly if it has exactly four friends.

Find all possible numbers of friendly pandas over all configurations of the $n$ lines.

Hint 1: understand the problem
On each line identify the first and last intersections. At a panda where two lines meet, how do those endpoint positions affect its number of neighbours?
Hint 2: key idea
Count line-end incidences to reduce the question to intersections that are ends on both lines. At one such intersection, look at the empty rays leading out along the two lines and compare their directions on a circle of directions. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Use the convex hull to bound the number of double ends below, and assign each double end a gap between successive ray directions for the upper bound. If every opposite pair of gaps supplies one double end, track the alternation of occupied gaps around the direction circle. For constructions, first find configurations attaining the extreme counts, then look for small moves of one line that change the double-end count by exactly one.
Common trap
An intersection that ends both lines appears twice when you count endpoint incidences.
Show the answer

for odd $n$: every integer from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2$; for even $n$: every integer from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$

Show solution 1 – Double ends: convex hull, directions of end rays, and a small rotation

Each line carries $n-1\ge3$ pandas (its intersections with the other lines; they are distinct because no three lines are concurrent). The first and the last panda on a line are its two ends. A panda $V=\ell\cap m$ has two friends on $\ell$, or one if it is an end of $\ell$, and likewise on $m$; the friends on $\ell$ differ from those on $m$. So $V$ is friendly iff it is an end of neither of its lines. Call $V$ a double end if it is an end of both of its lines; let $e$ be the number of double ends and $e'$ the number of pandas that are an end of exactly one line. Counting the $2n$ pairs (line, end) gives $e'+2e=2n$, so the number of friendly pandas is \[F=\binom n2-e-e'=\binom n2-2n+e=\frac{n(n-5)}2+e .\] We prove that the possible values of $e$ are exactly $3,4,\dots,n$ for odd $n$ and $3,4,\dots,n-1$ for even $n$; since $\frac{n(n-5)}2+3=\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ and $\frac{n(n-5)}2+n=\frac{n(n-3)}2$, this gives the answer.

Step 1: $e\ge3$. The pandas are not all collinear (the $n-1$ pandas of $\ell_1$ lie on $\ell_1$, while $\ell_2\cap\ell_3$ does not), so their convex hull is a polygon with at least $3$ vertices, all pandas. If a vertex $V=\ell\cap m$ were not an end of $\ell$, it would lie strictly between two pandas of $\ell$ and could not be a vertex. So every vertex is a double end.

Step 2: $e\le n$. For each end $E$ of a line $\ell$, the end ray is the open ray of $\ell$ starting at $E$ that contains no panda. There are $2n$ end rays; their directions are $2n$ distinct points of the circle of directions (each line gives two opposite directions, and no two lines are parallel). These points are centrally symmetric and cut the circle into $2n$ arcs, the gaps, which form $n$ pairs of opposite gaps.

Let $V=\ell\cap m$ be a double end with end rays $\rho\subset\ell$ and $\sigma\subset m$, and let $W$ be the closed angular region bounded by $\rho$ and $\sigma$ (its angle is less than $180^\circ$). (a) No other line $k$ meets $W$: $k$ does not pass through $V$, and it meets neither $\rho$ nor $\sigma$, which contain no pandas; a line meeting $W$ would have to cross its boundary, since $W$ contains no whole line. (b) No end-ray direction $w$ lies strictly inside the angle of $W$: $w$ would be a direction of some line $k\ne\ell,m$ (the other directions of $\ell,m$ are opposite to those of $\rho,\sigma$), and for a point $K\in k$ and large $s$ the point $K+sw=V+s\bigl(w+\frac{K-V}s\bigr)$ lies in $W$, contradicting (a). So the directions of $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are neighbours on the circle, and the arc between them is a gap: the gap of $V$.

Different double ends have different gaps (a gap determines its two end directions, hence the two end rays and $V$). Two opposite gaps cannot both be gaps of double ends: the opposite gap is bounded by the opposite directions of the same two lines $\ell,m$, so it could only be the gap of $V=\ell\cap m$ again, with end rays opposite to $\rho,\sigma$; then $V$ would be the end of $\ell$ in both directions, i.e. the only panda on $\ell$. So at most one gap of each opposite pair is used, and $e\le n$.

Step 3: $e\ne n$ for even $n$. Suppose $e=n$. Then $e'=0$, so every end is a double end and every end ray is used by exactly one double end (an end ray determines its endpoint), whose gap is one of the two gaps adjacent to that ray's direction. Number the lines so that, going counterclockwise, the directions are $u_1,\dots,u_n,-u_1,\dots,-u_n$ with $u_i$ a direction of $\ell_i$. The gaps are $g_i=(u_i,u_{i+1})$ for $i<n$, $g_n=(u_n,-u_1)$, and their opposites $-g_i$. Exactly one gap of each pair $\{g_i,-g_i\}$ is used; put $\varepsilon_i=1$ if it is $g_i$ and $\varepsilon_i=-1$ if it is $-g_i$.

  • For $2\le i\le n-1$ the gaps adjacent to $u_i$ are $g_{i-1},g_i$; exactly one is used, so $\varepsilon_i=-\varepsilon_{i-1}$. Hence $\varepsilon_{n-1}=(-1)^{n-2}\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_1$.

  • The gaps adjacent to $u_1$ are $-g_n$ and $g_1$; exactly one is used, so $\varepsilon_n=\varepsilon_1$.

  • The gaps adjacent to $u_n$ are $g_{n-1}$ and $g_n$; so $\varepsilon_n=-\varepsilon_{n-1}$.

Together $\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_n=-\varepsilon_{n-1}=-\varepsilon_1$, a contradiction.

Step 4: constructions. (a) Regular configurations. For odd $k=2s+1\ge3$ take the $k$ tangents of a unit circle at the points $T_j$ with angles $\frac{2\pi j}k$. No two are parallel (no two $T_j$ are antipodal) and no three are concurrent (from an outside point there are only two tangents). The tangents at angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$, $0<|\beta-\alpha|<\pi$, meet on the tangent at $\alpha$ at signed distance $\tan\frac{\beta-\alpha}2$ from $T_\alpha$. On the tangent $j$ the other tangents $j+d$ ($d=\pm1,\dots,\pm s$) therefore appear in the order of $d$, and its ends are its intersections with the tangents $j+s$ and $j-s$. Since the ends of tangent $j+s$ are with $j+2s=j-1$ and $j$, every pair $\{j,j+s\}$ gives a double end; these are $k$ different pandas, so $e=k$ by Step 2.

(b) Two operations. Take a configuration, a line $\ell$ with ends $E_1=\ell\cap m_1$, $E_2=\ell\cap m_2$, a point $P\in\ell$ that is not a panda, and let $\ell'$ be $\ell$ rotated about $P$ through a small angle $\theta\ne0$. Let $\delta>0$ be less than half the smallest distance between two different points among the pandas and $P$. For $\theta$ small enough, $\ell'$ is parallel to no line and passes through no panda, each new panda $\ell'\cap m$ is within $\delta$ of $\ell\cap m$, and the pandas of $\ell'$ appear along $\ell'$ in the same order as the corresponding pandas along $\ell$, with $P$ in the same place (all by continuity in $\theta$). Then on every line $k\ne\ell,\ell'$ the new panda $\ell'\cap k$ is next to $\ell\cap k$. Consequently:

  • A panda on neither $\ell$ nor $\ell'$ is a double end after the operation iff it was before.

  • For $m\in\{m_1,m_2\}$ with $E=\ell\cap m$ still an end of $\ell$: the pandas $\ell\cap m$ and $\ell'\cap m$ are ends of $\ell$ and $\ell'$, and they are neighbours on $m$; if $E$ was an end of $m$, exactly one of them is now an end of $m$, otherwise neither is. So they contribute exactly what $E$ contributed before.

Operation (I): $P$ strictly between two pandas of $\ell$. Then $\ell$ and $\ell'$ keep the ends $E_1,E_2$ (resp. their copies), $P$ is not an end, and by the two bullets $e$ does not change.

Operation (II): $P$ on the end ray at $E_2$, where $E_2$ is not a double end. Now the ends of $\ell$ are $E_1,P$ and those of $\ell'$ are $\ell'\cap m_1,P$; so $P$ is a new double end, $E_1$ contributes as before, and $\ell\cap m_2,\ell'\cap m_2$ are not double ends, just as $E_2$ was not. So $e$ increases by $1$.

(c) All values occur. For odd $k$ with $3\le k\le n$: start from (a) with $k$ lines and apply (I) $n-k$ times. For even $k$ with $4\le k\le n-1$: start from (a) with $k-1$ lines, apply (I) once ($k$ lines, $e=k-1$); now $e'=2k-2e>0$, so some end is not a double end; apply (II) at it ($k+1$ lines, $e=k$), and then (I) $n-k-1$ times. With Steps 1–3 the possible values of $e$ are exactly $3,\dots,n$ for odd $n$ and $3,\dots,n-1$ for even $n$.

Hence the number of friendly pandas can be exactly every integer from $\boxed{\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2}$ to $\boxed{\frac{n(n-3)}2}$ when $n$ is odd, and from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$ when $n$ is even.

Show solution 2 – Point-line duality: double ends are extreme slopes

Friendly pandas and double ends. Each line carries $n-1\ge3$ pandas (no three lines are concurrent). Call the first and last panda on a line its ends. A panda on lines $\ell,m$ has two friends on $\ell$, or one if it is an end of $\ell$, and likewise on $m$, so it is friendly iff it is an end of neither line. Let $D$ be the number of pandas that are ends of both their lines and $D'$ the number that are ends of exactly one. Counting the $2n$ pairs (line, end) gives $D'+2D=2n$, so the number of friendly pandas is $\binom n2-D-D'=\binom n2-2n+D$. We show that the possible values of $D$ are $3,4,\dots,n$ for odd $n$ and $3,\dots,n-1$ for even $n$, so the answer is: every integer from $\binom n2-2n+3=\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2$ for odd $n$, and to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$ for even $n$.

Duality. Rotate so that no line is vertical, and write the lines as $y=a_ix+b_i$; the $a_i$ are distinct (no parallel lines). Assign to line $i$ the point $Q_i=(a_i,b_i)$. Lines $i$ and $j$ meet at abscissa $x_{ij}=-\sigma_{ij}$, where $\sigma_{ij}=\frac{b_i-b_j}{a_i-a_j}$ is the slope of $Q_iQ_j$. Three lines are concurrent iff the three points are collinear ($y_0=a_ix_0+b_i$ means that $Q_i$ lies on the line $b=y_0-x_0a$). Conversely, any $n$ points with distinct abscissas and no three collinear give $n$ lines in general position. The pandas on line $i$ are ordered by abscissa, so $\ell_i\cap\ell_j$ is an end of line $i$ iff $\sigma_{ij}$ is the largest or the smallest of the slopes $\sigma_{ij'}$ $(j'\ne i)$; these slopes are distinct because no three points are collinear. So $D$ is the number of doubled pairs $\{i,j\}$: $\sigma_{ij}$ is extreme among the slopes from $Q_i$ and among the slopes from $Q_j$. Number the points so that $a_1<a_2<\dots<a_n$; call $\{i,i+1\}$ adjacent and $\{1,n\}$ outer. We use: if $m$ is to the right of $i$, then $\sigma_{im}<\sigma_{ij}$ iff $Q_m$ is below line $Q_iQ_j$; if $m$ is to the left of $i$, then $\sigma_{im}<\sigma_{ij}$ iff $Q_m$ is above it.

Upper bound. Let $\{i,j\}$, $i<j$, be doubled.

  • If $\sigma_{ij}$ is the maximum at both $i$ and $j$, there is no $m$ with $i<m<j$: the maximum at $i$ puts $Q_m$ below line $Q_iQ_j$, the maximum at $j$ puts it above. The same holds for two minima. So such a pair is adjacent.

  • If $\sigma_{ij}$ is the maximum at $i$ and the minimum at $j$ (or the reverse), there is no $m<i$ and no $m>j$: such a $Q_m$ would have to be both above and below line $Q_iQ_j$. So the pair is outer. It is doubled exactly when all of $Q_2,\dots,Q_{n-1}$ are on the same side of line $Q_1Q_n$ (if they are all below, $\sigma_{1n}$ is the maximum at $1$ and the minimum at $n$).

Hence $D\le(n-1)+1=n$. Two consecutive adjacent pairs $\{i-1,i\},\{i,i+1\}$ cannot both be doubled of the same kind: two maxima would give $\sigma_{i-1,i}>\sigma_{i,i+1}$ and $\sigma_{i,i+1}>\sigma_{i-1,i}$ at $Q_i$. So if all $n-1$ adjacent pairs are doubled, their kinds alternate. For even $n$, $n-1$ is odd, so $\{1,2\}$ and $\{n-1,n\}$ are of the same kind, say maxima. Then $\sigma_{12}>\sigma_{1n}$ and $\sigma_{n-1,n}>\sigma_{1n}$, so $\sigma_{1n}$ is the maximum at neither end and the outer pair is not doubled (minima: symmetric). Hence $D\le n-1$ for even $n$.

Lower bound $D\ge3$. The largest slope $\sigma_{ij}$ over all pairs is the maximum at both $i$ and $j$, and the smallest one is the minimum at both ends; these are two doubled pairs, different because not all points are collinear. If all inner points are on one side of $Q_1Q_n$, the outer pair is a third one (it is not adjacent, as $n\ge4$). Otherwise apply the shear $(a,b)\mapsto(a,b-\lambda a)$ that makes $Q_1Q_n$ horizontal: it subtracts $\lambda$ from every slope, so it changes neither the order of the slopes from any point nor the set of doubled pairs. Now some points are above and some below the common height of $Q_1,Q_n$. Let $Q_h$ be a highest and $Q_l$ a lowest point; both are inner. Suppose $h<l$ (otherwise reflect in the horizontal axis, which swaps maxima and minima).

  • Among the pairs inside $\{1,\dots,h\}$ take one, $\{i,j\}$, of largest slope $s_1$; then $s_1\ge\sigma_{1h}>0$. Let $e\in\{i,j\}$ and $m>h$, so $b_m\le b_h$. If $e<h$, then $b_h\ge b_e$ and $a_m>a_h$ give $\sigma_{em}=\frac{b_m-b_e}{a_m-a_e}\le\frac{b_h-b_e}{a_m-a_e}\le\frac{b_h-b_e}{a_h-a_e}=\sigma_{eh}\le s_1$; if $e=h$, then $\sigma_{hm}\le0<s_1$. Slopes from $e$ to points of $\{1,\dots,h\}$ are at most $s_1$ by the choice. So $s_1$ is the maximum at $i$ and at $j$: the pair is doubled.

  • In the same way, among the pairs inside $\{l,\dots,n\}$ take one of largest slope $s_2\ge\sigma_{ln}>0$. For an endpoint $e$ and $m<l$ (so $b_m\ge b_l$): if $e>l$, $\sigma_{me}=\frac{b_e-b_m}{a_e-a_m}\le\frac{b_e-b_l}{a_e-a_m}\le\frac{b_e-b_l}{a_e-a_l}=\sigma_{le}\le s_2$; if $e=l$, $\sigma_{ml}\le0<s_2$. So this pair is doubled too.

These two pairs are different (they lie in the disjoint index sets $\{1,\dots,h\}$ and $\{l,\dots,n\}$) and have positive slopes, while the smallest slope overall is at most $\sigma_{1l}<0$. So $D\ge3$.

Extreme examples. Take $a_i=i$.

  • $D=3$: $b_i=i^2$. The slopes $\sigma_{ij}=i+j$ grow with each index, so the maximum from $Q_i$ is towards $Q_n$ (towards $Q_{n-1}$ for $i=n$) and the minimum towards $Q_1$ (towards $Q_2$ for $i=1$). The doubled pairs are exactly $\{1,2\}$, $\{n-1,n\}$, $\{1,n\}$.

  • $D=n$ for odd $n$, $D=n-1$ for even $n$: start from $b_i=\lfloor\frac{i-1}2\rfloor$, so the adjacent slopes are $0,1,0,1,\dots$. A non-adjacent slope is an average of at least two consecutive adjacent slopes, hence lies strictly between $0$ and $1$. So at every point the adjacent slope equal to $1$ (if any) is the maximum and the one equal to $0$ (if any) is the minimum: all $n-1$ adjacent pairs are doubled. For odd $n$ add $\varepsilon i^2$ to $b_i$ with a small $\varepsilon>0$: the inner points were on or below the line $Q_1Q_n$ (it is $b=\frac{a-1}2$ and $\lfloor\frac{i-1}2\rfloor\le\frac{i-1}2$), and a strictly convex addition puts them strictly below, so the outer pair becomes doubled. Finally perturb all $b_i$ slightly to have no three points collinear. All the strict inequalities used survive small changes, so $D=n$ for odd $n$ and $D\ge n-1$, hence $D=n-1$, for even $n$.

All values in between. Keep $a_i=i$ and move the vector $(b_1,\dots,b_n)$ along the segment from an example with $D=3$ to one with the largest $D$. A triple $\{i,j,k\}$ is collinear on a hyperplane of $\mathbb R^n$, and different triples give different hyperplanes (the hyperplane involves exactly the coordinates $i,j,k$). Moving the two endpoints slightly (which does not change their $D$), we may assume that the segment avoids all intersections of two of these hyperplanes, so it crosses them at finitely many times, one triple at a time. Between crossings the slopes from each point stay distinct, their order does not change, and $D$ is constant. At a crossing where $\{i,j,k\}$ becomes collinear, a comparison $\sigma_{pq}$ versus $\sigma_{pr}$ can change only if $\{p,q,r\}=\{i,j,k\}$, so only the pairs inside $\{i,j,k\}$ can change status. Near the crossing at most one of them is doubled: at the crossing $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ik}$, while a fourth point $Q_m$ gives $\sigma_{im}\ne\sigma_{ij}$; so just before or after, $\sigma_{ij}$ and $\sigma_{ik}$ are close to each other and $\sigma_{im}$ is not between them, which means that they are not the maximum and the minimum at $i$ at the same time; and two of the three pairs always share a vertex. Hence $D$ changes by at most $1$ at each crossing, and it takes every integer value between $3$ and its largest value.

Together with the upper bounds, the possible numbers of friendly pandas are all integers from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2$ for odd $n$, and from $\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}2$ to $\frac{n(n-3)}2-1$ for even $n$.

Problem 6

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Reconstruction from Functional Conditions

Given a fixed positive integer $n$, find all polynomials $P$ and $Q$ with real coefficients such that \[P\bigl(Q(x)+Q(y)\bigr)=Q\bigl(P(x)+x^nQ(y)\bigr)+Q\bigl(P(y)+y^nQ(x)\bigr).\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
First see how the equation simplifies when one polynomial is constant. In the nonconstant case, list the degrees of the two terms inside the first $Q$ on the right.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare the highest total-degree parts of the identity, checking that the leading contributions on the right do not cancel. When the two degrees inside that $Q$ agree, write the resulting homogeneous identity in $u=x^{\deg Q}$ and $v=y^{\deg Q}$. (LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the first three coefficients of the homogeneous identity and test it at $(u,v)=(-1,1)$ and $(1,1)$ to restrict the degrees and leading coefficients. For each remaining case, compare lower-degree coefficients, starting with the highest contribution from any deviation from the leading terms. If a constant in $Q$ and a linear term in $P$ still survive in the case with monic leading terms, set one variable to zero and compare the resulting powers.
Common trap
Treat constant polynomials before dividing by degrees or comparing nonzero leading coefficients.
Show the answer

For every $n$: $Q\equiv c$ constant and $P$ any polynomial with $P(2c)=2c$. Besides these, for $n=1$: $(P,Q)=(x^{m+1},x^m)$ with $m\ge2$, $(x^2+cx,\,x)$ with $c\in\mathbb R$, and $(\frac{x^3}9,\,-\frac{x^2}3)$; for $n=2$: $(\frac{x^3}3+cx,\,x)$ and $(-\frac{x^3}3+cx,\,-x)$ with $c\in\mathbb R$; for $n\ge3$ there are no others

Show the solution – Leading homogeneous parts, then the lower terms

Write (E) for the identity $P(Q(x)+Q(y))=Q(P(x)+x^nQ(y))+Q(P(y)+y^nQ(x))$.

The listed pairs work. If $Q\equiv c$, (E) reads $P(2c)=2c$. For $n=1$, $Q=x^m$, $P=x^{m+1}$: $P(x)+xQ(y)=x(x^m+y^m)$, so the right side is $(x^m+y^m)^m(x^m+y^m)=P(x^m+y^m)$. For $n=1$, $Q=x$, $P=x^2+cx$: the right side is $P(x)+P(y)+2xy=(x+y)^2+c(x+y)$. For $n=1$, $Q=-\frac{x^2}3$, $P=\frac{x^3}9$: $P(x)+xQ(y)=\frac x9(x^2-3y^2)$, and the right side is $-\frac1{243}\bigl[x^2(x^2-3y^2)^2+y^2(y^2-3x^2)^2\bigr]=-\frac{(x^2+y^2)^3}{243}$, which is the left side. For $n=2$, $Q=\epsilon x$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+cx$ ($\epsilon=\pm1$): the right side is $\epsilon(P(x)+P(y))+xy(x+y)=\frac{(x+y)^3}3+\epsilon c(x+y)=P(\epsilon(x+y))$.

From now on $Q$ is not constant: $\deg Q=q\ge1$ with leading coefficient $b$.

Step 0: $P$ is not constant. If $P\equiv k$, put $y=x$: $2Q(k+x^nQ(x))=k$; as $x^nQ(x)$ takes infinitely many values, $Q$ would be constant. So $\deg P=p\ge1$; let $a$ be its leading coefficient.

Step 1: $p=n+q$. Compare the top homogeneous components of both sides. On the left it is $ab^p(x^q+y^q)^p$, of degree $pq$. Put $A=P(x)+x^nQ(y)$; its top component $A^*$ is $ax^p$, $bx^ny^q$ or $ax^p+bx^ny^q$ according as $p>n+q$, $p<n+q$ or $p=n+q$ (the two monomials are different). The right side has top component $b\bigl[A^*(x,y)^q+A^*(y,x)^q\bigr]$, which is not zero: if $p\ge n+q$, its value at $(1,0)$ is $ba^q\ne0$; if $p<n+q$ it is $b^{q+1}(x^{nq}y^{q^2}+x^{q^2}y^{nq})$. So the right side has degree $q\max(p,n+q)$, which forces $p\ge n+q$. If $p>n+q$, the top components are $ab^p(x^q+y^q)^p$ and $ba^q(x^{pq}+y^{pq})$; since $p\ge2$, the first contains $x^{q(p-1)}y^q$ with coefficient $pab^p\ne0$ and the second does not. Hence $p=n+q$, and with $u=x^q$, $v=y^q$ the top components give the polynomial identity \[ab^p(u+v)^p=b\bigl[u^n(au+bv)^q+v^n(av+bu)^q\bigr]. \tag{1}\] Step 2: the leading coefficients. Comparing the coefficients of $u^p$ gives $ab^p=ba^q$. With $\lambda=a/b$ this means $b^n=\lambda^{q-1}$, and dividing (1) by $b^{q+1}$ we get \[\lambda^q(u+v)^{n+q}=u^n(\lambda u+v)^q+v^n(\lambda v+u)^q, \tag{2}\] whose coefficients of $u^{p-k}v^k$ give (binomial coefficients with an index out of range are $0$) \[\lambda^q\binom pk=\binom qk\lambda^{q-k}+\binom q{k-n}\lambda^{k-n}. \tag{3}\] Putting $(u,v)=(-1,1)$ in (2): $0=(\lambda-1)^q\bigl((-1)^{n+q}+1\bigr)$, so $\lambda=1$ or $n+q$ is odd. Putting $(u,v)=(1,1)$: $\lambda^q2^{p}=2(\lambda+1)^q$.

  • $n\ge3$: (3) with $k=1$ gives $\lambda=\frac qp$. With $k=2$: if $q=1$ it gives $\lambda\binom p2=0$, impossible; if $q\ge2$ it gives $\lambda^2p(p-1)=q(q-1)$, i.e. $q(p-1)=p(q-1)$, so $p=q$, impossible. So there is no solution with $Q$ nonconstant.

  • $\lambda=1$: $(1,1)$ gives $2^{n+q}=2^{q+1}$, so $n=1$, and $b=\lambda^{q-1}=1=a$.

  • $\lambda\ne1$, $n=1$, $q$ even: $(1,1)$ gives $(2\lambda)^q=(\lambda+1)^q$, so $2\lambda=-(\lambda+1)$, $\lambda=-\frac13$. Then (3) with $k=1$ reads $\lambda^q(q+1)=q\lambda^{q-1}+1$, i.e. $3^q=4q+1$, so $q=2$ (for $q\ge3$, $3^q>4q+1$). Thus $b=\lambda=-\frac13$, $a=\frac19$.

  • $\lambda\ne1$, $n=2$, $q$ odd: $k=1$ gives $\lambda=\frac q{q+2}$, and $(1,1)$ becomes $2=(1+\frac1q)^q$; for $q\ge2$ the binomial theorem gives $(1+\frac1q)^q\ge2+\frac{q-1}{2q}>2$, so $q=1$, $\lambda=\frac13$, $b^2=1$, $a=\frac b3$.

Step 3: the lower terms. Four cases remain.

(i) $n=2$, $Q=\epsilon x+d$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+rx^2+sx+w$, $\epsilon=\pm1$. With $\sigma=x+y$ the left side is $\frac{\sigma^3}3+(2\epsilon d+r)\sigma^2+(\dots)\sigma+\dots$, and the right side is $\epsilon\bigl[P(x)+P(y)+\epsilon xy\sigma+d(x^2+y^2)\bigr]+2d=\frac{\sigma^3}3+\epsilon(r+d)(x^2+y^2)+\epsilon s\sigma+2\epsilon w+2d$. Comparing $xy$: $2\epsilon d+r=0$; comparing $x^2$: $r+d=0$. Hence $d(2\epsilon-1)=0$, so $d=r=0$. Then the constant terms give $w=2\epsilon w$, so $w=0$: $Q=\epsilon x$, $P=\frac\epsilon3x^3+sx$.

(ii) $n=1$, $q=1$, $Q=x+d$, $P=x^2+sx+w$. The left side is $\sigma^2+(4d+s)\sigma+4d^2+2sd+w$ and the right side is $P(x)+P(y)+2xy+d\sigma+2d=\sigma^2+(s+d)\sigma+2w+2d$. So $d=0$, then $w=0$: $Q=x$, $P=x^2+sx$.

(iii) $n=1$, $q=2$, $Q=-\frac{x^2}3+ex+d$, $P=\frac{x^3}9+rx^2+sx+w$. Expanding (a routine computation, see the check), the homogeneous part of degree $5$ of (left side $-$ right side) is \[\tfrac{x+y}{27}\bigl[(2r+e)(x^4+y^4)+2(e-r)(x^3y+xy^3)-(4r+6e)x^2y^2\bigr],\] so $r=e=0$; then the part of degree $4$ is $\frac1{27}\bigl[(2s+4d)(x^4+y^4)-(12s+8d)x^2y^2\bigr]$, so $s=d=0$; then the part of degree $3$ is $\frac{2w}{27}(x+y)(x^2-4xy+y^2)$, so $w=0$. Thus $Q=-\frac{x^2}3$, $P=\frac{x^3}9$.

(iv) $n=1$, $q\ge2$, $Q=x^q+Q_1$, $P=x^{q+1}+P_1$ with $\deg Q_1=j<q$, $\deg P_1=i\le q$ and leading coefficients $\beta,\alpha$ (if $Q_1$ or $P_1$ is zero, ignore it below). Suppose they are not both zero, and let $\mu=\max(i,j+1)$, $D=q^2+\mu-1$, $S_0=x^q+y^q$, $S_1=Q_1(x)+Q_1(y)$. Expand: \[P(Q(x)+Q(y))=S_0^{q+1}+(q+1)S_0^qS_1+R_1,\qquad Q(P(x)+xQ(y))=(xS_0)^q+q(xS_0)^{q-1}\bigl(P_1(x)+xQ_1(y)\bigr)+R_2,\] where $R_1$ (the higher powers of $S_1$, and $P_1(S_0+S_1)$) has degree at most $\max\bigl(q(q-1)+2j,\;iq\bigr)$ and $R_2$ (the higher powers of $P_1(x)+xQ_1(y)$, and $Q_1(\dots)$) has degree at most $\max\bigl((q+1)(q-2)+2\mu,\;(q+1)j\bigr)$; all these are less than $D$ because $j<q$, $i\le q$, $\mu\le q$ and $q\ge2$. Since $S_0^{q+1}=(xS_0)^q+(yS_0)^q$, the part of degree $D$ of (left side $-$ right side) is $S_0^{q-1}K$ with \[K=\beta\bigl[(q+1)(x^{q+j}+y^{q+j})+x^qy^j+x^jy^q\bigr]-q\alpha\bigl(x^{q-1+i}+y^{q-1+i}\bigr),\] where $\beta$ is to be read as $0$ unless $j=\mu-1$, and $\alpha$ as $0$ unless $i=\mu$. As $K=0$: if only the $\beta$-term is present, the coefficient of $x^{q+j}$, which is $(q+1)\beta$ or $(q+2)\beta$, vanishes; if only the $\alpha$-term is present, $\alpha=0$; if both are present and $j\ge1$, the mixed monomial $x^qy^j$ has coefficient $\beta$. All three are impossible. The only remaining possibility is $j=0$, $i=1$, $K=\bigl((q+2)\beta-q\alpha\bigr)(x^q+y^q)$, i.e. \[Q=x^q+\beta,\quad P=x^{q+1}+\alpha x+w,\quad \alpha=\tfrac{q+2}q\beta,\quad\beta\ne0 .\] Put $x=0$ in (E): $(y^q+2\beta)^{q+1}+\alpha(y^q+2\beta)+w=\bigl(y^{q+1}+(\alpha+\beta)y+w\bigr)^q+\beta+Q(w)$. The left side contains only powers $y^{qk}$, while on the right $y^{q^2-1}$ has coefficient $qw$; so $w=0$, and the right side becomes $y^q\bigl(y^q+\alpha+\beta\bigr)^q+2\beta$. For $q\ge3$ compare $y^{q^2-q}$: $\binom{q+1}2(2\beta)^2=\binom q2(\alpha+\beta)^2$ with $\alpha+\beta=\frac{2(q+1)}q\beta$ gives $q^2=q^2-1$, impossible. For $q=2$ ($\alpha=2\beta$) compare $y^2$: $12\beta^2+2\beta=9\beta^2$, so $\beta=-\frac23$; and the constant terms: $8\beta^3+2\alpha\beta=2\beta$, i.e. $4\beta^2+2\beta=1$, false for $\beta=-\frac23$. So $P_1=Q_1=0$: $Q=x^q$, $P=x^{q+1}$.

Problem 7

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Euler Line and Nine-Point Circle Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $\angle A<\angle B<\angle C$ and circumcenter $O$. Points $X$ and $Y$ lie on segments $AB$ and $AC$ respectively such that \[\angle OXA=\angle OYA=90^\circ+\angle A.\] Show that the line $XY$ is perpendicular to the Euler line of triangle $ABC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the central angles subtending $AB$ and $AC$. Use the corresponding isosceles triangles to identify the angles at $A$ in triangles $AXO$ and $AYO$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the sine rule to express $AX$ and $AY$. The squared-distance difference $ZO^2-ZH^2$ varies linearly as $Z$ moves, with its change determined by the projection of the displacement along $OH$. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Take $O$ as origin and use the vector expression for $H$ to project $OH$ onto $AB$ and $AC$. Rewrite each projection in side lengths and then in sines of the angles. Use $AX$ and $AY$ to set up a comparison of the two changes in squared-distance difference from $A$.
Common trap
Use signed projections when measuring along the side directions from $A$.
Show the solution – Lengths AX, AY and a linear function

Let $R$ be the circumradius, $H$ the orthocentre, and write $A,B,C$ also for the angles. As the triangle is acute and not equilateral, $O$ is inside it and $O\ne H$.

Step 1: $AX$ and $AY$. In triangle $AXO$ the angle at $X$ is $90^\circ+A$ and the angle at $A$ is $\angle OAB=90^\circ-C$ (triangle $OAB$ is isosceles with apex angle $2C$). Hence $\angle AOX=180^\circ-(90^\circ+A)-(90^\circ-C)=C-A>0$, and the law of sines gives \[AX=\frac{AO\sin(C-A)}{\sin(90^\circ+A)}=\frac{R\sin(C-A)}{\cos A}.\] In the same way ($\angle OAC=90^\circ-B$) we get $AY=\frac{R\sin(B-A)}{\cos A}$. Both are positive, so $X\ne A\ne Y$ and $X\ne Y$.

Step 2: a criterion. For a point $Z$ put $g(Z)=ZO^2-ZH^2$. In vectors, $g(Z)=2\,Z\cdot(H-O)+|O|^2-|H|^2$, so $g(X)=g(Y)$ if and only if $(X-Y)\cdot(H-O)=0$, i.e. $XY\perp OH$. Moving along line $AB$ with unit vector $\mathbf u$ from $A$ to $B$, $g(A+t\mathbf u)=g(A)+2t\,\mathbf u\cdot(H-O)$; similarly along $AC$ with unit vector $\mathbf v$. Thus $XY\perp OH$ iff \[AX\cdot\mathbf u\cdot(H-O)=AY\cdot\mathbf v\cdot(H-O). \tag{1}\]

Step 3: the projections of $OH$. With $O$ as origin, $H=A+B+C$ (Euler/Sylvester). Since $A$ and $B$ are symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, $(A+B)\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so $(H-O)\cdot\mathbf u=C\cdot\mathbf u$, the signed distance from the midpoint of $AB$ to the foot of the altitude from $C$, which is $b\cos A-\frac c2=\frac{b^2-a^2}{2c}$. With $a=2R\sin A$ etc. and $\sin^2B-\sin^2A=\sin(B-A)\sin(B+A)=\sin(B-A)\sin C$, \[(H-O)\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{4R^2\sin(B-A)\sin C}{4R\sin C}=R\sin(B-A),\qquad (H-O)\cdot\mathbf v=R\sin(C-A)\] (the second by the same computation with $B$ and $C$ exchanged).

Step 4. Both sides of (1) equal $\frac{R^2\sin(C-A)\sin(B-A)}{\cos A}$. Hence $XY\perp OH$: the line $XY$ is perpendicular to the Euler line.

Problem 8

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Let $\mathbb R^+$ be the set of positive real numbers. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb R^+\to\mathbb R^+$ such that $f(1)=1$ and, for every positive integer $n$, the numbers \[f(x+y)-\frac1n\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x)+f(y)-\frac1n\] are either both positive, both negative, or both zero for every $x,y\in\mathbb R^+$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the marked levels $1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$ on a number line. Compare how much the sign information tells you when one value is exactly a marked level and when it lies strictly between two levels.
Hint 2: key idea
At a marked level the sign condition forces exact equality, so halve $1$ repeatedly to obtain dyadic inputs with known values. Splitting one of those inputs into two positive parts gives a reflection relation for the error $f(x)-x$. (LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Combine the error reflections at successive dyadic levels to obtain a translation rule. Subtract dyadic lengths when possible to move a fixed input toward zero without changing its error, using positivity to bound the error at the resulting small input. Then decide exactly what the condition says about every remaining input.
Common trap
Away from the marked levels, identical signs only locate two values in the same interval and do not give equality.
Show the answer

$f(x)=x$ for $0<x\le1$, and $f(x)$ an arbitrary number greater than $1$ for each $x>1$

Show the solution – Dyadic anchors and periodicity of the error

Call positive reals $u,v$ equivalent, $u\sim v$, if $u-\frac1n$ and $v-\frac1n$ have the same sign for every positive integer $n$. The equivalence classes are the single points $\{\frac1n\}$, the open intervals $\bigl(\frac1{n+1},\frac1n\bigr)$ and the interval $(1,\infty)$. The condition says $f(x+y)\sim f(x)+f(y)$ for all $x,y>0$; in particular, if one side equals some $\frac1n$, so does the other.

These functions work. If $x+y\le1$, then $f(x+y)=x+y=f(x)+f(y)$. If $x+y>1$, then $f(x+y)>1$, and $f(x)+f(y)>1$ too (if $x,y\le1$ it equals $x+y$; otherwise one term exceeds $1$). Also $f(1)=1$.

Necessity for $x>1$. $f(x)\sim f(x-1)+f(1)=f(x-1)+1>1$, so $f(x)>1$.

Dyadic anchors. $f(1)=1\sim2f(\frac12)$ forces $2f(\frac12)=1$; inductively $f(2^{-k})=2^{-k}\sim2f(2^{-k-1})$ forces $f(2^{-k-1})=2^{-k-1}$. So $f(s)=s$ for $s=2^{-k}$, $k\ge0$, and then for $0<a<s$: $f(s)\sim f(a)+f(s-a)$ gives \[f(a)+f(s-a)=s. \tag{1}\] In particular $f(a)<2^{-k}$ whenever $a<2^{-k}$.

Periodicity. Put $e(a)=f(a)-a$ on $(0,1]$; by (1), $e(a)=-e(s-a)$ for $s=2^{-k}>a$. If $0<a<2^{-k-1}$, apply this with $s=2^{-k-1}$ and then, to $b=2^{-k-1}-a<2^{-k}$, with $s=2^{-k}$: \[e(a)=-e(2^{-k-1}-a)=e\bigl(2^{-k}-2^{-k-1}+a\bigr)=e\bigl(a+2^{-k-1}\bigr).\] Conclusion. Fix $a\in(0,1)$. Define $r_0=a$ and, for $K\ge1$, $r_K=r_{K-1}-2^{-K}$ if $r_{K-1}>2^{-K}$ and $r_K=r_{K-1}$ otherwise. By induction $0<r_K\le2^{-K}$. If $r_K=2^{-K}$ for some $K$, take the first such $K$; then $r_K=r_{K-1}$ (otherwise $r_{K-1}=2^{-K+1}$, an earlier such index, or $a=1$), and every earlier subtraction step produced a value $r_j<2^{-j}$. By periodicity (applied at $r_j<2^{-j}$ whenever $r_j=r_{j-1}-2^{-j}$), $e(r_K)=e(r_{K-1})=\dots=e(a)$; in the case $r_K=2^{-K}$ this gives $e(a)=e(2^{-K})=0$. If $r_K<2^{-K}$, then $0<f(r_K)<2^{-K}$, so $|e(a)|=|e(r_K)|<2^{-K}$. Letting $K\to\infty$ gives $e(a)=0$, i.e. $f(a)=a$ on $(0,1]$.

So the solutions are exactly: $f(x)=x$ for $0<x\le1$ and $f(x)$ any value greater than $1$ for $x>1$.

Problem 9

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Factorization and Algebraic Cleanup

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $A$ and $B$ be distinct lattice points in the plane. Prove that there exists a point $O$ with the following properties:

  • There is a circle with center $O$ passing through $A$ and $B$ and at least $2026$ other lattice points.

  • The value $\tan\angle OAB$ is a fraction with denominator $n$ in simplest form.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Move $A$ to the origin and mark the midpoint of $AB$. Compare the distance from the midpoint to a candidate center with half of $AB$ to express the required tangent.
Hint 2: key idea
Regard $B$ as a Gaussian integer and encode the center with a Gaussian factor $w=n+im$. A circle point can be obtained from a Gaussian integer of norm equal to that of $w$ and congruent to $-w$ modulo $2n$. (LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis)
Hint 3: approach
Use $g=1+2ni$ and its conjugate, which agree modulo $2n$, to build equal-norm candidates by varying the allocation of a fixed number of factors. Choose a Gaussian multiplier so that the real part of its product with a power of $\bar g$ is $n$ and the imaginary part is coprime to $n$. Check distinctness using the quotient $g/\bar g$ and the roots of unity in $\mathbb Q(i)$.
Common trap
Fixing the real part to be $n$ does not ensure the tangent has denominator $n$ after reduction, so the imaginary part must be coprime to it.
Show the solution – Gaussian integers

Identify the plane with $\mathbb C$ and lattice points with Gaussian integers; translating by $-A$ we may assume $A=0$, and put $\beta=B\ne0$ (a Gaussian integer).

The centre. For a Gaussian integer $w=n+im$ put $O=\frac{\beta w}{2n}$. Then $O=\frac\beta2+\frac{m}{2n}\,i\beta$: it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, at distance $\frac{m}{2n}|\beta|$ from the midpoint, so $\tan\angle OAB=\frac{(m/2n)|\beta|}{|\beta|/2}=\frac mn$ (for $m>0$). The circle through $A$ and $B$ has radius $|O|=\frac{|\beta||w|}{2n}$.

Lattice points on the circle. If $z$ is a Gaussian integer with $|z|=|w|$ and $z\equiv-w\pmod{2n}$ (i.e. $\frac{z+w}{2n}$ is a Gaussian integer), then $Z=\beta\,\frac{z+w}{2n}$ is a lattice point with $|Z-O|=\frac{|\beta||z|}{2n}=|O|$, so $Z$ lies on the circle; different $z$ give different $Z$.

Construction. Let $g=1+2ni$ and $K=2027$. Since $g\equiv\bar g\pmod{2n}$ (their difference is $4ni$), we have $g^k\bar g^{K-k}\equiv\bar g^K\pmod{2n}$ for $0\le k\le K$. Let $G=\bar g^K=c+di$.

  • $\gcd(c,d)=1$: a rational prime $q$ dividing $c$ and $d$ divides $\bar g^K$ in $\mathbb Z[i]$; then every Gaussian prime factor of $q$ divides $\bar g$, and so does its conjugate (if $q\equiv1\pmod4$ both factors of $q$ divide $\bar g^K$, hence $\bar g$; if $q\equiv3\pmod 4$, $q$ itself is prime; $q=2$ is impossible as $|g|^2=1+4n^2$ is odd). In all cases $q\mid\bar g=1-2ni$, which is false.

  • $|G|^2=(1+4n^2)^K$ is coprime to $n$.

Since $\gcd(c,d)=1$, the equation $cx-dy=n$ has integer solutions $x=x_0+dt$, $y=y_0+ct$ ($t\in\mathbb Z$). For $h=x+iy$ we get $\operatorname{Re}(Gh)=cx-dy=n$ and $\operatorname{Im}(Gh)=cy+dx=m_0+(c^2+d^2)t$ with $m_0=cy_0+dx_0$. As $c^2+d^2$ is invertible modulo $n$, we can choose $t$ so that $m=m_0+(c^2+d^2)t\equiv1\pmod n$ and $m>0$. Then $w=Gh=n+im$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$, so $\tan\angle OAB=\frac mn$ is in lowest terms with denominator $n$.

Now put $z_k=-h\,g^k\bar g^{K-k}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,K$. These are Gaussian integers with $|z_k|=|h||g|^K=|w|$, and $z_k\equiv-h\bar g^K=-w\pmod{2n}$. They are pairwise different: $z_k=-w\,(g/\bar g)^k$ and $g/\bar g$ is not a root of unity (the roots of unity in $\mathbb Q(i)$ are $\pm1,\pm i$, and $g=1+2ni$ is neither real, nor imaginary, nor a multiple of $1\pm i$). This gives $K+1=2028$ different lattice points on the circle; besides $A$ and $B$ at least $2026$ remain.

Problem 10

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

Ivan has $2025$ zeros on a circle. In each step he can do one of the two operations below:

  • take any three consecutive integers of the form $(a,a-1,a)$ and change them to $(a,a,a)$;

  • take any three consecutive integers of the form $(a,a,a)$ and change them to $(a,a+1,a)$.

Find the maximum possible integer that can appear on the circle after several of these operations.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try the moves on a circle of seven zeros and record the positions that have become positive. What is the largest difference you can create between neighboring entries?
Hint 2: key idea
For each height $h\ge1$, track the maximal runs of positions whose entries are at least $h$. A move either starts a singleton run at that height or fills a one-position gap, so track the parity of each run length. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
Show that filling a gap cannot close up the whole circle, using the parity of the run that would have surrounded that gap. Use the resulting zero and the bound on neighboring differences to limit the height. For the construction, work out how a run at the next height can be grown inside a run at the current height, and how long it can be made.
Common trap
The two ends of a one-position gap can belong to the same cyclic run, so rule out that case before treating the move as a merger.
Show the answer

$1012$

Show solution 1 – Level sets consist of arcs of odd length

Let the positions be the residues modulo $N=2025$ and let $x_j$ be the number at position $j$. Both operations raise one middle entry $x_i$ by $1$, provided $x_{i-1}=x_{i+1}$ and $x_i\in\{x_{i-1}-1,\,x_{i-1}\}$.

Neighbouring entries differ by at most $1$. This holds at the start; an operation changes $x_i$ from $a-1$ to $a$ or from $a$ to $a+1$ while both neighbours equal $a$, so it stays true.

Invariant. For $h\ge1$ let $S_h=\{j: x_j\ge h\}$. We claim: for every $h\ge1$, $S_h$ is not the whole circle and each of its maximal arcs (runs of consecutive positions) has odd length. Initially every $S_h$ is empty. An operation changes only one set:

  • Raising $x_i$ from $a$ to $a+1$ with $x_{i-1}=x_{i+1}=a$ adds $i$ to $S_{a+1}$ as a new arc $\{i\}$ of length $1$ (its neighbours are not in $S_{a+1}$).

  • Raising $x_i$ from $a-1$ to $a$ with $x_{i\pm1}=a$ adds $i$ to $S_a$, joining the arcs through $i-1$ and $i+1$. If these were one arc, it would be the whole circle except $i$, of length $N-1$, which is even, contradicting the invariant. So they are two different arcs, of odd lengths $\ell_1,\ell_2$, and they merge into one arc of odd length $\ell_1+\ell_2+1$. This arc is not the whole circle: otherwise $S_a$ minus $i$ would be the whole circle except $i$, a single arc, whereas the arcs through $i-1$ and $i+1$ are different.

Upper bound. By the invariant $S_1$ is never the whole circle: some entry is always $0$. Every position is at cyclic distance at most $\frac{N-1}2=1012$ from that zero, and entries change by at most $1$ per step along the circle, so every entry is at most $1012$.

Construction. Fix a position $c$; we reach $x_j=1012-d(j)$, where $d(j)$ is the cyclic distance from $j$ to $c$ (a tent with peak $1012$ at $c$ and two adjacent zeros at the positions $c\pm1012$). We build it level by level: after level $h-1$ is done ($h\ge1$), the entries are $\min(h-1,\,1012-d(j))$, so they equal $h-1$ on the arc $J=\{d(j)\le1013-h\}$ (for $h=1$, the whole circle), and the target arc of level $h$ is $J'=\{d(j)\le1012-h\}$, which is $J$ without its two ends ($J'=\{c\}$ when $h=1012$).

To raise $J'$ to $h$: if $J'=\{c\}$, raise $c$ (its neighbours and itself equal $h-1$). Otherwise $J'$ has odd length $2m-1$ with $m=1013-h\ge2$; pick a seed $s\in J'$ such that the distances from $s$ to the two ends of $J'$ are even (possible because the length is odd). Raise $s$ to $h$ (operation 2: $s$ and its neighbours equal $h-1$). Then repeatedly extend the arc of $h$'s by two positions on one side: if the arc ends at $e$ and $e-1,e-2$ are still inside $J'$, then $e-3\in J$, so $x_{e-3}=x_{e-2}=x_{e-1}=h-1$; raise $e-2$ to $h$ (operation 2), then $e-1$ (operation 1, between two $h$'s). Do the same on the right. Since the distances from $s$ to the ends of $J'$ are even, the arc of $h$'s becomes exactly $J'$, and entries outside $J'$ are unchanged. (For $h=1$, $J'$ omits only the two adjacent positions $c\pm1012$, which stay $0$.)

After level $1012$ the entry at $c$ is $1012$. Hence the maximum possible integer is $\boxed{1012}$.

Show solution 2 – A zero that never disappears, and a tent built layer by layer

Let the positions be the residues modulo $N=2025$ and let $x_j$ be the number at position $j$. Both operations raise one entry $x_i$ by $1$, and only when $x_{i-1}=x_{i+1}$ and $x_i\in\{x_{i-1}-1,\,x_{i-1}\}$. In particular the entries never decrease, and they stay nonnegative.

Neighbouring entries differ by at most $1$. This holds at the start, and an operation changes $x_i$ from $a-1$ to $a$ or from $a$ to $a+1$ while both neighbours equal $a$, so it stays true.

Some entry is always $0$. Suppose that at some moment all entries are positive. Then every position $j$ was raised from $0$ to $1$ at some move; at that move both neighbours of $j$ were equal, either to $0$ (we then call $j$ a seed) or to $1$.

  • Two neighbouring positions are not both seeds: when the later of the two was raised, the earlier one was already at least $1$, but a seed has two neighbours equal to $0$ when it is raised.

  • If $j$ is not a seed, both its neighbours are seeds. Indeed, when $j$ was raised both neighbours were already $1$. When the neighbour $j+1$ was raised from $0$ to $1$, the entry $x_j$ was still $0$, so the two (equal) neighbours of $j+1$ were $0$, and $j+1$ is a seed; similarly for $j-1$.

So seeds and non-seeds alternate around the circle, which is impossible because $N$ is odd. Hence at every moment some entry equals $0$.

Upper bound. Let $x_z=0$. Every position is at cyclic distance at most $\frac{N-1}2=1012$ from $z$, and neighbouring entries differ by at most $1$, so every entry is at most $1012$.

Construction. Number the positions $0,1,\dots,2024$ around the circle (so $2024$ and $0$ are neighbours). We show by induction on $h=0,1,\dots,1012$ that we can reach $x_j=\min(j,\,2024-j,\,h)$ for all $j$. For $h=0$ this is the start. Suppose it holds for some $h\le1011$. Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the factorial product explicitly for a few small primes and small $k$. Translate the absolute-value condition into two possible signs, and look at which small integers are forced to divide the product once $k$ grows.
Hint 2: key idea
For odd $p$, group the factors into blocks of residues modulo $p$ and use Wilson's theorem to link the sign to the parity of $k$. Hint 3: approach

Show the answer

Show the solution – Modular restrictions, then 2-adic and 3-adic sizes

Let $F=(pk)!_p$, a positive integer, for $k\ge1$. (For $k=0$ the empty product gives $F=1$ and only $(2,0,1)$.) If $t<0$ then $0<p^t<1$ and $F-p^t$ is not an integer, so $t\ge0$. If $t=0$ then $F\in\{0,2\}$, so $F=2$, which happens only for $(p,k)=(3,1)$; this gives $(3,1,0)$. From now on $t\ge1$ and $F=p^t\mp1$.

Case $p=2$. $F=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k-1)$. For $k=1,2,3$: $F=1=2^1-1$; $F=3=2^2-1=2^1+1$; $F=15=2^4-1$. This gives $(2,1,1)$, $(2,2,2)$, $(2,2,1)$, $(2,3,4)$. Let $k\ge4$, so $3\cdot5\cdot7\mid F$. Modulo $7$ the powers of $2$ are $1,2,4$, so $2^t\equiv\pm1\pmod7$ forces $F=2^t-1$ and $3\mid t$; modulo $5$, $2^t\equiv1$ forces $4\mid t$. Hence $12\mid t$ and $13\mid2^{12}-1\mid F$, so $2k-1\ge13$, $k\ge7$. Then $3\cdot9\mid F$, so $v_3(F)\ge3$; since $t$ is even, LTE gives $v_3(2^t-1)=v_3(4^{t/2}-1)=1+v_3(t)$, so $v_3(t)\ge2$, $9\mid t$, hence $36\mid t$ and $37\mid2^{36}-1\mid F$, so $k\ge19$. In general the odd multiples of $3$ below $2k$ number at least $\frac{k-1}3$, so $1+v_3(t)\ge\frac{k-1}3$ and $t\ge3^{(k-4)/3}$; but $2^t=F+1\le(2k)^k$, so $t\le k\log_2(2k)$. For $k=19$: $3^5=243>19\log_2 38\approx99.7$, and when $k$ increases by $1$ the left side grows by the factor $3^{1/3}>1.44$ while the right side grows by less. So no solution has $k\ge19$.

Case $p$ odd. The numbers $1,\dots,pk$ not divisible by $p$ form $k$ blocks, each congruent to $1,2,\dots,p-1$ modulo $p$, so $F\equiv((p-1)!)^k\equiv(-1)^k\pmod p$ by Wilson's theorem. As $F\equiv\mp1$, we get $F=p^t+(-1)^k$.

Restrictions. $(p-1)!\mid F$. Modulo $p-1$: $0\equiv F\equiv1+(-1)^k$, so if $k$ is even then $p-1\mid2$, i.e. $p=3$. For $p=3$ and $k\ge2$: $8\mid2\cdot4\mid F$, and $3^t+1\in\{2,4\}\pmod8$, so again $F=3^t-1$ with $k$ odd and $t$ even. So for all odd $p$ and $k\ge2$: $k$ is odd, $k\ge3$, and $F=p^t-1$. If $k\ge3$, also $p+1\mid F$ and $(-1)^t-1\equiv0\pmod{p+1}$, so $t$ is even.

$k=1$. Then $F=(p-1)!=p^t-1$. For $p=3$: $2=3^1-1$, giving $(3,1,1)$. For $p=5$: $24=5^2-1$, giving $(5,1,2)$. For $p\ge7$, $p-1$ is composite and at least $6$, so $(p-1)^2\mid(p-1)!$; but $p^t-1=(p-1)(1+p+\dots+p^{t-1})$ and the second factor is $\equiv t\pmod{p-1}$, so $p-1\mid t$ and $p^t\ge p^{p-1}>(p-1)!+1$, a contradiction.

$k\ge3$ odd. Now $F=p^t-1$ with $t$ even, so by LTE $v_2(F)=v_2(p^2-1)+v_2(t)-1$. On the other hand $v_2(F)=v_2((pk)!)-v_2(k!)\ge(pk-1-\log_2(pk))-(k-1)=(p-1)k-\log_2(pk)$, and $p^t\le F+1\le(pk)^{(p-1)k}$ gives $t\le(p-1)k\log_2(pk)$. With $u=(p-1)k$ (so $pk\le\frac32u$ and $p-1\le\frac u3$): \[u\le\log_2\tfrac{3u}{2}+\log_2\Bigl(\tfrac u3\bigl(\tfrac u3+2\bigr)\Bigr)-1+\log_2u+\log_2\log_2\tfrac{3u}2 .\] For $u=16$ the right side is about $15.1$, and it grows by less than $1$ per unit step of $u$, so $u\le15$. With $k\ge3$ odd this leaves $(p,k)\in\{(3,3),(3,5),(3,7),(5,3)\}$.

  • $p=3$: $5\mid F$ gives $3^t\equiv1\pmod5$, $4\mid t$; $7\mid F$ gives $6\mid t$; so $12\mid t$ and $13\mid3^{12}-1\mid F$, impossible for $k=3$ ($3k=9<13$). For $k=5,7$: $v_2(F)\ge v_2(2\cdot4\cdot8\cdot10\cdot14)=8$, while $v_2(3^t-1)=2+v_2(t)$, so $64\mid t$ and $3^t\ge3^{64}>21^{14}\ge F$, impossible.

  • $(5,3)$: $7,11,13\mid F$ give $6\mid t$, $5\mid t$, $4\mid t$, so $60\mid t$ and $5^t\ge5^{60}>15^{12}>F$, impossible.

So the solutions with $k\ge1$ are $\boxed{(2,1,1),(2,2,1),(2,2,2),(2,3,4),(3,1,0),(3,1,1),(5,1,2)}$.

Draw two lines through $A$ that are reflections of each other in its angle bisector, and place $X,Y$ on one and $Z,T$ on the other. Which existing vertices could serve as the required points on the circles through three vertices?
Hint 2: key idea
Aim for the two isogonal conjugations to pair the four vertices in two different ways. Show the solution – D the antipode of A: quadrilaterals whose vertices are paired by both conjugations

Write $\Omega=(ABC)$, and for a point $P$ let $P^*$ and $P^\sharp$ be its isogonal conjugates with respect to $ABC$ and to $DBC$. Recall: if $P$ is not on a side line of $ABC$ and not on $\Omega$, then $P^*$ is the common point of the reflections of the lines $AP,BP,CP$ in the bisectors of the angles at $A,B,C$; it is again a point not on the side lines and not on $\Omega$, and $(P^*)^*=P$. The same holds for $DBC$.

The point $D$. Let $D$ be the antipode of $A$ on $\Omega$. As $ABC$ is acute, $O$ is inside the triangle, so $D$ lies on the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, which is the minor arc since $\angle BAC<90^\circ$. Also $(DBC)=\Omega$ and $\angle ABD=\angle ACD=90^\circ$.

Step 1: pairings give trivial quadrilaterals. Let $P_1P_2P_3P_4$ satisfy the first two conditions of triviality with respect to a triangle, and suppose the isogonal conjugation of that triangle maps each $P_j$ to another vertex $P_k$. Then for each $i$ the conjugate of $P_{i+3}$ is one of $P_i,P_{i+1},P_{i+2}$, so it lies on $(P_iP_{i+1}P_{i+2})$, and the quadrilateral is trivial.

Step 2: the two conjugations at $B$ and $C$. Let $b$ and $b'$ be the bisectors of the angles $ABC$ and $DBC$. Since $A$ and $D$ lie on opposite sides of $BC$, the angle between $b$ and $b'$ is $\frac12\angle ABC+\frac12\angle CBD=\frac12\angle ABD=45^\circ$. The reflection in $b$ followed by the reflection in $b'$ is a rotation about $B$ through $90^\circ$, so it maps every line through $B$ to the perpendicular line through $B$. The same holds at $C$, because $\angle ACD=90^\circ$. Reflections also keep perpendicular lines perpendicular.

Step 3: the construction. Let $\ell$ be a line through $A$ and $\ell'$ its reflection in the bisector of angle $BAC$. Suppose $Y\in\ell$ and $T\in\ell'$ satisfy \[BY\perp BT\qquad\text{and}\qquad CY\perp CT,\tag{$\star$}\] and that neither $Y$ nor $T$ lies on $\Omega$ or on any of the lines $AB,AC,BC,DB,DC$. Put $X=T^*$ and $Z=Y^*$.

  • $X\in\ell$ and $Z\in\ell'$, because conjugation maps the line $AT=\ell'$ (minus $A$) into its reflection $\ell$, and $AY=\ell$ into $\ell'$. Hence, in directed angles, $\measuredangle BAX=\measuredangle BAY=\measuredangle(AB,\ell)=\measuredangle(\ell',AC)=\measuredangle ZAC=\measuredangle TAC$, the middle equality because a reflection reverses directed angles.

  • $Y^\sharp=X$. The line $BY^\sharp$ is the reflection of $BY$ in $b'$. By $(\star)$ and Step 2, $BY$ is the image of $BT$ under the reflection in $b$ followed by the reflection in $b'$; reflecting once more in $b'$ we get that $BY^\sharp$ is the reflection of $BT$ in $b$, which is the line $BT^*=BX$. Likewise $CY^\sharp=CX$, and since $X\notin BC$, $Y^\sharp=X$. In the same way $T^\sharp=Z$ (exchange the roles of $Y$ and $T$).

  • No vertex lies on $\Omega$ or on a side line of $ABC$ or of $DBC$: for $Y,T$ this is assumed; $X=T^*$ and $Z=Y^*$ avoid $\Omega$ and the side lines of $ABC$, and $X=Y^\sharp$, $Z=T^\sharp$ avoid $\Omega$ and the side lines of $DBC$.

  • The conjugation of $ABC$ pairs $X\leftrightarrow T$, $Y\leftrightarrow Z$, and that of $DBC$ pairs $X\leftrightarrow Y$, $Z\leftrightarrow T$. So, if no three of $X,Y,Z,T$ are collinear, $XYZT$ is trivial with respect to both triangles by Step 1.

  • The midpoints are collinear. By $(\star)$ and Thales, $B$ and $C$ lie on the circle with diameter $YT$, so the midpoint of $YT$ (its centre) lies on the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $BC$. The lines $BX,BZ$ are the reflections in $b$ of the perpendicular lines $BT,BY$, so $BX\perp BZ$; likewise $CX\perp CZ$; so the midpoint of $XZ$ also lies on $m$. The midpoint of $BC$ lies on $m$ too.

Step 4: infinitely many solutions of $(\star)$. Let $u,v$ be the unit vectors from $A$ towards $B$ and $C$. For a real $\theta$ let $u_\theta$ be $u$ rotated through $\theta$ towards $v$ and $v_\theta$ be $v$ rotated through $\theta$ towards $u$; the lines $\ell_\theta=A+\mathbb Ru_\theta$ and $\ell'_\theta=A+\mathbb Rv_\theta$ are reflections of each other in the bisector of angle $BAC$, with $\ell_0=AB$ and $\ell'_0=AC$. With $Y=A+su_\theta$ and $T=A+tv_\theta$, condition $(\star)$ (for $Y,T\ne B,C$) reads \[F_1=(Y-B)\cdot(T-B)=0,\qquad F_2=(Y-C)\cdot(T-C)=0 .\] For $\theta=0$ take $Y_0=AB\cap CD$ and $T_0=AC\cap BD$. They exist: $CD\perp AC$ and $AB\not\perp AC$, so $AB\nparallel CD$; similarly for $AC$ and $BD$. Since a line meets $\Omega$ in at most two points, $Y_0\notin\{A,B\}$ and $Y_0\notin\Omega$; likewise $T_0\notin\{A,C\}$ and $T_0\notin\Omega$. They solve the system: $Y_0-B$ is along $AB$ and $T_0-B$ along $BD\perp AB$; $T_0-C$ is along $AC$ and $Y_0-C$ along $CD\perp AC$. Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and $s_0,t_0$ the parameters of $Y_0,T_0$ (at $\theta=0$).

Reduction to one variable. Expanding, $F_1-F_2=(Y+T)\cdot(C-B)+|B|^2-|C|^2=(2A+su_\theta+tv_\theta)\cdot(C-B)+|B|^2-|C|^2$ is affine in $(s,t)$. The coefficient of $t$ is $v_\theta\cdot(C-B)$, which at $\theta=0$ equals $v\cdot(C-B)=b-c\cos A=a\cos C>0$; it depends continuously on $\theta$, so it is nonzero for small $|\theta|$, and $F_1=F_2$ is equivalent to $t=\tau(\theta,s)$, where $\tau$ is affine in $s$ with coefficients continuous in $\theta$; since $u\cdot(C-B)=b\cos A-c=-a\cos B$, we have $\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial s}(0,s)=\frac{\cos B}{\cos C}$. Then the system is equivalent to $q(\theta,s)=0$, $t=\tau(\theta,s)$, where $q(\theta,s)=F_1(\theta,s,\tau(\theta,s))$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2$ in $s$ with coefficients continuous in $\theta$.

A simple root. At $\theta=0$ we have $q(0,s_0)=0$ and \[\frac{\partial q}{\partial s}(0,s_0)=u\cdot(T_0-B)+\frac{\cos B}{\cos C}\,v\cdot(Y_0-B)=\frac{\cos B}{\cos C}\,v\cdot(Y_0-B)\ne0,\] because $T_0-B$ is along $BD\perp AB$, and $v\cdot(Y_0-B)=\pm BY_0\cos A\ne0$ ($Y_0\ne B$, and the triangle is acute). So $s_0$ is a simple root of the polynomial $q(0,\cdot)$, which therefore changes sign at $s_0$: for every small $\delta>0$ the numbers $q(0,s_0-\delta)$ and $q(0,s_0+\delta)$ are nonzero with opposite signs. By continuity in $\theta$ there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for $|\theta|<\varepsilon$ the numbers $q(\theta,s_0\pm\delta)$ still have opposite signs, and by the intermediate value theorem $q(\theta,\cdot)$ has a root $s(\theta)\in(s_0-\delta,s_0+\delta)$; put $t(\theta)=\tau(\theta,s(\theta))$. This solves the system, and as $\delta$ can be taken as small as we like (with $\varepsilon$ depending on $\delta$), $Y=A+s(\theta)u_\theta$ and $T=A+t(\theta)v_\theta$ are as close to $Y_0$ and $T_0$ as we wish. Choosing $\delta$ small enough, and then $\varepsilon$, we check the hypotheses of Step 3 for $0<|\theta|<\varepsilon$.

  • $Y$ and $T$ are close to $Y_0,T_0$, so they differ from $A,B,C$. The line $\ell_\theta$ meets $AB$ and $AC$ only at $A$, so $Y\notin AB\cup AC$; likewise $T\notin AB\cup AC$.

  • $Y_0$ is not on $BC$ or $DB$ (each meets $AB$ only at $B$) or on $\Omega$, and $T_0$ is not on $BC$ or $DC$ (each meets $AC$ only at $C$) or on $\Omega$; by continuity the same holds for $Y$ and $T$.

  • $Y\notin DC$: otherwise $CY=CD\perp CA$, and $CT\perp CY$ would give $T\in AC$. Likewise $T\notin DB$: otherwise $BT=BD\perp BA$ and $BY\perp BT$ would give $Y\in AB$.

  • No three of $X,Y,Z,T$ are collinear. In barycentrics $T=(\lambda:\mu:\nu)$ with $\mu\to0$ and $\lambda\nu\to$ a nonzero limit (as $T_0\in AC$, $T_0\ne A,C$), so $X=T^*=(a^2\mu\nu:b^2\lambda\nu:c^2\lambda\mu)\to B$; similarly $Z=Y^*\to C$. The limit points $B,Y_0,C,T_0$ have no three collinear ($B,Y_0$ lie on $AB$ and $C,T_0$ on $AC$, all different from $A$), and non-collinearity of a triple is preserved under small perturbations.

So for each $\theta$ with $0<|\theta|<\varepsilon$, Step 3 gives a quadrilateral $XYZT$, trivial with respect to both $ABC$ and $DBC$, satisfying both required conditions. Different $\theta$ give different lines $XY=\ell_\theta$, hence different quadrilaterals. So the antipode $D$ of $A$ has the required property.

Show the solution – Counting departures from each degree

Let $n=2026$. Each operation picks four students of the same degree $k$, adds four friendships, and raises each of their degrees by $2$; other degrees do not change. At the end every degree is $n-1=2025$.

Parity. Degrees change by $2$, so every initial degree $d_v$ is odd.

Departures. Fix an odd $k\le n-3$. A student whose initial degree is at most $k$ has degree exactly $k$ at some moment (degrees go up in steps of $2$ from an odd start to $n-1>k$) and leaves the value $k$ exactly once, in an operation performed on four students of degree $k$. Each such operation removes exactly four students from degree $k$. Hence \[N_k=\#\{v: d_v\le k\}\equiv0\pmod4\quad\text{for every odd }k\le n-3 .\] For $k=n-3$: $N_{n-3}=n-\#\{v: d_v=n-1\}$, and $n\equiv2\pmod4$, so the number of students with initial degree $n-1$ is $\equiv2\pmod4$, in particular at least $2$.

Lower bound. Let $U,V$ be two students who initially are friends with everybody. Every other student is a friend of $U$ and $V$, so has degree at least $2$, hence (odd) at least $3$. The number of friendships is at least \[\frac{2(n-1)+3(n-2)}{2}=\frac{2\cdot2025+3\cdot2024}{2}=5061 .\]

Construction. Let $U,V$ be friends with everyone (and with each other), and let the other $2024=4q$ students ($q=506$) be split into $2q=1012$ pairs of friends. That is $1+2\cdot2024+1012=5061$ friendships. The degrees of $U,V$ stay $2025$, and among the others, degree $=2+{}$(number of friends among the others); so it suffices to complete the graph on the $4q$ others.

Think of the $2q$ pairs as vertices $p_1,\dots,p_{2q}$ of an auxiliary graph, initially empty. If pairs $p=\{a,b\}$ and $p'=\{c,d\}$ have no friendships between them, and all four students have the same degree, the operation on the cycle $a,c,b,d$ adds exactly the four friendships between $p$ and $p'$; we record this as the edge $pp'$. A student in pair $p$ has degree $3+2\deg(p)$. The complete graph $K_{2q}$ is the union of $2q-1$ perfect matchings (a 1-factorisation, e.g. pairs with $i+j\equiv r\pmod{2q-1}$ together with the vertex $2q$). Add the matchings one after another: during round $r$ every pair-vertex not yet treated in this round has auxiliary degree $r-1$, so each matching edge joins two pairs whose four students all have degree $3+2(r-1)$, and the operation is allowed. After all rounds, all students are friends.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{5061}$.

Show solution 1 – Involution, then the error term h(x)=f(x)+x

Both functions work: for $f(x)=x$ each side is $xy+x+y$; for $f(x)=-x$ the left side is $f(-xy+y-x)=xy-y+x$ and the right side is $f(-xy)+x-y=xy+x-y$. Let $E(x,y)$ denote the equation.

Step 1: $f(0)=0$ and $f(f(x))=x$. Put $a=f(0)$. $E(x,0)$: $f(ax+f(x))=x+2a$, so $f$ is surjective. If $f(z)=0$, then $E(z,z)$ gives $f(z)=a+z$, so $z=-a$: the only zero of $f$ is $-a$. $E(x,-a)$: $f(f(x)-a)=f(-af(x))+x$, so $f(u)=f(v)$ implies $u=v$: $f$ is injective. $E(-a,y)$: $f(-af(y)+y)=f(0)-a+f(y)=f(y)$, so $-af(y)=0$ for all $y$; as $f$ is surjective, $a=0$. Then $E(x,0)$ reads $f(f(x))=x$.

Step 2: $b=f(1)=\pm1$. $E(x,1)$: $f(bx+1+f(x))=2x+b$; applying $f$, $f(2x+b)=bx+1+f(x)$. With $x=-b$: $f(-b)=-b^2+1+f(-b)$, so $b^2=1$.

Step 3: $b=1$. $E(x,1)$ gives $f(x+f(x)+1)=2x+1$, and $E(1,x)$ gives $f(f(x)+x+1)=f(x)+1+f(x)$. So $2x+1=2f(x)+1$ and $f(x)=x$.

Step 4: $b=-1$. Then $f(-1)=1$, and Step 2 gives \[f(2x-1)=f(x)-x+1. \tag{1}\] Let $h(x)=f(x)+x$. Then (1) says $h(2x-1)=h(x)$, and $f(f(x))=x$ gives $h(f(x))=h(x)$. From (1): $f(-1)=1$ gives $f(-3)=3$, so $f(3)=-3$; and $h(\frac12)=h(0)=0$, so $f(\frac12)=-\frac12$, $f(-\frac12)=\frac12$.

$h$ is even. $E(1,y)$: $f(f(y)+y-1)=f(-y)+1+f(y)$; applying $f$: $f\bigl(f(-y)+f(y)+1\bigr)=f(y)+y-1$. The left side does not change under $y\mapsto-y$, so $f(y)+y=f(-y)-y$, i.e. $h(y)=h(-y)$.

A master identity. Substituting $f=h-\mathrm{id}$ into $E(x,y)$ and simplifying gives, for all $x,y$, \[h\bigl(xf(y)+y+f(x)\bigr)=h\bigl(yf(x)\bigr)+(1+x)h(y)+(1-y)h(x). \tag{2}\] Write $s=h(y)$.

  • $x=1$ ($f(1)=-1$, $h(1)=0$): $h(s-1)=h(-y)+2s$, so $h(s-1)=3s$. (3)

  • $y=1$, then rename: $h(h(x)-2x+1)=h(f(x))=h(x)$. With $t=2x-1$, so $h(x)=h(t)$, and evenness: $h(t-h(t))=h(t)$, hence also $h(t+h(t))=h(t)$ (apply to $-t$). (4)

  • $x=-\frac12$ ($f(-\frac12)=\frac12$, $h(-\frac12)=0$): $h\bigl(\frac{3y-s+1}{2}\bigr)=h\bigl(\frac y2\bigr)+\frac s2$; by $h(u)=h(2u-1)$ this is $h(3y-s)=h(y-1)+\frac s2$. (5)

Fix $y$ and $s=h(y)$. By (3), $h(s-1)=3s$; by evenness $h(1-s)=3s$; by (4) at $t=1-s$, $h(1+2s)=3s$; and $h(1+2s)=h(2(s+1)-1)=h(s+1)$, so $h(s+1)=3s$. Apply (5) at the point $s+1$, whose $h$-value is $3s$: $h(3(s+1)-3s)=h(s)+\frac{3s}2$, i.e. $h(s)=h(3)-\frac32s=-\frac32s$. Now apply (4) in the form $h(t+h(t))=h(t)$ at $t=s$: $h\bigl(s-\frac32s\bigr)=-\frac32s$, so $h(\frac s2)=-\frac32s$ by evenness; but $h(\frac s2)=h(2\cdot\frac s2-1)=h(s-1)=3s$ by (3). Hence $3s=-\frac32s$, $s=0$. So $h\equiv0$ and $f(x)=-x$.

The solutions are $\boxed{f(x)=x}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=-x}$.

Show solution 2 – A dilation that fixes the error term

Step 1: $f(0)=0$ and $f(f(x))=x$. Step 2: $b=f(1)=\pm1$. $E(x,1)$ gives $f(bx+1+f(x))=2x+b$; applying $f$, \[f(2x+b)=f(x)+bx+1.\tag{1}\] At $x=-b$ this reads $f(-b)=f(-b)-b^2+1$, so $b^2=1$. Put $g(x)=f(x)-bx$. Then (1) says, using $b^2=1$, \[g(2x+b)=f(x)+bx+1-2bx-1=g(x).\tag{2}\]

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

Do there exist $2026$ infinite sets of integers $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_{2026}$ such that every integer $n$ can be written as a sum \[n=a_1+a_2+\dots+a_{2026},\qquad a_i\in A_i,\] in exactly:

  • (a) $1404$ ways?

  • (b) $n^{1404}+1$ ways?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Before deciding either part, ask whether every integer, positive or negative, could have a unique decomposition into contributions from different sets, and how such a decomposition could be altered. In part (b), ask whether counts could be fixed one integer at a time without disturbing counts already settled.
Hint 2: key idea
For part (a), think of a positional number system in which every integer has exactly one digit expansion with no sign, distribute its digit positions among the sets, and see how shifting one set changes the number of representations. For part (b), consider new tuples whose large entries cancel only when all of them are selected, so that sums from mixed tuples avoid the integers already treated. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
In part (a), prove uniqueness of the digits by taking parity and dividing by $-2$, then give one set only high digit positions so reduction modulo a power of two distinguishes its shift from its digit contribution. In part (b), enumerate the target integers and add one tuple at a time, choosing its scale so all mixed sums are fresh and have target counts at least as large as the number of new tuples. Check that a later step preserves the counts already fixed, and track which sets acquire a new element at each addition.
Common trap
Mixed old–new tuples can share a sum, so the allowance at such a sum must cover all new tuples that could land there.
Show the answer

(a) Yes; (b) Yes

Show solution 1 – Negabinary digits and a greedy construction

Base $-2$. Every integer has exactly one representation $\sum_{k\ge0}\varepsilon_k(-2)^k$ with digits $\varepsilon_k\in\{0,1\}$, finitely many nonzero. Indeed $\varepsilon_0\equiv n\pmod 2$ is forced, and then $\frac{n-\varepsilon_0}{-2}$ must be represented by the remaining digits; since $\bigl|\frac{n-\varepsilon_0}{-2}\bigr|<|n|$ for $|n|\ge2$, and $0=\emptyset$, $1=1$, $-1=1+(-2)$, induction on $|n|$ gives existence and uniqueness.

(a) Yes. Split the positions $k\ge0$ into $2026$ infinite classes: $P_1=\{2026m: m\ge1\}$, $P_2=\{k\equiv1\pmod{2026}\}\cup\{0\}$ and $P_i=\{k\equiv i-1\pmod{2026}\}$ for $3\le i\le2026$. Let $B_i$ be the set of numbers $\sum_{k\in P_i}\varepsilon_k(-2)^k$ (finite sums). Each $B_i$ is infinite, and by uniqueness of the base $-2$ representation, every integer is $b_1+\dots+b_{2026}$ ($b_i\in B_i$) in exactly one way. Every element of $B_1$ is a multiple of $2^{2026}$. Put \[A_1=\{b+j: b\in B_1,\ 0\le j\le1403\},\qquad A_i=B_i\ (i\ge2).\] The numbers $b+j$ are pairwise different (reduce modulo $2^{2026}>1403$), so each element of $A_1$ determines $b$ and $j$. Then $n=a_1+\dots+a_{2026}$ with $a_1=b+j$ means $b+a_2+\dots+a_{2026}=n-j$, which for each of the $1404$ values of $j$ has exactly one solution. So every $n$ has exactly $1404$ representations.

(b) Yes, by a greedy construction. Let $T(n)=n^{1404}+1\ge1$. Enumerate the integers as $0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots$ and build finite sets $A_1,\dots,A_{2026}$ step by step, keeping the following true after each step: integers already handled have exactly $T$ representations (among tuples from the current sets), and every other integer $m$ has at most $T(m)$.

To add one representation of a target $n$, choose a huge integer $L$ and add $y_i=L$ to $A_i$ for $i\le2025$ and $y_{2026}=n-2025L$ to $A_{2026}$. The new tuples are those using at least one new element. The tuple of all new elements sums to $n$. A tuple using the new elements exactly for $i\in S$ ($\emptyset\ne S\ne$ all) and old elements otherwise has sum $|S|L+(\text{old})$ if $2026\notin S$, and $n-(2026-|S|)L+(\text{old})$ if $2026\in S$; in both cases its absolute value is at least $L-|n|-\beta$, where $\beta$ bounds the absolute sum of old elements in any tuple. Choose $L$ so large that (i) these sums exceed in absolute value $|n|$, every handled integer and every sum created in earlier steps, and (ii) $T(m)\ge\kappa$ for all $|m|\ge L-|n|-\beta$, where $\kappa$ is the number of new tuples ($T$ grows to infinity, so this is possible). Then handled integers keep their counts, $n$ gains exactly one representation, and every other integer $m$ gains representations only in this step, at most $\kappa\le T(m)$.

When the enumeration reaches $n$, it has at most $T(n)$ representations; add the missing ones one at a time as above, then mark $n$ handled. Every step adds a new element to every set, so in the limit all $A_i$ are infinite, and every integer $n$ ends with exactly $n^{1404}+1$ representations (later steps never touch handled integers).

Show solution 2 – One representation at a time, for both parts

Let $k=2026$ and let $q(n)$ be the required number of representations: $q(n)=1404$ in (a), $q(n)=n^{1404}+1$ in (b). In both cases $q(n)\ge1$. For finite sets $A_1,\dots,A_k$ let $\rho(m)$ be the number of tuples $(a_1,\dots,a_k)\in A_1\times\dots\times A_k$ with sum $m$. We build finite sets step by step, enumerating the integers as $n_1,n_2,\ldots$ $(0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots)$, and keep the invariant

  • $\rho(m)\le q(m)$ for every integer $m$, and $\rho(n_i)=q(n_i)$ for every integer $n_i$ already handled.

Start with empty sets. To handle $n=n_i$, repeat the following addition while $\rho(n)<q(n)$.

An addition for the target $n$. For a large positive integer $T$ add $e_j=T^j$ to $A_j$ for $1\le j\le k-1$, and $e_k=n-(T+T^2+\dots+T^{k-1})$ to $A_k$. For large $T$ every $e_j$ is new in $A_j$. A new tuple uses the new element exactly in the positions of a nonempty set $S\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}$, and old elements elsewhere. For $S=\{1,\dots,k\}$ there is one tuple, with sum $n$. For a proper $S$ the sum is $p_S(T)+c$, where $c$ is a sum of old elements over the positions outside $S$ (finitely many possibilities) and \[p_S(T)=\sum_{j\in S,\,j<k}T^j+[k\in S]\Bigl(n-\sum_{j<k}T^j\Bigr).\] The coefficient of $T^j$ $(1\le j\le k-1)$ in $p_S$ is $[j\in S]-[k\in S]$. If $k\notin S$ these coefficients are the indicator of $S\ne\emptyset$; if $k\in S$ they are $-[j\notin S]$, not all $0$ because $S$ is proper. So each $p_S$ is a nonconstant polynomial, and the coefficients determine $S$ (nonnegative coefficients mean $k\notin S$), so different $S$ give different polynomials. Choose $T$ so large that

  • (i) the new sums for different proper $S$ are different from each other, from $n$ and from all old sums;

  • (ii) in (b): $q(s)\ge M$ for every new sum $s$ of a proper $S$, where $M=\prod_j(|A_j|+1)$ bounds the number of new tuples (possible because $|p_S(T)|\to\infty$).

The invariant survives. The old tuples are unchanged and the new tuples with sum $n$ are just the full one, so $\rho(n)$ grows by exactly $1$ and stays $\le q(n)$ (it was $<q(n)$). An integer $m\ne n$ with a new representation is, by (i), a sum for exactly one proper $S$ and had no representation before, and its new count is the number of old tuples in the positions outside $S$ with a fixed subtotal $\sigma$. In (a), fix an old element $a_j\in A_j$ for each $j\in S$ (the sets are nonempty after the first addition; at the first addition there are no tuples with proper $S$); adding them turns these tuples into distinct old representations of $\sigma+\sum_{j\in S}a_j$, so their number is at most $1404=q(m)$. In (b) their number is at most $M\le q(m)$ by (ii). All other counts are unchanged.

The construction works. Each addition raises $\rho(n_i)$ by $1$, so handling $n_i$ ends after finitely many additions, with $\rho(n_i)=q(n_i)$. Later additions never change $\rho(n_i)$: their targets differ from $n_i$, and by (i) their new sums avoid all old sums, among them $n_i$ (as $q(n_i)\ge1$). Let $A_j$ be the union of all stages. A representation of $m$ in the final sets uses finitely many elements, all present at some stage, so the final number of representations of each $n_i$ is $q(n_i)$. There are infinitely many additions (finite sets represent only finitely many integers, while every integer must be represented), and every addition puts a new element into each $A_j$; so every $A_j$ is infinite.

Hence the answer is Yes in both (a) and (b).

Problem 5

SAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Uniqueness by Degree Constraints

Find all positive real numbers $r$ such that the following statement is true:

There exists a non-constant polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that $|P(t)|<1$ for all $|t|\le r$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Think about which direction enlarging the interval makes the requirement harder. For an interval where small values are impossible, look for a lower bound that uses the fact that the leading coefficient is a nonzero integer.
Hint 2: key idea
For an obstruction, parametrize the interval by a substitution of the form $x=\lambda(z+z^{-1})$ with $|z|=1$ and a suitable $\lambda$, and use Parseval to extract the contribution of the extreme coefficients. For existence, expand in suitably scaled Chebyshev polynomials and use Minkowski's theorem on the set of monomial coefficient vectors whose Chebyshev coordinates are small. (LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation)
Hint 3: approach
Multiply the substituted polynomial by $z^{\deg P}$ to identify its constant and leading coefficients, then compare their contribution to the mean square with the desired bound. For the construction, use the leading coefficients of the scaled Chebyshev basis to compute the triangular change-of-basis determinant and choose a coefficient box whose inverse image has enough volume. Bound the maximum of the resulting integer polynomial by the sum of the Chebyshev coefficient bounds and study how that estimate changes with the degree.
Common trap
Minkowski's theorem gives a nonzero integer coefficient vector, so use the strict upper bound to rule out a nonzero constant polynomial.
Show the answer

$0<r<2$

Show solution 1 – Parseval for r at least 2, Minkowski for r below 2

$r\ge2$ fails. Let $P$ be nonconstant with integer coefficients, degree $n\ge1$ and leading coefficient $c\ne0$. For $|z|=1$, $t=z+\frac1z=2\cos\theta\in[-2,2]$, and $R(z)=z^nP\bigl(z+\frac1z\bigr)$ is a polynomial of degree $2n$ whose coefficients of $z^{2n}$ and $z^0$ both equal $c$. By Parseval, the mean of $|R(z)|^2$ over the unit circle is the sum of the squares of its coefficients, at least $2c^2\ge2$. So $|P(t)|=|R(z)|\ge\sqrt2>1$ for some $t\in[-2,2]\subseteq[-r,r]$.

$r<2$ works. Let $T_k$ be the Chebyshev polynomials ($T_k(\cos\theta)=\cos k\theta$, so $|T_k|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$; $T_0=1$ and $T_k$ has leading coefficient $2^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$). Every polynomial of degree at most $n$ can be written as $P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^nb_kT_k(x/r)$, and then $|P(t)|\le\sum|b_k|$ for $|t|\le r$. The map from the coefficient vector $(c_0,\dots,c_n)$ of $P$ (in the basis $1,x,\dots,x^n$) to $(b_0,\dots,b_n)$ is linear and triangular with diagonal entries $1$ and $\frac{r^k}{2^{k-1}}$ ($k\ge1$), so its determinant is $\Delta=\frac{r^{n(n+1)/2}}{2^{n(n-1)/2}}$.

Let $\varepsilon=\Delta^{1/(n+1)}$. The set of real vectors $(c_k)$ with all $|b_k|\le\varepsilon$ is a centrally symmetric convex body of volume $\frac{(2\varepsilon)^{n+1}}{\Delta}=2^{n+1}$, so by Minkowski's theorem it contains a nonzero integer vector: a nonzero integer polynomial $P$ with $\max_{[-r,r]}|P|\le(n+1)\varepsilon$. Here \[\varepsilon=\frac{r^{n/2}}{2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2(n+1)}}}=\frac{r^{n/2}}{2^{\frac n2-\frac{n}{n+1}}}\le2\Bigl(\frac r2\Bigr)^{n/2},\] so $\max|P|\le2(n+1)(r/2)^{n/2}\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ (because $r<2$). Choose $n$ with $2(n+1)(r/2)^{n/2}<1$. Then $|P(t)|<1$ on $[-r,r]$, and $P$ is not constant (a nonzero integer constant has absolute value at least $1$).

The answer is $\boxed{0<r<2}$.

Show solution 2 – Chebyshev alternation, then pigeonhole on a box of coefficients

Let $T_j$ be the Chebyshev polynomials, $T_j(\cos\theta)=\cos j\theta$. From $T_0=1$, $T_1=x$, $T_{j+1}=2xT_j-T_{j-1}$, the polynomial $T_j$ has degree $j$ and leading coefficient $2^{j-1}$ for $j\ge1$, and $|T_j|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$.

$r\ge2$ fails. Let $P$ have integer coefficients, degree $d\ge1$ and leading coefficient $a\ne0$, and suppose $|P(t)|<1$ for $|t|\le r$, in particular on $[-2,2]$. The polynomial $Q(x)=2T_d(x/2)$ is monic of degree $d$, so $R=P/a-Q$ has degree at most $d-1$. At the $d+1$ distinct points $x_j=2\cos\frac{j\pi}d$ $(j=0,\dots,d)$ of $[-2,2]$ we have $Q(x_j)=2\cos j\pi=2(-1)^j$ and $|P(x_j)/a|<1$ (as $|a|\ge1$), so $R(x_j)$ is nonzero with the sign of $-(-1)^j$. Hence $R$ changes sign between consecutive points $x_j$ and has at least $d$ zeros, while it is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $d-1$: a contradiction.

Every $r<2$ works. Let $C_0=1$ and $C_j(x)=2(r/2)^jT_j(x/r)$ for $j\ge1$. Then $C_j$ is monic of degree $j$ and $|C_j(t)|\le B_j$ for $|t|\le r$, where $B_0=1$ and $B_j=2(r/2)^j$. Fix $d$ and consider the box $K$ of real vectors $(u_0,\dots,u_d)$ with $|u_j|<\frac1{4(d+1)B_j}$. The map sending $(u_j)$ to the coefficient vector of $\sum_ju_jC_j$ (in the basis $1,x,\dots,x^d$) is linear and triangular with $1$'s on the diagonal (the $C_j$ are monic), so it preserves volume, and the image $L$ of $K$ has volume \[\prod_{j=0}^d\frac{1}{2(d+1)B_j}=\frac{1}{\bigl(2(d+1)\bigr)^{d+1}\,2^d}\Bigl(\frac2r\Bigr)^{d(d+1)/2},\] which exceeds $1$ for large $d$, because $\frac{d(d+1)}2\ln\frac2r$ grows faster than $(d+1)\ln\bigl(2(d+1)\bigr)+d\ln2$. Fix such a $d$. Cut $L$ along the integer grid into the pieces $L\cap(m+[0,1)^{d+1})$, $m\in\mathbb Z^{d+1}$, and translate each piece by $-m$ into the unit cube. The total volume exceeds $1$, so two pieces overlap: there are distinct $c,c'\in L$ with $c-c'=m-m'\in\mathbb Z^{d+1}$.

Let $P$ be the polynomial with coefficient vector $c-c'$: it has integer coefficients and is not zero. If $c,c'$ come from $(u_j),(u'_j)\in K$, then $P=\sum_j(u_j-u'_j)C_j$ with $|u_j-u'_j|<\frac1{2(d+1)B_j}$, so for $|t|\le r$ \[|P(t)|\le\sum_{j=0}^d|u_j-u'_j|\,B_j<\frac{d+1}{2(d+1)}=\frac12 .\] A nonzero integer constant has absolute value at least $1$, so $P$ is not constant.

The answer is $\boxed{0<r<2}$.
