Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

An integer $N$ is called ofemic if it can be written as $N=2(m^2+n^2)$ and also as $N=(m+n)^2+1$, for the same integers $m$, $n$. Which are the two ofemic integers closest to $2026$?

Show the answer

$2026$ and $1850$

Show the solution – The ofemic numbers are k squared plus 1 with k odd

Since $2(m^2+n^2)=(m+n)^2+(m-n)^2$, the two expressions are equal exactly when $(m-n)^2=1$, i.e. $m-n=\pm1$.

So $N$ is ofemic if and only if $N=(m+n)^2+1$ with $m-n=\pm1$. Then $m+n=2n\pm1$ is odd. Conversely, for every odd integer $k$ the integers $m=\frac{k+1}2$, $n=\frac{k-1}2$ satisfy $m-n=1$ and $m+n=k$. Hence \[\text{the ofemic integers are exactly the numbers } k^2+1 \text{ with } k \text{ odd.}\] Since $44^2<2025<46^2$, the odd squares near $2025$ are $43^2=1849$, $45^2=2025$, $47^2=2209$. Thus $2026=45^2+1$ is itself ofemic (take $m=23$, $n=22$: $2(23^2+22^2)=2026=45^2+1$), at distance $0$ from $2026$. The next ofemic integers below and above are \[43^2+1=1850\quad(\text{distance }176)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 47^2+1=2210\quad(\text{distance }184),\] and all other ofemic integers are farther away. Hence the two ofemic integers closest to $2026$ are \[\boxed{2026\text{ and }1850}.\] (If $2026$ itself is not counted, the nearest ofemic integers on either side of it are $1850$ and $2210$.)

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · GCD and LCM Structure

Determine all integers $d\ge1$ with the following property: there exist positive integers $a\ne b$ such that $\gcd(a,b)=d$ and $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{a+b}$ is an integer.

Show the answer

all $d\ge2$

Show solution 1 – Reduce to coprime numbers

Write $a=dx$, $b=dy$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$, $x\neq y$. Then \[\frac{a^2+b^2}{a+b}=d\cdot\frac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}.\] Since $x\equiv-y\pmod{x+y}$, we have $x^2+y^2\equiv2y^2\pmod{x+y}$, and $\gcd(x+y,y)=\gcd(x,y)=1$; hence $\gcd(x+y,\,x^2+y^2)=\gcd(x+y,\,2)$. Writing $s=x+y$ and $g=\gcd(s,2)$, the fraction is an integer if and only if $\dfrac sg$ divides $d$.

$d=1$ fails. Then $s/g=1$, so $s\le2$, which forces $x=y=1$; but $x\ne y$.

Every $d\ge2$ works. Take $x=1$, $y=2d-1$ (coprime, and distinct as $d\ge2$). Then $s=2d$ is even, $s/g=d$ divides $d$. Explicitly, $a=d$, $b=d(2d-1)$ have $\gcd(a,b)=d$ and \[\frac{a^2+b^2}{a+b}=\frac{d^2\bigl(1+(2d-1)^2\bigr)}{2d^2}=2d^2-2d+1 .\] The answer is: $\boxed{\text{all } d\ge2}$.

Show solution 2 – Coprime pairs directly

$d=1$ is impossible. Suppose $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $a+b\mid a^2+b^2$. Since $a^2+b^2=(a+b)^2-2ab$, we get $a+b\mid 2ab$. Any prime $q$ dividing both $a+b$ and $ab$ divides $a$ or $b$, and then also the other one (as it divides $a+b$), contradicting $\gcd(a,b)=1$. So $\gcd(a+b,ab)=1$ and $a+b\mid2$, i.e. $a=b=1$, which is excluded.

$d\ge2$ is possible. Look for $a=d$, $b=dk$ with $k\ge2$, so $\gcd(a,b)=d$. Then \[\frac{a^2+b^2}{a+b}=\frac{d(1+k^2)}{1+k},\qquad 1+k^2=(1+k)(k-1)+2,\] so we need $1+k\mid 2d$. The choice $k=2d-1$ gives $1+k=2d$, which works, and $k\ge3>1$. For example $d=2$: $a=2$, $b=6$, $\frac{4+36}{8}=5$.

Hence the integers with the property are exactly $\boxed{d\ge2}$.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Homothety Centers and Image Tracking

Let $ABC$ be a scalene triangle and let $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$ be the two circles which pass through $A$ and are tangent to the line $BC$ and to the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $ABC$. The line $BC$ is tangent to $\omega_1$ at $D_1$ and to $\omega_2$ at $D_2$. Lines $AD_1$ and $AD_2$ meet $\Gamma$ again at $P_1$ and $P_2$, respectively. Prove that $P_1P_2$ is perpendicular to $BC$.

Show solution 1 – Homothety at A

Let $M$ and $M'$ be the midpoints of the arcs $BC$ of $\Gamma$ not containing and containing $A$. Both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, which passes through the center of $\Gamma$; so $MM'$ is a diameter perpendicular to $BC$, and the tangents to $\Gamma$ at $M$ and $M'$ are the two tangents parallel to $BC$. As the triangle is scalene, $AB\neq AC$, so $A\neq M'$ (and $A\ne M$).

Tangency happens at $A$. A circle through $A$ tangent to $\Gamma$ has only one common point with $\Gamma$, so it touches $\Gamma$ at $A$. Hence there is a homothety $h$ with center $A$ that maps $\Gamma$ onto $\omega$ (ratio positive for internal tangency, negative for external tangency).

The image of the tangency point. Let $\omega$ be one of the two circles, tangent to $BC$ at $D$, and let $h$ be as above. The preimage $h^{-1}(BC)$ is a line parallel to $BC$ and tangent to $\Gamma$ (because $BC$ is tangent to $\omega=h(\Gamma)$), touching $\Gamma$ at the point $P$ with $h(P)=D$. So $P\in\{M,M'\}$, and since a homothety with center $A$ keeps $A$, $P$, $h(P)$ collinear, $P$ is the second intersection of $AD$ with $\Gamma$.

Two different circles give $M$ and $M'$. Conversely, for $P=M$ and for $P=M'$ (neither tangent line passes through $A$) there is exactly one homothety with center $A$ mapping the tangent at $P$ onto $BC$, and it maps $\Gamma$ to a circle through $A$ tangent to $\Gamma$ and to $BC$. These give the two circles $\omega_1,\omega_2$; each circle determines its point $P$ as shown above, and different circles come from different points. Hence $\{P_1,P_2\}=\{M,M'\}$, and $P_1P_2=MM'$ is perpendicular to $BC$.

Show solution 2 – Equal tangents and angle bisectors

We use directed angles between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$ and written $\measuredangle$.

Let $\omega$ be one of the circles, tangent to $BC$ at $D$, and let $P$ be the second intersection of $AD$ with $\Gamma$. A circle through $A$ tangent to $\Gamma$ meets $\Gamma$ only at $A$, so $\omega$ and $\Gamma$ are tangent at $A$ and have a common tangent $t$ there. Since the triangle is scalene, $A$ is not a midpoint of an arc $BC$, so $t$ is not parallel to $BC$; let $T=t\cap BC$.

From $T$ the two tangents to $\omega$ are $TA$ and $TD$, so $TA=TD$ and the base angles of the isosceles triangle $TAD$ are equal: $\measuredangle(t,AD)=\measuredangle(AD,BC)$. By the tangent-chord angle in $\Gamma$, $\measuredangle(t,AB)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)$. Hence \[\measuredangle(t,AD)=\measuredangle(t,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,AD)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)+\measuredangle(AB,AD),\] \[\measuredangle(AD,BC)=\measuredangle(AD,AC)+\measuredangle(AC,BC).\] Comparing, $\measuredangle(AB,AD)=\measuredangle(AD,AC)$: the line $AD$ makes equal directed angles with $AB$ and $AC$, i.e. it is the internal or the external bisector of angle $BAC$. Therefore $P$ is the midpoint of one of the two arcs $BC$.

The two circles give different lines: if $AD_1$ and $AD_2$ were the same bisector, then $D_1=D_2$, and both circles would be tangent to $t$ at $A$ and to $BC$ at the same point $D_1$; a circle tangent to two lines at two given points is unique (its center is the intersection of the two perpendiculars), so $\omega_1=\omega_2$, which is false. Hence $AD_1$ and $AD_2$ are the two bisectors, and $P_1$, $P_2$ are the two arc midpoints of $BC$. Both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, so $P_1P_2\perp BC$.

Show solution 3 – Inversion at A

Use coordinates with $A$ at the origin and $BC$ the horizontal line $y=h$, where $h>0$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $1$, $\iota(z)=z/|z|^2$; write $Z'=\iota(Z)$. Recall that $\iota$ maps circles through $A$ to lines not through $A$ and conversely, and that every line through $A$ is mapped onto itself.

The images. A point $(x,y)\ne A$ goes to a point of the line $y=h$ exactly when $\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}=h$, i.e. $x^2+y^2=\frac yh$. So the image of line $BC$ is the circle $\kappa$ with centre $K=(0,R)$ and radius $R=\frac1{2h}$ (with $A$ removed). The image of $\Gamma$ is the line $\ell=B'C'$, which does not pass through $A$. Each $\omega_i$ passes through $A$ and meets $\Gamma$ only at $A$ (two different circles tangent at $A$ have no other common point), so its image is a line with no common point with $\ell$, that is, a line parallel to $\ell$; it is tangent to $\kappa$ at $D_i'$, because $\omega_i$ is tangent to $BC$ at $D_i$.

Write $\ell$ as $\nu\cdot z=k$ with a unit normal vector $\nu=(\alpha,\beta)$ and $k\ne0$ (as $A\notin\ell$). The lines with normal $\nu$ tangent to $\kappa$ touch it at \[U_+=K+R\nu,\qquad U_-=K-R\nu .\] The line $\ell$ is not horizontal. Otherwise $B'$ and $C'$ would have the same height; but the height of $B'=B/AB^2$ is $h/AB^2$ and that of $C'$ is $h/AC^2$, and $AB\ne AC$ because the triangle is scalene. Hence $\beta\ne\pm1$ and $U_\pm\ne A$ (a tangent to $\kappa$ at $A$ would be horizontal). So both tangent lines avoid $A$; they are not $\ell$ (which cuts $\kappa$ at $B'$ and $C'$); and their images under $\iota$ are circles through $A$, tangent to $BC$, meeting $\Gamma$ only at $A$, that is, the two circles $\omega_1,\omega_2$. Thus $\{D_1',D_2'\}=\{U_+,U_-\}$.

The points $P_i$. The line $AD_i$ is mapped onto itself, so $P_i'$ is the intersection of line $AU_\pm$ with $\ell$. We have \[\nu\cdot U_\pm=\nu\cdot K\pm R=R(\beta\pm1)\ne0,\qquad |U_\pm|^2=|K|^2\pm2R\,\nu\cdot K+R^2=2R^2(1\pm\beta).\] The point $tU_\pm$ lies on $\ell$ when $t\,\nu\cdot U_\pm=k$, so \[P'=\frac{k}{\nu\cdot U_\pm}\,U_\pm,\qquad P=\frac{P'}{|P'|^2}=\frac{\nu\cdot U_\pm}{k\,|U_\pm|^2}\,U_\pm=\frac{R(\beta\pm1)}{2kR^2(1\pm\beta)}\,U_\pm=\pm\frac{U_\pm}{2kR}.\] Hence, for the two points, \[P_+-P_-=\frac{U_++U_-}{2kR}=\frac{2K}{2kR}=\frac{1}{k}(0,1),\] a nonzero vertical vector. So the line $P_1P_2$ is vertical, that is, $P_1P_2\perp BC$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

Let $n\ge3$ be an integer. Ana plays by moving $n$ stones between the vertices of a regular $n$-gon. Starting from a certain initial distribution of the $n$ stones on the $n$ vertices, Ana makes a sequence of moves of the following type: choose a vertex with two or more stones and “shoot” it, that is, move one stone to each of its two adjacent vertices. The game ends if Ana manages to have each vertex of the polygon end up with one stone, in which case we say that Ana has won.

  • (a) Prove that for all $n\ge3$ there exists an initial distribution of the $n$ stones on the $n$ vertices from which Ana cannot win.

  • (b) Show that, if Ana can win from the initial distribution, then for every winning sequence there exists some vertex that has never been shot in that sequence.

Show the solution – A weighted sum mod n, and the vertex that stops first

Number the vertices $0,1,\dots,n-1$ around the polygon (indices modulo $n$) and let $s_i$ be the number of stones at vertex $i$.

(a) Consider $I=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\,s_i \pmod n$. Shooting vertex $i$ changes $I$ by $(i-1)+(i+1)-2i\equiv0\pmod n$ (also when $i=0$ or $i=n-1$, since indices are taken mod $n$). So $I$ is invariant. In the winning position $I\equiv0+1+\cdots+(n-1)=\frac{n(n-1)}2=:W\pmod n$.

The distribution with all $n$ stones on vertex $0$ has $I\equiv0$, and the distribution with $n-1$ stones on vertex $0$ and one stone on vertex $1$ has $I\equiv1$. Since $0\not\equiv1\pmod n$, at least one of them has $I\not\equiv W$, and from that distribution Ana can never win (it is not the winning position either, since its invariant differs).

(b) Suppose, for contradiction, that in some winning sequence every vertex is shot at least once. For each vertex consider the moment of its last shot, and let $v$ be the vertex whose last shot is the earliest of these $n$ moments. Its two neighbours are different vertices (as $n\ge3$), and each of them is shot after that moment, because their last shots come later. Each such shot puts a stone on $v$, while $v$ itself is never shot again, so $v$ never loses a stone again. Right after its last shot $v$ has at least $0$ stones, so at the end it has at least $2$ stones. This contradicts the final position with one stone on every vertex. Hence some vertex is never shot.
