Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Divisibility Chains and Factor Relations

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let \[1=d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_m=n\] be all positive divisors of $n$. Suppose $m\ge4$, and for any $i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}$, there exists $k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}$ such that $d_i,d_j,d_k$ can be arranged in some order to form an arithmetic progression. Find all possible values of $m$.

Show the answer

$m=4$

Show the solution

Solution (The pair $d_1$, $d_{m-1}$)

$m=4$ occurs. For $n=6$ the divisors are $1,2,3,6$, and every pair from $\{1,2,3\}$ completes to the progression $1,2,3$ (for $i=j$ take $k=i$).

No other $m$. Let $p=d_2$ be the smallest prime factor of $n$ and $x=d_{m-1}=n/p$; since $m\ge4$, $x>1$. Apply the condition to $d_1=1$ and $d_{m-1}=x$. A third term $z$ with $1,x,z$ in arithmetic progression in some order is $2x-1$, $2-x$ or $\frac{x+1}2$. Now $2-x<1$ is not a divisor, and $2x-1$ would be a divisor with $x<2x-1<2x\le n$, so it would have to be $n=px$; then $x(p-2)=-1$, impossible. Hence \[y=\frac{x+1}2\ \text{ is a divisor of } n=px .\] As $\gcd(y,x)$ divides $2y-x=1$, we get $y\mid p$, so $y=1$ or $y=p$. $y=1$ means $x=1$, excluded; so $x=2p-1$ and $n=p(2p-1)$. Every prime factor of $2p-1$ is at least $p$ (as $p$ is the smallest prime factor of $n$), and $2p-1<p^2$, so $2p-1$ is a prime $q\ne p$. Then $n=pq$ has exactly $4$ divisors.

Hence $\boxed{m=4}$.

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Coordinate Optimization of Synthetic Figures

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB<AC$, and $AD\perp BC$ with $D$ on $BC$. Let $O$ and $H$ be the circumcenter and the orthocenter of $\triangle ABC$, and let $N$ be the midpoint of $OH$. A point $P$ on segment $BD$ satisfies $\angle PND=2\angle PAD$. Prove that $BC$ is tangent to the circle $(AOP)$.

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates)

Put $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(-b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a,b,c>0$; $AB<AC$ means $b<c$. Let $P=(-t,0)$ with $0<t\le b$. Here $P\ne D$: for $P=D$ both angles are $0^\circ$, so the condition holds trivially, but the claim can fail (for $a=2$, $b=1$, $c=2$ the only circle through $A$ tangent to $BC$ at $D$ has centre $(0,1)$ and radius $1$, while $O=(\frac12,\frac12)$ is at distance $\frac{\sqrt2}2$ from that centre); so the problem concerns $P\ne D$.

The centres. The orthocentre is $H=(0,\frac{bc}a)$ (indeed $\overrightarrow{BH}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}=bc-a\cdot\frac{bc}a=0$). The circumcentre lies on $x=\frac{c-b}2$ and is equidistant from $A$ and $B$, which gives $O=\bigl(\frac{c-b}2,\kappa\bigr)$ with $\kappa=\frac{a^2-bc}{2a}$. Hence \[N=(\nu,\eta),\qquad \nu=\frac{c-b}4,\quad \eta=\frac{a^2+bc}{4a}>0 .\]

The angle condition. Let $\theta=\angle PND$ and $\psi=\angle PAD$, so $\tan\psi=\frac ta$ and $\sin2\psi=\frac{2at}{a^2+t^2}$, $\cos2\psi=\frac{a^2-t^2}{a^2+t^2}$. With $\overrightarrow{ND}=(-\nu,-\eta)$ and $\overrightarrow{NP}=(-t-\nu,-\eta)$, the cross product has absolute value $\eta t$ and the dot product is $\nu(\nu+t)+\eta^2$; so $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ are these numbers divided by $ND\cdot NP$. Since $\theta,2\psi\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$, $\theta=2\psi$ is equivalent to $\sin\theta\cos2\psi-\cos\theta\sin2\psi=0$, i.e. (dividing by $t>0$) to \[E_1:=\eta(a^2-t^2)-2a\bigl(\nu^2+\nu t+\eta^2\bigr)=0 .\]

The tangency condition. The circle through $A$ tangent to $BC$ at $P$ has centre $(-t,r)$ with $t^2+(a-r)^2=r^2$, i.e. $r=\frac{a^2+t^2}{2a}$. It passes through $O$ iff \[E_2:=\Bigl(\frac{c-b}2+t\Bigr)^2+\kappa^2-\frac{\kappa(a^2+t^2)}a=0 .\] Since $A,O,P$ determine one circle, $BC$ touches $(AOP)$ (necessarily at $P$) exactly when $E_2=0$.

Comparison. Expanding both expressions, \[4a^2E_2=-8aE_1=2(a^2+bc)t^2+4a^2(c-b)t+a^2(c-b)^2+b^2c^2-a^4 .\] Hence $E_1=0$ implies $E_2=0$: the circle $(AOP)$ is tangent to $BC$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

Let $n>100$ be an integer, and let real numbers $a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{n-1}$ satisfy

  • (1) $0\le a_{n-1}\le\cdots\le a_1\le a_0\le2$;

  • (2) for all $0\le i\le j\le k\le n-1$ with $i+j+k\ge n$, \[a_ia_j+a_ja_k+a_ka_i\le a_i+a_j+a_k .\]

Prove that $a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_{n-1}\le n-1$ and $a_0+a_2+\cdots+a_{n-1}\le n$.

Show the solution

Solution (Pairing $j$ with $n-j$)

Lemma. If $0\le j\le k\le n-1$ and $2j+k\ge n$, then $a_j+a_k\le2$; in particular ($j=k$, $3k\ge n$) $a_k\le1$.

Proof. Apply (2) to $(i,j,k)=(j,j,k)$, which is allowed since $j\le j\le k$ and $j+j+k\ge n$. With $u=a_j\ge v=a_k\ge0$ it reads $u^2+2uv\le2u+v$, i.e. \[u(u+v-2)+v(u-1)\le0 .\] If $u+v>2$, then $u>1$ (as $u\ge v$), so both terms on the left are $\ge0$ and the first is $>0$: a contradiction. Hence $u+v\le2$. $\square$

First inequality. For $1\le j\le\frac{n-1}2$ the pair $(j,n-j)$ satisfies $j<n-j\le n-1$ and $2j+(n-j)=n+j\ge n$, so $a_j+a_{n-j}\le2$. These pairs partition $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ when $n$ is odd; when $n$ is even, the index $\frac n2$ is left over and $a_{n/2}\le1$ because $3\cdot\frac n2\ge n$. In both cases \[a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_{n-1}\le2\cdot\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-1}2\Bigr\rfloor+\bigl(1\text{ if }n\text{ even}\bigr)=n-1 .\]

Second inequality. The indices $2,\dots,n-2$ are covered in the same way by the pairs $(j,n-j)$, $2\le j\le\frac{n-1}2$, and (for even $n$) the single index $\frac n2$, so $a_2+\cdots+a_{n-2}\le n-3$. Moreover $a_0\le2$ and $a_{n-1}\le1$ (Lemma with $j=k=n-1$). Adding, $a_0+a_2+\cdots+a_{n-1}\le n-3+2+1=n$.

Problem 4

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Weighted Counting

Given an integer $n\ge2$. In a $4\times n$ grid, a set of four cells forming an S-shape (an S- or Z-tetromino, in any of its orientations) is called an S-shape. A nonnegative real number is written in each cell. It is known that the sum of the numbers in the four cells of every S-shape is at least $1$. Find the minimum possible value of the sum of all numbers in the grid.

Show the answer

$n-1$ for odd $n$; $\dfrac{n^2}{n+1}$ for even $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Disjoint shapes for odd $n$, a weighted family for even $n$)

Number the rows $1,2,3,4$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,n$. A horizontal S-shape occupies two adjacent rows, with two adjacent cells in each; a vertical one occupies two adjacent columns, with two vertically adjacent cells in each.

Odd $n$: lower bound $n-1$. In rows $1,2$ take the shapes with cells $(1,c),(1,c+1),(2,c+1),(2,c+2)$ for $c=1,3,\dots,n-2$; consecutive ones use different cells, so these $\frac{n-1}2$ shapes are disjoint. Do the same in rows $3,4$. The $n-1$ disjoint shapes each have sum at least $1$, and all numbers are nonnegative, so the total is at least $n-1$.

Odd $n$: the value $n-1$ is attained. Write $1$ in the cells of rows $2$ and $4$ lying in the even columns $2,4,\dots,n-1$, and $0$ elsewhere; the total is $2\cdot\frac{n-1}2=n-1$. Any two adjacent columns include an even column, and any two adjacent rows include row $2$ or row $4$. A horizontal shape has, in each of its two rows, two adjacent cells, one of them in an even column; one of its rows is $2$ or $4$; so it contains a $1$. A vertical shape has in each of its two columns two adjacent cells (rows $r,r+1$ in one column and $r+1,r+2$ in the other); in the even column among the two, the two rows include $2$ or $4$. So every shape has sum at least $1$, and the minimum for odd $n$ is $\boxed{n-1}$.

Even $n=2m$: lower bound $\frac{n^2}{n+1}$. Let $T$ be the sum of all numbers, and write $(r,c)$ for the cell in row $r$ and column $c$. Rows $2,3$ are inner rows and rows $1,4$ outer rows. Group the columns into $m$ blocks $\{2i-1,2i\}$, $i=1,\dots,m$, and the rows into the top strip (outer row $1$, inner row $2$) and the bottom strip (outer row $4$, inner row $3$). Make the following list $\mathcal L$ of shapes, repetitions allowed.

  • (a) For every block, the four vertical shapes inside its two columns, once each: $\{(1,2i-1),(2,2i-1),(2,2i),(3,2i)\}$, $\{(1,2i),(2,2i),(2,2i-1),(3,2i-1)\}$ and their mirror images in rows $2$–$4$, namely $\{(4,2i-1),(3,2i-1),(3,2i),(2,2i)\}$ and $\{(4,2i),(3,2i),(3,2i-1),(2,2i-1)\}$.

  • (b) For every block $i$ and each strip, take the two inner cells of the block in that strip, $(\rho,2i-1),(\rho,2i)$ with $\rho$ the inner row, and add the outer cells in columns $2i,2i+1$ (a shape “shifted right”, taken $n-2i$ times), or the outer cells in columns $2i-2,2i-1$ (a shape “shifted left”, taken $2i-2$ times). The right shift is needed only for $i<m$ and the left shift only for $i>1$, since the other multiplicity is $0$.

All of these are S- or Z-tetrominoes. Their number is \[|\mathcal L|=4m+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl((n-2i)+(2i-2)\bigr)=4m+2m(n-2)=n^2 .\] Each cell lies in at most $n+1$ shapes of $\mathcal L$. An inner cell of block $i$, say $(2,2i-1)$, lies in three shapes of (a) (all but the one that misses it), and in (b) only in the shapes of block $i$ in its strip, $(n-2i)+(2i-2)=n-2$ of them: $n+1$ in all. An outer cell lies in exactly one shape of (a). The outer cell in column $2i-1$ (with $i\ge2$) also lies in the left shifts of block $i$ ($2i-2$ shapes) and the right shifts of block $i-1$ ($n-2i+2$ shapes): $n+1$ in all; for $i=1$ it lies in one shape only. The outer cell in column $2i$ (with $i<m$) lies in the right shifts of block $i$ ($n-2i$) and the left shifts of block $i+1$ ($2i$): again $n+1$; for $i=m$ it lies in one shape only.

Now add the conditions “sum $\ge1$” over all shapes of $\mathcal L$. The right side is $|\mathcal L|=n^2$; on the left each number is counted at most $n+1$ times and is nonnegative, so the left side is at most $(n+1)T$. Hence \[T\ge\frac{n^2}{n+1}.\]

Even $n=2m$: the value $\frac{n^2}{n+1}$ is attained. Let $\varepsilon=\frac1{n+1}$ and, for $i=1,\dots,m$, let $u_{2i-1}=i-1$ and $u_{2i}=m-i$. In column $c$ write $u_c\varepsilon$ in rows $1$ and $4$ and $(u_c+1)\varepsilon$ in rows $2$ and $3$ (for $n=6$ see the right grid). Then \[u_{2i-1}+u_{2i}=m-1,\qquad u_{2i}+u_{2i+1}=m,\qquad\text{so}\quad u_c+u_{c+1}\ge m-1,\quad u_c+2u_{c+1}+u_{c+2}=2m-1\] for all admissible $c$. We check every shape, measuring sums in units of $\varepsilon$; the target is $n+1=2m+1$.

  • A vertical shape lies in columns $c,c+1$, with two cells in each, three cells in inner rows and one in an outer row (rows $1,2,2,3$ or $2,3,3,4$). Its sum is $2(u_c+u_{c+1})+3\ge2(m-1)+3=2m+1$.

  • A horizontal shape in the top or bottom strip has cells in columns $c,c+1$ of one row and $c+1,c+2$ of the other, two of them inner. Its sum is $u_c+2u_{c+1}+u_{c+2}+2=2m+1$.

  • A horizontal shape in rows $2,3$ has all four cells inner; its sum is $u_c+2u_{c+1}+u_{c+2}+4=2m+3$.

So every shape has sum at least $1$, and the total is \[\varepsilon\sum_{c=1}^{n}\bigl(4u_c+2\bigr)=\varepsilon\bigl(4m(m-1)+4m\bigr)=\frac{4m^2}{n+1}=\frac{n^2}{n+1}.\]

Answer. The minimum is $\boxed{n-1}$ for odd $n$ and $\boxed{\dfrac{n^2}{n+1}}$ for even $n$.

Remark (S-shapes only). If only one of the two mirror forms counts, say the shapes $\{(r,c+1),(r,c+2),(r+1,c),(r+1,c+1)\}$ and $\{(r,c),(r+1,c),(r+1,c+1),(r+2,c+1)\}$, the answer is still $n-1$ for odd $n$ (in the disjoint packing use these shapes in rows $1,2$ and $3,4$ starting at columns $1,3,\dots,n-2$; the construction above works for all shapes), but it becomes $\frac{n^2}{n+2}$ for even $n=2m$. The same method proves this. Lower bound: take each of the two vertical shapes inside every block twice, and in each of the strips rows $1,2$ and rows $3,4$ take the horizontal shape starting at column $c$ ($1\le c\le n-2$) $n-1-c$ times for odd $c$ and $c$ times for even $c$; this is a list of $4m+2(m-1)n=n^2$ shapes covering each cell at most $n+2$ times. Construction, in units of $\frac1{n+2}$, rows $1$ to $4$: column $2i-1$ gets $2i-2,\ 2,\ 2i-2,\ 0$ and column $2i$ gets $0,\ n-2i,\ 2,\ n-2i$; the total is $4m^2$ units.
