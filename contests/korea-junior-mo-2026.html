Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 36: Power of a Point and Circle Measurement · Secant-Tangent Power

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle, and let $P$ and $Q$ be two points on side $BC$ such that $BP=QC$. (Assume that $B,P,Q,C$ are four distinct points lying on line $BC$ in this order.)

The circumcircle of triangle $ABP$ intersects line $AC$ again at $D$ ($D\neq A$), and the circumcircle of triangle $ACQ$ intersects line $AB$ again at $E$ ($E\neq A$).

Let the two circumcircles intersect again at $R$ ($R\neq A$). Let line $AR$ intersect line $BC$ at $S$, and let lines $PD$ and $EQ$ intersect at $T$.

Prove that $TS\perp BC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Radical axis and two mirror-image lines)

$S$ is the midpoint of $PQ$ (and of $BC$). Line $AR$ is the radical axis of the two circles, so $S$ has equal powers with respect to them: $SB\cdot SP=SQ\cdot SC$ (signed lengths along $BC$). Put coordinates on line $BC$ with $B=0$, $P=p$, $Q=c-p$, $C=c$ (this uses $BP=QC$) and $S=s$. Then \[s(s-p)=(s-c+p)(s-c)\iff s^2-ps=s^2-(2c-p)s+c(c-p)\iff 2(c-p)s=c(c-p),\] and $c\ne p$, so $s=\frac c2$. Thus $S$ is the midpoint of $BC$, and also of $PQ$.

The lines $PD$ and $QE$ are mirror images. We use directed angles: $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ is the angle through which line $\ell$ must be turned counterclockwise to become parallel to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. With them the inscribed angle theorem reads “$W,X,Y,Z$ concyclic $\Rightarrow\measuredangle(YW,YX)=\measuredangle(ZW,ZX)$” in every position of the points, so we need not worry whether $D$ and $E$ lie on the sides or on their extensions. Since $A,B,P,D$ are concyclic, $\measuredangle(PD,BC)=\measuredangle(PD,PB)=\measuredangle(AD,AB)=\measuredangle(AC,AB)$. Since $A,C,Q,E$ are concyclic, $\measuredangle(QE,BC)=\measuredangle(QE,QC)=\measuredangle(AE,AC)=\measuredangle(AB,AC)$. So the lines $PD$ and $QE$ make opposite directed angles with $BC$. They are not parallel: that would need $2\measuredangle(AB,AC)=0^\circ$, i.e. $\angle A=90^\circ$, but the triangle is acute. Let $\ell$ be the line through their intersection $T$ perpendicular to $BC$. The reflection in $\ell$ maps $BC$ to itself and maps line $PD$ to the line through $T$ making the opposite angle with $BC$, which is line $QE$. Hence it maps $P=PD\cap BC$ to $QE\cap BC=Q$. So $\ell$ is the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, which passes through its midpoint $S$. Therefore $TS=\ell\perp BC$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates: opposite slopes)

Choose coordinates with $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $P=(-t,0)$, $Q=(t,0)$ where $0<t<1$ (possible because $BP=QC$), and $A=(a,h)$ with $h>0$. Since $\angle A<90^\circ$, $A$ lies outside the circle with diameter $BC$, so $a^2+h^2>1$.

The point $S$. The circle through $A,B,P$ has an equation $x^2+y^2+(1+t)x+e_1y+t=0$ (on the $x$-axis its roots are $-1$ and $-t$), and the circle through $A,C,Q$ has an equation $x^2+y^2-(1+t)x+e_2y+t=0$. Subtracting, their radical axis $AR$ is $2(1+t)x+(e_1-e_2)y=0$, which passes through the origin. So $S=(0,0)$.

The points $D$ and $E$. The power of $C$ with respect to the circle $ABP$ gives $CD\cdot CA=CP\cdot CB=2(1+t)$, with $D$ on ray $CA$, so \[D=C+\frac{2(1+t)}{(a-1)^2+h^2}(A-C)=\Big(\frac{a^2+2at+h^2-2t-1}{(a-1)^2+h^2},\ \frac{2h(1+t)}{(a-1)^2+h^2}\Big).\] Similarly $BE\cdot BA=BQ\cdot BC=2(1+t)$ gives \[E=\Big(\frac{-a^2+2at-h^2+2t+1}{(a+1)^2+h^2},\ \frac{2h(1+t)}{(a+1)^2+h^2}\Big).\]

Slopes. For line $PD$: $D_x+t=\frac{(1+t)(a^2+h^2-1)}{(a-1)^2+h^2}$, so its slope is $\frac{2h}{a^2+h^2-1}$. For line $QE$: $E_x-t=-\frac{(1+t)(a^2+h^2-1)}{(a+1)^2+h^2}$, so its slope is $-\frac{2h}{a^2+h^2-1}$. The two lines through $P=(-t,0)$ and $Q=(t,0)$ have opposite slopes, so they are symmetric about the $y$-axis and meet on it: $T=\big(0,\frac{2ht}{a^2+h^2-1}\big)$. Since $S$ is the origin, $TS$ is the $y$-axis, which is perpendicular to $BC$.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Invariants

There are $2000$ equally spaced points on a circle. Paint $1000$ consecutive points black and the remaining $1000$ consecutive points white. In one move, choose three distinct points among the $2000$ points that form a right triangle, and reverse the colors of all three chosen points (that is, change black to white and white to black). We repeat this operation in order to make all $2000$ points the same color. Find the minimum number of moves required.

Show the answer

$1000$

Show the solution

Solution (Diametric pairs)

Number the points $0,1,\dots,1999$ around the circle, with $0,\dots,999$ black. A triangle inscribed in a circle is right-angled exactly when one of its sides is a diameter; three points cannot contain two diameters. So a move chooses a diametric pair $\{i,i+1000\}$ and one more point $j$ outside that pair.

Call a diametric pair mixed if its two points have different colours. Initially all $1000$ pairs $\{i,i+1000\}$ ($0\le i\le999$) are mixed, and at the end none is. A move flips both points of one pair (which does not change whether that pair is mixed) and one point $j$ of another pair (which changes whether that pair is mixed). So each move changes the status of exactly one pair. Every pair must change its status at least once, hence at least $1000$ moves are needed.

$1000$ moves suffice, for example to make everything white. Do the $500$ moves $\{0,1000,j\}$ for $j=500,\dots,999$, then the $500$ moves $\{500,1500,j\}$ for $j=0,\dots,499$. Each of the points $0,\dots,999$ other than $0$ and $500$ is flipped exactly once. The point $0$ is flipped $500$ times as a diameter end and once as a third vertex, $501$ times in all; the same holds for $500$. The points $1000$ and $1500$ are flipped $500$ times, and the other white points are never flipped. So every black point is flipped an odd number of times and every white point an even number of times, and all points end white. The minimum is $\boxed{1000}$.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking · Strategic Residues

Find all ordered triples $(x,y,z)$ of nonnegative integers satisfying \[|6^x+10^y-15^z|=1.\] (Here, $a^0=1$ for every nonzero integer $a$.)

Show the answer

$(x,y,z)=(0,0,0),\ (1,1,1),\ (3,1,2)$

Show the solution

Solution (Parity, modulo 3, and powers of 2, 27 and 125)

Small cases. If $x=y=0$, then $|2-15^z|=1$ forces $15^z\in\{1,3\}$, so $z=0$: this gives $(0,0,0)$. If exactly one of $x,y$ is $0$, then $6^x+10^y$ is odd, $15^z$ is odd, and their difference is even, so it cannot be $\pm1$.

So let $x,y\ge1$. Then $z\ge1$ (for $z=0$ the left side is at least $15$). Modulo $3$, $6^x+10^y-15^z\equiv0+1-0=1$, so the expression equals $+1$: \[6^x+10^y=15^z+1.\tag{1}\] For $z=1$ we get $6^x+10^y=16$, so $(x,y)=(1,1)$. From now on $z\ge2$, and we compare the powers of $2$ on both sides of (1).

Right side. If $z$ is even, $15^z\equiv1\pmod 4$, so $15^z+1$ is $2$ times an odd number. If $z$ is odd, $15^z+1=16\,(15^{z-1}-15^{z-2}+\cdots+1)$, and the bracket is a sum of $z$ odd terms, hence odd; so $15^z+1$ is $16$ times an odd number.

Left side. If $x\ne y$, the exact power of $2$ dividing $2^x3^x+2^y5^y$ is $2^{\min(x,y)}$. If $x=y$, the left side is $2^x(3^x+5^x)$; for odd $x$, $3^x+5^x=8\,(3^{x-1}-3^{x-2}5+\cdots+5^{x-1})$ with an odd bracket, and for even $x$, $3^x+5^x\equiv1+1=2\pmod 4$. So the exact power of $2$ is $2^{x+3}$ or $2^{x+1}$.

Case $z$ even. The exponent of $2$ is $1$, so $x\ne y$ and $\min(x,y)=1$ (the case $x=y$ gives an exponent at least $3$).

  • $x=1$, $y\ge2$: (1) becomes $10^y+5=15^z$, i.e. $2^y5^{y-1}+1=3^z5^{z-1}$. The left side is $\equiv1\pmod5$ and the right side is divisible by $5$. Impossible.

  • $y=1$, $x\ge2$: (1) becomes $6^x+9=15^z$. For $x=2$ we get $45$, not a power of $15$. For $x\ge3$, $27\mid6^x$, so $6^x+9$ is divisible by $9$ but not by $27$, whereas $27\mid15^z$ for $z\ge3$. Hence $z=2$, $6^x=216$, $x=3$: this gives $(3,1,2)$.

Case $z$ odd, $z\ge3$. The exponent of $2$ is $4$. If $x=y$ is odd, $x+3=4$ gives $x=y=1$ and then (1) reads $16=15^z+1$, so $z=1$, excluded. If $x=y$ is even, $x+1=4$ is impossible. So $x\ne y$ and $\min(x,y)=4$; in particular $x,y\ge4$, and one of them equals $4$.

  • If $y=4$: reduce (1) modulo $27$. Since $x\ge4$ and $z\ge3$, both $6^x$ and $15^z$ are divisible by $3^3=27$, so $10^4\equiv1\pmod{27}$. But $10^4=10000=370\cdot27+10\equiv10\pmod{27}$. Contradiction.

  • If $x=4$: reduce (1) modulo $125$. Since $y\ge4$ and $z\ge3$, both $10^y$ and $15^z$ are divisible by $5^3=125$, so $6^4\equiv1\pmod{125}$. But $6^4=1296=10\cdot125+46\equiv46\pmod{125}$. Contradiction.

The solutions are $\boxed{(0,0,0),\ (1,1,1),\ (3,1,2)}$: indeed $1+1-1=1$, $6+10-15=1$ and $216+10-225=1$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 38: Inequalities: Convexity and Functional Methods · Tangent Line and Supporting Bounds

Prove that for all positive real numbers $a,b,c,d$, \[27\left(\frac{a^3}{(a+b)^2}+\frac{b^3}{(b+c)^2}+\frac{c^3}{(c+d)^2}\right)+16d\ge 20a+4b+4c.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A tangent-line bound)

Lemma. For all positive $u,v$, \[\frac{27u^3}{(u+v)^2}\ge 20u-16v .\] If $20u-16v\le0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise multiply by $(u+v)^2>0$; we need $27u^3-(20u-16v)(u+v)^2\ge0$. Expanding, $(20u-16v)(u^2+2uv+v^2)=20u^3+24u^2v-12uv^2-16v^3$, so the left side is \[7u^3-24u^2v+12uv^2+16v^3=(u-2v)^2(7u+4v)\ge0 .\] (The bound is the tangent line of the convex function $t\mapsto\frac{27}{(1+t)^2}$ at $t=\frac12$, multiplied by $u$; equality holds for $u=2v$.)

Apply the lemma to $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$, $(c,d)$ and add: \[27\Big(\frac{a^3}{(a+b)^2}+\frac{b^3}{(b+c)^2}+\frac{c^3}{(c+d)^2}\Big)\ge(20a-16b)+(20b-16c)+(20c-16d)=20a+4b+4c-16d,\] which is the claim. Equality holds when $a=2b=4c=8d$.

Solution 2 (AM-GM with three terms)

For positive $u,v$, apply AM-GM to the three numbers $\frac{u^3}{(u+v)^2}$, $\frac{8}{27}(u+v)$, $\frac{8}{27}(u+v)$: \[\frac{u^3}{(u+v)^2}+\frac{16}{27}(u+v)\ge3\sqrt[3]{\frac{u^3}{(u+v)^2}\cdot\frac{64}{729}(u+v)^2}=3\cdot\frac{4u}{9}=\frac{4u}{3}.\] Hence $\frac{u^3}{(u+v)^2}\ge\frac{4u}{3}-\frac{16(u+v)}{27}=\frac{20u-16v}{27}$, with equality when $\frac{u^3}{(u+v)^2}=\frac{8}{27}(u+v)$, i.e. $u=2v$. (The weight $\frac{8}{27}$ is chosen so that equality is possible at $u=2v$.)

Multiplying by $27$ and adding the three instances for $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$, $(c,d)$, the terms telescope: \[27\sum\frac{a^3}{(a+b)^2}\ge20a-16b+20b-16c+20c-16d=20a+4b+4c-16d .\] Adding $16d$ to both sides gives the inequality, with equality exactly for $a:b:c:d=8:4:2:1$.

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Recursive Definitions

Let $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ be a sequence of real numbers satisfying the following two conditions: \[a_1=a_2=1,\] and \[a_{n-1}a_{n+1}-a_n^2=1\] for every integer $n\ge 2$. Prove that \[a_{n-2}a_{n+2}-a_n^2=9\] for every integer $n\ge 3$.

Show the solution

Solution (A hidden linear recurrence)

All terms are positive. $a_1=a_2=1$, and if $a_{n-1},a_n>0$ then $a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n^2+1}{a_{n-1}}>0$. By induction every $a_n>0$.

A linear recurrence. For $n\ge2$ subtract the relations $a_{n-1}a_{n+1}-a_n^2=1$ and $a_na_{n+2}-a_{n+1}^2=1$: \[a_{n+1}(a_{n-1}+a_{n+1})=a_n(a_n+a_{n+2}),\qquad\text{so}\qquad\frac{a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{a_n+a_{n+2}}{a_{n+1}} .\] So the ratio $\frac{a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}}{a_n}$ does not depend on $n\ge2$. With $a_3=\frac{1+1}{1}=2$, it equals $\frac{a_1+a_3}{a_2}=3$. Hence \[a_{n+1}=3a_n-a_{n-1}\qquad(n\ge2).\]

The identity. Let $n\ge3$. Using $a_{n-2}=3a_{n-1}-a_n$, $a_{n+2}=3a_{n+1}-a_n$, then $a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}=3a_n$ and $a_{n-1}a_{n+1}=a_n^2+1$: \[a_{n-2}a_{n+2}=9a_{n-1}a_{n+1}-3a_n(a_{n-1}+a_{n+1})+a_n^2=9(a_n^2+1)-9a_n^2+a_n^2=a_n^2+9 .\] So $a_{n-2}a_{n+2}-a_n^2=9$ for every $n\ge3$.

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 43: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Mobius-Flavored Cancellation

Let $\mathbb{N}$ denote the set of positive integers. Find all functions \[f:\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}\] satisfying the following two conditions: \[f(1)=1,\] and for every integer $n\ge 2$, if $d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_k$ are all the positive divisors of $n$, then \[f(d_1)+f(d_2)+\cdots+f(d_k)\] is even.

Show the answer

$f(n)=1$ if $n$ is squarefree and $f(n)=0$ otherwise

Show the solution

Solution (Uniqueness by recursion, existence by counting squarefree divisors)

At most one such function. For $n\ge2$ the condition says \[f(n)\equiv\sum_{d\mid n,\ d<n}f(d)\pmod 2,\] and $f(n)\in\{0,1\}$ is fixed by its parity. So $f(1)=1$ and the values on smaller numbers determine $f(n)$; by strong induction there is at most one function.

The squarefree indicator works. Let $f(n)=1$ if $n$ is squarefree (no square of a prime divides $n$; $1$ counts as squarefree) and $f(n)=0$ otherwise. Then $f(1)=1$. For $n\ge2$, write $n=p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_k^{e_k}$ with $k\ge1$. The squarefree divisors of $n$ are the products of distinct primes chosen from $p_1,\dots,p_k$, so there are $2^k$ of them, and \[\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)=2^k,\] which is even. Hence the unique function is $\boxed{f(n)=1 \text{ for squarefree } n,\ f(n)=0 \text{ otherwise}}$.

Problem 7

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Equation-Based Figure Analysis

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle, and let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$.

Let $P$ be a point on segment $AD$, where $P\neq A,D$. Let $E$ and $F$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $P$ to lines $CA$ and $AB$, respectively.

Let $S$ be the circumcenter of triangle $DEF$, and let $T$ be the circumcenter of triangle $PBC$.

Prove that the three points $A,S,T$ are collinear.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates with the altitude as an axis)

Put $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(-b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a,b,c>0$, and $P=(0,p)$ with $0<p<a$.

The feet $E$ and $F$. The foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $CA$ is \[E=\Big(\frac{ac(a-p)}{a^2+c^2},\ \frac{a(ap+c^2)}{a^2+c^2}\Big),\] (one checks that $E$ lies on $\frac xc+\frac ya=1$ and that $E-P$ is perpendicular to $C-A=(c,-a)$). Replacing $c$ by $-b$ gives $F=\Big(\frac{-ab(a-p)}{a^2+b^2},\ \frac{a(ap+b^2)}{a^2+b^2}\Big)$. A short computation gives \[|E|^2=\frac{a^2\big(c^2(a-p)^2+(ap+c^2)^2\big)}{(a^2+c^2)^2}=\frac{a^2(c^2+p^2)}{a^2+c^2},\qquad |F|^2=\frac{a^2(b^2+p^2)}{a^2+b^2}.\]

The circumcenter $S$ of $DEF$. The circle through $D=(0,0)$ has an equation $x^2+y^2+ux+vy=0$ and center $\big(-\frac u2,-\frac v2\big)$. Substituting $E$ and $F$ and multiplying by $\frac{a^2+c^2}{a}$, resp. $\frac{a^2+b^2}{a}$: \[c(a-p)\,u+(ap+c^2)\,v=-a(c^2+p^2),\qquad -b(a-p)\,u+(ap+b^2)\,v=-a(b^2+p^2).\] Multiply the first equation by $b$, the second by $c$ and add; the $u$-terms cancel and \[(b+c)(ap+bc)\,v=-a(b+c)(bc+p^2),\qquad v=-\frac{a(bc+p^2)}{ap+bc}.\] Putting this back into the first equation, $c(a-p)u=\frac{a}{ap+bc}\big[(ap+c^2)(bc+p^2)-(c^2+p^2)(ap+bc)\big]=\frac{a\,cp(a-p)(b-c)}{ap+bc}$, so $u=\frac{ap(b-c)}{ap+bc}$. Therefore \[S=\Big(\frac{ap(c-b)}{2(ap+bc)},\ \frac{a(bc+p^2)}{2(ap+bc)}\Big).\]

The circumcenter $T$ of $PBC$. It lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=\frac{c-b}{2}$ of $BC$, and $TB=TP$ gives $(x+b)^2+y^2=x^2+(y-p)^2$, i.e. $y=\frac{p^2-b^2-2bx}{2p}$. Hence \[T=\Big(\frac{c-b}{2},\ \frac{p^2-bc}{2p}\Big).\]

Collinearity. Let $k=\frac{ap}{ap+bc}$. Then \[S-A=\Big(\frac{ap(c-b)}{2(ap+bc)},\ \frac{a(p^2-2ap-bc)}{2(ap+bc)}\Big)=k\Big(\frac{c-b}{2},\ \frac{p^2-bc-2ap}{2p}\Big)=k\,(T-A).\] So $S$ lies on line $AT$, i.e. $A$, $S$, $T$ are collinear. (In fact $S$ divides $AT$ in the ratio $AS:ST=ap:bc$.)

Solution 2 (A homothety centred at $A$)

We use directed angles: $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ is the angle through which line $\ell$ must be turned counterclockwise to become parallel to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. With them, “$W,X,Y,Z$ concyclic” gives $\measuredangle(YW,YX)=\measuredangle(ZW,ZX)$ in every position of the points, and conversely.

The point $P'$ and the homothety. Since the triangle is acute, $D$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$, so $D$ is inside the circle $PBC$. Hence line $AD$ meets this circle at $P$ and at a second point $P'$ on the other side of $BC$. Let $h$ be the homothety (enlargement) with center $A$ and ratio $\frac{AD}{AP'}<1$; it maps $P'$ to $D$. Let $B_1=h(B)$ and $C_1=h(C)$: they lie on the segments $AB$ and $AC$, and $DB_1\parallel P'B$, $DC_1\parallel P'C$.

Claim: $B_1$ and $C_1$ lie on the circle $DEF$. If $B_1=F$ there is nothing to prove, so let $B_1\ne F$. Then line $B_1F$ is line $AB$, and $B_1D\parallel BP'$, so \[\measuredangle(B_1D,B_1F)=\measuredangle(BP',BA)=\measuredangle(BP',BC)+\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(PP',PC)+\measuredangle(BC,BA),\] using that $B,C,P,P'$ are concyclic. Here line $PP'$ is line $AD$. On the other hand, $\angle PDC=\angle PEC=90^\circ$, so $C,D,P,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $PC$; and $\angle AEP=\angle AFP=90^\circ$, so $A,E,P,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $AP$. Hence \[\measuredangle(ED,EF)=\measuredangle(ED,EP)+\measuredangle(EP,EF)=\measuredangle(CD,CP)+\measuredangle(AP,AF)=\measuredangle(BC,PC)+\measuredangle(AD,AB).\] Subtracting, \[\begin{aligned}\measuredangle(B_1D,B_1F)-\measuredangle(ED,EF)&=\big[\measuredangle(AD,PC)+\measuredangle(PC,BC)\big]+\big[\measuredangle(BC,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,AD)\big]\\&=\measuredangle(AD,BC)+\measuredangle(BC,AD)=0^\circ .\end{aligned}\] So $\measuredangle(B_1D,B_1F)=\measuredangle(ED,EF)$, and $B_1$ lies on the circle through $D,E,F$. In the same way $C_1$ lies on it.

Conclusion. The homothety $h$ maps the circle through $P',B,C$, which is the circle $PBC$ with center $T$, to the circle through $h(P')=D$, $B_1$ and $C_1$. These are three distinct points of the circle $DEF$ ($B_1$ and $C_1$ lie on different sides of the triangle and are not $A$; $D$ is on neither $AB$ nor $AC$), so the image circle is the circle $DEF$. A homothety maps the center of a circle to the center of its image, so $S=h(T)$. Since $h$ is centred at $A$, the points $A$, $T$ and $h(T)=S$ are collinear.

Solution 3 (Inversion at $A$)

Put $k=AD\cdot AP>0$. For a point $X\ne A$ let $X^*$ be the point on ray $AX$ with $AX\cdot AX^*=k$ (inversion with center $A$).

Three pairs of points. Since $P$ lies on ray $AD$ and $AD\cdot AP=k$, we have $D^*=P$. In the right triangle $APE$ (right angle at $E$) we get $AE=AP\cos\angle PAC$, and in the right triangle $ADC$ (right angle at $D$) we get $AD=AC\cos\angle DAC$. The angles $\angle PAC$ and $\angle DAC$ are equal, because $P$ lies on segment $AD$. Hence \[AE\cdot AC=AP\cdot AC\cos\angle PAC=AP\cdot AD=k .\] This angle is acute, so $E$ lies on ray $AC$, and therefore $E^*=C$. In the same way $AF\cdot AB=k$ and $F^*=B$.

$A$ is not on the circle $DEF$. The right angles at $E$ and $F$ put $A,E,F,P$ on the circle with diameter $AP$. If $A$ were on circle $DEF$, then circle $DEF$ would pass through $A,E,F$, so it would be this circle with diameter $AP$; but $D$ lies on line $AP$ beyond $P$, and a line meets a circle in at most two points ($A$ and $P$). So $A$ is not on circle $DEF$.

The image of a circle. Take $A$ as the origin and write points as vectors. The circle $DEF$ has an equation $|X|^2-2\,S\cdot X+c=0$, where $S$ is its center and $c\ne0$ because the origin $A$ is not on it. For $X$ on this circle, $X^*=\frac{k}{|X|^2}X$, so $X=\frac{k}{|X^*|^2}X^*$ and $|X|^2=\frac{k^2}{|X^*|^2}$. Substituting and multiplying by $\frac{|X^*|^2}{c}$: \[|X^*|^2-2\cdot\frac kc\,S\cdot X^*+\frac{k^2}c=0 .\] So all the points $X^*$ lie on one circle with center $\frac kc\,S$. This circle contains $D^*=P$, $E^*=C$ and $F^*=B$, which are not collinear; hence it is the circle $PBC$, and its center is $T=\frac kc\,S$.

Thus the vector $T$ is a multiple of the vector $S$ (with $A$ as origin), which means exactly that $A$, $S$, $T$ are collinear.

Problem 8

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Strategic State Tracking

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer. Initially, $n$ copies of the number $1$ are written on a blackboard. Seoa and Ihyeon play a game, with Seoa going first, taking turns according to the following rules:

On her turn, Seoa chooses two numbers $x$ and $y$ currently written on the blackboard such that $x+y$ leaves a remainder of $2$ when divided by $4$. She erases these two numbers and writes their sum $x+y$ on the blackboard. On his turn, Ihyeon chooses two numbers $x$ and $y$ currently written on the blackboard such that $x+y$ does not leave a remainder of $2$ when divided by $4$. He erases these two numbers and writes their sum $x+y$ on the blackboard. A player loses if it is their turn and either only one number remains on the blackboard or they cannot choose two numbers satisfying their rule. Assuming that both players use optimal strategies, find all values of $n$ for which Seoa wins.

Show the answer

$n\equiv 2\pmod 4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Residues modulo 4 and the number of numbers)

Only residues modulo $4$ matter. A merge keeps the total equal to $n$ and lowers the count of numbers by $1$. Seoa may merge residues $\{1,1\}$, $\{3,3\}$ or $\{0,2\}$; Ihyeon may merge any pair whose residues are not one of these. Before the $k$-th move the board has $n-k+1$ numbers; Seoa makes the odd-numbered moves.

$n$ odd: Ihyeon wins. After each of Seoa's moves an odd number of moves has been made, so the board has an even number of numbers, at least $2$. One of them is the number she has just written, which is $\equiv2$, hence even. Since the total $n$ is odd, some number is odd. Ihyeon merges an odd number with an even one; the sum is odd, so the move is legal. Thus Ihyeon always has a move, and as the game lasts at most $n-1$ moves, Seoa loses.

$n\equiv0\pmod4$: Ihyeon wins. Ihyeon keeps the following property at Seoa's turns: either all numbers are $\equiv1$ and their count is $\equiv0\pmod4$ (type I), or all are $\equiv1$ except one $\equiv3$ and the count is $\equiv2\pmod4$ (type II). At the start the board is of type I. In both types the only legal move of Seoa is to merge two numbers $\equiv1$ (a single $\equiv3$ cannot be paired, and $1+3\equiv0$). On a type II board with $2$ numbers, i.e. $\{1,3\}$, she has no move and loses. Otherwise:

  • from type I (count $c\ge4$), after her move there are numbers $\equiv1$ and one $\equiv2$; Ihyeon merges the $2$ with a $1$ to get a $3$ (legal, $3\not\equiv2$). The board is of type II with count $c-2$;

  • from type II (count $c\ge6$), after her move there are numbers $\equiv1$, one $\equiv3$ and one $\equiv2$; Ihyeon merges the $2$ and the $3$ to get $5\equiv1$ (legal). The board is of type I with count $c-2$.

At Ihyeon's turns the count is odd and at least $3$, so he is never stuck. The game is finite, so Seoa eventually loses.

$n\equiv2\pmod4$: Seoa wins, whatever she plays. At each of her turns an even number of moves has been made, so the count is even; in particular it is never $1$. Suppose she has no legal move. Writing $c_r$ for the number of numbers $\equiv r$, this means $c_1\le1$, $c_3\le1$, and $c_0=0$ or $c_2=0$. The total is $\equiv c_1+3c_3+2c_2\equiv2\pmod4$ (numbers $\equiv0$ contribute nothing). If $c_2=0$, then $c_1+3c_3\in\{0,1,3,4\}$ is never $\equiv2$. So $c_2\ge1$, hence $c_0=0$; then $c_1+3c_3$ must be even, so $(c_1,c_3)$ is $(0,0)$ or $(1,1)$, and $2c_2\equiv2\pmod4$, so $c_2$ is odd. The count $c_2+c_1+c_3$ is then odd, a contradiction. So Seoa always has a legal move and never faces a single number. The game ends after at most $n-1$ moves, so Ihyeon loses.

Hence Seoa wins exactly when $\boxed{n\equiv2\pmod4}$.

Solution 2 (Who can get stuck)

Only remainders modulo $4$ matter. Every merge keeps the total equal to $n$ and lowers the count of numbers by $1$; the game ends after at most $n-1$ moves, so somebody loses. Seoa makes the $1$st, $3$rd, $5$th, $\dots$ moves.

Ihyeon can get stuck only if $n\equiv2\pmod4$. Look at a turn of Ihyeon. Seoa has just written a number $x\equiv2\pmod4$.

  • If at least three numbers are on the board: if some other number $y$ is not $\equiv0$, then $x+y$ is odd or $\equiv0$, so merging $x$ and $y$ is legal; otherwise there are two other numbers $\equiv0$, and their sum is $\equiv0$, also legal. So Ihyeon has a move.

  • If exactly two numbers are left, their sum is $n$; he is stuck exactly when $n\equiv2\pmod4$.

  • If one number is left, it is the sum $n$ that Seoa just wrote, so $n\equiv2\pmod4$.

Hence if $n\not\equiv2\pmod4$, Ihyeon is never stuck, whatever both players do, and Seoa loses.

If $n\equiv2\pmod4$, Seoa is never stuck. At each of her turns an even number of moves has been made, so the count of numbers has the same parity as $n$: it is even, hence at least $2$. Suppose she has no legal move. Then there is at most one number $\equiv1$ (two of them would sum to $2$), at most one $\equiv3$ (as $3+3\equiv2$), and the remainders $0$ and $2$ do not both occur (as $0+2\equiv2$). The total $n$ is even, so the number of odd numbers is even: either there are none, or there is one $\equiv1$ and one $\equiv3$; in both cases the odd numbers add up to a multiple of $4$. Then the number of even numbers is even too, and they are all $\equiv0$ or all $\equiv2$; an even number of numbers $\equiv2$ also adds up to a multiple of $4$. So $n\equiv0\pmod4$, a contradiction. Hence Seoa always has a move, whatever she played before, and Ihyeon loses.

Therefore Seoa wins exactly when $\boxed{n\equiv2\pmod4}$.
