Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

Find the largest possible value of the ratio of a four-digit number to the sum of its digits.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare two four-digit numbers with the same digit sum but with a nonzero digit in different positions. Which positions contribute more to the number itself?
Hint 2: key idea
Expand the number by place value and compare it with a constant multiple of its digit sum. Look for a multiplier large enough that this multiple is never smaller than the number, whatever the digits are. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Subtract the actual place-value expansion from that common-weight sum and check the signs of the coefficients. Look for digit choices that make the difference vanish.
Common trap
The leading digit must stay nonzero when you look for equality.
Show the answer

$1000$

Show solution 1 – Compare digit by digit

Write the number as $N=1000a+100b+10c+d$ with digits $a\ge1$ and $b,c,d\ge0$. Then \[1000(a+b+c+d)-N=900b+990c+999d\ge0,\] so $\dfrac{N}{a+b+c+d}\le 1000$, with equality exactly when $b=c=d=0$. For example $1000/1=1000$. The largest value of the ratio is $\boxed{1000}$, attained by $1000,2000,\dots,9000$.

Show solution 2 – Fix the leading digit

Let $N$ be a four-digit number with leading digit $a$ and digit sum $s$.

  • If the other three digits are all $0$, then $N=1000a$, $s=a$, and $N/s=1000$.

  • Otherwise $s\ge a+1$, while $N\le 1000a+999<1000(a+1)\le 1000s$, so $N/s<1000$.

Hence the largest value of the ratio is $\boxed{1000}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

Let $a$, $b$ and $c$ be three positive real numbers such that $a+b+c=1$. Prove that \[\frac{1}{a+2b+3c}+\frac{1}{b+2c+3a}+\frac{1}{c+2a+3b}\ge\frac32.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the coefficient of $a$ in each denominator. Compare that list with the coefficients of $b$ and of $c$.
Hint 2: key idea
Name the three denominators $u,v,w$. Their sum is fixed, and Cauchy–Schwarz can link $\frac1u+\frac1v+\frac1w$ to $u+v+w$. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Add the denominators and collect terms using $a+b+c=1$. Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the square roots of those denominators and their reciprocals, keeping the two resulting sums as separate factors.
Common trap
Keep the denominators positive when using their square roots and reciprocals.
Show solution 1 – Sum of the denominators

Let $x=a+2b+3c$, $y=b+2c+3a$, $z=c+2a+3b$; they are positive and $x+y+z=6(a+b+c)=6$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (or AM–HM), \[\Bigl(\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z\Bigr)(x+y+z)\ge 9,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z\ge\frac96=\frac32 .\] Equality holds when $x=y=z=2$, i.e. $a=b=c=\frac13$.

Show solution 2 – A tangent line bound

For every $t>0$, \[\frac1t-\Bigl(\frac12-\frac{t-2}{4}\Bigr)=\frac{(t-2)^2}{4t}\ge0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\frac1t\ge\frac12-\frac{t-2}4 .\] Apply this to $t=a+2b+3c$, $b+2c+3a$, $c+2a+3b$ and add. The three numbers sum to $6(a+b+c)=6$, so the sum of the $t-2$ is $0$, and the left side is at least $3\cdot\frac12=\frac32$. Equality needs every $t=2$, i.e. $a=b=c=\frac13$.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

A four-digit number $X$, with no digit $9$, is a perfect square. If each digit is increased by $1$, another perfect square is obtained. Find all such four-digit perfect squares.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Apply the digit change to a sample four-digit number with no $9$. Compare the original and new numbers by their place values.
Hint 2: key idea
With no carries, the digit change adds the same integer to every eligible number. Write the original and new numbers as squares and factor their difference. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Find the common increment and list its positive factor pairs. Treat the larger factor as the sum of the square roots and the smaller one as their difference to reconstruct the candidates. Check the original square's digit conditions before keeping a candidate.
Common trap
A factor pair must give a four-digit original square with no digit $9$.
Show the answer

$2025$

Show the solution – A difference of squares

Since no digit of $X$ is $9$, increasing every digit by $1$ causes no carries, so the new number is $X+1111$. Write $X=n^2$ and $X+1111=m^2$ with $m>n>0$. Then \[(m-n)(m+n)=1111=11\cdot101,\] and since $m-n<m+n$, either $m-n=1$, $m+n=1111$, or $m-n=11$, $m+n=101$.

  • The first gives $n=555$ and $X=308025$, which has six digits.

  • The second gives $n=45$, $m=56$, $X=2025$, and indeed $3136=56^2$; $2025$ has no digit $9$.

So the only such number is $\boxed{2025}$.

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

Consider two positive integers $a$ and $b$. With those numbers, four operations were performed:

  • (1) Add the two numbers.

  • (2) Subtract the smaller number from the larger one.

  • (3) Multiply the two numbers.

  • (4) Divide the larger number by the smaller one.

Find all pairs $a$ and $b$ such that the sum of the four numbers obtained by operations (1)–(4) is $243$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Name the larger integer $a$ and the smaller one $b$. Write the four results with that order and identify which result could be nonintegral.
Hint 2: key idea
The total and the other three results are integers, so the quotient must be an integer too. Substitute $a=qb$ and look for a perfect-square factor after taking out $q$. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Factor $243$ into primes and list its square divisors that could arise from $b+1$. Use each to determine the quotient and set up the reconstruction of the larger integer. Check the resulting pairs against the four original operations.
Common trap
The square divisor $1$ would give a smaller integer of zero.
Show the answer

$\{a,b\}=\{54,2\}$ or $\{24,8\}$

Show the solution – A perfect square factor

By symmetry let $a\ge b$. The four results are $a+b$, $a-b$, $ab$ and $a/b$, and \[(a+b)+(a-b)+ab+\frac ab=243 .\] Then $a/b=243-2a-ab$ is an integer; write $a=qb$ with $q$ a positive integer. The equation becomes \[2qb+qb^2+q=243,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad q\,(b+1)^2=243=3^5 .\] So $(b+1)^2$ is a square divisor of $3^5$ with $b+1\ge2$: $(b+1)^2=9$ or $81$.

  • $b+1=3$: $b=2$, $q=27$, $a=54$. Check: $56+52+108+27=243$.

  • $b+1=9$: $b=8$, $q=3$, $a=24$. Check: $32+16+192+3=243$.

The pairs are $\boxed{(54,2),\ (24,8)}$ and the same pairs in the other order.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

$100$ points around a circle form a regular polygon $P$. Half of those points are coloured yellow, and the remaining are coloured blue. Show that there are $25$ vertex-disjoint (i.e. having no common vertices) rectangles formed by points in $P$ such that in each rectangle, two vertices are yellow and two are blue.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the point directly opposite one vertex of the polygon. If you pick two such opposite pairs, what do the diagonals of their four-vertex figure have in common?
Hint 2: key idea
Two diameters form the diagonals of a rectangle. Sort all diameters by whether their endpoints are both yellow, both blue, or of different colours. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
Count the yellow and blue vertices separately using the three diameter types. Use the two counts to compare the monochromatic types and determine the parity of the mixed type. Work out how to pair the types while using each diameter once.
Common trap
Different rectangles must use disjoint pairs of diameters to avoid reusing a vertex.
Show the solution – Pairing diameters

Since $100$ is even, the $100$ points split into $50$ diametrically opposite pairs; call these pairs diameters. Any two different diameters are the diagonals of a rectangle (the diagonals are equal and bisect each other), and the rectangles built from disjoint sets of diameters have no common vertices.

Classify the diameters: $y$ of them have two yellow ends, $z$ have two blue ends, and $w$ have one end of each colour. Counting yellow and blue points, \[2y+w=50,\qquad 2z+w=50,\] so $y=z$ and $w=50-2y$ is even.

Now pair each yellow-yellow diameter with a different blue-blue diameter ($y$ rectangles), and pair the $w$ mixed diameters among themselves ($w/2$ rectangles). Each rectangle has two yellow and two blue vertices, the rectangles are vertex-disjoint, and their number is \[y+\frac w2=y+25-y=25 .\]

Problem 6

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Direct Similarity

Let $ABC$ be right-angled with the right angle at $C$. Let $D$ be the foot of the height from $C$. Assume that the distance between the centres of the in-circles of $\triangle ADC$ and $\triangle BDC$ equals $1$. Find the length of the radius of the in-circle of $\triangle ABC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the altitude and mark the right angles in the two smaller triangles. In each, sketch the equal perpendicular distances from its incenter to the two legs.
Hint 2: key idea
Match each smaller triangle with the original right triangle using a shared acute angle. Use the corresponding hypotenuses to express the two smaller inradii in terms of the original inradius. (LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use $AB$ and $CD$ as coordinate axes through $D$ and place the two incenters using their distances from the axes. Square the distance between them and simplify the sum of squares. Substitute the two similarity ratios and use the Pythagorean relation in $ABC$.
Common trap
In the coordinates centred at $D$, the incenters lie on opposite sides of the vertical axis.
Show the answer

$\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$

Show solution 1 – Similar triangles and coordinates

Let $BC=a$, $CA=b$, $AB=c$ (so $a^2+b^2=c^2$), let $r$ be the inradius of $ABC$, and let $I_1$, $I_2$ be the incenters of $ADC$ and $BDC$, with inradii $r_1$, $r_2$.

The triangles $ADC$ and $CDB$ are similar to $ACB$ (each shares an acute angle with it and has a right angle), with ratios $\frac{AC}{AB}=\frac bc$ and $\frac{CB}{AB}=\frac ac$. Hence $r_1=\frac{b}{c}r$ and $r_2=\frac ac r$.

Put $D$ at the origin with $AB$ on the $x$-axis ($A$ to the left) and $C$ on the positive $y$-axis. The incenter $I_1$ lies inside the right angle $ADC$ at distance $r_1$ from both of its sides, so $I_1=(-r_1,r_1)$; similarly $I_2=(r_2,r_2)$. Therefore \[I_1I_2^2=(r_1+r_2)^2+(r_1-r_2)^2=2(r_1^2+r_2^2)=2r^2\,\frac{a^2+b^2}{c^2}=2r^2 .\] So $I_1I_2=r\sqrt2=1$, and $r=\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}}$.

Show solution 2 – The incenter as an orthocenter

Let $I$ be the incenter of $ABC$ and $I_1$, $I_2$ those of $ADC$, $BDC$. Write $\angle A=\alpha$, $\angle B=\beta$, so $\alpha+\beta=90^\circ$, $\angle ACD=\beta$ and $\angle DCB=\alpha$.

The bisector of angle $A$ in triangle $ADC$ is the bisector of angle $A$ in $ABC$, so $A,I_1,I$ are collinear; similarly $B,I_2,I$ are collinear. The line $CI_2$ bisects $\angle DCB$, so it makes the angle $90^\circ-\frac\alpha2$ with $CA$; together with the angle $\frac\alpha2$ between $AI$ and $AC$ this gives a right angle, so $AI\perp CI_2$. Likewise $BI\perp CI_1$.

Hence in triangle $CI_1I_2$ the lines $I_1I$ and $I_2I$ are altitudes, and $I$ is its orthocenter. The angle of this triangle at $C$ is $\angle I_1CI_2=\frac\beta2+\frac\alpha2=45^\circ$. In any triangle the distance from a vertex to the orthocenter is $2R'\lvert\cos\gamma\rvert$ and the opposite side is $2R'\sin\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the angle at that vertex and $R'$ the circumradius; with $\gamma=45^\circ$ these are equal, so $CI=I_1I_2=1$.

Finally $I$ is at distance $r$ from both legs of the right angle at $C$, so $CI=r\sqrt2$. Thus $r\sqrt2=1$ and $r=\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}}$.
