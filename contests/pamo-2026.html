Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

$A$ and $B$ play a game with $8$ identical cards arranged in a straight line. Each turn, a player removes a card if it still has at least one adjacent original neighbor. The player left with no moves loses. Here the neighbors of a card are the cards next to it in the original line; removing a card does not make new neighbors. Who has the winning strategy?

Show the answer

$A$ (the first player) wins

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An explicit strategy)

We assume that $A$ moves first. Number the cards $1,\dots,8$ from left to right. Two cards are neighbours only if their numbers differ by $1$, and this never changes. Call a block a maximal set of remaining cards with consecutive numbers. A card can be removed exactly when its block has at least $2$ cards; removing it splits the block into the parts on either side of it. A card alone in its block stays forever.

Twin pairs. Suppose it is $B$'s turn and the only blocks with $2$ or more cards are two blocks of exactly $2$ cards. Then $B$ must remove a card from one of them, which leaves a single card there; $A$ does the same in the other block; now every block has one card and $B$ cannot move. So such a position is lost for the player to move.

A's strategy. $A$ first removes card $3$. The blocks are $\{1,2\}$ and $\{4,5,6,7,8\}$. By the symmetry $k\leftrightarrow 12-k$ of the second block we only need the following cases for $B$'s reply.

  • $B$ removes $1$ or $2$. Then $A$ removes $6$, leaving the twin pairs $\{4,5\},\{7,8\}$.

  • $B$ removes $6$. The blocks of size $2$ are $\{1,2\},\{4,5\},\{7,8\}$; $A$ removes $1$, leaving twin pairs.

  • $B$ removes $4$ (or $8$). Then $A$ removes $1$, and the only block that matters is $\{5,6,7,8\}$ (or $\{4,5,6,7\}$), four cards in a row. If $B$ removes an end card, three cards in a row remain and $A$ removes the middle one; if $B$ removes an inner card, a block of $1$ and a block of $2$ remain and $A$ removes a card of the block of $2$. Either way $B$ has no move left.

  • $B$ removes $5$ (or $7$). The blocks are $\{1,2\},\{4\},\{6,7,8\}$ (or $\{1,2\},\{4,5,6\},\{8\}$). $A$ removes $8$ (or $4$), leaving the twin pairs $\{1,2\},\{6,7\}$ (or $\{1,2\},\{5,6\}$).

In every case $B$ eventually has no move, so the first player $A$ has a winning strategy: \[\boxed{A}\]

Solution 2 (Sprague–Grundy values)

Assume $A$ moves first. Two cards are neighbours only if their original positions differ by $1$, so the remaining cards split into blocks of consecutive original positions. A card can be removed exactly when its block has at least two cards, and removing the $i$-th card of a block of $m$ cards leaves blocks of $i-1$ and $m-i$ cards. So the game is a sum of independent games, one per block, and the player who cannot move loses (normal play).

By the Sprague–Grundy theorem the position is lost for the player to move exactly when the XOR (nim-sum) of the Grundy values of its blocks is $0$. Let $g(m)$ be the Grundy value of a block of $m$ cards. Then $g(0)=g(1)=0$ and, for $m\ge2$, \[g(m)=\operatorname{mex}\{\,g(i-1)\oplus g(m-i): 1\le i\le m\,\},\] where $\operatorname{mex}$ is the least non-negative integer not in the set. Computing:

$m$012345678
$g(m)$001201231

For example $g(4)=\operatorname{mex}\{g(0)\oplus g(3),\,g(1)\oplus g(2)\}=\operatorname{mex}\{2,1\}=0$, and $g(8)=\operatorname{mex}\{g(0)\oplus g(7),\,g(1)\oplus g(6),\,g(2)\oplus g(5),\,g(3)\oplus g(4)\}=\operatorname{mex}\{3,2,0,2\}=1$.

Since $g(8)=1\ne0$, the first player wins. A winning first move goes to a position of nim-sum $0$: removing card $3$ (or card $6$) leaves blocks of sizes $2$ and $5$, and $g(2)\oplus g(5)=1\oplus1=0$. So the winner is $\boxed{A}$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle with orthocenter $H$ and $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ be its altitudes. Let $G$ be the intersection of $EF$ and $AD$ and $I$ be the antipode of $A$ with respect to $(ABC)$. Let $J$ be the intersection of $AI$ and $BC$.

Show that $HI$ and $GJ$ are parallel.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The similar triangle $AEF$)

Since the triangle is acute, $E$ lies on segment $AC$, $F$ on segment $AB$, and $H$ inside the triangle.

Similarity. In the right triangles $ABE$ and $ACF$ we have $AE=AB\cos A$ and $AF=AC\cos A$. With the common angle at $A$, triangle $AEF$ is similar to triangle $ABC$ with $E\leftrightarrow B$, $F\leftrightarrow C$ and ratio $\cos A$ (SAS).

Corresponding points. The similarity maps the circumcircle of $ABC$ onto the circumcircle of $AEF$, and the antipode of $A$ onto the antipode of $A$. Since $\angle AEH=\angle AFH=90^\circ$, the segment $AH$ is a diameter of the circle $(AEF)$, so the antipode of $A$ in $(AEF)$ is $H$. Thus $I\leftrightarrow H$, and therefore \[AH=\cos A\cdot AI .\] The line $AI$ meets side $BC$ at $J$; the corresponding line $AH$ (that is, $AD$) meets the corresponding side $EF$ at $G$. So $J\leftrightarrow G$ and \[AG=\cos A\cdot AJ .\]

Conclusion. Dividing, $\dfrac{AG}{AH}=\dfrac{AJ}{AI}$. The points $G,H$ lie on ray $AD$ and $J,I$ on ray $AI$, so by the converse of the intercept (Thales) theorem in the angle $DAI$, the lines $GJ$ and $HI$ are parallel. (If $AB=AC$, both lines coincide with the axis of symmetry $AD$, a degenerate case.)

Solution 2 (Law of sines)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths, $A,B,C$ the angles and $R$ the circumradius; the triangle is acute, so $H$, $D$, $E$, $F$, $G$, $J$ lie inside the triangle or on its sides. It suffices to prove $\frac{AG}{AH}=\frac{AJ}{AI}$: then, as $G,H$ are on ray $AD$ and $J,I$ on ray $AI$, the converse of the intercept theorem gives $GJ\parallel HI$.

$AI$ and $AH$. $AI=2R$. In the right triangle $AFC$, $AF=b\cos A$; in triangle $AFH$ (right angle at $F$), $\angle AHF=180^\circ-\angle FHD=\angle B$ because $BDHF$ is cyclic. Hence $AH=\dfrac{AF}{\sin B}=\dfrac{b\cos A}{\sin B}=2R\cos A$.

$AJ$. Let $O$ be the circumcentre. The line $AI$ passes through $O$, and in the isosceles triangle $AOB$ with $\angle AOB=2C$ we get $\angle BAJ=90^\circ-C$. In triangle $ABJ$, $\angle AJB=180^\circ-B-(90^\circ-C)=90^\circ-B+C$, so by the law of sines \[AJ=\frac{c\sin B}{\sin(90^\circ-B+C)}=\frac{c\sin B}{\cos(B-C)} .\]

$AG$. The points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, so $\angle AFE=\angle C$. Also $\angle FAG=\angle BAD=90^\circ-B$. In triangle $AFG$, $\angle AGF=180^\circ-C-(90^\circ-B)=90^\circ+B-C$, and the law of sines gives \[AG=\frac{AF\sin C}{\sin(90^\circ+B-C)}=\frac{b\cos A\,\sin C}{\cos(B-C)} .\]

Comparison. \[\frac{AG}{AH}=\frac{b\sin C}{2R\cos(B-C)},\qquad \frac{AJ}{AI}=\frac{c\sin B}{2R\cos(B-C)},\] and $b\sin C=c\sin B$ by the law of sines. Hence the ratios are equal and $HI\parallel GJ$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Find all polynomials $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that:

  • $P(n)>0$ for all positive integers $n$;

  • for all positive integers $n$, $k$ and primes $p$ that divide $3^n-2^n$ we have: \[p^k\mid 3^n-2^n\iff p^k\mid 3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)}.\]

Show the answer

$P(x)=2^a3^b\,x$ with integers $a,b\ge 0$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (LTE and the Chinese remainder theorem)

Since the condition holds for all $k\ge1$, it says: for every positive integer $n$ and every prime $p$ dividing $3^n-2^n$, \[v_p\bigl(3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)}\bigr)=v_p\bigl(3^n-2^n\bigr).\tag{$*$}\] Such primes satisfy $p\ge5$. We use the Lifting the Exponent lemma: if $p$ is an odd prime, $p\mid x-y$ and $p\nmid xy$, then $v_p(x^m-y^m)=v_p(x-y)+v_p(m)$ for every positive integer $m$.

These polynomials work. Let $P(x)=cx$ with $c=2^a3^b$. Then $P(n)>0$. If $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, apply LTE with $x=3^n$, $y=2^n$, $m=c$: $v_p(3^{cn}-2^{cn})=v_p(3^n-2^n)+v_p(c)=v_p(3^n-2^n)$, since $p\ge5$. So $(*)$ holds.

Step 1: $P(0)=0$. Take $p=5$. Since $9\equiv 4\pmod 5$, LTE with $x=9$, $y=4$ gives $v_5(9^m-4^m)=1+v_5(m)$. For $n=2\cdot5^j$ ($j\ge1$), $v_5(3^n-2^n)=1+j\ge1$. By $(*)$, $5\mid 3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)}$. As $3/2\equiv 4\pmod5$ has order $2$, this forces $P(n)$ to be even, and then $v_5(3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)})=1+v_5(P(n)/2)=1+v_5(P(n))$. So $(*)$ gives \[v_5\bigl(P(2\cdot5^j)\bigr)=j\qquad\text{for all }j\ge1 .\] On the other hand $P(2\cdot5^j)\equiv P(0)\pmod{5^j}$. If $P(0)\ne0$, choose $j>v_5(P(0))$; then $v_5(P(2\cdot 5^j))=v_5(P(0))<j$, a contradiction. So $P(0)=0$ and $P(x)=xQ(x)$ with $Q$ having integer coefficients; $Q(n)=P(n)/n>0$ for $n\ge1$.

Step 2: $Q(n)$ is coprime to $3^n-2^n$. If $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, LTE with $x=3^n$, $y=2^n$, $m=Q(n)$ gives $v_p(3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)})=v_p(3^n-2^n)+v_p(Q(n))$, so $(*)$ means $p\nmid Q(n)$.

Step 3: $Q(n)$ has no prime factor $p\ge5$. Suppose $p\ge5$ divides $Q(m)$ for some $m\ge1$. By the Chinese remainder theorem (as $\gcd(p,p-1)=1$) there is a positive $n$ with $n\equiv m\pmod p$ and $n\equiv0\pmod{p-1}$. Then $Q(n)\equiv Q(m)\equiv0\pmod p$, while by Fermat's little theorem $3^n\equiv 1\equiv 2^n\pmod p$. This contradicts Step 2.

Step 4: $Q$ is constant. Let $c=Q(1)=2^a3^b$. For $t\ge1$, $Q(1+6ct)-Q(1)$ is divisible by $6ct$, so $Q(1+6ct)=c(1+6tu)$ for some integer $u$. The positive integer $1+6tu$ is coprime to $6$, but its prime factors are among those of $Q(1+6ct)$, i.e. among $2,3$ (Step 3). Hence $1+6tu=1$ and $Q(1+6ct)=c$ for all $t\ge1$. A polynomial taking the value $c$ infinitely often is constant: $Q\equiv c$.

Therefore \[\boxed{P(x)=2^a3^b\,x\quad(a,b\ \text{non-negative integers})}.\]

Solution 2 (Zsigmondy and Schur)

The condition (for all $k\ge1$) says: for every $n\ge1$ and every prime $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, $v_p(3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)})=v_p(3^n-2^n)$; in particular $p\mid 3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)}$. All such $p$ are at least $5$. We use the LTE lemma (for an odd prime $p$ with $p\mid x-y$, $p\nmid xy$: $v_p(x^m-y^m)=v_p(x-y)+v_p(m)$) and two classical theorems:

  • Zsigmondy: for coprime $a>b\ge1$ and $n\ge2$, $a^n-b^n$ has a prime factor dividing no $a^j-b^j$ with $j<n$, except when $n=2$ and $a+b$ is a power of $2$, or $(a,b,n)=(2,1,6)$. For $a=3$, $b=2$ there is no exception.

  • Schur: a non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients has infinitely many primes dividing at least one of its values at integers.

Sufficiency. For $P(x)=cx$, $c=2^a3^b$, and $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, LTE with $x=3^n$, $y=2^n$, $m=c$ gives $v_p(3^{cn}-2^{cn})=v_p(3^n-2^n)+v_p(c)=v_p(3^n-2^n)$ (as $p\ge5$), and $P(n)>0$.

Step 1: $n\mid P(n)$ for $n\ge2$. Let $p$ be a Zsigmondy prime for $3^n-2^n$ and let $r\equiv 3\cdot 2^{-1}\pmod p$. Then $p\mid3^j-2^j$ iff $r^j\equiv1$, so the order of $r$ modulo $p$ is exactly $n$. Since $p\mid 3^{P(n)}-2^{P(n)}$, we get $r^{P(n)}\equiv1$ and $n\mid P(n)$.

Step 2: $P(x)=xQ(x)$. For every $n\ge2$, $n\mid P(n)-P(0)$ and $n\mid P(n)$, hence $n\mid P(0)$. So $P(0)=0$ and $P(x)=xQ(x)$ with integer coefficients, $Q(n)>0$ for $n\ge 1$.

Step 3: $\gcd(Q(n),3^n-2^n)=1$. For $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, LTE gives $v_p(3^{nQ(n)}-2^{nQ(n)})=v_p(3^n-2^n)+v_p(Q(n))$, so the condition forces $p\nmid Q(n)$.

Step 4: $Q$ is constant. If $Q$ were not constant, by Schur's theorem some prime $p\ge5$ divides $Q(m_0)$ for some integer $m_0$; adding a multiple of $p$ we may take $m_0\ge1$. Choose $n\ge1$ with $n\equiv m_0\pmod p$ and $(p-1)\mid n$ (Chinese remainder theorem). Then $p\mid Q(n)$ and, by Fermat, $p\mid 3^n-2^n$, contradicting Step 3. So $Q\equiv c$, a positive integer. Finally, every prime $p\ge5$ divides $3^{p-1}-2^{p-1}$, so by Step 3 (with $n=p-1$) $p\nmid c$. Hence $c=2^a3^b$ and \[\boxed{P(x)=2^a3^b\,x\quad(a,b\ge0)}.\]

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation

Determine all polynomials $P$ with natural number coefficients such that: \[P(\sqrt2)=\sqrt{P(2)}.\]

Show the answer

$P\equiv 0$ or $P(x)=x^m$ ($m=0,1,2,\dots$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Rational and irrational parts)

Here natural numbers include $0$, so the coefficients are non-negative integers. Write $P(x)=\sum_i c_ix^i$ with non-negative integer coefficients $c_i$. Then \[P(\sqrt2)=E+O\sqrt2,\qquad E=\sum_j c_{2j}2^j,\quad O=\sum_j c_{2j+1}2^j,\] with $E,O$ non-negative integers. The condition implies $P(\sqrt2)^2=P(2)$, i.e. $E^2+2O^2+2EO\sqrt2=P(2)$. Since $\sqrt2$ is irrational and $P(2)$ is an integer, $EO=0$.

A lemma. Let $R(y)=\sum_j r_jy^j$ have non-negative integer coefficients. Then \[R(2)^2=\sum_j r_j^2 4^j+2\sum_{i<j}r_ir_j2^{i+j}\ \ge\ \sum_j r_j4^j=R(4),\] because $r_j^2\ge r_j$ for integers $r_j\ge 0$. Equality holds iff every $r_j\in\{0,1\}$ and all products $r_ir_j$ ($i<j$) vanish, i.e. iff $R=0$ or $R(y)=y^j$ for some $j$.

Case $O=0$. Then all odd coefficients vanish and $P(x)=R(x^2)$ with $R(y)=\sum_j c_{2j}y^j$. The condition $E^2=P(2)$ reads $R(2)^2=R(4)$, so $R=0$ or $R(y)=y^j$, i.e. $P=0$ or $P(x)=x^{2j}$.

Case $E=0$. Then all even coefficients vanish and $P(x)=xR(x^2)$ with $R(y)=\sum_jc_{2j+1}y^j$. Now $P(\sqrt2)=\sqrt2\,R(2)$ and $P(2)=2R(4)$, so $2R(2)^2=2R(4)$; again $R=0$ or $R(y)=y^j$, i.e. $P(x)=x^{2j+1}$.

Check. For $P(x)=x^m$: $P(\sqrt2)=2^{m/2}=\sqrt{2^m}=\sqrt{P(2)}$. The zero polynomial also works ($0=\sqrt0$). So the answer is \[\boxed{P\equiv 0\quad\text{or}\quad P(x)=x^m\ (m=0,1,2,\dots)}.\]

Solution 2 (A polynomial with non-negative coefficients)

Natural numbers include $0$ here. Let $P(x)=\sum_i c_ix^i$ with non-negative integer coefficients and consider \[F(x)=P(x)^2-P(x^2).\] The coefficient of $x^k$ in $P(x)^2$ is $\sum_{i+j=k}c_ic_j$. For odd $k$, $P(x^2)$ has no $x^k$ term, so the coefficient of $x^k$ in $F$ is $\sum_{i+j=k}c_ic_j\ge0$. For $k=2l$, it is \[\sum_{i+j=2l}c_ic_j-c_l=(c_l^2-c_l)+\sum_{i+j=2l,\ i\ne j}c_ic_j\ \ge 0,\] since $c_l^2\ge c_l$ for a non-negative integer. So all coefficients of $F$ are non-negative.

The hypothesis gives $P(\sqrt2)^2=P(2)$, i.e. $F(\sqrt2)=0$. But $F(\sqrt2)$ is a sum of non-negative coefficients times the positive numbers $(\sqrt2)^k$; it vanishes only if every coefficient of $F$ is $0$. In particular, for every $l$, $c_l^2=c_l$, so $c_l\in\{0,1\}$, and $c_ic_j=0$ whenever $i\ne j$ (each such product appears in the coefficient of $x^{i+j}$). Thus at most one coefficient is non-zero, and it equals $1$: $P=0$ or $P(x)=x^m$.

Conversely $P(x)=x^m$ gives $P(\sqrt2)=2^{m/2}=\sqrt{P(2)}$, and $P\equiv0$ gives $0=\sqrt0$. The answer is \[\boxed{P\equiv 0\quad\text{or}\quad P(x)=x^m\ (m=0,1,2,\dots)}.\]

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Determine all pairs $(a,n)$ of strictly positive integers such that $a$ is a prime power, $n\ge 2$, and $a^n(a-1)+1$ is a perfect square.

Show the answer

$(a,n)=(2,3)$ and $(4,2)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor and compare sizes)

(We do not count $1=p^0$ as a prime power; for $a=1$ the expression equals $1$ for every $n$.) Let $a=p^k$ with $p$ prime and $k\ge1$, $n\ge2$, and $a^n(a-1)+1=m^2$ with $m$ a positive integer. Then \[(m-1)(m+1)=a^n(a-1),\qquad m^2=a^{n+1}-a^n+1 .\] Here $a^n(a-1)$ is even ($a$ or $a-1$ is even), so $m$ is odd and $m-1$, $m+1$ are consecutive even numbers.

$p$ odd. Then $\gcd(m-1,m+1)=2$, so the odd prime $p$ cannot divide both factors, and $a^n=p^{kn}$ divides one of them. Hence $a^n\le m+1$, i.e. $m\ge a^n-1\ge 0$, and \[(a^n-1)^2\le m^2=a^{n+1}-a^n+1\ \Longrightarrow\ a^{2n}-a^n\le a^{n+1}\ \Longrightarrow\ a^n\le a+1 .\] But $a\ge3$ and $n\ge2$ give $a^n\ge a^2>a+1$. No solutions.

$p=2$, $a=2^k$. Now $a-1$ is odd and $v_2\bigl((m-1)(m+1)\bigr)=kn$. One of the two consecutive even numbers $m\pm1$ is $\equiv2\pmod 4$, so the other is divisible by $2^{kn-1}$. Hence $m\ge 2^{kn-1}-1\ge 0$ and \[(2^{kn-1}-1)^2\le 2^{kn}(2^k-1)+1\ \Longrightarrow\ 2^{2kn-2}-2^{kn}\le 2^{kn+k}-2^{kn}\ \Longrightarrow\ 2kn-2\le kn+k,\] that is, $k(n-1)\le 2$. With $k\ge1$, $n\ge2$ this leaves $(k,n)\in\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,2)\}$: \[a=2,n=2:\ 4\cdot1+1=5\ \text{(not a square)};\quad a=2,n=3:\ 8\cdot1+1=9=3^2;\quad a=4,n=2:\ 16\cdot3+1=49=7^2 .\] Therefore \[(a,n)\in\boxed{\{(2,3),\ (4,2)\}}.\]

Solution 2 (Splitting the factors exactly)

Let $a=p^k$ ($p$ prime, $k\ge1$), $n\ge2$, and $a^n(a-1)+1=m^2$ with $m\ge1$, so $(m-1)(m+1)=a^n(a-1)$. The left side is even, so $m$ is odd.

$p$ odd. Since $\gcd(m-1,m+1)=2$, the power $a^n$ divides $m+\varepsilon$ for one sign $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$. Write $m+\varepsilon=a^ns$; then $m-\varepsilon=t$ with $st=a-1$, so $s,t$ are positive integers. Subtracting, $a^ns-t=2\varepsilon$, and multiplying by $s$: \[a^ns^2-2\varepsilon s=st=a-1 .\] Since $a^ns^2-2s-(a^n-2)=(s-1)\bigl(a^n(s+1)-2\bigr)\ge0$, we get $a-1\ge a^ns^2-2s\ge a^n-2$, so $a^n\le a+1$, impossible for $a\ge3$, $n\ge2$.

$p=2$, $a=2^k$. The numbers $m\pm1$ are consecutive even numbers, so one of them is $\equiv 2\pmod4$; their product has $2$-adic valuation $kn\ge2$. Hence for a suitable sign $\varepsilon$, $m+\varepsilon=2^{kn-1}s$ and $m-\varepsilon=2t$ with $s,t$ positive integers, $st=2^k-1$. Subtracting, $2^{kn-1}s-2t=2\varepsilon$, i.e. $t=2^{kn-2}s-\varepsilon$, and so \[2^k-1=st=2^{kn-2}s^2-\varepsilon s\ \ge\ 2^{kn-2}s^2-s\ \ge\ 2^{kn-2}-1,\] where the last step is $(s-1)\bigl(2^{kn-2}(s+1)-1\bigr)\ge0$. Thus $kn-2\le k$, i.e. $k(n-1)\le2$, leaving $(a,n)=(2,2),(2,3),(4,2)$. Checking: $2^2\cdot1+1=5$ is not a square, while $2^3\cdot1+1=9$ and $4^2\cdot3+1=49$ are. The answer is \[\boxed{(a,n)=(2,3)\ \text{or}\ (4,2)}.\]

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $n$ be a non-zero natural number. Consider all grids of size $n\times 1$ where each cell of the grid is colored either black or white. Two grids are said to be “similar” if one can be transformed into the other by a finite sequence of moves. A move consists of selecting two consecutive cells of the same color and changing the color of both cells. Determine the maximum possible size of a collection of grids such that no two grids in the collection are similar to each other.

Show the answer

$n+1$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Recolouring turns moves into swaps)

Number the cells $1,\dots,n$ and write $c_i=1$ for black and $c_i=0$ for white. A move is reversible (after it the two cells still have equal colours, and repeating it restores them), so similarity is an equivalence relation, and the answer is the number of equivalence classes.

Define a new word by $d_i=c_i$ for odd $i$ and $d_i=1-c_i$ for even $i$ (flip every second cell). Neighbouring cells have different parities, so $c_i=c_{i+1}$ exactly when $d_i\ne d_{i+1}$. A move flips both $c_i$ and $c_{i+1}$, hence flips both $d_i$ and $d_{i+1}$; since they were different, this simply swaps them. So in the $d$-language a move is: swap two neighbouring different digits.

Such swaps do not change the number $w$ of $1$'s in $d$, so grids with different $w$ are not similar. Conversely, swaps of neighbouring different digits can sort any word into the form $11\dots100\dots0$ (bubble sort: move each $1$ to the left past the $0$'s), and this sorted word depends only on $w$. So two grids are similar if and only if they have the same $w$. As $w$ takes every value $0,1,\dots,n$, there are $n+1$ classes, and the maximum size of a collection of pairwise non-similar grids is \[\boxed{n+1}.\]

Solution 2 (An invariant and induction)

Write $c_i=1$ if cell $i$ is black and $0$ if white ($1\le i\le n$), and let \[D=\sum_{i\ \mathrm{odd}}c_i-\sum_{i\ \mathrm{even}}c_i .\] Moves are reversible (the two cells stay equal-coloured), so similarity is an equivalence relation.

$D$ is invariant. A move acts on cells $i,i+1$, one odd and one even, of the same colour. If both were black, both sums drop by $1$; if both were white, both rise by $1$. Either way $D$ is unchanged.

Range. There are $\lceil n/2\rceil$ odd and $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ even positions, so $D$ takes exactly the $n+1$ values $-\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\dots,\lceil n/2\rceil$, and at the two extreme values the grid is unique (all odd cells black and all even cells white, or the reverse).

Claim: grids $X,Y$ of length $n$ with $D(X)=D(Y)$ are similar. Strong induction on $n$ (length $0$ allowed: the empty grid, with $D=0$). For $n\le1$, $D$ determines the grid. Let $n\ge2$. If $D$ is extreme, $X=Y$. Otherwise $D(X)$ lies in $[-\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1,\ \lceil n/2\rceil-1]=[-\lfloor (n-2)/2\rfloor,\ \lceil (n-2)/2\rceil]$, the range of $D$ for length $n-2$.

  • If $X_n=Y_n$, the first $n-1$ cells of $X$ and of $Y$ have equal $D$; by induction we transform one into the other using moves inside the first $n-1$ cells, which do not touch cell $n$.

  • If $X_n\ne Y_n$, choose a grid $Z'$ of length $n-1$ with $Z'_{n-1}=X_n$ and $D(Z')=D(X')$, where $X'$ is the first $n-1$ cells of $X$. Such $Z'$ exists: cell $n-1$ contributes $\pm X_n$ to $D$ and cell $n$ contributes $\mp X_n$ (opposite parities), so $D(X')\mp X_n=D(X)$, and we need the first $n-2$ cells of $Z'$ to have $D$ equal to $D(X)$, which is in the range for length $n-2$. By induction, turn $X'$ into $Z'$. Now cells $n-1$ and $n$ both have colour $X_n$; flip them. Cell $n$ now has colour $Y_n$, and we finish as in the first case (the value of $D$ never changed).

Hence the classes correspond exactly to the $n+1$ values of $D$, and the largest collection of pairwise non-similar grids has $\boxed{n+1}$ grids.
