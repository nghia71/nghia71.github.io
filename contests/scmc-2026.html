Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search · also Individual Round B, Problem 10

For positive integer $n$, define $s(n)$ as the sum of the digits of $n$. Compute the number of $n<10000$ such that $\dfrac{s(n)}{s(n+1)}$ is an integer.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the digit sums before and after adding one to a few small numbers. Try an example ending in $9$ and another with no $9$ at the end.
Hint 2: key idea
Let $k$ be the number of trailing nines and express the change in digit sum when the carry passes through them. Subtract the new digit sum from the old one to get a divisibility test involving only $k$ and the new sum. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Write the numbers with four digits, allowing leading zeros, and separate them by the exact value of $k$. For each possible divisor of $9k-1$, find the required digit sum of the prefix and count prefixes of that sum whose final digit is below $9$. Handle the all-nines number separately.
Common trap
An all-zero prefix still gives a positive number when it is followed by trailing nines.
Show the answer

$56$

Show the solution – Count the trailing nines

Write $n$ with exactly $k\ge0$ trailing nines. Adding $1$ turns these $k$ nines into zeros and raises the digit before them (which is not $9$) by $1$, so \[s(n+1)=s(n)-9k+1.\] Put $t=s(n+1)$. Then $s(n)=t+9k-1$, and $t\mid s(n)$ if and only if $t\mid 9k-1$.

If $k=0$, then $t\mid -1$ forces $t=1$, i.e. $s(n)=0$, impossible. So $k\ge1$. Write $n$ with four digits (leading zeros allowed, $1\le n\le 9999$) for $k\le3$ as a prefix $p$ of $4-k$ digits, whose last digit is not $9$, followed by $k$ nines (for $k=4$ only $n=9999$ remains, treated directly). Then $n+1$ is $p+1$ followed by zeros, and $t=s(p)+1$ (no carry, since the last digit of $p$ is not $9$). The number of strings of $j$ digits with digit sum $\sigma\le9$ is $\binom{\sigma+j-1}{j-1}$.

  • $k=1$: $t\mid8$, so $s(p)\in\{0,1,3,7\}$ with $p$ of $3$ digits (a digit sum $\le7$ never has last digit $9$): $1+3+10+36=50$.

  • $k=2$: $t\mid17$, so $s(p)\in\{0,16\}$ with $p$ of $2$ digits, last digit not $9$: $00$, $88$, $97$: $3$.

  • $k=3$: $t\mid26$, so $s(p)\in\{0,1,12,25\}$ with $p$ a single digit other than $9$: $p=0,1$: $2$.

  • $k=4$: $n=9999$, $s(n+1)=1$ divides $36$: $1$.

The prefix $p=0\cdots0$ gives $n=9,99,999$, which are positive, so all are allowed. The total is $50+3+2+1=\boxed{56}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework · also Individual Round B, Problem 11

Consider a bijection $f:\{1,2,\dots,7\}\to\{1,2,\dots,7\}$ such that for every $x$, either $x\mid f(x)$ or $f(x)\mid x$. Compute the number of possible functions $f$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For each number, list the images it is allowed to have. Compare the list for $1$ with those for the other numbers.
Hint 2: key idea
View a bijection as a collection of directed cycles and remove $1$ from its cycle. If $1$ was between $b$ and $a$, reconnect $b$ directly to $a$ and allow that one new arrow to ignore the divisibility rule. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
Separate the case where $1$ was fixed from the cases indexed by its predecessor $b$. Decide why $5$ and $7$ must remain fixed in the reduced permutation even if one is free. For each case, enumerate injective choices for the constrained images and let the free arrow, if present, take the leftover image.
Common trap
If $1$ was not fixed, keep track of its specific predecessor so the original cycle can be recovered.
Show the answer

$41$

Show the solution – Remove $1$ from its cycle

Call $x,y$ compatible if $x\mid y$ or $y\mid x$. Every number is compatible with $1$ and with itself. Among $2,\dots,7$ the other compatible pairs are $\{2,4\}$, $\{2,6\}$, $\{3,6\}$; the numbers $5$ and $7$ are compatible only with $1$ and themselves.

Removing $1$. Let $b=f^{-1}(1)$ and $a=f(1)$. Define $g$ on $\{2,\dots,7\}$ by $g(x)=f(x)$ for every $x\ne b$ and, when $b\ne1$, $g(b)=a$ (that is, cut $1$ out of its cycle). If $f(1)=1$, $g$ is a valid permutation of $\{2,\dots,7\}$. If $f(1)\ne1$, $g$ is a permutation of $\{2,\dots,7\}$ in which every $x\ne b$ goes to a compatible number, while $g(b)$ is arbitrary; and conversely any such $g$ and choice of $b$ give back a valid $f$ (insert $1$ between $b$ and $g(b)$; $b$ and $g(b)$ are compatible with $1$). So the answer is $Q+\sum_{b=2}^{7}Q_b$, where $Q$ counts valid permutations of $\{2,\dots,7\}$ and $Q_b$ those in which $b$ is free.

The numbers $5$, $7$. If $5$ is not free, it must be fixed; if $5$ is free, all of $2,3,4,6,7$ map into $\{2,3,4,6,7\}$ (no number other than $5$ is compatible with $5$), so again $g(5)=5$. The same holds for $7$. So everything happens on $\{2,3,4,6\}$, with allowed images $2\to\{2,4,6\}$, $3\to\{3,6\}$, $4\to\{2,4\}$, $6\to\{2,3,6\}$.

No free number ($Q$, also $Q_5$ and $Q_7$). If $g(3)=3$: on $\{2,4,6\}$ we get the identity, $(2\,4)$ and $(2\,6)$; if $g(3)=6$, then only $6$ can go to $3$, so $g(6)=3$, and $\{2,4\}$ gives $2$ ways. So $Q=Q_5=Q_7=5$.

One free number. Choose the images of the other three numbers injectively; the free number takes the leftover.

  • $b=2$: $(g(3),g(4),g(6))\in\{(3,2,6),(3,4,2),(3,4,6),(6,4,2),(6,2,3),(6,4,3)\}$: $Q_2=6$.

  • $b=3$: $(g(2),g(4),g(6))\in\{(4,2,3),(4,2,6),(6,2,3),(2,4,3),(2,4,6),(6,4,2),(6,4,3)\}$: $Q_3=7$.

  • $b=4$: $(g(2),g(3),g(6))\in\{(2,3,6),(4,3,2),(4,3,6),(6,3,2),(2,6,3),(4,6,2),(4,6,3)\}$: $Q_4=7$.

  • $b=6$: $(g(2),g(3),g(4))\in\{(2,3,4),(4,3,2),(6,3,2),(6,3,4),(2,6,4),(4,6,2)\}$: $Q_6=6$.

The total is $5+(6+7+7+5+6+5)=\boxed{41}$.

Problem 8

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Angle Bisector and Median Geometry · also Individual Round B, Problem 12

$\triangle ABC$ has a right angle at $A$, $AC=1$, and $AB=2$. Points $D$ and $M$ lie on $BC$ such that $AD$ and $AM$ are the angle bisector and median, respectively, from $A$ to $BC$. The reflection of $AM$ over $AD$ intersects $BC$ at $M'$. Then the value of $AM'$ can be expressed in the form $\dfrac{a\sqrt b}{c}$, where $a,b,c$ are positive integers, $b$ is squarefree, and $a,c$ are relatively prime. Compute $a+b+c$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the right triangle and mark $M$ halfway along $BC$. Sketch the ray from $A$ on the opposite side of the angle bisector from $AM$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use $A$ as the origin and the legs as coordinate axes. Reflection in the right-angle bisector interchanges the two coordinates of a point. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Find the coordinates of $M$ by averaging the endpoints, then reflect that point to determine the line through $A$ that contains $M'$. Intersect this line with $BC$ and set up the distance from the resulting point to the origin.
Common trap
The reflected midpoint determines the new line, while $M'$ is where that line meets $BC$.
Show the answer

$12$ (from $AM\'=\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}$)

Show solution 1 – Coordinates

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(2,0)$, $C=(0,1)$. The bisector of the right angle at $A$ is the line $y=x$, and $M=\left(1,\tfrac12\right)$. Reflection in $y=x$ swaps the coordinates, so the reflected line passes through $\left(\tfrac12,1\right)$: it is $y=2x$. Line $BC$ is $\frac x2+y=1$; substituting $y=2x$ gives $x=\frac25$, $y=\frac45$. Hence \[AM'=\sqrt{\tfrac4{25}+\tfrac{16}{25}}=\frac{2\sqrt5}{5},\] so $a=2$, $b=5$, $c=5$ and $a+b+c=\boxed{12}$.

Show solution 2 – The reflected median is the altitude

The midpoint $M$ of the hypotenuse is the circumcentre, so $MA=MB$ and $\angle MAB=\angle B$. Reflecting in the bisector of $\angle A$ exchanges the rays $AB$ and $AC$, so the reflected line $AM'$ makes the same angle with $AC$: $\angle M'AC=\angle B$. In triangle $AM'C$, \[\angle AM'C=180^\circ-\angle C-\angle B=90^\circ,\] since $\angle B+\angle C=90^\circ$. So $AM'$ is the altitude from $A$, and comparing two expressions for twice the area, \[AM'=\frac{AB\cdot AC}{BC}=\frac{2\cdot1}{\sqrt5}=\frac{2\sqrt5}{5}.\] Thus $a+b+c=2+5+5=\boxed{12}$.

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Orders and Primitive Behavior · also Individual Round B, Problem 15

For each integer $m$ with $1\le m\le2026$, define \[g(m)=\gcd\left(2^m+1,\ 2^{2026}+1\right).\] Find the remainder when \[\sum_{m=1}^{2026}g(m)\] is divided by $1000$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a small fixed exponent in place of $2026$ and compute the gcd for a few values of $m$. Record which pairs of exponents lead to a gcd greater than one.
Hint 2: key idea
Put $h=\gcd(m,2026)$ and consider the two exponents after division by $h$. Any common divisor also divides both corresponding differences of squares, so use the gcd of the resulting differences of powers to narrow down its possible factors. (LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Factor $2^{2h}-1$ and rule out the factor $2^h-1$ by comparing $2^m+1$ with $2^m-1$. Use parity of the reduced exponents to finish classifying the gcd, then group the values of $m$ by that classification. For the remaining large power, compute its residues modulo $8$ and $125$ to prepare the reduction modulo $1000$.
Common trap
The parity test concerns the exponents after division by their gcd.
Show the answer

$914$

Show the solution – The gcd of $2^m+1$ and $2^n+1$

Lemma. Let $n\ge1$, $m\ge1$, $h=\gcd(m,n)$, $m=hm'$, $n=hn'$. Then $\gcd(2^m+1,2^n+1)=2^h+1$ if $m'$ and $n'$ are both odd, and $1$ otherwise.

Proof. Let $d=\gcd(2^m+1,2^n+1)$; $d$ is odd. Since $d\mid 2^{2m}-1$ and $d\mid2^{2n}-1$, and $\gcd(2^{2m}-1,2^{2n}-1)=2^{\gcd(2m,2n)}-1=2^{2h}-1$ (Euclid's algorithm on the exponents), we get $d\mid(2^h-1)(2^h+1)$. Since $h\mid m$, $2^h-1$ divides $2^m-1$, so any common divisor of $2^m+1$ and $2^h-1$ divides $(2^m+1)-(2^m-1)=2$; both numbers are odd, so $\gcd(2^m+1,2^h-1)=1$. Hence $d$ is coprime to $2^h-1$, and $d\mid2^h+1$.

  • If $m'$ is even, then $2^m=(2^{2h})^{m'/2}\equiv1\pmod{2^h+1}$, so modulo $d$ we have $-1\equiv2^m\equiv1$, and $d\mid2$, i.e. $d=1$. The same if $n'$ is even.

  • If $m',n'$ are odd, then $2^h+1\mid(2^h)^{m'}+1=2^m+1$ (as $x+1\mid x^k+1$ for odd $k$), and likewise $2^h+1\mid2^n+1$; so $d=2^h+1$. $\square$

Apply it with $n=2026=2\cdot1013$ ($1013$ is prime). Then $h=\gcd(m,2026)\in\{1,2,1013,2026\}$, and $n'=2026/h$ is odd only for $h\in\{2,2026\}$.

  • $m=2026$: $g(m)=2^{2026}+1$.

  • $h=2$ with $m'=m/2$ odd: $m\equiv2\pmod 4$ and $m\ne2026$. The numbers $2,6,\dots,2026$ are $507$, so there are $506$ such $m$, each with $g(m)=5$.

  • All other $2026-507=1519$ values give $g(m)=1$.

So $\sum g(m)=2^{2026}+1+5\cdot506+1519=2^{2026}+4050$.

Finally $2^{2026}\bmod1000$: it is $\equiv0\pmod8$, and modulo $125$ (where $2^{100}\equiv1$ by Euler) $2^{2026}\equiv2^{26}=67108864\equiv114$. The number below $1000$ that is $\equiv114\pmod{125}$ and divisible by $8$ is $864$. Hence the sum is $\equiv864+4050\equiv\boxed{914}\pmod{1000}$.

Problem 12

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Angle Bisector and Median Geometry

Consider triangle $ABC$ with $AB=6$, $AC=8$, and suppose the angle bisector of $\angle BAC$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Denote the inradius of $\triangle ABD$ by $r$ and the radius of the $A$-excircle of $\triangle ADC$ by $R$. Given that \[\frac rR=\frac12,\] find $BC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $AD$ and label the two angles sharing vertex $A$ on either side of it. In each of those angles, sketch its circle and a radius to a point of tangency on one ray.
Hint 2: key idea
The two angles at $A$ are equal, so the right triangles from $A$ to the points of tangency have the same acute angles. Relate the radius ratio to the ratio of the lengths along the rays from $A$ to the tangency points. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Express $BD$ and $DC$ using the angle bisector theorem, then write the two tangent lengths from $A$ in terms of the sides and $AD$. Use their ratio to express $AD$ in terms of $BC$. Substitute that expression into the bisector length formula and check the restrictions on a feasible root.
Common trap
The tangent length from $A$ for the $A$-excircle uses the full semiperimeter of $ADC$.
Show the answer

$7$

Show solution 1 – Tangent lengths from $A$

Let $a=BC$, $d=AD$. By the angle bisector theorem $BD=\frac{3a}{7}$ and $DC=\frac{4a}{7}$.

Both circles are inscribed in an angle of size $\frac12\angle A$ with vertex $A$: the incircle of $ABD$ in the angle $BAD$, the $A$-excircle of $ADC$ in the angle $DAC$. A circle of radius $\rho$ inscribed in an angle $\theta$ touches its sides at distance $\rho\cot\frac\theta2$ from the vertex, so the radii are in the same ratio as the tangent lengths from $A$: \[\frac rR=\frac{\tfrac12(AB+AD-BD)}{\tfrac12(AD+DC+CA)}=\frac{6+d-\frac{3a}7}{8+d+\frac{4a}7}.\] (The tangent length from $A$ to the incircle is $s-BD$, and to the $A$-excircle it is the semiperimeter.) Setting this equal to $\frac12$: \[12+2d-\tfrac{6a}{7}=8+d+\tfrac{4a}{7}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d=\tfrac{10a}{7}-4.\] The length of the bisector is given by $d^2=AB\cdot AC-BD\cdot DC=48-\frac{12a^2}{49}$ (Stewart's theorem). Hence \[\left(\tfrac{10a}{7}-4\right)^2=48-\tfrac{12a^2}{49}\iff\tfrac{16a^2}{7}-\tfrac{80a}{7}-32=0\iff a^2-5a-14=0,\] so $a=7$ (the other root is negative). Then $d=6>0$, and $a=7$ does give a triangle. So $BC=\boxed{7}$.

Show solution 2 – Areas and semiperimeters

Let $a=BC$, $d=AD$, so $BD=\frac{3a}7$, $DC=\frac{4a}7$, and $d^2=48-\frac{12a^2}{49}$ (Stewart's theorem). Let $\phi=\frac12\angle BAC$. Then \[[ABD]=\tfrac12\cdot6d\sin\phi,\qquad [ADC]=\tfrac12\cdot8d\sin\phi,\] so $[ADC]=\frac43[ABD]$. With $s_1=\frac12(6+d+\frac{3a}7)$ the semiperimeter of $ABD$ and $s_2=\frac12(8+d+\frac{4a}7)$ that of $ADC$, we have $r=\frac{[ABD]}{s_1}$ and $R=\frac{[ADC]}{s_2-DC}$. Therefore \[\frac rR=\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{s_2-DC}{s_1}=\frac34\cdot\frac{8+d-\frac{4a}7}{6+d+\frac{3a}7}=\frac12,\] which gives $3\left(8+d-\frac{4a}7\right)=2\left(6+d+\frac{3a}{7}\right)$, that is, $d=\frac{18a}{7}-12$. Combining with $d^2=48-\frac{12a^2}{49}$: \[\left(\tfrac{18a}{7}-12\right)^2=48-\tfrac{12a^2}{49}\iff 336a^2-3024a+4704=0\iff a^2-9a+14=0,\] so $a\in\{2,7\}$. For $a=2$ we get $d=\frac{36}{7}-12<0$, impossible. Hence $BC=\boxed{7}$ (and then $d=6$).

Problem 14

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Basic Graph Properties

The vertices of a regular decagon are labeled $1,2,\dots,10$ in clockwise order. A noncrossing perfect matching is a set of $5$ chords connecting the vertices in pairs so that every vertex is an endpoint of exactly one chord and no two chords intersect in their interiors. A chord is called short if it connects two adjacent vertices of the decagon. How many noncrossing perfect matchings have exactly $2$ short chords?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a noncrossing matching on a circle with fewer vertices. Mark the regions cut out by the chords and see how a short chord affects the sketch.
Hint 2: key idea
Make a graph whose vertices are the regions of the disk, with one edge for each chord separating two regions. Show that the graph is a tree and identify the regions that correspond to its leaves. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Use the number of short chords to determine the shape of the region tree. Starting from a chosen short chord, follow the tree and use adjacency along the circle to determine the next chord. Track how many choices of starting chord describe the same matching before counting.
Common trap
Use the whole circular disk, where a short chord cuts off a cap.
Show the answer

$5$

Show the solution – The regions form a tree

Work in the disk bounded by the circle through the ten vertices (in the decagon itself a short chord is a side and cuts off nothing). The $5$ chords do not meet, so adding them one at a time, each splits one region into two: the disk is cut into $6$ regions. Join two regions when they share a chord. This graph is connected and has $6$ vertices and $5$ edges, so it is a tree.

The boundary of a region consists of chords and arcs of the circle. An arc of the boundary contains no vertex in its interior: the chord at such a vertex would leave it into the region and also bound the region. So a region is a leaf (it touches only one chord) exactly when it is bounded by one chord and the arc between two adjacent vertices, i.e. it is the cap of a short chord. Each short chord has exactly one cap (its other side contains $8$ vertices and further chords), so the number of short chords equals the number of leaves of the tree.

A tree with exactly $2$ leaves is a path. Then every region other than the two end regions touches exactly two chords, say $PQ$ and $P'Q'$, and two arcs of the boundary. These arcs contain no vertex strictly between the chord endpoints (such a vertex would need a chord inside the region), so $P'$ is adjacent to $P$ and $Q'$ to $Q$. Starting from a short chord $\{i,i+1\}$, the matching is forced to be \[\{i,i+1\},\ \{i-1,i+2\},\ \{i-2,i+3\},\ \{i-3,i+4\},\ \{i-4,i+5\}\] (indices mod $10$): five parallel chords. Its short chords are exactly the first and the last, so it does have exactly $2$.

There are $10$ choices of the short chord $\{i,i+1\}$, and each such matching contains exactly $2$ short chords, so it is counted twice. The answer is $10/2=\boxed{5}$.

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · GCD and LCM Structure

Let $N=2^8\cdot3^7\cdot5^4$. Count the ordered pairs $(a,b)$ of positive divisors of $N$ such that $ab$ is a perfect square and $\dfrac{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}{\gcd(a,b)}$ is a perfect cube.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Choose a sample pair of divisors and record the exponent of one prime in each. Follow that prime through the product, the gcd and the lcm.
Hint 2: key idea
Work with one prime at a time and call its two exponents $x$ and $y$. Express the exponent in each required perfect power using $x+y$ and the difference between the larger and smaller exponent. (LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the square and cube conditions to turn the exponent difference into a single congruence. Partition each allowed exponent range into the resulting residue classes and count the ordered choices within each class. Keep the three prime counts separate until they are ready to combine.
Common trap
The pairs are ordered, so reversing unequal exponents within a class gives a second choice.
Show the answer

$900$

Show the solution – One prime at a time

For a prime $p\in\{2,3,5\}$ let $x,y$ be the exponents of $p$ in $a,b$, with $0\le x,y\le e$ where $e=8,7,4$ respectively. The exponent of $p$ in $ab$ is $x+y$, and in $\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}{\gcd(a,b)}$ it is $\max(x,y)-\min(x,y)=|x-y|$. So the conditions are, for each prime separately, \[x+y\ \text{even}\quad\text{and}\quad 3\mid x-y,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x\equiv y\pmod 6.\] Count pairs $(x,y)$ in $\{0,\dots,e\}$ with $x\equiv y\pmod6$ by residue class: a class with $k$ members contributes $k^2$.

  • $e=8$: classes $\{0,6\},\{1,7\},\{2,8\},\{3\},\{4\},\{5\}$: $3\cdot4+3=15$.

  • $e=7$: $\{0,6\},\{1,7\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\},\{5\}$: $2\cdot4+4=12$.

  • $e=4$: five singletons: $5$.

The choices for different primes are independent, so the number of pairs is $15\cdot12\cdot5=\boxed{900}$.

Problem 9

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Explicit Bijections

Let $P$ be the number of lattice paths from $(0,0)$ to $(10,10)$ using only unit steps $U=(0,1)$ and $R=(1,0)$ that never go above the line $y=x+2$ and that touch the line $y=x+2$ at least once. Compute $P$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a short path and track the value of $y-x$ after each step. Mark the values that correspond to touching the line in the question or passing above it.
Hint 2: key idea
To count paths that touch $y=x+c$, reflect the part of the path from its start to its first contact with that line. Follow where this sends the starting point and what it does to the steps. (LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence)
Hint 3: approach
Determine the reflected starting point and explain how to reverse the reflection using the first contact. Set up a binomial count of paths from that new point to $(10,10)$. Apply the count to the line in the question and to the next parallel lattice line to isolate the allowed paths.
Common trap
A path may touch the given line and later go above it, so merely counting paths that touch includes forbidden paths.
Show the answer

$48450$

Show the solution – Reflection principle, twice

All paths have $20$ steps, $10$ of each kind.

Paths touching a line. Let $c\ge1$. A path from $(0,0)$ to $(10,10)$ touches $y=x+c$ if and only if it meets that line; reflect the part of the path up to its first point on the line in the line $y=x+c$ (this swaps $U$ and $R$ steps). The result is a path from $(-c,c)$ to $(10,10)$, and every path from $(-c,c)$ to $(10,10)$ must cross the line (it starts above it and ends below it), so the map is a bijection. Paths from $(-c,c)$ to $(10,10)$ have $10+c$ steps $R$ and $10-c$ steps $U$, so there are $\binom{20}{10-c}$ of them.

A path never goes above $y=x+2$ and touches it exactly when it touches $y=x+2$ but not $y=x+3$ (a lattice path that goes above $y=x+2$ must touch $y=x+3$, and to reach $y=x+3$ it must first touch $y=x+2$). Hence \[P=\binom{20}{8}-\binom{20}{7}=125970-77520=\boxed{48450}.\]

Problem 14

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 35: Generating Functions and Algebraic Encoding · Extraction of Coefficients

Let \[F(x)=\left(1+x+x^3\right)^6.\] When $F(x)$ is expanded, let $S$ be the sum of the coefficients of all terms $x^k$ for which $3\mid k$. Compute $S$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a smaller power of $1+x+x^3$ and mark which exponents would have their coefficients included. Notice that inclusion depends only on the exponent modulo $3$.
Hint 2: key idea
Let $\omega$ be a nonreal cube root of unity. Compare the value of $1+\omega^k+\omega^{2k}$ in the three residue classes of $k$ to see how the three polynomial evaluations select the wanted coefficients. (LPS VII, Chapter 35: Generating Functions and Algebraic Encoding)
Hint 3: approach
Set up the average of the evaluations at $1,\omega,\omega^2$. Use $\omega^3=1$ to simplify the bases at the two complex inputs, then use polar form and conjugation to handle the sixth powers before combining the evaluations.
Common trap
Use both nonreal cube roots in the average.
Show the answer

$225$

Show solution 1 – Roots of unity filter

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · Dependent and Independent Events

Two fair dice are rolled repeatedly. Maintain a “six-count” that starts at $0$. On any roll:

  • if the total is $6$, increase the six-count by $1$;

  • if the total is $7$, reset the six-count to $0$;

  • if the total is $10$, the game ends immediately with a loss;

  • any other total leaves the six-count unchanged.

The game ends immediately upon either the six-count first reaching $2$ (win) or a roll totaling $10$ (loss). If the probability of winning is $\frac mn$ in lowest terms, compute $m+n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the possible totals that affect the game. For a six-count of zero and a six-count of one, mark which of those totals change the state or end the game.
Hint 2: key idea
Use a separate winning probability for each unfinished state. Rolls that leave a state unchanged can be skipped, so condition on the next roll that changes that state or ends the game. (LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value)
Hint 3: approach
Count the ordered dice outcomes for each relevant total. Set up one equation for a six-count of zero, then another for a six-count of one that includes the chance of resetting. Use the equations to isolate the initial winning probability.
Common trap
A total of $7$ is a reset from a count of one and has no effect from a count of zero.
Show the answer

$107$ (probability $\frac{25}{82}$)

Show the solution – Two states

With two dice, $P(6)=\frac5{36}$, $P(7)=\frac6{36}$, $P(10)=\frac3{36}$. Rolls with any other total change nothing, so we may ignore them and look only at the next roll that is a $6$, $7$ or $10$; given that it is one of those, it is $6$, $7$, $10$ with probabilities proportional to $5:6:3$.

Let $W_0$, $W_1$ be the winning probabilities when the six-count is $0$, $1$.

  • At count $0$ a $7$ also changes nothing, so only $6$ (prob. $\frac58$ among $6,10$) or $10$ matters: $W_0=\frac58W_1$.

  • At count $1$: a $6$ wins, a $7$ returns to count $0$, a $10$ loses: $W_1=\frac5{14}+\frac6{14}W_0$.

Then $W_0=\frac58\left(\frac5{14}+\frac{6}{14}W_0\right)=\frac{25}{112}+\frac{30}{112}W_0$, so $82W_0=25$ and $W_0=\frac{25}{82}$. The game ends with probability $1$ (each roll ends it with probability at least $\frac{3}{36}$), and $\gcd(25,82)=1$, so $m+n=\boxed{107}$.

Problem 8-1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · Lifting the Exponent (LTE)

Let $n$ be the least positive integer such that \[2^8\cdot3^3\cdot5^2\mid 11^n-1,\] but \[2^9\nmid 11^n-1,\qquad 3^4\nmid 11^n-1,\qquad 5^3\nmid 11^n-1.\] Find $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Translate each divisibility and nondivisibility pair into a statement about the exact exponent of a prime. Compare an odd and an even exponent of $11$ modulo $3$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use lifting the exponent separately for $2$, $3$, and $5$. For the prime $3$, first use the parity of $n$ to rewrite the base as $11^2$ so that the difference of the bases is divisible by $3$. (LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent)
Hint 3: approach
Determine which parity of $n$ is possible, then derive a valuation formula for each of the three primes. Solve for the required prime exponents in $n$ and check that no additional factor is needed to meet the exact conditions.
Common trap
Use the even-exponent version of lifting the exponent for the prime $2$.
Show the answer

$2880$

Show the solution – Lifting the exponent for each prime

We need $v_2(11^n-1)=8$, $v_3(11^n-1)=3$ and $v_5(11^n-1)=2$ exactly.

$p=3$. $11\equiv2\pmod3$, so $3\mid11^n-1$ forces $n$ even. With $n=2k$, $11^n-1=121^k-1$ and $3\parallel121-1=120$, so by LTE $v_3(11^n-1)=1+v_3(k)=1+v_3(n)$. We need $v_3(n)=2$.

$p=5$. $11\equiv1\pmod5$ and $5\parallel10$, so by LTE $v_5(11^n-1)=1+v_5(n)$. We need $v_5(n)=1$.

$p=2$. For even $n$ (already required), LTE for $p=2$ gives $v_2(11^n-1)=v_2(11-1)+v_2(11+1)+v_2(n)-1=1+2+v_2(n)-1=2+v_2(n)$. We need $v_2(n)=6$.

So $n$ must be divisible by $2^6\cdot3^2\cdot5=2880$, and $n=2880$ itself has exactly these valuations. Hence the least $n$ is $\boxed{2880}$.

Problem 8-2

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Euler Totient and Related Functions

Find the sum of all positive integers $n$ such that $\varphi(n)=144$ and the congruence $x^2\equiv1\pmod n$ has exactly $16$ solutions.

The Euler totient function $\varphi(n)$ counts the number of positive integers not exceeding $n$ that are relatively prime to $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write $n$ as a product of prime powers. For a sample modulus, see what $x^2\equiv1$ tells you about the product $(x-1)(x+1)$.
Hint 2: key idea
Count roots separately for odd prime powers and for powers of $2$, using the fact that the two factors differ by $2$. Then use the Chinese remainder theorem to relate the exponent of $2$ in $n$ to the number of distinct odd primes. (LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Split into cases according to the exponent of $2$ in $n$ and use the root count to fix the number of odd primes in each case. Compare their contributions of factors of $2$ to the totient with the available exponent in $144$. Use the remaining totient factors to check the candidate odd primes and their exponents.
Common trap
For an odd root, both $x-1$ and $x+1$ are even, so the argument for odd prime powers needs a separate case at $2$.
Show the answer

$960$ ($n=456$ and $n=504$)

Show the solution – Count square roots of $1$, then match $\varphi$

Square roots of $1$. For an odd prime power $p^e$, $p^e\mid(x-1)(x+1)$ and $\gcd(x-1,x+1)\mid2$ force $x\equiv\pm1$: $2$ solutions. Modulo $2^a$ there are $1$ solution for $a\le1$, $2$ for $a=2$, and $4$ for $a\ge3$ (namely $\pm1$, $2^{a-1}\pm1$: one of $x\pm1$ is $\equiv2\pmod4$, so the other is divisible by $2^{a-1}$). By the Chinese remainder theorem, if $n=2^a\cdot m$ with $m$ odd having $k$ distinct prime factors, the number of solutions is $c_a\cdot2^k$ with $c_a\in\{1,2,4\}$ as above. So $16$ solutions means: $a\le1,k=4$; or $a=2,k=3$; or $a\ge3,k=2$.

The power of $2$ in $\varphi(n)$. $\varphi(n)=\varphi(2^a)\prod p^{e-1}(p-1)$, and $144=2^4\cdot3^2$. Each odd prime $p\mid n$ contributes $v_2(p-1)\ge1$ (the factor $p^{e-1}$ is odd), and $\varphi(2^a)$ contributes $a-1$ for $a\ge1$ and $0$ for $a=0$; the total must be exactly $4$. Also $p-1\mid144$ with $v_2(p-1)=1$ allows only $p-1\in\{2,6,18\}$, i.e. $p\in\{3,7,19\}$.

  • $a\le1$, $k=4$: four odd primes with total $v_2$ at most $4$, so each has $v_2(p-1)=1$; but only $3,7,19$ qualify. Impossible.

  • $a=2$, $k=3$: $1+\sum v_2(p-1)=4$ forces $p\in\{3,7,19\}$, all three; then $\varphi(n)\ge2\cdot2\cdot6\cdot18>144$. Impossible.

  • $a\ge3$, $k=2$: $(a-1)+\sum v_2(p-1)=4$ with two terms $\ge1$ forces $a=3$ (for $a\ge4$ the sum would exceed $4$) and $v_2(p-1)=1$ for both primes, so $p,q\in\{3,7,19\}$; moreover $\varphi(p^e)\varphi(q^f)=144/\varphi(8)=36$. For $\{3,7\}$: $2\cdot3^{e-1}\cdot6\cdot7^{f-1}=36$ gives $e=2,f=1$, $n=8\cdot9\cdot7=504$. For $\{3,19\}$: $2\cdot3^{e-1}\cdot18\cdot19^{f-1}=36$ gives $e=f=1$, $n=8\cdot3\cdot19=456$. For $\{7,19\}$: $6\cdot18>36$.

Both work ($\varphi(504)=4\cdot6\cdot6=144$, $\varphi(456)=4\cdot2\cdot18=144$, and each has $4\cdot2\cdot2=16$ square roots of $1$). The sum is $504+456=\boxed{960}$.

Problem 8-3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Implicit or Structural Correspondence

A circular word of length $8$ is a sequence $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_8)$ with each $a_i\in\{0,1,2\}$, where indices are taken modulo $8$. How many such circular words satisfy both $a_i\ne a_{i+1}$ for all $i$, and \[a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_8\equiv0\pmod3?\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a short example and check each adjacent pair, including the pair formed by the last and first positions. Record the sum of the letters modulo $3$ separately.
Hint 2: key idea
Record the clockwise differences of neighboring letters modulo $3$ to encode the adjacency condition. Then add $1$ modulo $3$ to every letter of a word and ask how that changes its sum class. (LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence)
Hint 3: approach
Express the cycle-closing condition in terms of how many of the differences are $2$, and set up a count by choosing their positions. Account for the starting letter and use the shift of all letters to relate the three sum classes.
Common trap
Include the difference from the last letter back to the first when testing whether the cycle closes.
Show the answer

$86$

Show solution 1 – Shifting all letters

All valid words. Let $d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i\pmod 3$, so each $d_i\in\{1,2\}$, and $d_1+\cdots+d_8\equiv0$. Conversely, $a_1$ and $d_1,\dots,d_7$ determine the word, and the wrap-around condition $a_1\ne a_8$ is the requirement that $d_8=-(d_1+\cdots+d_7)\not\equiv0$. Writing $d_i=1+e_i$ with $e_i\in\{0,1\}$, the condition $\sum_{i=1}^8d_i\equiv0$ becomes $\sum e_i\equiv1\pmod3$, i.e. $\sum e_i\in\{1,4,7\}$: $\binom81+\binom84+\binom87=86$ sequences $(d_1,\dots,d_8)$. With $3$ choices of $a_1$, there are $258$ valid words.

The sum condition. Adding $1$ to every letter keeps the word valid and changes the sum by $8\equiv2\pmod 3$. This map permutes the valid words and sends words with sum $\equiv0$ to sum $\equiv2$, those to sum $\equiv1$, and those back to sum $\equiv0$. So the three residue classes have the same size, and the answer is $258/3=\boxed{86}$.

Show solution 2 – One good starting letter for each difference pattern

Since consecutive letters differ, the differences $d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i\pmod 3$ ($i=1,\dots,8$, indices mod $8$) lie in $\{1,2\}$, and $d_1+\cdots+d_8\equiv a_9-a_1=0$. Conversely, $a_1$ and any $d_1,\dots,d_8\in\{1,2\}$ with $d_1+\cdots+d_8\equiv0$ give exactly one valid word (the sum condition makes the word close up at $a_9=a_1$). There are $\binom81+\binom84+\binom87=86$ admissible difference sequences (write $d_i=1+e_i$, $e_i\in\{0,1\}$; the condition is $\sum e_i\in\{1,4,7\}$).

Fix the differences. Then $a_j=a_1+D_j$ with $D_j=d_1+\cdots+d_{j-1}$, and \[\sum_{j=1}^8a_j=8a_1+\sum_jD_j\equiv2a_1+\sum_jD_j\pmod3.\] As $a_1$ runs over $0,1,2$, the value $2a_1$ runs over all residues, so exactly one choice of $a_1$ makes the sum $\equiv0$. Hence the number of words is the number of difference sequences, $\boxed{86}$.
