Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Let $x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots$ be a sequence of positive integers defined as follows: $x_1=1$ and for each $n\ge 1$ we have \[x_{n+1}=x_n+\left\lfloor\sqrt{x_n}\right\rfloor.\] Determine all positive integers $m$ for which $x_n=m^2$ for some $n\ge 1$. (Here $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the greatest integer less or equal to $x$ for every real number $x$.)

Show the answer

$m=2^k$ for $k=0,1,2,\dots$ (the powers of $2$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tracking the offset inside each square interval)

For $s\ge1$ let $I_s=\{s^2,s^2+1,\dots,s^2+2s\}$, the set of integers $x$ with $\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor=s$. While the sequence is in $I_s$, every step adds exactly $s$. Since the sequence is increasing and a step from a term of $I_s$ lands at most at $s^2+2s+s<(s+2)^2$, the sequence never jumps over a whole interval: starting from $x_1=1\in I_1$ it visits every $I_s$.

Let $s^2+e_s$ be the first term of the sequence in $I_s$ (so $0\le e_s\le 2s$). The square $m^2$ is the smallest element of $I_m$, so $m^2$ is a term if and only if $e_m=0$.

How $e_{s+1}$ is obtained from $e_s$.

  • If $e_s=0$: the terms in $I_s$ are $s^2,\ s^2+s,\ s^2+2s$, and the next term is $s^2+3s=(s+1)^2+(s-1)$. So $e_{s+1}=s-1$.

  • If $1\le e_s\le s$: the terms in $I_s$ are $s^2+e_s$ and $s^2+e_s+s\le s^2+2s$, and the next term is $s^2+e_s+2s=(s+1)^2+(e_s-1)$. So $e_{s+1}=e_s-1$.

By induction $e_s\le s$ for every $s$: $e_1=0$, and in both cases above $e_{s+1}\le s<s+1$. Hence these two cases are the only ones that occur.

The zeros of $e_s$. If $e_s=0$, then $e_{s+1}=s-1$, and the second rule lowers $e$ by one at each step until it reaches $0$: \[e_{s+1}=s-1,\ e_{s+2}=s-2,\ \dots,\ e_{2s}=0,\] with $e_t\ne0$ for $s<t<2s$ (for $s=1$ we simply get $e_2=0$). Starting from $e_1=0$, the indices $s$ with $e_s=0$ are therefore exactly $1,2,4,8,\dots$

So $x_n=m^2$ for some $n$ if and only if $m$ is a power of $2$: \[\boxed{m=2^k,\ k\ge0}\]

Solution 2 (An explicit description between consecutive squares that are reached)

Lemma. If $x_n=s^2$ for some $s\ge1$, then the terms after it are, in order, \begin{gather*}s^2+s,\quad s^2+2s,\\ \text{then } t^2-t+2s,\ t^2+2s\ \text{ for } t=s+1,s+2,\dots,2s-1,\\ \text{then } (2s)^2 .\end{gather*}

Proof. From $s^2$ we add $s$ twice, because $s^2+s<(s+1)^2$; this gives $s^2+s$ and $s^2+2s$. Since $s^2+2s<(s+1)^2$, the next term is $s^2+3s=(s+1)^2-(s+1)+2s$. In general, suppose a term equals $y=t^2-t+2s$ with $s<t\le 2s$. If $t=2s$, then $y=4s^2$ and we are done. If $t<2s$, then $t^2<y<(t+1)^2$ (because $2s>t$ and $2s<3t+1$), so the next term is $y+t=t^2+2s$. Again $t^2<t^2+2s<(t+1)^2$ (because $2s<2t+1$), so the next term is $t^2+2s+t=(t+1)^2-(t+1)+2s$, which has the same form with $t+1$ in place of $t$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

No other squares. In the list of the lemma, $s^2+s$ and $s^2+2s$ lie strictly between $s^2$ and $(s+1)^2$, and for $s<t<2s$ both $t^2-t+2s$ and $t^2+2s$ lie strictly between $t^2$ and $(t+1)^2$ (shown in the proof). So the first square after $s^2$ is $(2s)^2$.

Since $x_1=1=1^2$, applying the lemma repeatedly shows that the squares in the sequence are exactly $1^2,2^2,4^2,8^2,\dots$ Hence \[\boxed{m=2^k,\ k\ge0}\]

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Let $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ be a function satisfying the following condition: for each $k>2026$, the number $f(k)$ equals the maximum number of times a number appears in the list $f(1),f(2),\dots,f(k-1)$. Prove that \[f(n)=f(n+f(n))\] for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

(Here $\mathbb{N}$ denotes the set $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ of positive integers.)

Show the solution

Solution (Long blocks of equal values)

For $k\ge2$ let $M_k$ be the largest number of times a value occurs in the list $f(1),\dots,f(k-1)$. The hypothesis says $f(k)=M_k$ for $k\ge2027$.

Step 1: $f$ is non-decreasing from $2027$ on, with steps $0$ or $1$. Passing from the list $f(1),\dots,f(k-1)$ to the list $f(1),\dots,f(k)$ increases one multiplicity (that of $f(k)$) by $1$ and leaves the others unchanged. Hence $M_{k+1}\in\{M_k,M_k+1\}$, so $f(k+1)-f(k)\in\{0,1\}$ for $k\ge2027$.

Step 2: $f$ is unbounded. Otherwise, by Step 1, $f(k)=c$ for all large $k$. Then $c$ occurs infinitely often, so $M_k\to\infty$, contradicting $f(k)=M_k=c$. Together with Step 1, every integer $v\ge f(2027)$ is a value $f(k)$ with $k\ge2027$.

Step 3: a block of length $v+1$. Let $V=\max\{f(1),\dots,f(2027)\}$ and let $v>V$ be an integer. Let $k$ be the smallest index with $f(k)=v$. Then $k>2027$ (the first $2027$ values are at most $V<v$), and by the choice of $k$ the value $v$ does not occur among $f(1),\dots,f(k-1)$. Since $f(k)=M_k=v$, some value occurs exactly $v$ times among $f(1),\dots,f(k-1)$, and no value occurs more often.

We claim $f(j)=v$ for $k\le j\le k+v$. Suppose $f(k)=\dots=f(j-1)=v$ for some $j$ with $k<j\le k+v$. The list $f(1),\dots,f(j-1)$ is the list $f(1),\dots,f(k-1)$ followed by $j-k$ copies of $v$. In it, every value other than $v$ occurs at most $v$ times (its multiplicity did not change), $v$ occurs $j-k\le v$ times, and some value still occurs $v$ times. So $M_j=v$, that is $f(j)=v$. By induction the claim holds.

Conclusion. For $n=k$ we get $f(n)=v$ and $f(n+f(n))=f(k+v)=v=f(n)$. Different values $v>V$ give different indices $k$, so there are infinitely many $n$ with $f(n)=f(n+f(n))$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled scalene triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and $N$ be the midpoint of the minor arc $BC$ of $\Gamma$. Points $P$ and $Q$ lie on segments $AB$ and $AC$ respectively such that $BP=BN$ and $CQ=CN$. Point $K\ne N$ lies on line $AN$ with $MK=MN$. Prove that $\angle PKQ=90^\circ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates on the angle bisector, Ptolemy and the law of cosines)

Write $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $\alpha=\angle BAC$, and let $R$ be the circumradius. Since $N$ is the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, the line $AN$ bisects $\angle BAC$ and $BN=CN=:t$. Then $BP=t$ and $CQ=t$, so $AP=c-t$ and $AQ=b-t$.

Three metric facts.

  • 1. $\angle NBC=\angle NAC=\frac\alpha2$ and $\angle NCB=\angle NAB=\frac\alpha2$ (inscribed angles), so the isosceles triangle $BNC$ gives $a=2t\cos\frac\alpha2$, that is $a^2=2t^2(1+\cos\alpha)$.

  • 2. Ptolemy's theorem in the cyclic quadrilateral $ABNC$ gives $AN\cdot a=c\cdot t+b\cdot t$, so $AN=\dfrac{t(b+c)}{a}=\dfrac{b+c}{2\cos\frac\alpha2}$.

  • 3. $AK=AN\cos\alpha$. Indeed, $O$, $M$, $N$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, with $M$ between $O$ and $N$ (the triangle is acute, so $O$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$). As $\angle BOM=\alpha$, we get $OM=R\cos\alpha$ and $MN=R-R\cos\alpha$. The circle with centre $M$ through $N$ meets line $AN$ again at $K$, and $NK$ is twice the projection of $NM$ on that line: $NK=2\,MN\cos\angle MNA$. In the isosceles triangle $OAN$ ($OA=ON=R$) we have $\cos\angle ONA=\frac{AN}{2R}$, and $\angle MNA=\angle ONA$. Hence $NK=AN(1-\cos\alpha)<AN$, so $K$ lies on segment $AN$ and $AK=AN\cos\alpha$.

The dot product. Take $A$ as origin and the ray $AN$ as the positive $x$-axis, with $B$ above it. Then \begin{gather*}P=(c-t)\Bigl(\cos\tfrac\alpha2,\sin\tfrac\alpha2\Bigr),\qquad Q=(b-t)\Bigl(\cos\tfrac\alpha2,-\sin\tfrac\alpha2\Bigr),\\ K=(k,0),\quad k=AN\cos\alpha .\end{gather*} Using $\cos^2\frac\alpha2-\sin^2\frac\alpha2=\cos\alpha$, \[E:=(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=k^2-k\cos\tfrac\alpha2\,(b+c-2t)+(c-t)(b-t)\cos\alpha .\] By fact 2, $k\cos\frac\alpha2=\frac12(b+c)\cos\alpha$ and $k^2=\dfrac{(b+c)^2\cos^2\alpha}{2(1+\cos\alpha)}$. Substituting and expanding $(c-t)(b-t)=bc-t(b+c)+t^2$, the terms $\pm t(b+c)\cos\alpha$ cancel and \[E=\frac{(b+c)^2\cos^2\alpha}{2(1+\cos\alpha)}-\frac{(b+c)^2\cos\alpha}{2}+(bc+t^2)\cos\alpha .\] Multiplying by $\frac{2(1+\cos\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}$ (recall $\cos\alpha>0$): \begin{align*}\frac{2(1+\cos\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}\,E&=-(b+c)^2+2bc(1+\cos\alpha)+2t^2(1+\cos\alpha)\\&=-b^2-c^2+2bc\cos\alpha+a^2=0\end{align*} by fact 1 and the law of cosines. So $(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=0$.

Finally $K\ne P$ and $K\ne Q$: $K$ lies on the bisector strictly between $A$ and $N$, which meets the lines $AB$ and $AC$ only at $A$. Therefore $\angle PKQ=90^\circ$.

Solution 2 (Reflecting N in BC)

Let $t=BN=CN$, $\alpha=\angle BAC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and for points $X\ne Y$ let $u_{XY}$ be the unit vector from $X$ towards $Y$. Let $N'$ be the reflection of $N$ in $M$. As $MN\perp BC$, $N'$ is also the reflection of $N$ in $BC$, and $BNCN'$ is a rhombus with side $t$; in vectors $N'-B=C-N$ and $N'-C=B-N$.

Step 1: $(P-N')\cdot(Q-N')=0$. We have $P-B=t\,u_{BA}$ and $N'-B=t\,u_{NC}$, so $P-N'=t(u_{BA}-u_{NC})$; similarly $Q-N'=t(u_{CA}-u_{NB})$. Now

  • $u_{BA}\cdot u_{CA}=\cos\alpha$ (both vectors point into $A$ along its sides);

  • $u_{NC}\cdot u_{NB}=\cos\angle BNC=\cos(180^\circ-\alpha)=-\cos\alpha$;

  • $u_{BA}\cdot u_{NB}=-u_{BA}\cdot u_{BN}=-\cos\angle ABN$ and $u_{NC}\cdot u_{CA}=-u_{CN}\cdot u_{CA}=-\cos\angle ACN$.

Hence \[\frac{(P-N')\cdot(Q-N')}{t^2}=\cos\alpha+\cos\angle ABN+\cos\angle ACN-\cos\alpha=0,\] because $ABNC$ is cyclic with $B$, $C$ opposite, so $\angle ABN+\angle ACN=180^\circ$.

Step 2: $K$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $N'$ to $AN$. The circle with centre $M$ through $N$ has diameter $NN'$. The point $K\ne N$ lies on it and on line $AN$; by Thales, either $K=N'$ or $\angle NKN'=90^\circ$. In both cases $K$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $N'$ to line $AN$.

Step 3. Put $w=N'-K$, a vector perpendicular to $AN$. Using Step 1, \[(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=\bigl((P-N')+w\bigr)\cdot\bigl((Q-N')+w\bigr)=w\cdot\bigl(P+Q-N'-K\bigr).\] Let $d(X)$ be the signed distance from a point $X$ to line $AN$, positive on the side of $B$. Because $w$ is normal to $AN$ and $K\in AN$, we have $w\cdot(X-K)=\pm|w|\,d(X)$ for every $X$ (one fixed sign), so \[(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=\pm|w|\bigl(d(P)+d(Q)-d(N')\bigr).\] The function $d$ is affine, $d(A)=d(N)=0$, $d(B)=c\sin\frac\alpha2$ and $d(C)=-b\sin\frac\alpha2$ ($AN$ bisects angle $A$). As $AP=c-t$ and $AQ=b-t$, \[d(P)+d(Q)=\frac{c-t}{c}\,d(B)+\frac{b-t}{b}\,d(C)=(c-t)\sin\tfrac\alpha2-(b-t)\sin\tfrac\alpha2=(c-b)\sin\tfrac\alpha2 ,\] and since $N'=B+C-N$, also $d(N')=d(B)+d(C)-d(N)=(c-b)\sin\frac\alpha2$. Therefore $(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=0$.

(If $w=0$, i.e. $K=N'$, the product is $0$ directly.)

Finally $K\ne A$: the point of the circumcircle nearest to $M$ is $N$ (because $M$ lies on segment $ON$: the triangle is acute, so $O$ and $N$ are on opposite sides of $BC$), so $MA>MN=MK$. The lines $AB$ and $AC$ meet line $AN$ only at $A$, hence $K\ne P$ and $K\ne Q$, and $(P-K)\cdot(Q-K)=0$ means $\angle PKQ=90^\circ$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Two integers $a$ and $b$ are called companions if every prime number $p$ either divides both or none of $a$, $b$. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{N}_0\to\mathbb{N}_0$ such that $f(0)=0$ and the numbers $f(m)+n$ and $f(n)+m$ are companions for all $m,n\in\mathbb{N}_0$.

(Here $\mathbb{N}_0$ denotes the set of all non-negative integers.)

Show the answer

$f(n)=n$ for all $n\in\mathbb N_0$

Show the solution

Solution (Primes dividing differences of values)

The identity works: for $f(n)=n$ the numbers $f(m)+n$ and $f(n)+m$ are equal, hence companions.

Now let $f$ satisfy the conditions and fix a prime $p$. The condition says: for all $m,n\ge0$, \[p\mid f(m)+n\iff p\mid f(n)+m. \tag{1}\]

Claim. If $p\mid f(m)-f(m')$, then $p\mid m-m'$.

Choose $n\ge0$ with $n\equiv-f(m)\pmod p$. Then $p\mid f(m)+n$, and also $p\mid f(m')+n$ because $f(m')\equiv f(m)$. By (1), $p\mid f(n)+m$ and $p\mid f(n)+m'$; subtracting, $p\mid m-m'$.

Conclusion. Since the Claim holds for every prime $p$:

  • no prime divides $f(m+1)-f(m)$, and $f(m+1)\ne f(m)$ (otherwise every prime would divide $1$); hence $f(m+1)-f(m)=\pm1$;

  • $f(m+2)\ne f(m)$, since otherwise every prime would divide $2$.

Now $f(0)=0$ and $f(1)=f(0)\pm1\ge0$ give $f(1)=1$. If $f(n-1)=n-1$ and $f(n)=n$, then $f(n+1)\in\{n-1,n+1\}$, and $f(n+1)=n-1=f(n-1)$ is excluded; so $f(n+1)=n+1$. By induction \[\boxed{f(n)=n\ \text{ for all } n\ge0}\]

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

Three lines $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ form an acute angled triangle $\mathcal{T}$ in the plane. Point $P$ lies in the interior of $\mathcal{T}$. Let $\tau_i$ denote the transformation of the plane such that the image $\tau_i(X)$ of any point $X$ in the plane is the reflection of $X$ in $\ell_i$, for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}$. Denote by $P_{ijk}$ the point $\tau_k(\tau_j(\tau_i(P)))$ for each permutation $(i,j,k)$ of $(1,2,3)$.

Prove that $P_{123},P_{132},P_{213},P_{231},P_{312},P_{321}$ are concyclic if and only if $P$ coincides with the orthocentre of $\mathcal{T}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Complex numbers)

Name the vertices $A=\ell_2\cap\ell_3$, $B=\ell_3\cap\ell_1$, $C=\ell_1\cap\ell_2$, let $H$ be the orthocentre and $D,E,F$ the feet of the altitudes on $BC,CA,AB$. We use two classical facts about an acute triangle: the orthic triangle $DEF$ has incentre $H$, and its excentres are $A,B,C$ (the altitudes bisect the angles of $DEF$ and the sides of $ABC$ are the external bisectors).

Put the circumcircle on the unit circle, write lower-case letters for complex coordinates and $\bar z$ for conjugates. The reflection in the line through two points $u,v$ of the unit circle is $z\mapsto u+v-uv\bar z$.

The six points. For a permutation $(i,j,k)$ let $x=\ell_i\cap\ell_j$, $y=\ell_j\cap\ell_k$, $z=\ell_k\cap\ell_i$ (three vertices; $\ell_i=xz$, $\ell_j=xy$, $\ell_k=yz$). Then \[\tau_i(p)=x+z-xz\bar p,\qquad \tau_j\tau_i(p)=x+y-xy\Bigl(\frac1x+\frac1z-\frac{p}{xz}\Bigr)=x-\frac{xy}{z}+\frac yz\,p,\] \[P_{ijk}=y+z-yz\Bigl(\frac1x-\frac{z}{xy}+\frac zy\,\bar p\Bigr)=-z^2\bar p+y+z+\frac{z(z-y)}{x}.\] Swapping $i$ and $k$ swaps $x$ and $y$ and keeps $z$. So the six points form three pairs, one for each vertex $z$: \begin{gather*}\Pi_z^{\pm}=-z^2\bar p+s_z\pm t_z,\\ s_z=\tfrac12\Bigl(x+y+2z+\frac{z(z-y)}{x}+\frac{z(z-x)}{y}\Bigr),\qquad t_z=\frac{(y-x)(z-x)(z-y)}{2xy}.\end{gather*}

Three properties.

  • $\sigma_z:=\dfrac{s_z}{z}=\dfrac12\Bigl(\dfrac{x+y}{z}+2+\dfrac zx+\dfrac zy-\dfrac yx-\dfrac xy\Bigr)$ equals its conjugate (conjugation swaps $\frac{x+y}{z}$ with $\frac zx+\frac zy$ and fixes $\frac yx+\frac xy$), so it is real.

  • $\dfrac{t_z}{iz}=\dfrac{(y-x)(z-x)(z-y)}{2ixyz}$ also equals its conjugate, so $t_z$ is a real multiple of $iz$; and $|t_z|=\frac12|x-y|\,|y-z|\,|z-x|=:\tau$ is the same for all three vertices.

  • Define the real number $r_z(P)=\dfrac pz+z\bar p-\sigma_z=2\,\mathrm{Re}(p\bar z)-\sigma_z$. Then \[\Pi_z^{\pm}-p=-z\,r_z(P)\pm t_z ,\] a sum of a vector parallel to $z$ and a vector parallel to $iz$. Moreover $r_z(P)$ is twice the signed distance from $P$ to the line $\lambda_z=\{X:\ 2\,\mathrm{Re}(X\bar z)=\sigma_z\}$.

The foot of the altitude from $x$ is $\frac12\bigl(x+y+z-\frac{yz}{x}\bigr)$; substituting it into $\frac Xz+z\bar X$ gives exactly $\sigma_z$, and the same holds for the foot from $y$. So $\lambda_z$ is the side of the orthic triangle joining the feet of the altitudes from $x$ and $y$ (the two feet on the sides through $z$).

Concyclic $\Rightarrow$ $P=H$. Suppose the six points lie on a circle with centre $W$. The two points $\Pi_z^\pm$ are distinct ($t_z\ne0$), so $W$ lies on their perpendicular bisector, which is the line through their midpoint $p-z\,r_z(P)$ parallel to $z$; this line passes through $P$. The three lines through $P$ parallel to $a$, $b$, $c$ are distinct (no two vertices are diametrically opposite, as the triangle is acute), so $W=P$. Now $|\Pi_z^\pm-P|^2=r_z(P)^2+\tau^2$, and equal radii give $|r_a(P)|=|r_b(P)|=|r_c(P)|$: $P$ is equidistant from the three side lines of the orthic triangle. Hence $P$ is $H$ or one of $A,B,C$; as $P$ is interior, $P=H$.

$P=H$ $\Rightarrow$ concyclic. $H$ is equidistant from the three sides of $DEF$, so $|r_a(H)|=|r_b(H)|=|r_c(H)|=:r$ and all six points are at distance $\sqrt{r^2+\tau^2}$ from $H$. (Directly: $r_z(H)=\frac{(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)}{2xyz}$, symmetric in the vertices.)

Therefore the six points are concyclic if and only if $P$ is the orthocentre.

Solution 2 (Unfolding along the orthic triangle)

Name the vertices $A=\ell_2\cap\ell_3$, $B=\ell_3\cap\ell_1$, $C=\ell_1\cap\ell_2$; let $D,E,F$ be the feet of the altitudes on $BC=\ell_1$, $CA=\ell_2$, $AB=\ell_3$ (interior points of the sides, as $\mathcal T$ is acute). Recall $\angle BDF=\angle CDE=\angle A$, $\angle CED=\angle AEF=\angle B$, $\angle AFE=\angle BFD=\angle C$ (from the cyclic quadrilaterals $ACDF$, $ABDE$, $BCEF$). Consequently the altitudes bisect the angles of triangle $DEF$, the sides of $\mathcal T$ are its external bisectors, $H$ is its incentre and $A,B,C$ are its excentres. Let $L=DE+EF+FD$.

Lemma. Let $g=\tau_3\tau_2\tau_1$ (so $P_{123}=g(P)$) and let $u$ be the unit vector from $F$ to $D$. Then $g(X)=\rho(X)-Lu$ for all $X$, where $\rho$ is the reflection in line $DF$.

Proof. Let $u'$, $u''$ be the unit vectors from $D$ to $E$ and from $E$ to $F$. Since $\angle BDF=\angle CDE$, the reflection $\tau_1$ in $BC$ maps the ray from $D$ through $F$ onto the ray from $D$ opposite to $E$; as $\tau_1$ fixes $D$, it maps $D+\lambda u$ to $D+\lambda u'$ for every real $\lambda$. In the same way $\tau_2(E+\lambda u')=E+\lambda u''$ and $\tau_3(F+\lambda u'')=F+\lambda u$. For a point $X=F+su=D+(s-FD)u$ of line $DF$: \begin{gather*}\tau_1X=D+(s-FD)u'=E+(s-FD-DE)u',\\ \tau_2\tau_1X=F+(s-FD-DE-EF)u'',\qquad gX=F+(s-L)u .\end{gather*} So $g$ moves every point of line $DF$ by $-Lu$. The map $X\mapsto g(X)+Lu$ is an isometry reversing orientation (three reflections and a translation) that fixes every point of line $DF$; an isometry fixing two points is the identity or the reflection in the line through them, and it reverses orientation, so it is $\rho$. $\square$

Since $u$ is parallel to the axis, $\rho$ commutes with the translation by $Lu$, and $g^{-1}=\tau_1\tau_2\tau_3$ is $X\mapsto\rho(X)+Lu$. Hence \[P_{123}=\rho(P)-Lu,\qquad P_{321}=\rho(P)+Lu .\] The same argument for the other orders gives: for each vertex $V$, the two points $P_{ijk}$, $P_{kji}$ with $\ell_i\cap\ell_k=V$ are $\rho_V(P)\mp L\,u_V$, where $\rho_V$ is the reflection in the side of $DEF$ joining the two feet on the sides through $V$, and $u_V$ is a unit vector along that side; the length $L$ is the same for all three pairs.

The centre must be $P$. Suppose the six points lie on a circle with centre $W$. The two points of the pair at $V$ are distinct, so $W$ is on their perpendicular bisector: the line through $\rho_V(P)$ perpendicular to $u_V$. This line also contains $P$, because $\rho_V(P)$ is the mirror image of $P$ in a line parallel to $u_V$. The three sides of $DEF$ are pairwise non-parallel, so we get three distinct lines through $P$, and $W=P$.

Equal radii. The vector $P-\rho_V(P)$ is perpendicular to $u_V$, so \[|P-P_{ijk}|^2=|P-\rho_V(P)|^2+L^2=4\,\delta_V^2+L^2 ,\] where $\delta_V$ is the distance from $P$ to the corresponding side of $DEF$. Thus the six points are concyclic (necessarily with centre $P$) if and only if $P$ is equidistant from the three side lines of $DEF$, that is, $P$ is the incentre $H$ or one of the excentres $A,B,C$. Since $P$ is an interior point, this happens exactly when $P=H$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Two decks $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ of $40$ cards each are placed on a table at noon. Every minute thereafter, we pick the top cards $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $b\in\mathcal{B}$ and perform a duel.

For any two cards $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $b\in\mathcal{B}$, each time $a$ and $b$ duel, the outcome remains the same and is independent of all other duels. A duel has three possible outcomes:

  • If a card wins, it is placed back at the top of its deck and the losing card is placed at the bottom of its deck.

  • If $a$ and $b$ are evenly matched, they are both removed from their respective decks.

  • If $a$ and $b$ do not interact with each other, then both are placed at the bottom of their respective decks.

The process ends when both decks are empty. A process is called a game if it ends. Prove that the maximum time a game can last equals $356$ hours.

Show the answer

$356$ hours ($21360$ minutes)

Show the solution

Solution (Phases between two ties)

Let $n=40$. Call a duel a tie if the two cards are evenly matched. Only ties remove cards, one from each deck, so both decks always have the same size, and a game consists of exactly $n$ ties. For $k=n,n-1,\dots,1$, phase $k$ is the stretch of duels while each deck has $k$ cards; it ends with a tie. The game lasts as many minutes as there are duels.

Upper bound: phase $k$ has at most $k^2-k+1$ duels. During phase $k$ a non-tie duel only moves the top card of one or both decks to the bottom. Hence the cyclic order of each deck does not change during the phase, and the state of both decks is determined by the pair $(a,b)$ of top cards. If a pair $(a,b)$ were on top twice in the same phase, the whole state would repeat, and since the process is deterministic it would repeat forever and the game would not end. So the pairs on top during phase $k$ are distinct.

The $2k$ cards present in phase $k$ are all removed later, by ties among themselves, so they split into $k$ evenly matched pairs. Each of these $k$ pairs, if it ever came to the top, would tie at once. Hence every duel of phase $k$ except the last one uses one of the other $k^2-k$ pairs, and phase $k$ has at most $k^2-k+1$ duels. In total a game lasts at most \[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k^2-k+1)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6-\frac{n(n+1)}2+n=\frac{n^3+2n}3\] minutes. For $n=40$ this is $\frac{64000+80}{3}=21360$ minutes, i.e. $356$ hours.

A game attaining the bound. Number the cards of each deck $1,\dots,n$ from top to bottom, and let the outcome of a duel between $a=i\in\mathcal A$ and $b=j\in\mathcal B$ be \[\text{tie if } i+j=n+1,\qquad \text{no interaction if } i+j=n,\qquad \text{$a$ wins otherwise.}\] Claim. Suppose that at the start of a phase deck $\mathcal A$ is $1,2,\dots,k$ and deck $\mathcal B$ is $n-k+1,\dots,n$ (top to bottom). Then the phase has exactly $k^2-k+1$ duels, and at its end deck $\mathcal A$ is $1,\dots,k-1$ and deck $\mathcal B$ is $n-k+2,\dots,n$.

Write $j=b-(n-k)\in\{1,\dots,k\}$ for the cards of $\mathcal B$; the rule becomes: tie if $i+j=k+1$, no interaction if $i+j=k$, $a$ wins otherwise. When $a$ wins, only $\mathcal B$ rotates. (For $k=1$ the phase consists of the tie alone.) Suppose $a=i\le k-1$ is on top of $\mathcal A$ and $j=k-i+2$ (read cyclically, so $j=1$ when $i=1$) is on top of $\mathcal B$. Then $\mathcal B$ rotates through $j=k-i+2,\dots,k,1,\dots,k-i$; the first of the special values $k-i$, $k-i+1$ reached is $k-i$ (no interaction). So card $i$ meets $k-1$ cards (all except $j=k-i+1$), after which both decks rotate and card $i+1$ faces $j=k-i+1=k-(i+1)+2$, the same situation with $i+1$. At the start $i=1$, $j=1$, as required. Finally card $i=k$ faces $j=2$; since $k+j=k$ is impossible, $\mathcal B$ rotates through $2,\dots,k$ and card $k$ ties with $j=1$ after $k$ duels. In total the phase has $(k-1)(k-1)+k=k^2-k+1$ duels. Just before the tie, $\mathcal A$ is $k,1,\dots,k-1$ and $\mathcal B$ is $j=1,2,\dots,k$; removing the top cards leaves exactly the decks of the claim with $k-1$.

Starting from both decks $1,\dots,n$ (the case $k=n$ of the claim), the game ends after $\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k^2-k+1)=21360$ minutes. Hence the maximum length of a game is $21360$ minutes $=\boxed{356\text{ hours}}$.
