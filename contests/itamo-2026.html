Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 16: Diophantine Equations: Linear and Structural Foundations · Modeling with Integer Constraints

Andromeda has a number of special coins worth $3$, $5$ or $7$ dollars respectively. She would like to buy snacks from a vending machine that doesn't offer change. With the coins she has, in order to buy a $29$ dollar snack, she has to pay $32$ dollars. What's the minimum amount that Andromeda has to spend for a $24$ dollar snack?

Show the answer

$25$ dollars

Show the solution – Recovering the purse

The statement means: with her coins Andromeda can pay exactly $32$ dollars, but she cannot pay exactly $29$, $30$ or $31$ dollars (otherwise she would pay less for the $29$ dollar snack).

Take a set $Q$ of her coins worth exactly $32$ dollars. If $Q$ contained a $3$-dollar coin, removing it would leave exactly $29$ dollars, which is impossible. So $Q$ consists of $5$- and $7$-dollar coins: $5u+7v=32$. Then $7v\equiv 32\equiv 2\pmod 5$, so $v\equiv 1\pmod 5$, and $7v\le 32$ forces $v=1$, $u=5$. Hence Andromeda owns at least five $5$-dollar coins and at least one $7$-dollar coin.

She owns nothing else:

  • a $3$-dollar coin would give $3+5+5+5+5+7=30$;

  • a sixth $5$-dollar coin would give $6\cdot 5=30$;

  • a second $7$-dollar coin would give $7+7+5+5+5=29$;

and each of these amounts is forbidden. So her coins are exactly five $5$-dollar coins and one $7$-dollar coin, and the amounts she can pay are $5u$ and $5u+7$ with $0\le u\le 5$.

For the $24$ dollar snack she cannot pay exactly $24$ (it is not a multiple of $5$, and neither is $24-7=17$), but she can pay $25=5\cdot 5$. So the minimum amount she has to spend is $\boxed{25}$ dollars.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Consider a circle of center $O$ and let $V$ be a point outside the circle. Points $A$ and $B$ are on the circle such that $VA$ and $VB$ are tangents. Let $K$ be the intersection of $VO$ with the circle. If the distance from $V$ to $AB$ is $10$, show that the length of segment $VK$ is greater than $5$.

Show solution 1 – A short computation

Let $r$ be the radius and $d=VO>r$. By symmetry about line $VO$, the chord $AB$ is perpendicular to $VO$ and meets it at a point $M$, so the distance from $V$ to $AB$ is $VM=10$.

In triangle $OAV$ the angle at $A$ is right (a radius is perpendicular to the tangent) and $AM$ is the altitude, so $OA^2=OM\cdot OV$, that is, $OM=r^2/d$. Hence \[10=VM=d-\frac{r^2}{d}=\frac{(d-r)(d+r)}{d}.\] The point $K$ of segment $VO$ on the circle satisfies $VK=d-r$, so \[VK=d-r=\frac{10d}{d+r}>\frac{10d}{2d}=5,\] because $r<d$. (The other intersection point of line $VO$ with the circle is at distance $d+r>d>VM=10$ from $V$.)

Show solution 2 – K is the incenter of VAB

Let $M$ be the point where $AB$ meets $VO$; by symmetry $AB\perp VO$, so $VM=10$. The point $K$ of segment $VO$ on the circle is the midpoint of the arc $AB$ facing $V$.

By the tangent-chord angle, $\angle VAK=\angle ABK$, and since $KA=KB$, $\angle ABK=\angle BAK$. So $AK$ bisects $\angle VAB$. Since $VK$ bisects $\angle AVB$ by symmetry, $K$ is the incenter of triangle $VAB$, and its inradius is $\rho=KM$, the distance from $K$ to $AB$.

Let $T$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $K$ to $VA$; then $KT=\rho$ and $VK$ is the hypotenuse of the right triangle $VTK$, so $VK>KT=KM$. Since $K$ lies inside triangle $VAB$, it lies between $V$ and $M$, and \[2\,VK>VK+KM=VM=10,\] so $VK>5$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Prime Factorization and Structure

  • (a) Find all pairs of different positive integers $(n,m)$ such that there exists a positive integer $a$ with the property that $n+a$ and $m+a$ have the same smallest prime divisor.

  • (b) Find all pairs of different positive integers $(n,m)$ such that there exists a positive integer $a$ with the property that the smallest prime divisor of $n+a$ coincides with the largest prime divisor of $m+a$.

Show the answer

(a) the pairs with $n-m$ even; (b) the pairs with $|n-m|\ge 2$

Show the solution – Parity, then a smooth number

Write $p(x)$ and $P(x)$ for the smallest and the largest prime divisor of an integer $x\ge 2$; note that $n+a\ge 2$ and $m+a\ge 2$ for every positive integer $a$.

(a) If $n-m$ is even, choose $a\in\{1,2\}$ with $n+a$ even. Then $m+a=(n+a)-(n-m)$ is even as well, and $p(n+a)=p(m+a)=2$.

If $n-m$ is odd, then for every $a$ the numbers $n+a$ and $m+a$ have different parity. The even one has smallest prime divisor $2$ and the odd one does not, so $p(n+a)\ne p(m+a)$.

So the answer to (a) is: all pairs with $n-m$ even (that is, $n$ and $m$ of the same parity).

(b) Necessity. If $p(n+a)=P(m+a)=p$, then $p$ divides both $n+a$ and $m+a$, hence $p\mid m-n$. Therefore $|m-n|\ge 2$.

Sufficiency. Let $|m-n|\ge 2$, let $p$ be a prime divisor of $m-n$, and write $m-n=pe$ with $e$ a nonzero integer (possibly negative). Let $Q$ be the product of the primes $q<p$ that do not divide $e$ (with $Q=1$ if there are none). For an integer $t\ge 1$ put \[M=p^tQ,\qquad y=M-e,\qquad a=py-n .\] Taking $t$ large enough we get $y\ge 1$ and $a\ge 1$. Then \[n+a=py,\qquad m+a=py+pe=pM=p^{t+1}Q .\]

  • All prime factors of $Q$ are less than $p$, so $P(m+a)=p$.

  • Let $q<p$ be a prime. If $q\mid e$, then $q\nmid Q$, so $q\nmid M$ and $q\nmid M-e=y$. If $q\nmid e$, then $q\mid M$, so $y=M-e\equiv -e\not\equiv 0\pmod q$. In both cases $q\nmid y$. Hence $y$ has no prime factor below $p$, and $p(n+a)=p(py)=p$.

So $p(n+a)=P(m+a)$, and the answer to (b) is: all pairs with $|n-m|\ge 2$.

Problem 4

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Geometric Reflection and Isometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AC>AB$ and let $D$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$. The line through $B$ perpendicular to $AD$ intersects the line through $C$ parallel to $AD$ in point $E$. The line parallel to $AB$ passing through $E$ intersects $AC$ in $F$. Prove that $\angle AFD=90^\circ$.

Show solution 1 – Reflect B in the bisector

Since $D$ is the midpoint of arc $BC$, the line $AD$ bisects $\angle BAC$ and $DB=DC$. Let $B'$ be the reflection of $B$ in the line $AD$. Then $B'$ lies on ray $AC$ with $AB'=AB<AC$, so $B'$ lies strictly between $A$ and $C$. Also $BB'\perp AD$, so $B'$ lies on the line $BE$. The line $BE$ meets line $AC$ only at $B'$, and the line $CE$ meets line $AC$ only at $C$; hence $E\ne B'$ and $E\ne C$.

Step 1: $FE=FB'$. The lines $AB$ and $FE$ are parallel, $A,F$ lie on a line through $B'$ (line $AC$), and $B,E$ lie on another line through $B'$ (line $BE$). So the homothety with center $B'$ taking $A$ to $F$ takes $B$ to $E$, and triangle $B'FE$ is similar to triangle $B'AB$. As $AB'=AB$, we get $FB'=FE$.

Step 2: $F$ is the midpoint of $B'C$. Since $BE\perp AD$ and $CE\parallel AD$, the angle $B'EC$ is right. By Thales, the midpoint $N$ of $B'C$ satisfies $NB'=NE$. The points of line $AC$ equidistant from $B'$ and $E$ are the points where the perpendicular bisector of $B'E$ meets line $AC$; there is only one such point, because $B'E$ is not perpendicular to $AC$ (it is perpendicular to $AD$, which is not parallel to $AC$). By Step 1, $F$ is such a point, so $F=N$.

Step 3. Since $D$ lies on the mirror $AD$, $DB'=DB=DC$. So $D$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $B'C$, which is the line through $F$ perpendicular to $AC$. Finally $D\ne F$ ($D$ is on the circumcircle, while $F$ lies inside the chord $AC$) and $F\ne A$, so $\angle AFD=90^\circ$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates along the bisector

Put $A$ at the origin with the bisector $AD$ along the positive $x$-axis, let $\angle BAC=2\alpha$ ($0<\alpha<90^\circ$), $AB=b$, $AC=c$, $b<c$: \[B=b(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha),\qquad C=c(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha).\] The line through $B$ perpendicular to $AD$ is $x=b\cos\alpha$, and the line through $C$ parallel to $AD$ is $y=c\sin\alpha$, so $E=(b\cos\alpha,\,c\sin\alpha)$.

The line through $E$ parallel to $AB$ consists of the points $E+t(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha)$, and line $AC$ of the points $s(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$. Equating coordinates (and dividing by $\cos\alpha$, $\sin\alpha\ne 0$): $b+t=s$ and $c-t=s$, so $s=\frac{b+c}{2}$ and \[F=\frac{b+c}{2}(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha).\] The point $D=(x,0)$ satisfies $DB=DC$: \[(x-b\cos\alpha)^2+b^2\sin^2\alpha=(x-c\cos\alpha)^2+c^2\sin^2\alpha\iff 2x\cos\alpha\,(c-b)=c^2-b^2,\] so $x=\dfrac{b+c}{2\cos\alpha}$ (here $b\ne c$ is used). Then \[D-F=\frac{b+c}{2}\left(\frac1{\cos\alpha}-\cos\alpha,\;-\sin\alpha\right)=\frac{(b+c)\sin\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha},\;-1\right),\] whose dot product with the direction $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ of $AC$ is $\frac{(b+c)\sin\alpha}{2}(\sin\alpha-\sin\alpha)=0$. Since $D\ne F$ and $F\ne A$, $\angle AFD=90^\circ$.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Density and Averaging Arguments

A set of positive integers is called spaced if no difference of any two of its elements lies in the set $\{5,10,12,17\}$. What is the largest amount of elements a spaced subset of the set $\{1,2,\dots,2026\}$ could have?

Show the answer

$646$

Show the solution – Working modulo 22

The key observation is $5+17=10+12=22$: modulo $22$ the forbidden differences are exactly $\pm5$ and $\pm10$.

Upper bound. Let $W$ be a block of $22$ consecutive integers and $S$ a spaced set. The elements of $W$ have distinct residues modulo $22$. If $x<y$ are in $W$ and $y-x\equiv\pm5$ or $\pm10\pmod{22}$, then $0<y-x<22$ forces $y-x\in\{5,17,10,12\}$. So the residues of $S\cap W$ contain no two residues differing by $\pm5$ or $\pm10$ modulo $22$.

Since $\gcd(5,22)=1$, every residue is $5k$ for exactly one $k\in\mathbb Z_{22}$. Two residues $5k$ and $5l$ differ by $\pm5$ or $\pm10$ exactly when $k-l\equiv\pm1$ or $\pm2\pmod{22}$. Arrange $\mathbb Z_{22}$ around a circle: then among any three cyclically consecutive values $k,k+1,k+2$ at most one is used by $S\cap W$. There are $22$ such triples and each $k$ lies in exactly $3$ of them, so $3\,|S\cap W|\le 22$, that is, $|S\cap W|\le 7$.

Now $2026=92\cdot 22+2$. The blocks $\{1,\dots,22\},\{23,\dots,44\},\dots,\{2003,\dots,2024\}$ contain at most $7$ elements each, and $\{2025,2026\}$ at most $2$, so $|S|\le 92\cdot7+2=646$.

Construction. Take $k=0,3,6,9,12,15,18$; the residues $5k \bmod 22$ are $0,15,8,1,16,9,2$. Let \[S=\{x\le 2026:\ x\bmod 22\in\{0,1,2,8,9,15,16\}\}.\] Any two of these residues differ by $\pm1,\pm2,\pm6,\pm7,\pm8$ or $\pm9$ modulo $22$, never by $\pm5$ or $\pm10$; so no difference of two elements of $S$ is congruent to $5,10,12$ or $17$ modulo $22$, and $S$ is spaced. It has $7$ elements in each of the $92$ full blocks, plus $2025\equiv1$ and $2026\equiv2$, so $|S|=646$.

The largest spaced subset has $\boxed{646}$ elements.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Nine people stand in line, initially each spaced one meter apart from the next. Every minute, the following process takes place: the first person moves one meter in front; then, one after another in this order, the second person moves at the midpoint between the first and the third; the third moves at the midpoint between the second and the fourth, and so on until the eighth person; the ninth person will stand exactly one meter behind the eighth. Initially, the line formed by the people is $8$ meters long. Can it ever surpass $42$ meters, after a finite amount of minutes?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution – Comparing the gaps with an ideal line

We use the reading of the official text: in each minute the first person steps forward, then the second, third, $\dots$, eighth move one after another, each to the midpoint between the current positions of the person in front (who has already moved) and the person behind (who has not yet moved), and finally the ninth person moves.

Let the positions be $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_9$ (the first person is in front) and let $g_i=x_i-x_{i+1}$ ($1\le i\le 8$) be the gaps; the length of the line is $g_1+\dots+g_8$. Primes denote values after one minute.

How the gaps change. After the $i$-th person ($2\le i\le 8$) moves, he is at the midpoint of $x_{i-1}'$ and $x_{i+1}$, so $x_{i-1}'-x_i'=x_i'-x_{i+1}=\tfrac12(x_{i-1}'-x_{i+1})$. This gives \[g_1'=\tfrac12\bigl(x_1+1-x_3\bigr)=\tfrac12(g_1+g_2+1),\qquad g_i'=\tfrac12\bigl(g_{i-1}'+g_{i+1}\bigr)\ (2\le i\le 7),\qquad g_8'=1 .\] (Indeed, for $2\le i\le 7$, person $i$ has just moved to the midpoint of $x_{i-1}'$ and $x_{i+1}$, so $x_i'-x_{i+1}=g_{i-1}'$; then person $i+1$ moves to the midpoint of $x_i'$ and $x_{i+2}$, so $g_i'=\frac12(x_i'-x_{i+2})=\frac12(g_{i-1}'+g_{i+1})$.)

The ideal gaps. Put $e_i=9-i$, so $e=(8,7,\dots,1)$. These satisfy the same rules: $\frac12(e_1+e_2+1)=8=e_1$, $\frac12(e_{i-1}+e_{i+1})=e_i$, and $e_8=1$. Let $h_i=g_i-e_i$. Subtracting, \[h_1'=\tfrac12(h_1+h_2),\qquad h_i'=\tfrac12(h_{i-1}'+h_{i+1})\ (2\le i\le 7),\qquad h_8'=0 .\]

Invariant: all $h_i\le 0$. Initially $g_i=1$, so $h_i=i-8\le0$. If all $h_i\le 0$, then $h_1'\le0$, then $h_2'\le 0$, and so on up to $h_7'\le0$, and $h_8'=0$. By induction, at every moment $g_i\le e_i$ for all $i$, so the length is at most \[e_1+\dots+e_8=8+7+\dots+1=36<42 .\] So the line can never be longer than $42$ meters: the answer is No.
