Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

We say that a number is superstitious when it is equal to $13$ times the sum of its digits. Find all superstitious numbers.

Show the answer

$117,\ 156,\ 195$

Show solution 1 – Place value

Let $n$ be superstitious and positive, with $d$ digits. Then $10^{d-1}\le n=13S(n)\le13\cdot9d=117d$, which fails for $d\ge4$ ($1000>468$). So $n$ has at most three digits; write $n=100a+10b+c$ with digits $a,b,c$. The condition reads \[100a+10b+c=13(a+b+c)\iff 87a=3b+12c\iff 29a=b+4c .\] Since $b+4c\le9+36=45<58$, $a\le1$; and $a=0$ would force $b=c=0$, i.e. $n=0$. So $a=1$ and $b+4c=29$ with $0\le b\le9$: $c=5,b=9$; $c=6,b=5$; $c=7,b=1$ (for $c\le4$, $b\ge13$; for $c\ge8$, $b<0$).

The superstitious numbers are $\boxed{117,\ 156,\ 195}$ (indeed $13\cdot9=117$, $13\cdot12=156$, $13\cdot15=195$). (If $0$ is allowed, it is trivially superstitious too.)

Show solution 2 – Casting out nines

Let $S$ be the digit sum of a positive superstitious number $n=13S$. As in any case $n\le 13\cdot 9d$ for a $d$-digit number and $10^{d-1}\le n$, we get $d\le3$, so $S\le27$ and $n\le351$.

A number and its digit sum are congruent modulo $9$, so $13S\equiv S\pmod 9$, i.e. $9\mid12S$, i.e. $3\mid S$. Hence $S\in\{3,6,9,\dots,27\}$ and $n\in\{39,78,117,156,195,234,273,312,351\}$. Checking digit sums: \[\begin{gathered}S(39)=12,\ S(78)=15,\ S(117)=9,\ S(156)=12,\ S(195)=15,\\ S(234)=9,\ S(273)=12,\ S(312)=6,\ S(351)=9 .\end{gathered}\] Only $117=13\cdot9$, $156=13\cdot12$, $195=13\cdot15$ work. The answer is $\boxed{117,\ 156,\ 195}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Telescoping Sums and Products

The sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge1}$ of positive real numbers satisfies, for every positive integer $n$, the relation \[x_{n+1}=x_n+\sqrt{x_n+\frac14}+\sqrt{x_{n+1}+\frac14}.\] Prove that \[\frac1{x_2}+\frac1{x_3}+\frac1{x_4}+\dots+\frac1{x_{2026}}<\frac1{\sqrt{x_1}}.\]

Show the solution – Square roots form an arithmetic progression

Put $y_n=\sqrt{x_n+\frac14}$, so $y_n>\frac12$ and $x_n=y_n^2-\frac14$. The relation becomes \[y_{n+1}^2-y_{n+1}=y_n^2+y_n\iff\Bigl(y_{n+1}-\tfrac12\Bigr)^2=\Bigl(y_n+\tfrac12\Bigr)^2 .\] Both $y_{n+1}-\frac12$ and $y_n+\frac12$ are positive, so $y_{n+1}=y_n+1$. Let $z_n=y_n-\frac12>0$; then $z_{n+1}=z_n+1$ and \[x_n=\Bigl(y_n-\tfrac12\Bigr)\Bigl(y_n+\tfrac12\Bigr)=z_nz_{n+1},\qquad \frac1{x_n}=\frac1{z_n}-\frac1{z_{n+1}} .\] Telescoping, \[\sum_{n=2}^{2026}\frac1{x_n}=\frac1{z_2}-\frac1{z_{2027}}<\frac1{z_2}=\frac1{z_1+1}.\] Finally $x_1=z_1(z_1+1)<(z_1+1)^2$, so $\sqrt{x_1}<z_1+1$ and $\frac1{z_1+1}<\frac1{\sqrt{x_1}}$. This proves the inequality.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center · also Germany TST 2026, AIMO 3, Problem 1

Let $ABC$ be a triangle such that $\angle A$ is obtuse. Let $D$ and $E$ be points on sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively, such that $BDEC$ is cyclic. Suppose the circumcircle of triangle $ADE$ intersects side $BC$ at two points $X$ and $Y$, where $B,X,Y$ and $C$ lie in that order. Let the circumcircles of triangles $BDX$ and $CEY$ be $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, respectively. Let $P$ be a point such that $PD$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $PE$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Let $Q$ be a point such that $QB$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $QC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Prove that points $A$, $P$ and $Q$ are collinear.

Show the solution – A, P and Q all have equal powers

Let $\omega$ be the circle $ADEXY$. We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. We show that $A$, $P$, $Q$ have equal powers with respect to $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$; then all three lie on the radical axis of $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$. (These circles are different: one circle cannot meet line $BC$ in the four distinct points $B,X,Y,C$.)

The point $A$. Line $AB$ meets $\Omega$ at $D$ and $B$, and line $AC$ meets $\Gamma$ at $E$ and $C$; so the powers of $A$ are $AD\cdot AB$ and $AE\cdot AC$ (signed products). They are equal because $B,D,E,C$ are concyclic (power of $A$ with respect to that circle).

Step 1: $AX=AY$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. By the tangent-chord angle in $\omega$ and the cyclic quadrilateral $BDEC$, \[\angle(t,AB)=\angle(t,AD)=\angle(EA,ED)=\angle(EC,ED)=\angle(BC,BD)=\angle(BC,AB),\] so $t\parallel BC$. The tangent at $A$ is parallel to the chord $XY$, hence $A$ is the midpoint of an arc $XY$ and $AX=AY$. In directed angles, $\angle(XA,XY)=\angle(YX,YA)$.

Step 2: $QB=QC$. The tangent-chord angle in $\Omega$ (tangent $QB$, chord $BX$, point $D$) and then the circle $\omega$ give \[\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(BQ,BX)=\angle(DB,DX)=\angle(DA,DX)=\angle(YA,YX).\] Similarly in $\Gamma$ (tangent $QC$, chord $CY$, point $E$): \[\angle(CB,CQ)=\angle(CY,CQ)=\angle(EY,EC)=\angle(EY,EA)=\angle(XY,XA).\] By Step 1 the right sides are equal, so $\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(CB,CQ)$: triangle $QBC$ has equal base angles, and $QB=QC$. Thus the powers of $Q$ with respect to $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, namely $QB^2$ and $QC^2$, are equal.

Step 3: $PD=PE$. Using the tangents $PD$ (to $\Omega$) and $PE$ (to $\Gamma$), the circles $\Omega$, $\Gamma$, $\omega$, and the fact that $BX$ and $CY$ are both the line $BC$: \[\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(DP,DX)+\angle(DX,DE)=\angle(BD,BX)+\angle(YX,YE)=\angle(BA,BC)+\angle(BC,YE)=\angle(BA,YE),\] \[\angle(ED,EP)=\angle(ED,EY)+\angle(EY,EP)=\angle(XD,XY)+\angle(CY,CE)=\angle(XD,BC)+\angle(BC,CA)=\angle(XD,CA).\] These are equal if and only if $\angle(CA,YE)=\angle(XD,BA)$, i.e. $\angle(EA,EY)=\angle(DX,DA)$. In $\omega$, $\angle(EA,EY)=\angle(XA,XY)$ and $\angle(DX,DA)=\angle(YX,YA)$, which are equal by Step 1. So $\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(ED,EP)$, triangle $PDE$ is isosceles with $PD=PE$, and the powers of $P$ (namely $PD^2$ and $PE^2$) are equal.

Therefore $A$, $P$, $Q$ all lie on the radical axis of $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, which is a line: they are collinear.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

Let $m,n\ge2$ be integers. Each square of an $m\times n$ board either contains a rook or is empty, such that each rook is attacked by an odd number of other rooks. Determine the maximum possible number of rooks on the board.

Note: Two rooks attack each other if and only if they are in the same row or column and there is no other rook between them in that row or column.

Show the answer

$2(m+n)-8$ if $m+n\ge6$; $4$ for a $2\times3$ board; $2$ for a $2\times2$ board

Show the solution – Ends of lines and thin lines

Call a row or column a line; a line is full if it contains at least two rooks and thin otherwise. A rook is attacked along its row by $0$, $1$ or $2$ rooks, and by exactly $1$ precisely when it is an end (leftmost or rightmost rook) of a full row; the same holds for columns. So a rook is attacked an odd number of times exactly when it is an end of exactly one of its two lines (an end of a full line, and not an end of the other full line). Let $N$ be the number of rooks, $a$ the number of full rows and $b$ the number of full columns.

Step 1: $N=2a+2b$. Every rook is an end of exactly one full line. A full row has exactly two ends, and a full column too, and no rook is counted twice. So $N=2a+2b$; in particular $N$ is even.

Step 2: corner rooks. Call a rook a corner rook if it is an end of its row and also the top or bottom rook of its column (not assuming the lines are full). A corner rook cannot have both lines full (it would be an end of both), nor both thin (it would not be attacked at all). So it is the only rook of one of its lines, which is thin. Two different corner rooks give different thin lines, because a thin line contains at most one rook. Hence \[\#\{\text{thin lines}\}\ \ge\ \#\{\text{corner rooks}\}. \tag{$*$}\]

Step 3: four thin lines, or at most four rooks. If all rooks are in one row, an inner rook of that row would be attacked exactly twice, so there are at most $2$ rooks; similarly for one column. So assume at least two rows and two columns are occupied, and let $T,T'$ be the top and bottom occupied rows and $K,K'$ the leftmost and rightmost occupied columns.

If row $T$ is full, its two ends are the top rooks of their columns and are ends of a full row, so they are not ends of full columns: their columns are thin, and contain only a rook of row $T$. So row $T$ yields either itself as a thin row, or two thin columns whose only rook lies in $T$. The same holds for $T'$, and (with rows and columns exchanged) for $K$ and $K'$. The two columns obtained from $T$ differ from those obtained from $T'$, and the rows obtained from $K$ differ from those obtained from $K'$.

  • If $T$ and $T'$ are both full, we get four thin columns; if $K$ and $K'$ are both full, four thin rows.

  • Otherwise, after reflections, $T$ is thin and $K$ is thin. If $T'$ is full, it gives two thin columns, at most one of which is $K$; if moreover $K'$ is full, it gives two thin rows, at most one of which is $T$ (the other is not $T'$, which is full): four thin lines. If $T'$ is full and $K'$ is thin, we get the four lines $T,K,K'$ and the two columns of $T'$, which are four distinct lines unless the two columns of $T'$ are exactly $K$ and $K'$. If $T'$ is thin, then $T,T',K$ are thin; $K'$ gives a fourth line unless it is full and its top and bottom rooks are the single rooks of $T$ and $T'$.

The exception in the second item ($T'$ full, its two end rooks being the only rooks of $K$ and $K'$, and $T$ thin) is the last exception with rows and columns exchanged. So, up to reflections and exchanging rows with columns, the only remaining case is: the rows $T$, $T'$ each contain one rook, these are the top and bottom rooks of the full extreme column $K'$, and the other extreme column $K$ contains a single rook $r$. Let $Z$ be the set of rooks outside $K'$; they lie strictly between $T$ and $T'$. In the highest row meeting $Z$, let $z$ be the rook farthest from $K'$; it belongs to $Z$, it is an end of its row, and it is the top rook of its column (higher rows contain only rooks of $K'$). So $z$ is a corner rook, and if $z\ne r$ its thin line is none of $T,T',K',K$: a fourth thin line. The same argument applies to the lowest row meeting $Z$. If both give $r$, then all of $Z$ lies in the row of $r$; every rook of $Z$ is then alone in its column, so it must be an end of that row, and $|Z|\le2$; the inner rooks of $K'$ must be ends of full rows, and only the row of $r$ can be full, so $K'$ has at most $3$ rooks. Then at most one row and one column are full, and $N=2a+2b\le4$.

So either there are at least four thin lines, whence $a+b\le m+n-4$ and $N\le2(m+n)-8$, or $N\le4$.

Step 4: the bound. If $m+n\ge6$, then $2(m+n)-8\ge4$, so $N\le2(m+n)-8$. For a $2\times3$ board we get $N\le4$. For a $2\times2$ board, $N$ is even and $N=4$ fails (each rook would be attacked twice), so $N\le2$.

Step 5: constructions (rows numbered from the top; by symmetry let $m\le n$).

  • $m,n\ge4$: rooks in all of column $1$; in column $2$, rows $2,\dots,m-1$; in row $2$, columns $1,\dots,n-2$ and $n$; in row $m-1$, columns $1,\dots,n-1$. Count: $m+(m-2)+2(n-4)+2=2(m+n)-8$.

  • $m=3\le n$, $n\ge4$: row $1$: column $2$; row $2$: columns $2,\dots,n-2$ and $n$; row $3$: columns $1,\dots,n-1$. Count $2n-2$.

  • $m=2$, $n\ge4$: row $1$: columns $1$, $3,\dots,n-2$, $n-1$; row $2$: columns $2$, $3,\dots,n-2$, $n$. Count $2n-4$.

  • $3\times3$: column $1$ and the centre ($4$ rooks); $2\times3$: the whole top row and the middle of the bottom row ($4$ rooks); $2\times2$: two rooks in a row.

In each case one checks directly that every rook is the end of exactly one full line (for example, in the first construction the inner rooks of column $1$ are left ends of rows, the rooks of rows $2$ and $m-1$ in columns $2,\dots,n-2$ are ends of their columns and inner in their rows, and the rooks in columns $n-1$, $n$ are alone in their columns and right ends of their rows).

Hence the maximum is $\boxed{2(m+n)-8}$ when $m+n\ge6$, and it is $4$ for the $2\times3$ board and $2$ for the $2\times2$ board.

Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Solve in integers the equation $x+y=x^2-xy+y^2$.

Show the answer

$(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,1),(1,2),(2,2)$

Show solution 1 – Sum of three squares

Multiply by $2$ and rearrange: \[2x^2-2xy+2y^2-2x-2y=0\iff (x-y)^2+(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2=2 .\] A sum of three integer squares equals $2$ only as $1+1+0$. So exactly two of $x-y$, $x-1$, $y-1$ are $\pm1$ and the third is $0$:

  • $x=y$: then $(x-1)^2=1$, so $x=y\in\{0,2\}$;

  • $x=1$: then $(1-y)^2=1$, so $y\in\{0,2\}$;

  • $y=1$: then $x\in\{0,2\}$.

The solutions are $\boxed{(0,0),(2,2),(1,0),(1,2),(0,1),(2,1)}$.

Show solution 2 – Discriminant

View the equation as a quadratic in $x$: $x^2-(y+1)x+(y^2-y)=0$. It has a real root only if the discriminant \[(y+1)^2-4(y^2-y)=-3y^2+6y+1=4-3(y-1)^2\] is non-negative, so $(y-1)^2\le1$ and $y\in\{0,1,2\}$.

  • $y=0$: $x^2-x=0$, $x\in\{0,1\}$.

  • $y=1$: $x^2-2x=0$, $x\in\{0,2\}$.

  • $y=2$: $x^2-3x+2=0$, $x\in\{1,2\}$.

All integer solutions: $\boxed{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,1),(1,2),(2,2)}$.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

A word is a finite sequence of 0s and 1s. A triple-word is a word formed by repeating three identical words. For example, 010010010 is a triple-word, but 1001 is not.

A change to a word consists of adding or removing a triple-word from the original word. For example, the word 10001 can be changed to 11, which in turn can be changed to 11010101.

Is it possible to obtain the word 10 after a sequence of changes starting from the word 01?

Show the answer

No

Show solution 1 – Positions of the ones modulo 3

No. For a word $w=w_1w_2\dots w_\ell$ let \[I(w)=\sum_{i:\ w_i=1} i \pmod 3,\] the sum of the positions of its $1$s modulo $3$.

$I$ is unchanged by a change. Insert a triple-word $uuu$ ($u$ of length $k$) after position $p$. Letters after the insertion move $3k$ places to the right, which does not change their positions modulo $3$. If $u$ has $1$s in positions $j_1,\dots,j_r$ of $u$, the inserted block has $1$s at the positions $p+j_s$, $p+k+j_s$, $p+2k+j_s$, whose sum is $3\sum_s(p+k+j_s)\equiv0\pmod3$. So $I$ does not change; removing a triple-word is the reverse operation.

Since $I(01)=2$ and $I(10)=1$, the word $10$ cannot be obtained from $01$.

Show solution 2 – A group in which every cube is trivial

No. Work with $3\times3$ matrices with entries modulo $3$, and let \[\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.\] To a word assign the product $\phi(w)$ obtained by replacing each $0$ by $\mathbf A$ and each $1$ by $\mathbf B$ (the empty word gives $\mathbf I$); then $\phi(vw)=\phi(v)\phi(w)$.

Every product $\mathbf G$ of such matrices is upper unitriangular, $\mathbf G=\mathbf I+\mathbf M$ with $\mathbf M$ strictly upper triangular, so $\mathbf M^3=0$ and \[\mathbf G^3=\mathbf I+3\mathbf M+3\mathbf M^2+\mathbf M^3\equiv\mathbf I\pmod 3 .\] Hence $\phi(uuu)=\phi(u)^3=\mathbf I$ for every word $u$, and inserting or removing a triple-word anywhere does not change $\phi$: $\phi(v\,uuu\,w)=\phi(v)\phi(w)$.

But $\phi(01)=\mathbf A\mathbf B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(10)=\mathbf B\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ are different. So $10$ cannot be reached from $01$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

The number $3$ is initially written on the board. A permitted operation consists of erasing a positive integer $x$ and writing a positive integer $y$ on the board, provided that $|x^2-y^2|$ is a perfect square. Determine all positive integers $n$ that can be written on the board.

Show the answer

all $n\ge3$

Show the solution – Pythagorean triples and scaling

Write $x\sim y$ if $x\ne y$ and $|x^2-y^2|$ is a perfect square; equivalently, one of $x,y$ is the hypotenuse and the other a leg of a right triangle with integer sides. The relation is symmetric, so the numbers that can appear are exactly those connected to $3$ by a chain of $\sim$; call this set $\mathcal C$. Two facts:

  • Scaling: if $x\sim y$ then $kx\sim ky$ for every $k\ge1$; so a chain from $x$ to $y$ gives a chain from $kx$ to $ky$.

  • Triples: for odd $n\ge3$, $n\sim\frac{n^2+1}2\sim\frac{n^2-1}2$ (the triple $n,\frac{n^2-1}2,\frac{n^2+1}2$); for even $n\ge4$, $n\sim\frac{n^2}4+1\sim\frac{n^2}4-1$.

$1$ and $2$ never appear. $|1-y^2|=k^2$ with $y\ge2$ would give $(y-k)(y+k)=1$, impossible; $|4-y^2|=k^2$ with $y\ne2$ gives $y=1$ ($3$ is not a square) or $(y-k)(y+k)=4$ with factors of equal parity, i.e. $y=2$. So $1$ and $2$ are related to nothing.

Small numbers. $3\sim5\sim4$; $9\sim15\sim12\sim13\sim5$ (the triples $9,12,15$ and $5,12,13$); so $3,4,5,9,12,13,15\in\mathcal C$.

$\mathcal C$ is closed under products. If $a\in\mathcal C$, scaling a chain $3\to a$ by $3$ gives a chain $9\to3a$, so $3a\in\mathcal C$. If $a,b\in\mathcal C$, scaling a chain $3\to b$ by $a$ gives a chain $3a\to ab$, so $ab\in\mathcal C$.

Next $8\sim17\sim15$, so $8\in\mathcal C$; $6\sim10\sim8$, so $6\in\mathcal C$; $7\sim25\sim24=4\cdot6$, so $7\in\mathcal C$.

Induction. Let $n\ge9$ and suppose $3,4,\dots,n-1\in\mathcal C$. If $n$ is odd, $n\sim\frac{n^2+1}2\sim\frac{n^2-1}2=(n-1)\cdot\frac{n+1}2$ with $3\le\frac{n+1}2<n$ and $3\le n-1<n$; both factors are in $\mathcal C$, hence so is their product and so is $n$. If $n$ is even, $n\sim\frac{n^2}4+1\sim\frac{n^2}4-1=\bigl(\frac n2-1\bigr)\bigl(\frac n2+1\bigr)$ with $3\le\frac n2-1$ and $\frac n2+1<n$; again $n\in\mathcal C$.

Therefore exactly the integers $\boxed{n\ge3}$ can be written on the board.

Problem 4

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a scalene triangle with incenter $I$ and incircle $\omega$. Let $D$ be the point of tangency of $\omega$ with $BC$. Suppose that there exist points $X$ and $Y$ on $\omega$ such that $IX$, $IY$ and $AD$ are tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $AXY$. Prove that $XY$ passes through the midpoint of $BC$.

Show solution 1 – The power of a point as a function

Let $\Gamma$ be the circle $AXY$, $r$ the inradius and $M$ the midpoint of $BC$.

Reformulation. $IX$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $X$, so the power of $I$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $IX^2=r^2$; and $X,Y$ lie on both $\omega$ and $\Gamma$, so $XY$ is the radical axis of $\omega$ and $\Gamma$. The power of $M$ with respect to $\omega$ is $MD^2$ (as $MD$ is tangent to $\omega$). So it suffices to show \[\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(M)=MD^2. \tag{1}\]

Circles tangent to $AD$ at $A$. Let $\nu$ be a unit vector perpendicular to $AD$ and $f(Z)=(Z-A)\cdot\nu$ the signed distance from $Z$ to line $AD$. A circle tangent to $AD$ at $A$ has center $A+\lambda\nu$ and radius $|\lambda|$, so its power function is \[\operatorname{pow}(Z)=|Z-A-\lambda\nu|^2-\lambda^2=ZA^2-2\lambda f(Z).\] For $\Gamma$, the condition $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(I)=r^2$ gives $2\lambda f(I)=IA^2-r^2=AF^2$, where $AF$ is the tangent length from $A$ to $\omega$. Note $f(I)\ne0$: otherwise $I$ would lie on $AD$, which happens only when $AB=AC$. So (1) becomes \[\bigl(MA^2-MD^2\bigr)f(I)=AF^2\,f(M). \tag{2}\]

Coordinates. Put $D$ at the origin, $BC$ on the $x$-axis, and let $u=BD=s-b$, $v=DC=s-c$, $w=AF=s-a$ (tangent lengths, $s$ the semiperimeter). Then $B=(-u,0)$, $C=(v,0)$, $I=(0,r)$, $M=(\frac{v-u}2,0)$ and, from $AB=u+w$, $AC=v+w$ and the area $rs$, \[A=\Bigl(\frac{(u-v)w}{u+v},\ \frac{2rs}{u+v}\Bigr).\] With $\nu$ perpendicular to $DA=A$, we have $f(Z)=\frac{Z\times A}{|A|}$ (cross product), so $f(I)=\frac{-rx_A}{|A|}$ and $f(M)=\frac{(v-u)y_A}{2|A|}$, where $A=(x_A,y_A)$. Also $MA^2-MD^2=|A|^2-(v-u)x_A$. Thus (2) reads \[-rx_A\bigl(|A|^2-(v-u)x_A\bigr)=w^2\,\frac{(v-u)}{2}\,y_A .\] Insert $x_A=\frac{(u-v)w}{u+v}$ and $y_A=\frac{2rs}{u+v}$ and divide by $-\frac{(u-v)rw}{u+v}\ne0$ (the triangle is scalene, so $u\ne v$): \[|A|^2+\frac{(u-v)^2w}{u+v}=ws. \tag{3}\] Finally, Stewart's theorem for the cevian $AD$ gives \[AD^2=\frac{(u+w)^2v+(v+w)^2u}{u+v}-uv=w^2+\frac{4uvw}{u+v},\] and the right side of (3) minus the second term on the left is $w(u+v+w)-\frac{(u-v)^2w}{u+v}=w^2+\frac{4uvw}{u+v}$. So (3) holds, hence (1), and $M$ lies on the radical axis $XY$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates centred at the incentre and a polar line

Let $\Gamma$ be the circle $AXY$ with centre $O$. Scale so that the inradius is $1$ and place $I$ at the origin with $BC$ horizontal below it: $\omega$ is the unit circle, $D=(0,-1)$, and $BC$ is the line $y=-1$. Let $A=(u,v)$ with $v>-1$. We have $u\ne0$: otherwise $A$ lies on the line $ID$, which is perpendicular to $BC$, so $AD$ would be both an altitude and contain $I$, forcing $AB=AC$.

The line $XY$. Since $IX$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $X$, the radius $OX$ is perpendicular to $IX$, i.e. $(O-X)\cdot X=0$. As $|X|=1$, this says $O\cdot X=1$; similarly $O\cdot Y=1$. The equation $O\cdot Z=1$ describes a line (note $O\ne(0,0)$), so $XY$ is the line \[O\cdot Z=1 .\] Thus we must show $O\cdot M=1$ for the midpoint $M$ of $BC$.

The centre $O$. The tangent length from $I$ to $\Gamma$ is $IX=1$, so the power of $I$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $1$: $|O|^2-|O-A|^2=1$, i.e. $2\,O\cdot A=|A|^2+1$. Since $\Gamma$ touches $AD$ at $A$, $O$ lies on the perpendicular to $AD$ at $A$; as $D-A=(-u,-1-v)$, this perpendicular has direction $(v+1,-u)$, so $O=A+t\,(v+1,-u)$ for some real $t$. Then $O\cdot A=|A|^2+tu$, and the power condition gives $2tu=1-u^2-v^2$. Hence \[O=\Bigl(u+\frac{(v+1)(1-u^2-v^2)}{2u},\ \frac{u^2+v^2+2v-1}{2}\Bigr).\]

The midpoint $M$. Lines $AB$ and $AC$ are the two tangents from $A$ to $\omega$, so $B$ and $C$ are exactly the points $Z=(x,-1)$ for which line $AZ$ is at distance $1$ from the origin. The distance from the origin to line $AZ$ is $\frac{|uy_Z-vx|}{|A-Z|}$ with $y_Z=-1$, so the condition is \[(u+vx)^2=(x-u)^2+(v+1)^2\iff(1-v^2)x^2-2u(v+1)x+(v+1)^2=0 .\] This equation has the two distinct roots $x_B\ne x_C$, so it is a genuine quadratic ($1-v^2\ne0$), and by Vieta \[M=\Bigl(\frac{x_B+x_C}{2},-1\Bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{u(v+1)}{1-v^2},-1\Bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{u}{1-v},-1\Bigr).\]

Conclusion. We need $O_x\cdot\frac{u}{1-v}-O_y=1$, i.e. $u\,O_x=(1-v)(O_y+1)$. Now \[2u\,O_x=2u^2+(v+1)(1-u^2-v^2)=u^2(1-v)+(1-v)(1+v)^2,\] \[2(1-v)(O_y+1)=(1-v)\bigl(u^2+(v+1)^2\bigr),\] and the two right sides are equal. Hence $O\cdot M=1$, so $M$ lies on $XY$: the line $XY$ passes through the midpoint of $BC$.

Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · Comparing Valuations Across Terms

Gugu chose $10$ consecutive positive integers. He wrote each of the chosen numbers either in red or in blue (both colors are present). He then calculates the LCM of the red numbers and the LCM of the blue numbers. Is it possible for the sum of these two LCMs to have a decimal representation ending in $4096$?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution – The sum is never divisible by 16

No. A number ending in $4096$ is $\equiv4096\pmod{10000}$, and since $16\mid4096$ and $16\mid10000$, it is divisible by $16$. We show that the sum $S=L_R+L_B$ of the two LCMs always has $v_2(S)\le3$, where $v_2$ is the exponent of $2$.

For a colour class $X$, $v_2(L_X)$ is the largest $v_2$ of a number in $X$. Among $10$ consecutive integers there is a multiple of $8$, and at most one multiple of $16$.

  • If $v_2(L_R)\ne v_2(L_B)$, then $v_2(S)=\min(v_2(L_R),v_2(L_B))$. This is at least $4$ only if both colours contain a multiple of $16$, which is impossible. So $v_2(S)\le3$.

  • If $v_2(L_R)=v_2(L_B)=e$, then both classes contain a number with exactly $e$ factors of $2$ and none with more. The class containing a multiple of $8$ has $e\ge3$; but two numbers with the same $v_2=e\ge3$ differ by at least $2^{e+1}\ge16>9$, impossible among $10$ consecutive integers.

So $16\nmid S$, and the sum can never end in $4096$.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Reformulation of Classical Geometry

Let $D$ be a point on the side $BC$ of a triangle $ABC$ such that $AD>BC$. Let $E$ be a point on the side $AC$ such that $\dfrac{AE}{EC}=\dfrac{BD}{AD-BC}$. Show that $AD>BE$.

Show the solution – A vector decomposition and the triangle inequality

We assume $D\ne B$ (if $D=B$, then $E=A$ and $BE=BA=AD$).

The case $D=C$. Then $AD=AC$ and $\frac{AE}{EC}=\frac{BC}{AC-BC}$ gives $AE=BC$, $EC=AC-BC$. In triangle $BEC$, $BE<BC+CE=AC=AD$ (strict, since $E\ne C$ is not on line $BC$).

Now let $D$ be strictly between $B$ and $C$. Put $t=\frac{AE}{AC}$. From $\frac{AE}{EC}=\frac{BD}{AD-BC}$, \[t=\frac{BD}{BD+AD-BC}=\frac{BD}{AD-DC},\qquad\text{so }0<t<1\ \text{(because }BD<AD-DC\iff BC<AD).\] As vectors, $E=(1-t)A+tC$ and, since $D$ lies between $B$ and $C$, $B-D=-\frac{BD}{DC}(C-D)$. Hence \[B-E=(D-E)+(B-D)=(1-t)(D-A)+t(D-C)+\frac{BD}{DC}(D-C)=(1-t)(D-A)+\Bigl(t+\frac{BD}{DC}\Bigr)(D-C).\] Both coefficients are positive, so by the triangle inequality \[BE\le(1-t)AD+\Bigl(t+\frac{BD}{DC}\Bigr)DC=AD-t(AD-DC)+BD=AD,\] using $t(AD-DC)=BD$. Equality would need $D-A$ and $D-C$ to point in the same direction, i.e. $A$ on line $BC$, which is false. Hence $AD>BE$.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n\ge3$ be a natural number. Esmeralda plays the following game. Initially, a token is at the origin of the Cartesian $xy$-plane. Then, for each integer $1\le k\le n$, Esmeralda's $k$th move consists of moving the token parallel to one of the axes by a distance of $2^{k-1}$ from its current position, and calculating the distance $d_k$ from the origin of the Cartesian plane to the position of the token after the move. After the $n$th move, her loss is the greatest value among $d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n$. What is the minimum loss Esmeralda can obtain?

Show the answer

$\sqrt{4^{\,n-2}+1}$

Show the solution – The last two moves

Write $(x_k,y_k)$ for the position after the $k$-th move.

Lower bound. By symmetry suppose the last move is vertical. Then $x_{n-1}=x_n$ and $|y_n-y_{n-1}|=2^{n-1}$, so $\max(|y_{n-1}|,|y_n|)\ge2^{n-2}$.

  • If some move is horizontal, then $x_n$ is a sum $\pm2^{k_1-1}\pm\dots\pm2^{k_r-1}$ over the horizontal moves $k_1<\dots<k_r$; it is nonzero because it is $\equiv2^{k_1-1}\pmod{2^{k_1}}$. So $|x_n|\ge1$, and one of $d_{n-1},d_n$ is at least $\sqrt{1+4^{n-2}}$.

  • If all moves are vertical, every $y_k$ is odd (the first move is $\pm1$, the others even). Since $2^{n-2}$ is even ($n\ge3$), $\max(|y_{n-1}|,|y_n|)\ge2^{n-2}+1>\sqrt{4^{n-2}+1}$.

So the loss is at least $\sqrt{4^{n-2}+1}$.

Construction. Make moves $1,\dots,n-3$ in the $+x$ direction (reaching $(2^{n-3}-1,0)$), move $n-2$ in the $-x$ direction (to $(-1,0)$), move $n-1$ in the $+y$ direction (to $(-1,2^{n-2})$) and move $n$ in the $-y$ direction (to $(-1,-2^{n-2})$). The distances are at most $2^{n-3}-1$, then $1$, then $\sqrt{1+4^{n-2}}$ twice. (For $n=3$ the first phase is empty.)

The minimum loss is $\boxed{\sqrt{4^{\,n-2}+1}}$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments · also Germany TST 2026, AIMO 6, Problem 1

The sunshine cost of a sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of integers is the largest possible value of \[\left|(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_i)-a_j\right|\] as $i$ and $j$ vary over all integers $1,2,\ldots,100$.

Determine the smallest possible sunshine cost over all sequences $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of pairwise distinct integers.

Show the answer

$75$

Show the solution – All partial sums lie in one short interval

Let $s_i=a_1+\dots+a_i$, let $\mathrm{hi}$ and $\mathrm{lo}$ be the largest and smallest terms, and let $C$ be the sunshine cost. As the $100$ terms are distinct integers, $\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo}\ge99$.

Lower bound. For every $i$, $|s_i-\mathrm{hi}|\le C$ and $|s_i-\mathrm{lo}|\le C$, so every $s_i$ lies in the interval $J=[\mathrm{hi}-C,\ \mathrm{lo}+C]$, of length $\ell=2C-(\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo})$. For $i\ge2$, $a_i=s_i-s_{i-1}$ is a difference of two points of $J$, so $|a_i|\le\ell$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-\ell,\ell]$, which has $2\ell+1$ integers; so $\ell\ge49$, and \[2C=\ell+(\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo})\ge49+99=148,\qquad C\ge74 .\] If $C=74$, all these inequalities are equalities: $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ are exactly $-49,\dots,49$ and the $100$ terms are $100$ consecutive integers, so $a_1=50$ or $a_1=-50$. But then $|s_1-a_j|=|a_1-a_j|=99>74$ for $a_j=\mp49$. Hence $C\ge75$.

Construction. Take $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, and then the pairs $j,-j$ for $j=1,2,\dots,50$, $j\ne25$. These are $2+98=100$ distinct integers in $[-50,50]$. The partial sums equal $-25$ after $a_1$, after $a_2$ and after each complete pair, and $-25+j$ in the middle of the pair $j,-j$; so they lie in $[-25,25]$. Therefore $|s_i-a_j|\le\max\bigl(25-(-50),\ 50-(-25)\bigr)=75$.

The smallest sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.
