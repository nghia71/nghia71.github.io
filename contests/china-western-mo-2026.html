Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n,b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n>0$ and $\frac{1}{a_i}-\frac{1}{b_i}\le1$ $(1\le i\le n)$. Prove that \[\frac{1}{a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n}-\frac{1}{b_1+b_2+\dots+b_n}\le1.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
For $n=1$ the claim is the given condition. For $n=2$, see what the hypotheses say about how small each $a_i$ can be once its $b_i$ is fixed.
Hint 2: key idea
Isolate $1/a_i$ and take reciprocals to get a lower bound for $a_i$. Look for a common denominator that will let you add these lower bounds. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Write $B=\sum_i b_i$ and use $b_i\le B$ to replace the denominator of each lower bound by $1+B$. Add the resulting bounds and use positivity of both totals to prepare to take reciprocals.
Common trap
Taking reciprocals of positive quantities reverses the inequality.
Show solution 1 – A lower bound for each $a_i$

Let $A=\sum a_i$ and $B=\sum b_i$. The hypothesis $\frac1{a_i}\le1+\frac1{b_i}=\frac{1+b_i}{b_i}$ gives $a_i\ge\frac{b_i}{1+b_i}$. Since $b_i\le B$, \[A\ge\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{b_i}{1+b_i}\ge\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{b_i}{1+B}=\frac{B}{1+B}.\] Therefore $\frac1A\le\frac{1+B}{B}=1+\frac1B$, which is the claim.

Show solution 2 – Clearing denominators

Let $A=\sum a_i$ and $B=\sum b_i$. Multiplying the hypothesis by $a_ib_i>0$ gives $b_i-a_i\le a_ib_i$. Adding these inequalities, \[B-A\le\sum_{i=1}^na_ib_i\le\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^na_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^nb_j\Bigr)=AB,\] because expanding the product gives all the terms $a_ib_i$ plus further positive terms $a_ib_j$ ($i\ne j$). Dividing by $AB>0$ gives $\frac1A-\frac1B\le1$.

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry · Circle and Line Structure via Complex Equations

Let $A$ be a point on a circle $\omega$, and let $\ell$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. Let $A'$ and $A''$ be points on $\ell$ such that $AA'=AA''$. Through $A'$ and $A''$, draw secants $A'TP$ and $A''SQ$ of $\omega$, respectively. Through $A$, draw lines parallel to $TP$ and $SQ$, meeting $\omega$ again at $X$ and $Y$, respectively. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $PQ$. Prove that the lines $PY$, $QX$, and $AM$ are concurrent.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $A'$ and $A''$ on the tangent and mark the two pairs of parallel chords. Which point of intersection would you want to locate relative to $A$ and $M$?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the complex unit circle with $A=1$. Write the chord through $u,v$ as $z+uv\bar z=u+v$, then use parallelism to express the coordinates of $X$ and $Y$ in terms of the secant endpoints. (LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios)
Hint 3: approach
Intersect the secants with the tangent $z+\bar z=2$ and use the opposite signed offsets from $A$ to eliminate the coordinate of $S$. Find $K=PY\cap QX$ by eliminating $\bar z$ from two chord equations and simplify the ratio $(k-1)/(m-1)$ using the secant relation. Set up a comparison with its conjugate using $\bar z=1/z$ for the circle points.
Common trap
$A'$ and $A''$ must be distinct points on opposite sides of $A$, so their signed offsets from $A$ along the tangent are negatives of each other; also check that $PY$ and $QX$ actually meet.
Show solution 1 – Complex numbers

Let $\omega$ be the unit circle and $A=1$; lower-case letters denote the complex coordinates of points of $\omega$, so $\bar z=1/z$ for them. We assume $A'\ne A''$ (so they lie on opposite sides of $A$) and that the lines $PY$, $QX$ meet.

Chords and the tangent. The chord through $u,v\in\omega$ is $z+uv\bar z=u+v$, and the tangent at $1$ is $z+\bar z=2$. Chords $AX$ and $TP$ are parallel exactly when $1\cdot x=tp$; so $x=tp$ and likewise $y=sq$. Intersecting line $TP$ with the tangent: $\bar z=2-z$ gives $z+tp(2-z)=t+p$, so \[A'=\frac{t+p-2tp}{1-tp},\qquad A'-1=-\frac{(1-t)(1-p)}{1-tp},\qquad A''-1=-\frac{(1-s)(1-q)}{1-sq}.\] The condition $AA'=AA''$ with $A'\ne A''$ means $A''-1=-(A'-1)$: \[\frac{(1-t)(1-p)}{1-tp}+\frac{(1-s)(1-q)}{1-sq}=0 .\tag{1}\] With $E=(1-t)(1-p)$ and $F=(1-q)(1-tp)$, (1) reads $(1-s)F=-E(1-sq)$, i.e. \[s=\frac{E+F}{F+qE}.\tag{2}\]

The point $K=PY\cap QX$. For chords $uv$ and $wz$ the intersection is $\frac{uv(w+z)-wz(u+v)}{uv-wz}$. With $u=p$, $v=y=sq$, $w=q$, $z=x=tp$ this gives, after cancelling $pq$, \[K=\frac{sq(1-t)-tp(1-s)}{s-t},\qquad K-1=\frac{s(pt-qt+q-1)+t(1-p)}{s-t}.\] Substituting (2) and expanding (with $F+qE=2pqt-pq-pt-qt+1$) one finds \[s-t=-\frac{(t-1)\,G}{F+qE},\qquad s(pt-qt+q-1)+t(1-p)=-\frac{(q-1)(t-1)(pt-1)(p+q-2)}{F+qE},\] where $G=2pqt-pt-p-qt-q+2$. Hence \[K-1=\frac{(q-1)(pt-1)(p+q-2)}{G}.\]

Collinearity. The midpoint of $PQ$ is $m=\frac{p+q}2$, and \[\frac{K-1}{m-1}=\frac{2(q-1)(pt-1)}{G}.\] Conjugating and multiplying numerator and denominator by $pqt$: $(\tfrac1q-1)(\tfrac1{pt}-1)\cdot pqt=(q-1)(pt-1)$ and $\bar G\cdot pqt=2-q-qt-p-pt+2pqt=G$. So the ratio equals its conjugate, i.e. it is real, and $K$ lies on line $AM$. Thus $PY$, $QX$, $AM$ are concurrent.

Show solution 2 – Trigonometry and the sine form of Ceva

We work in the configuration of the figure: $A'$, $T$, $P$ and $A''$, $S$, $Q$ in this order, $X,Y$ on the arc $PQ$ not containing $A$, and $K=PY\cap QX$ on the other side of $PQ$ from $A$. Let $d$ be the diameter of $\omega$; the sine of an inscribed angle equals the chord it subtends divided by $d$.

Step 1: $AP\cdot AT/AX=AA'$. The chord $AX$ makes with the tangent $\ell$ the same angle $\theta=\angle AA'P$ as the parallel line $TP$, so $AX=d\sin\theta$. By the tangent–chord angle, $\angle A'PA=\angle TPA$ equals the angle between $\ell$ and chord $AT$, so $\sin\angle A'PA=AT/d$. The law of sines in triangle $AA'P$ gives \[AA'=\frac{AP\sin\angle A'PA}{\sin\theta}=\frac{AP\cdot AT/d}{AX/d}=\frac{AP\cdot AT}{AX}.\] In the same way $AA''=\dfrac{AQ\cdot AS}{AY}$. As $AA'=AA''$, \[AP\cdot AT\cdot AY=AQ\cdot AS\cdot AX .\tag{1}\] Moreover $AX\parallel TP$ makes $ATPX$ an isosceles trapezoid, so $PX=AT$; likewise $QY=AS$. Thus (1) becomes \[AP\cdot PX\cdot AY=AQ\cdot QY\cdot AX .\tag{2}\]

Step 2: $AK$ is the median. Since $K$ and $A$ are on opposite sides of $PQ$, the segment $AK$ meets segment $PQ$ at a point $U$, and by the ratio lemma \[\frac{PU}{UQ}=\frac{AP\sin\angle PAK}{AQ\sin\angle QAK}.\] By the law of sines in triangles $APK$, $AQK$, $PQK$: \[\sin\angle PAK=\frac{PK\sin\angle APK}{AK},\quad \sin\angle QAK=\frac{QK\sin\angle AQK}{AK},\quad \frac{PK}{QK}=\frac{\sin\angle PQK}{\sin\angle QPK}.\] Since $K$ lies on lines $PY$ and $QX$, each angle here equals or is supplementary to an inscribed angle: $\sin\angle APK=\sin\angle APY=\frac{AY}d$, $\sin\angle AQK=\frac{AX}d$, $\sin\angle PQK=\frac{PX}d$, $\sin\angle QPK=\frac{QY}d$. Therefore \[\frac{PU}{UQ}=\frac{AP\cdot PX\cdot AY}{AQ\cdot QY\cdot AX}=1\] by (2). So $U$ is the midpoint $M$ of $PQ$: the line $AM$ passes through $K$, and the three lines are concurrent.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

Let $f:[n]\to[n]$ be a bijection such that \[a\mid b\implies f(a)\mid f(b).\] Prove that if $k\in[n]$ and every prime divisor of $k$ is less than $\sqrt n$, then $f(k)=k$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick a small $b$ and list its divisors. Mark where $f$ sends them among the divisors of $f(b)$ and ask whether there could be unused places.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare the two divisor counts for each $b$ and add the comparisons over $[n]$. After using bijectivity to account for the totals, apply the resulting two-way divisibility condition to count multiples. (LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Follow the chain of divisors of a prime power to identify its image. For a prime below $\sqrt n$, use the existence of its square and the square of its image to compare the two multiple counts. Then use the prime powers dividing $k$ to compare the divisor sets of $k$ and $f(k)$.
Common trap
Divisibility in the reverse direction needs to be proved before you use it.
Show solution 1 – Counting divisors and multiples

Write $\mathrm{Div}(b)$ for the set of divisors of $b$ and $\mathrm{Mult}(a)=\{b\le n: a\mid b\}$.

(i) $f$ maps $\mathrm{Div}(b)$ onto $\mathrm{Div}(f(b))$. It maps it injectively into $\mathrm{Div}(f(b))$, so $d(b)\le d(f(b))$. Summing over $b\in[n]$ and using that $f$ is a bijection, $\sum_bd(b)=\sum_bd(f(b))$; so $d(b)=d(f(b))$ for all $b$ and the map is onto. Consequently $f(a)\mid f(b)$ implies $a\mid b$: $f(a)\in\mathrm{Div}(f(b))=f(\mathrm{Div}(b))$, so $f(a)=f(c)$ with $c\mid b$, and $a=c$.

(ii) $\lfloor n/a\rfloor=\lfloor n/f(a)\rfloor$. $f$ maps $\mathrm{Mult}(a)$ into $\mathrm{Mult}(f(a))$, and by (i) $f^{-1}$ maps $\mathrm{Mult}(f(a))$ into $\mathrm{Mult}(a)$; both are injective, so the two sets have the same size.

(iii) Prime powers. If $p$ is prime, $d(f(p))=d(p)=2$, so $f(p)$ is prime. If $p^e\le n$, the divisors of $p^e$ form a chain $1\mid p\mid\dots\mid p^e$; by (i) the divisors of $f(p^e)$ are their images, which again form a chain of $e+1$ numbers. A number whose divisors are totally ordered by divisibility is a prime power, so $f(p^e)=q^e$ for a prime $q$, and $f(p)\mid f(p^e)$ gives $q=f(p)$.

(iv) $f(p)=p$ for primes $p<\sqrt n$. Let $q=f(p)$. Since $p^2<n$, (iii) gives $q^2=f(p^2)\le n$, so $p,q\le\sqrt n$ and $pq\le n$. If $p\ne q$, say $p<q$ (the other case is symmetric), then \[\frac np-\frac nq=\frac{n(q-p)}{pq}\ge\frac{n}{pq}\ge1,\] so $\lfloor n/p\rfloor>\lfloor n/q\rfloor$, contradicting (ii). Hence $f(p)=p$, and by (iii) $f(p^e)=p^e$ whenever $p^e\le n$.

(v) Conclusion. Let $k=\prod p_i^{e_i}\le n$ with all $p_i<\sqrt n$. Each $p_i^{e_i}\le k\le n$ divides $k$, so $p_i^{e_i}=f(p_i^{e_i})\mid f(k)$; hence $k\mid f(k)$. If $f(k)\ne k$, then $\mathrm{Div}(k)\subsetneq\mathrm{Div}(f(k))$ and $d(f(k))>d(k)$, contradicting (i). So $f(k)=k$.

Show solution 2 – An automorphism of the divisibility order

(i) $f^{-1}$ also preserves divisibility. For each $b$, $f$ maps the divisors of $b$ injectively to divisors of $f(b)$, so $d(b)\le d(f(b))$; as $f$ is a bijection of $[n]$, $\sum d(b)=\sum d(f(b))$, hence $d(b)=d(f(b))$ and the divisors of $f(b)$ are exactly the images of the divisors of $b$. So $f(a)\mid f(b)\iff a\mid b$.

(ii) $f$ preserves least common multiples. Let $a,b\in[n]$ with $L=\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)\le n$. Then $f(a),f(b)\mid f(L)$, so $L'=\mathrm{lcm}(f(a),f(b))$ divides $f(L)$; in particular $L'\le n$ and $L'=f(c)$ for some $c$. By (i), $a\mid c$ and $b\mid c$, so $L\mid c$ and $f(L)\mid f(c)=L'$. Hence $f(L)=L'$.

(iii) Primes and prime powers. The elements with exactly two divisors are the primes, so $f$ permutes the primes. If $p^e\le n$, the divisors of $f(p^e)$ are the images of $1,p,\dots,p^e$, a chain; so $f(p^e)$ is a power of the prime $f(p)$ with $e+1$ divisors: $f(p^e)=f(p)^e$.

(iv) $f(p)=p$ for $p<\sqrt n$. The multiples of $p$ in $[n]$ are mapped by $f$ into the multiples of $f(p)$, and by (i) the inverse maps back; so $\lfloor n/p\rfloor=\lfloor n/f(p)\rfloor$. As $p^2<n$, $f(p)^2=f(p^2)\le n$. Two different numbers $u<v$ with $uv\le n$ have $\frac nu-\frac nv=\frac{n(v-u)}{uv}\ge1$, hence different values of $\lfloor n/\cdot\rfloor$. Applied to $u,v\in\{p,f(p)\}$ this forces $f(p)=p$, and then $f(p^e)=p^e$ for $p^e\le n$.

(v) Conclusion. Let $k=p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_r^{e_r}\le n$ with all $p_i<\sqrt n$. The numbers $\mathrm{lcm}(p_1^{e_1},\dots,p_j^{e_j})$ ($j\le r$) all divide $k$, so they are at most $n$, and by (ii) and induction on $j$ \[f(k)=\mathrm{lcm}\bigl(f(p_1^{e_1}),\dots,f(p_r^{e_r})\bigr)=\mathrm{lcm}(p_1^{e_1},\dots,p_r^{e_r})=k.\]

Problem 4

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Weighted Counting

Let $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i|=1$. Define \[D_{i,j}=\max\{x_i,\dots,x_{i+j-1}\}-\min\{x_i,\dots,x_{i+j-1}\},\] and determine the minimum value of $\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{i,j}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Arrange the entries in a circle and list the starting positions and lengths of the windows for a small $n$. Mark which windows have zero range.
Hint 2: key idea
Think of a window's range as the length of the interval between its smallest and largest entries. At a given level $\lambda$, count how many windows have entries on both sides of $\lambda$. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the number of windows missing one side by counting subwindows of the uninterrupted arcs that avoid that side. For positive and negative levels separately, use concavity in the threshold count to get a bound proportional to that count, then integrate using the fixed mass of each sign. Optimize the two sign counts and look for an arrangement for which every estimate is tight.
Common trap
Windows that run past $x_n$ wrap around to $x_1$, so read the indices cyclically when counting the windows that avoid a set of positions.
Show the answer

$\dfrac{3n}2-1$ for even $n$, $\dfrac{n(3n+1)}{2(n+1)}$ for odd $n$

Show the solution – Layer-cake counting

Indices are cyclic ($x_{k+n}=x_k$); a window is a pair $(i,j)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$, standing for the cyclic block $x_i,\dots,x_{i+j-1}$ of length $j$. There are $n^2$ windows. Put $T(m)=\frac{m(m+1)}2$. Note $n\ge2$ (for $n=1$ the conditions are contradictory).

Layer cake. For a window $W$, $D(W)=\max_W-\min_W$ is the length of the set of $\lambda$ such that $W$ contains an entry $>\lambda$ and an entry $<\lambda$. Hence \[\sum_WD(W)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N(\lambda)\,d\lambda,\] where $N(\lambda)$ is the number of windows containing an entry above $\lambda$ and an entry below $\lambda$.

A bound for $N(\lambda)$. Let $a(\lambda)=\#\{k:x_k>\lambda\}$ and $b(\lambda)=\#\{k:x_k<\lambda\}$, both at least $1$ for $\min x<\lambda<\max x$. A window that misses all $a$ entries above $\lambda$ lies inside one of the arcs formed by the other $n-a$ positions; an arc of length $L$ contains $T(L)$ windows, and $\sum T(L_r)\le T(\sum L_r)$, so at most $T(n-a)$ windows miss them. Similarly at most $T(n-b)$ windows miss the entries below $\lambda$. Therefore \[N(\lambda)\ge n^2-T(n-a(\lambda))-T(n-b(\lambda)).\]

The positive levels. Let $p$ and $q$ be the numbers of positive and negative $x_k$; as $\sum x_k=0$ and $\sum|x_k|=1$, the positive entries add up to $\frac12$, the negative ones to $-\frac12$, and $p,q\ge1$, $p+q\le n$. For $0<\lambda<\max x$ we have $b(\lambda)\ge n-p$ and $1\le a(\lambda)\le p$, so $N(\lambda)\ge G(a(\lambda))$ with \[G(a)=n^2-T(p)-T(n-a).\] $G$ is concave in $a$ and $G(0)=n^2-T(p)-T(n)\ge n^2-T(n-1)-T(n)=0$, so $G(a)/a$ is non-increasing for $a>0$, and $G(a(\lambda))\ge a(\lambda)\frac{G(p)}p$. Since $\int_0^{\max x}a(\lambda)\,d\lambda=\sum_{x_k>0}x_k=\frac12$, \[\int_0^{\infty}N(\lambda)\,d\lambda\ge\frac{G(p)}{2p}=\Phi(p),\qquad \Phi(s)=\frac{n^2-T(s)-T(n-s)}{2s}=\frac{n(n-1)}{4s}+\frac{n-s}2 .\] The same argument for $\lambda<0$ gives $\int_{-\infty}^0N\ge\Phi(q)$. So \[\sum D_{i,j}\ge\Phi(p)+\Phi(q).\]

Minimising. $\Phi$ is decreasing, so with $p+q\le n$ the right side is at least $\Phi(p)+\Phi(n-p)=\frac{n^2(n-1)}{4p(n-p)}+\frac n2$, which is smallest when $p(n-p)$ is largest. For even $n$, $p(n-p)\le\frac{n^2}4$ gives the bound $(n-1)+\frac n2=\frac{3n}2-1$; for odd $n$, $p(n-p)\le\frac{n^2-1}4$ gives $\frac{n^2}{n+1}+\frac n2=\frac{n(3n+1)}{2(n+1)}$.

Equality. Take $p=\lceil n/2\rceil$ consecutive entries equal to $\frac1{2p}$ followed by $q=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ entries equal to $-\frac1{2q}$. A window has $D=0$ if it lies inside one block and $D=\frac1{2p}+\frac1{2q}$ otherwise; the blocks contain $T(p)+T(q)$ windows, so the sum is $\bigl(\frac1{2p}+\frac1{2q}\bigr)\bigl(n^2-T(p)-T(q)\bigr)$, which equals $\frac{3n}2-1$ for $p=q=\frac n2$ and $\frac{n(3n+1)}{2(n+1)}$ for $p=\frac{n+1}2$, $q=\frac{n-1}2$.

The minimum is $\boxed{\tfrac{3n}2-1}$ for even $n$ and $\boxed{\tfrac{n(3n+1)}{2(n+1)}}$ for odd $n$.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

Triangle $ABC$ has orthocenter $H$. $D$ and $E$ are the feet of the altitudes from $B$ and $C$ respectively. $P$ is a point inside $\triangle ABC$ on the circumcircle of $BHC$. $BP$ and $AC$ intersect at $Q$, and $CP$ and $AB$ intersect at $R$. Show that $DE$ bisects $QR$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the midpoint of $QR$ as the point you want to place on $DE$. Record which two points lie along each side ray from $A$.
Hint 2: key idea
Reflect the circumcircle of $ABC$ across $BC$ to recognize the circle through $B$, $H$, and $C$. Use the angle subtended by $BC$ at $P$ to relate $\angle ABQ$ and $\angle ACR$. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Express $D,E,Q,R$ in the basis of unit vectors along $AC$ and $AB$ and write the midpoint condition by comparing coefficients on $DE$. Use the sine rule in $ABQ$ and $ACR$ to express the two distances to $Q$ and $R$. Substitute the angle relation and look for a sum-to-product identity.
Common trap
The position of $P$ inside the triangle determines which inscribed angle subtends $BC$.
Show solution 1 – Positions along the sides

Write $A,B,C$ also for the angles, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and let $e_1,e_2$ be the unit vectors from $A$ towards $C$ and $B$.

The angle at $P$. The circle $BHC$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $BC$, so its points on the same side of $BC$ as $A$ see $BC$ under the angle $180^\circ-A$. Hence $\angle BPC=180^\circ-A$ and $\angle PBC+\angle PCB=A$. With $\varphi=\angle ABQ$ and $\psi=\angle ACR$ ($P$ is inside, so $\angle PBC=B-\varphi$, $\angle PCB=C-\psi$) this gives \[\varphi+\psi=180^\circ-2A ;\] in particular $A<90^\circ$, so $D$ and $E$ lie on the rays $AC$ and $AB$, with $AD=c\cos A$ and $AE=b\cos A$.

A criterion. Put $Q=AQ\,e_1$, $R=AR\,e_2$, $D=c\cos A\,e_1$, $E=b\cos A\,e_2$ (origin $A$). The midpoint $\frac12(AQ\,e_1+AR\,e_2)$ lies on line $DE$ iff it equals $(1-\lambda)c\cos A\,e_1+\lambda b\cos A\,e_2$ for some $\lambda$; comparing coefficients, iff \[\frac{AQ}{c}+\frac{AR}{b}=2\cos A .\tag{1}\]

Computation. In triangle $ABQ$, $\angle AQB=180^\circ-A-\varphi$, so $AQ=\dfrac{c\sin\varphi}{\sin(A+\varphi)}$; similarly $AR=\dfrac{b\sin\psi}{\sin(A+\psi)}$. Since $\psi=180^\circ-2A-\varphi$, $\sin\psi=\sin(2A+\varphi)$ and $\sin(A+\psi)=\sin(A+\varphi)$. Thus \[\frac{AQ}c+\frac{AR}b=\frac{\sin\varphi+\sin(2A+\varphi)}{\sin(A+\varphi)}=\frac{2\sin(A+\varphi)\cos A}{\sin(A+\varphi)}=2\cos A,\] which is (1). So the midpoint of $QR$ lies on $DE$.

Show solution 2 – A cyclic quadrilateral and two right triangles

Let $b=CA$, $c=AB$, and write $A$ also for $\angle BAC$.

$ARPQ$ is cyclic. The circle $BHC$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $BC$, so from its points on $A$'s side of $BC$ the segment $BC$ is seen under $180^\circ-A$. Hence $\angle RPQ=\angle BPC=180^\circ-A$ (vertical angles), and $A,R,P,Q$ are concyclic. Consequently \[\angle AQB+\angle ARC=\angle AQP+\angle ARP=180^\circ .\tag{1}\] (Also $\angle PBC+\angle PCB=A$ forces $A<90^\circ$, so the feet $D,E$ lie on the rays $AC$, $AB$.)

Signed positions. Measure signed lengths along $AC$ in the direction from $A$ to $C$, and along $AB$ from $A$ to $B$. In the right triangle formed by $B$, its foot $D$ and $Q$ on line $AC$ one has \[\overline{DQ}=BD\cot\angle AQB,\] (positive exactly when $\angle AQB$ is acute, i.e. when $Q$ is beyond $D$), and likewise $\overline{ER}=CE\cot\angle ARC$. Here $BD=c\sin A$ and $CE=b\sin A$. By (1), $\cot\angle ARC=-\cot\angle AQB$, so \[b\,\overline{DQ}+c\,\overline{ER}=bc\sin A\,(\cot\angle AQB+\cot\angle ARC)=0 .\tag{2}\]

Midpoint. Take $A$ as origin and unit vectors $e_1$ (towards $C$), $e_2$ (towards $B$). Then $D=AD\,e_1$, $E=AE\,e_2$ with $AD=c\cos A$, $AE=b\cos A$, and the midpoint of $QR$ is \[N=\tfrac12\bigl((AD+\overline{DQ})\,e_1+(AE+\overline{ER})\,e_2\bigr).\] Put $\mu=\dfrac{\overline{ER}}{b\cos A}$; then $\overline{ER}=\mu\,AE$ and, by (2), $\overline{DQ}=-\frac cb\overline{ER}=-\mu\,c\cos A=-\mu\,AD$. Hence \[N=\frac{1-\mu}2\,AD\,e_1+\frac{1+\mu}2\,AE\,e_2=\frac{1-\mu}2\,D+\frac{1+\mu}2\,E,\] an affine combination of $D$ and $E$. So $N$ lies on line $DE$, i.e. $DE$ bisects $QR$.

Show solution 3 – The locus is a circle

Let $c=AB$, $b=AC$ and $h=\cos\angle BAC$. Since $(BHC)$ is a circle, $B$, $H$, $C$ are distinct and not collinear, so the angles at $B$ and $C$ are not right. Let $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ be the unit vectors from $A$ towards $B$ and $C$, so $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=h$, and give each point $X$ the oblique coordinates $(x,y)$ defined by $\overrightarrow{AX}=x\mathbf u+y\mathbf v$. Then \[AX^2=x^2+y^2+2hxy. \tag{1}\]

The points. $B=(c,0)$ and $C=(0,b)$. The foot $E$ of the altitude from $C$ is the projection of $C$ on line $AB$, so $\overrightarrow{AE}=(\overrightarrow{AC}\cdot\mathbf u)\,\mathbf u=bh\,\mathbf u$, i.e. $E=(bh,0)$; likewise $D=(0,ch)$. The line $DE$ is assumed to exist, so $D\ne E$; since $D=E$ would force $D=E=A$, i.e. $h=0$, we have $h\ne0$, and line $DE$ is $\frac{x}{bh}+\frac{y}{ch}=1$.

Let $P=(x,y)$. As $P$ is inside the triangle, $x>0$, $y>0$ and $\frac xc+\frac yb<1$; in particular $x<c$ and $y<b$. The points of line $BP$ are $B+s(P-B)=(c+s(x-c),\,sy)$; the first coordinate vanishes for $s=\frac c{c-x}$, so $Q=\bigl(0,\frac{cy}{c-x}\bigr)$. In the same way $R=\bigl(\frac{bx}{b-y},0\bigr)$. The midpoint of $QR$ is $\bigl(\frac{bx}{2(b-y)},\frac{cy}{2(c-x)}\bigr)$, and it lies on $DE$ if and only if \[\frac{x}{b-y}+\frac{y}{c-x}=2h .\] Multiplying by $(b-y)(c-x)>0$, this is equivalent to $x(c-x)+y(b-y)=2h(b-y)(c-x)$, that is, to $F(x,y)=0$, where \[F(x,y)=x^2+y^2+2hxy-(c+2hb)x-(b+2hc)y+2hbc .\]

The curve $F=0$ is the circle $(BHC)$. By (1), $F(x,y)=AX^2+\ell(X)$, where $\ell(X)=-(c+2hb)x-(b+2hc)y+2hbc$ is an affine function of the point $X$ (the oblique coordinates depend linearly on $\overrightarrow{AX}$). Writing $\ell(X)=-2\,\mathbf w\cdot\overrightarrow{AX}+k$ for a suitable vector $\mathbf w$ and number $k$, and completing the square, \[F=\bigl|\overrightarrow{AX}-\mathbf w\bigr|^2+k-|\mathbf w|^2 .\] So the set $F=0$ is a circle, a single point or empty. It contains three non-collinear points:

  • $F(c,0)=c^2-c(c+2hb)+2hbc=0$, so $B$ is on it, and by symmetry $F(0,b)=0$, so $C$ is on it;

  • for $H=(x,y)$: $H$ lies on line $CE$, which is $\frac x{bh}+\frac yb=1$, so $x=h(b-y)$; and $H$ lies on line $BD$, which is $\frac xc+\frac y{ch}=1$, so $y=h(c-x)$. Then $x(c-x)+y(b-y)=h(b-y)(c-x)+h(c-x)(b-y)=2h(b-y)(c-x)$, so $F$ vanishes at $H$.

Hence $F=0$ is exactly the circle through $B$, $H$, $C$.

Conclusion. Since $P$ lies on $(BHC)$, $F(x,y)=0$, and by the equivalence above (valid because $P$ is inside the triangle) the midpoint of $QR$ lies on $DE$. So $DE$ bisects $QR$.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Reformulation of Real Problems

Complex numbers $z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4$ satisfy \[z_1+z_2+z_3+z_4=0,\qquad |z_i-\bar z_j|\le1\ \ (1\le i,j\le4).\] Determine the maximum of $\left|\sum_{1\le i<j\le4}z_iz_j\right|$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Expand the square of the zero sum and mark where the products with distinct indices appear. Also record what the distance condition says when the two indices are equal.
Hint 2: key idea
Square the distance bounds and add them over every ordered pair $(i,j)$. Group the mixed terms and use the vanishing sum to see what cancels. (LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool)
Hint 3: approach
Use the square of the zero sum to rewrite the objective in terms of the $z_i^2$, then apply the triangle inequality and the bound from the squared distances. To test equality, trace the equality conditions: every distance bound must be tight and all the $z_i^2$ must point in the same direction. Look for a symmetric configuration meeting both requirements.
Common trap
The squared-distance sum includes diagonal pairs and both orders of every off-diagonal pair.
Show the answer

$1$

Show solution 1 – Summing all the constraints

Since $\sum z_i=0$, \[\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j=\frac{\bigl(\sum z_i\bigr)^2-\sum z_i^2}{2}=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^4z_i^2 .\] Now add the squared constraints over all ordered pairs $(i,j)$, including $i=j$: \[\sum_{i,j}|z_i-\bar z_j|^2=\sum_{i,j}\bigl(|z_i|^2+|z_j|^2-z_iz_j-\bar z_i\bar z_j\bigr)=8\sum_i|z_i|^2-\Bigl(\sum z_i\Bigr)^2-\Bigl(\sum\bar z_i\Bigr)^2=8\sum_i|z_i|^2 .\] The left side is at most $16$, so $\sum|z_i|^2\le2$ and \[\Bigl|\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j\Bigr|=\frac12\Bigl|\sum z_i^2\Bigr|\le\frac12\sum|z_i|^2\le1 .\] Equality: $z_1=z_4=-\frac{1+i}2$, $z_2=z_3=\frac{1+i}2$. The sum is $0$. If $z_i,z_j$ have the same sign $\varepsilon$, then $z_i-\bar z_j=\varepsilon\frac{(1+i)-(1-i)}2=\varepsilon i$; if they have opposite signs, $z_i-\bar z_j=\varepsilon\frac{(1+i)+(1-i)}2=\varepsilon$ where $\varepsilon$ is the sign of $z_i$. All these have modulus $1$; and $\sum z_i^2=4\cdot\frac i2=2i$, so $|\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j|=1$. The maximum is $\boxed{1}$.

Show solution 2 – Real and imaginary parts

Write $z_k=x_k+iy_k$. Then $|z_i-\bar z_j|^2=(x_i-x_j)^2+(y_i+y_j)^2$, and $\sum x_k=\sum y_k=0$.

The target. As $\sum z_k=0$, $\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j=-\frac12\sum_kz_k^2$, so \[\Bigl|\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j\Bigr|\le\frac12\sum_k|z_k|^2=\frac12\Bigl(\sum_kx_k^2+\sum_ky_k^2\Bigr).\]

The imaginary parts. The constraint with $i=j=k$ reads $|2y_k|\le1$, so $\sum_ky_k^2\le4\cdot\frac14=1$.

The real parts. Since $\sum x_k=0$, $\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2=4\sum_kx_k^2-\bigl(\sum_kx_k\bigr)^2=4\sum_kx_k^2$. Similarly $\sum_{i<j}(y_i+y_j)^2=3\sum_ky_k^2+2\sum_{i<j}y_iy_j=2\sum_ky_k^2+\bigl(\sum_ky_k\bigr)^2=2\sum_ky_k^2$. Adding the six constraints with $i<j$, \[4\sum_kx_k^2+2\sum_ky_k^2=\sum_{i<j}\Bigl((x_i-x_j)^2+(y_i+y_j)^2\Bigr)\le6 .\]

Conclusion. Hence \[\sum_kx_k^2+\sum_ky_k^2\le\frac{6-2\sum_ky_k^2}4+\sum_ky_k^2=\frac32+\frac12\sum_ky_k^2\le2,\] and $\bigl|\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j\bigr|\le1$.

For $z_1=z_4=-\frac{1+i}2$, $z_2=z_3=\frac{1+i}2$ the sum is $0$ and every $x_k$, $y_k$ is $\pm\frac12$ with $y_k=x_k$. For any $i,j$ either $x_i=x_j$ and $|y_i+y_j|=1$, or $|x_i-x_j|=1$ and $y_i+y_j=0$; so all constraints hold. Moreover $\sum z_k^2=4\cdot\frac{(1+i)^2}4=2i$, so $\bigl|\sum_{i<j}z_iz_j\bigr|=1$. The maximum is $\boxed{1}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

For a fixed positive integer $n$, shade $n^2$ unit squares of an infinite square grid so that no two shaded squares share a vertex (or an edge). Find the minimum number of cells that are not shaded but share at least one vertex with a shaded cell.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try placing four shaded cells in a compact arrangement allowed by the rule. Mark the unshaded neighbors and notice where several shaded cells contribute the same neighbor.
Hint 2: key idea
Name each cell by its lower-left corner and collect all vertices of shaded cells in a set $W$. Describe possible lower-left corners of cells touching $W$ by shifting that set one step at a time in the two coordinate directions. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
In the horizontal shift and union, count the growth in each occupied row and track the change in the number of occupied columns. Repeat vertically and use the product of the row and column counts together with AM–GM. For attainability, look for an arrangement in which every estimate is tight, including equality in AM–GM.
Common trap
The shift count includes the shaded cells themselves, so remove them from the count of neighbors.
Show the answer

$3n^2+4n+1$

Show the solution – Corners of the shaded cells

Name each cell by its lower-left corner $(x,y)\in\mathbb Z^2$; its four corners are $(x,y)+\{0,1\}^2$.

Reformulation. Let $W$ be the set of corners of the shaded cells. Two shaded cells share no vertex, so their corner sets are disjoint and $|W|=4n^2$. A cell shares a vertex with a shaded cell (or is shaded) exactly when one of its corners lies in $W$, i.e. when its lower-left corner lies in $W-\{0,1\}^2$. So the number we want is $|W-\{0,1\}^2|-n^2$, and $|W-\{0,1\}^2|=|W+\{0,1\}^2|$ (reflect the plane).

Lemma. For a finite $Z\subset\mathbb Z^2$ meeting $r$ rows and $c$ columns, $|Z+\{0,1\}^2|\ge|Z|+r+c+1$. Proof. Let $Z_1=Z+\{(0,0),(1,0)\}$. In each row, a finite set $S$ of integers satisfies $|S\cup(S+1)|\ge|S|+1$; so $|Z_1|\ge|Z|+r$, and $Z_1$ meets at least $c+1$ columns (the set of columns grows the same way). Then $Z+\{0,1\}^2=Z_1+\{(0,0),(0,1)\}$, and the same argument in columns gives $|Z+\{0,1\}^2|\ge|Z_1|+(c+1)\ge|Z|+r+c+1$. $\square$

Bound. $W$ meets $r$ rows and $c$ columns with $rc\ge|W|=4n^2$, so $r+c\ge2\sqrt{rc}\ge4n$. By the lemma, \[|W+\{0,1\}^2|\ge4n^2+4n+1=(2n+1)^2,\] and at least $(2n+1)^2-n^2=3n^2+4n+1$ unshaded cells share a vertex with a shaded cell.

Construction. Shade the cells $(2i,2j)$, $0\le i,j\le n-1$. The cells sharing a vertex with them (or shaded) form exactly the $(2n+1)\times(2n+1)$ square of cells $(x,y)$ with $-1\le x,y\le2n-1$, so exactly $3n^2+4n+1$ unshaded cells touch a shaded one. The answer is $\boxed{3n^2+4n+1}$.

Problem 8

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

Find the smallest positive integer $m$ for which there exists a set $\mathcal{F}$ of $m$ ordered triples $(i,j,k)$ satisfying the following conditions:

  • (i) For every $(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{F}$, the integers $i,j,k$ are distinct elements of $\{1,2,\dots,100\}$.

  • (ii) For any $100$ positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{100}$, if \[a_i\mid a_j+a_k\quad\text{for all }(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{F},\] then at least $99$ of the numbers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ are equal.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Distinguish the first index of a triple from its two other indices. Try giving two indices the value $2$ and all others the value $1$, and check when a triple detects the two exceptions.
Hint 2: key idea
Count how many unordered pairs each triple covers by putting one member first and the other later. To make this bound sharp, seek a cyclic arrangement of triples in which every pair is covered once and a prime dividing the values at two indices must spread to all indices. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Use residues modulo $100$ and choose, for each first index, tail offsets so that every pair is covered exactly once, handling leftover distances with a few extra triples. Check that closure under the triples spreads a common prime factor all the way around the cycle. Divide out the common gcd, use the closure to get pairwise coprimality, and examine the parity of the tail sums required by the largest remaining value, also considering the next largest if the largest is even.
Common trap
A triple whose two tails are $u$ and $w$ does nothing for the pair $\{u,w\}$, so each triple covers only two pairs, not three.
Show the answer

$2475$

Show the solution – Pair counting and a cyclic construction

Call $i$ the head and $j,k$ the tails of a triple $(i,j,k)$; the order of $j,k$ does not matter for the condition.

Lower bound: every pair $\{u,w\}$ must occur as head and tail of some triple. Suppose no triple has head $u$ and tail $w$, nor head $w$ and tail $u$. Put $a_u=a_w=2$ and all other $a_i=1$. For a triple with head of value $1$ the condition is automatic. A triple with head $u$ does not contain $w$ and its tails are different from $u$, so both tails have value $1$ and $2\mid1+1$; the same for head $w$. So all conditions hold, but only $98$ numbers are equal. Each triple $(i,j,k)$ covers the two pairs $\{i,j\}$, $\{i,k\}$, so \[2m\ge\binom{100}2=4950,\qquad m\ge2475 .\]

Construction with $2475$ triples. Number the indices by the residues $0,1,\dots,99$ modulo $100$ (index $100$ becomes $0$); all indices below are taken modulo $100$, and a triple is written $(i;j,k)$ with head $i$. Let $\mathcal F$ consist of

  • (T1) $(i;\,i+2k,\,i+2k+1)$ for every $i$ and $k=1,2,\dots,24$  ($2400$ triples);

  • (T2) $(i;\,i+1,\,i+50)$ for $i=0,1,\dots,49$  ($50$ triples);

  • (T3) $(j;\,j-1,\,j+1)$ for $j=51,53,\dots,99$  ($25$ triples).

So $|\mathcal F|=2475$, and the three indices of each triple are distinct. Call two indices at distance $d\in\{1,\dots,50\}$ if they differ by $\pm d$ modulo $100$. The pairs at distance $2k$ or $2k+1$ ($1\le k\le24$, i.e. distances $2,\dots,49$) are covered by (T1), the pairs at distance $50$ by (T2), the pairs $\{i,i+1\}$ with $0\le i\le49$ by (T2) and those with $50\le i\le99$ by (T3). Hence:

  • (E) every pair $\{x,y\}$ occurs as head and tail in exactly one triple; in particular a head never has the same tail in two triples;

  • (H) every index is the head of at least $24$ triples.

We also need a closure property. Call a set $A$ of indices closed if for every triple whose head and one tail lie in $A$, the other tail lies in $A$ as well.

Lemma 1. A closed set with at least two elements contains all $100$ indices.

Proof. By (E), for any $x,y\in A$ some triple has head $x$ and tail $y$ or head $y$ and tail $x$; its third index lies in $A$.

  • $A$ contains two consecutive indices. Take $x\ne y$ in $A$; replacing the pair by $y,x$ if necessary, $y=x+d$ with $1\le d\le50$. If $d=1$ there is nothing to show. If $d=2k$ or $d=2k+1$ with $1\le k\le24$, the triple $(x;x+2k,x+2k+1)$ puts both $x+2k$ and $x+2k+1$ into $A$. If $d=50$, write the pair as $\{i,i+50\}$ with $0\le i\le49$; the triple $(i;i+1,i+50)$ puts $i+1$ into $A$.

  • $A$ contains three consecutive indices. Let $y,y+1\in A$. If $50\le y\le99$: for odd $y$ the triple $(y;y-1,y+1)$ gives $y-1\in A$, and for even $y$ the triple $(y+1;y,y+2)$ gives $y+2\in A$. If $1\le y\le49$: $(y;y+1,y+50)$ gives $y+50\in A$, and then $(y+1;y+49,y+50)$ (type (T1), $k=24$) gives $y+49\in A$; now $y+49,y+50\in A$ with $50\le y+49\le98$, the previous case. If $y=0$: as before $50,49\in A$; then $(49;50,99)$ gives $99\in A$, and $(99;98,0)$ gives $98\in A$, so $98,99,0\in A$.

  • All indices. If $z,z+1,z+2\in A$, the triple $(z;z+2,z+3)$ gives $z+3\in A$; repeating, $A$ contains every index. $\square$

$\mathcal F$ works. Let $a_1,\dots,a_{100}$ satisfy all conditions and let $g$ be their greatest common divisor. The numbers $b_i=a_i/g$ satisfy the same conditions and have no common prime factor; we show that at least $99$ of them equal $1$.

Step 1: the $b_i$ are pairwise coprime. Let $p$ be a prime and $A=\{i:p\mid b_i\}$. If $(h;j,k)\in\mathcal F$ with $h,j\in A$, then $p\mid b_h\mid b_j+b_k$ and $p\mid b_j$, so $p\mid b_k$ and $k\in A$. Thus $A$ is closed. It is not the set of all indices (the $b_i$ have no common prime factor), so by Lemma 1 it has at most one element.

Step 2: at most one $b_i$ exceeds $1$. Suppose $S=\{i:b_i>1\}$ has at least two elements. Being pairwise coprime, the numbers $b_i$ with $i\in S$ are distinct. Let $u\in S$ carry the largest value $M$; then $M\ge3$. For every triple $(u;j,k)$ we have $b_j,b_k<M$, so $M\mid b_j+b_k<2M$ forces $b_j+b_k=M$.

  • If $M$ is odd, one of $b_j,b_k$ is even. At most one index $e$ has $b_e$ even (pairwise coprime), and by (E) at most one triple with head $u$ has $e$ as a tail. So $u$ heads at most one triple, contradicting (H).

  • If $M$ is even, all other $b_i$ are odd. Let $w\in S\setminus\{u\}$ carry the largest value $M'$ there; $M'$ is odd and $M'\ge3$. Take a triple $(w;j,k)$ with $u\notin\{j,k\}$. Then $b_j,b_k\le M'$, and $b_j,b_k\ne M'$ because only $w$ has this value; so $b_j+b_k$ is even and lies strictly between $0$ and $2M'$. An even multiple of the odd number $M'$ is a multiple of $2M'$, which is impossible. Hence every triple with head $w$ contains $u$ as a tail; by (E) there is at most one such triple, contradicting (H).

So at least $99$ of the $b_i$ equal $1$, that is, at least $99$ of the $a_i$ equal $g$. Hence $\mathcal F$ satisfies (ii), and the smallest $m$ is $\boxed{2475}$.
