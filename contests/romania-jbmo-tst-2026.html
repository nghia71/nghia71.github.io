Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

Let $ABC$ be a right triangle with $\angle BAC=90^\circ$. Let $D$ be a point on segment $AC$ and $E$ a point on segment $BD$ such that $\angle ABC=\angle ECD=\angle CED$. Prove that $BE=2AD$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reflect $D$ in $A$)

Let $\beta=\angle ABC$. In triangle $ECD$ we have $\angle ECD=\angle CED=\beta$, so it is isosceles with \[DE=DC.\] Since $E$ lies on segment $BD$, the ray $DE$ is the ray $DB$, hence $\angle BDC=\angle EDC=180^\circ-2\beta$.

If $D=A$, then $\angle BAC=180^\circ-2\beta$ gives $\beta=45^\circ$, so $AB=AC$; then $E$ lies on segment $AB$ with $AE=DE=AC=AB$, so $E=B$ and $BE=0=2AD$. From now on let $D\neq A$. Then $D$ is an interior point of segment $AC$, so $\angle ADB=180^\circ-\angle BDC=2\beta$, and from the right triangle $ABD$, \[\angle ABD=90^\circ-2\beta .\]

Let $D'$ be the reflection of $D$ in $A$; it lies on line $AC$, on the other side of $A$ from $C$, and $D'A=AD$. Because $BA\perp DD'$ and $A$ is the midpoint of $DD'$, line $BA$ is the perpendicular bisector of $DD'$, so \[BD'=BD\qquad\text{and}\qquad \angle ABD'=\angle ABD=90^\circ-2\beta .\] The points $D'$ and $C$ lie on opposite sides of line $AB$, so \[\angle D'BC=\angle D'BA+\angle ABC=(90^\circ-2\beta)+\beta=90^\circ-\beta,\] while $\angle D'CB=\angle ACB=90^\circ-\beta$. Thus triangle $D'BC$ is isosceles with $D'B=D'C$. Therefore \[BD=BD'=D'C=D'A+AD+DC=2AD+DC.\] Finally, $E$ lies on segment $BD$ and $DE=DC$, so \[BE=BD-DE=(2AD+DC)-DC=2AD,\] which is what we wanted to prove.

Solution 2 (Trigonometry in the two right triangles)

Let $\beta=\angle ABC$. Since $\angle ECD=\angle CED=\beta$, triangle $ECD$ is isosceles with $DE=DC$, and $\angle BDC=\angle EDC=180^\circ-2\beta$ because $E$ lies on segment $BD$. If $D=A$, then $\angle BAC=180^\circ-2\beta$ forces $\beta=45^\circ$, so $AB=AC$; then $E$ is the point of segment $AB$ with $AE=AC=AB$, i.e. $E=B$, and $BE=0=2AD$. So let $D\ne A$; then $D$ lies inside segment $AC$ and $\angle ADB=180^\circ-\angle BDC=2\beta$. Note $2\beta<90^\circ$ because $\angle ADB$ is an angle of the right triangle $ABD$.

Put $c=AB$ and $d=AD$. In the right triangles $ABC$ and $ABD$ (right angle at $A$), \[AC=c\tan\beta,\qquad d=AD=c\cot 2\beta,\qquad BD=\frac{c}{\sin 2\beta}.\] Hence $DC=AC-AD=c\tan\beta-c\cot2\beta$, and \begin{align*} BE=BD-DE=BD-DC&=\frac{c}{\sin2\beta}+\frac{c\cos2\beta}{\sin2\beta}-c\tan\beta\\ &=\frac{c\,(1+\cos2\beta)}{\sin2\beta}-c\tan\beta . \end{align*} Using $1+\cos2\beta=2\cos^2\beta$ and $\sin2\beta=2\sin\beta\cos\beta$, the fraction equals $c\cot\beta$, so \[BE=c\,(\cot\beta-\tan\beta)=c\cdot\frac{\cos^2\beta-\sin^2\beta}{\sin\beta\cos\beta}=c\cdot\frac{2\cos2\beta}{\sin2\beta}=2c\cot2\beta=2d .\] Thus $BE=2AD$.

Remark. The data force $30^\circ<\beta\le45^\circ$: $\angle ABD=90^\circ-2\beta\ge0$, and $\angle DBC=\beta-(90^\circ-2\beta)=3\beta-90^\circ>0$ because $D\neq C$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $n$ be a positive integer and let $S$ be a set of $2n$ real numbers with sum $0$. Prove that there exist two disjoint subsets $A$ and $B$ of $S$, each with $n$ elements, such that for every permutation $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ of the elements of $A$ and every permutation $b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n$ of the elements of $B$, the following inequality holds: \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i<0.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Shift by a separating number)

Since $S$ is a set, its $2n$ elements are distinct. Let $A$ be the set of the $n$ smallest elements of $S$ and $B$ the set of the $n$ largest. Then $\max A<\min B$, so the interval $[\max A,\min B]$ has positive length and we can choose a number $c$ in it with $c\neq0$.

Take any orderings $a_1,\dots,a_n$ of $A$ and $b_1,\dots,b_n$ of $B$. Expanding, \[\sum_{i=1}^n (a_i-c)(b_i-c)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i-c\sum_{i=1}^n a_i-c\sum_{i=1}^n b_i+nc^2=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i+nc^2,\] because $\sum a_i+\sum b_i$ is the sum of all elements of $S$, which is $0$. Hence \[\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i=\sum_{i=1}^n (a_i-c)(b_i-c)-nc^2 .\] For every $i$ we have $a_i\le\max A\le c$ and $b_i\ge\min B\ge c$, so $(a_i-c)(b_i-c)\le0$. Therefore \[\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\le -nc^2<0,\] for every pair of orderings. So the sets $A$ and $B$ have the required property.

Remark. Distinctness is essential: for $S=\{0,0\}$ (a multiset) the strict inequality fails.

Solution 2 (Bounding each product by the smallest element of $B$)

Again let $A$ consist of the $n$ smallest and $B$ of the $n$ largest elements of $S$; as the elements are distinct, $a<m$ for every $a\in A$, where $m=\min B$. Fix any pairing of $A$ with $B$ (this is exactly what a choice of the two orderings does); we show that the sum of the $n$ products is negative.

Reduction to $m>0$. If $m\le0$, then every element of $A$ is negative. Replace every element $x$ of $S$ by $-x$: the new set $-S$ still has $2n$ distinct elements with sum $0$, its $n$ smallest elements form $-B$ and its $n$ largest form $-A$, the smallest of which is $-\max A>0$, and every product $ab$ is unchanged because $(-a)(-b)=ab$. So we may assume $m>0$; then all elements of $B$ are positive.

The estimate. Let $A^-$ be the set of negative elements of $A$ and $A^+=A\setminus A^-$. For $a\in A^-$ paired with $b$: since $a<0$ and $b\ge m$, we get $ab\le am$. For $a\in A^+$ paired with $b$: since $0\le a<m$ and $b>0$, we get $ab< mb$. Denote by $B^+$ the set of partners of the elements of $A^+$ and by $B^-$ that of the elements of $A^-$. Adding, \[\sum ab\le m\Big(\sum_{a\in A^-}a+\sum_{b\in B^+}b\Big),\] with strict inequality if $A^+\neq\varnothing$. Since the total sum of $S$ is $0$, \[\sum_{a\in A^-}a+\sum_{b\in B^+}b=-\sum_{a\in A^+}a-\sum_{b\in B^-}b\le 0,\] with strict inequality if $A^-\ne\varnothing$ (then $B^-$ is a nonempty set of positive numbers). As $m>0$ and at least one of $A^-$, $A^+$ is nonempty, one of the two inequalities is strict, so $\sum ab<0$.

Hence $A$ (the $n$ smallest elements) and $B$ (the $n$ largest) satisfy the condition for all orderings.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $n\ge 5$. An $n\times n$ grid is filled with $n^2$ distinct positive integers such that whenever we choose several numbers from the grid, any two of them lying in different rows and different columns, the arithmetic mean of the chosen numbers is a positive integer. Determine the smallest possible value of the largest number in the grid.

Show the answer

$1+(n^2-1)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n-1)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (All entries are congruent modulo $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n-1)$)

Call a set of cells admissible if no two of its cells share a row or a column. The condition says: for every admissible set of $k$ cells ($1\le k\le n$), the sum of its entries is divisible by $k$. Let $L=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n-1)$. We prove that the answer is \[1+(n^2-1)L .\]

Step 1: for $2\le k\le n-1$, all entries are congruent modulo $k$. First let $x$ and $y$ be the entries of two cells in different rows and different columns. Delete the two rows and the two columns of these cells; an $(n-2)\times(n-2)$ grid remains, and since $k-1\le n-2$ we can pick $k-1$ cells $T$ on its diagonal. Then $T$ together with the cell of $x$ is admissible, and so is $T$ together with the cell of $y$ (both have $k$ cells). If $t$ is the sum of the entries in $T$, then $k\mid x+t$ and $k\mid y+t$, so $x\equiv y\pmod k$.

If the cells of $x$ and $y$ share a row (or a column), choose a cell $z$ outside the rows and columns of both (possible since $n\ge3$); by the previous case $x\equiv z\equiv y\pmod k$.

Step 2: the lower bound. By Step 1, any two entries are congruent modulo every $k\in\{2,\dots,n-1\}$, hence modulo $L$. So the $n^2$ entries are distinct positive integers in one residue class modulo $L$. Listed in increasing order, consecutive ones differ by at least $L$, so the largest is at least $1+(n^2-1)L$.

Step 3: a grid attaining the bound. Number rows and columns $0,1,\dots,n-1$ and write \[x_{ij}=1+L\,(ni+j)\] in row $i$, column $j$. As $ni+j$ runs through $0,1,\dots,n^2-1$, the entries are distinct positive integers with largest $1+(n^2-1)L$. Check the condition for an admissible set of $k$ cells:

  • If $k\le n-1$, the sum is $k+L\cdot(\text{integer})$ and $k\mid L$, so the mean is a positive integer.

  • If $k=n$, the cells are $(i,\sigma(i))$ for a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$, and \[\sum_{i}\big(ni+\sigma(i)\big)=n\cdot\frac{n(n-1)}2+\frac{n(n-1)}2=\frac{n(n-1)(n+1)}2 .\] So the mean is $1+L\cdot\dfrac{n^2-1}{2}$, an integer because $L$ is even ($n-1\ge2$).

Hence the smallest possible value of the largest number is \[\boxed{1+(n^2-1)\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n-1)};\] for example $289$ for $n=5$ and $2101$ for $n=6$.

Remark. The argument works for every $n\ge3$. If $0$ were allowed as an entry (and the mean only required to be a natural number), the same proof would give $(n^2-1)\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n-1)$ (shift the construction down by $1$; the $k=n$ mean becomes $L(n^2-1)/2$).

Solution 2 (Row values plus column values)

Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,n$, let $x_{ij}$ be the entry in row $i$, column $j$, and call a set of cells admissible if no two of its cells share a row or a column; the condition says that the entries of every admissible set of $k$ cells have a sum divisible by $k$. Let $L=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n-1)$. We show that the answer is $1+(n^2-1)L$.

Step 1: modulo $k$, every entry is a row value plus a column value ($2\le k\le n$). Take rows $i\ne i'$ and columns $j\ne j'$. Delete these two rows and two columns; on the diagonal of the remaining $(n-2)\times(n-2)$ grid choose a set $U$ of $k-2$ cells (possible as $k-2\le n-2$). Both $U\cup\{(i,j),(i',j')\}$ and $U\cup\{(i,j'),(i',j)\}$ are admissible sets of $k$ cells, so their sums are divisible by $k$, and subtracting, \[x_{ij}+x_{i'j'}\equiv x_{ij'}+x_{i'j}\pmod k .\] With $i'=j'=1$ this gives, for all $i,j$ (trivially if $i=1$ or $j=1$), \[x_{ij}\equiv r_i+c_j\pmod k,\qquad r_i=x_{i1},\quad c_j=x_{1j}-x_{11}.\]

Step 2: for $2\le k\le n-1$ all row values agree and all column values agree. Take a cell $(i,j)$ and another row $i'$. In the rows other than $i,i'$ and the columns other than $j$ there are $n-2$ rows and $n-1$ columns, so we can choose a set $V$ of $k-1\le n-2$ cells in distinct rows and columns there. Then $V\cup\{(i,j)\}$ and $V\cup\{(i',j)\}$ are admissible $k$-sets, whence $x_{ij}\equiv x_{i'j}\pmod k$, that is $r_i+c_j\equiv r_{i'}+c_j$, so $r_i\equiv r_{i'}\pmod k$. In the same way (swap the roles of rows and columns) all $c_j$ are congruent modulo $k$. Hence all $n^2$ entries are congruent to $r_1+c_1$ modulo $k$.

Step 3: the bound. By Step 2 all entries are congruent modulo every $k\le n-1$, hence modulo $L$. They are $n^2$ distinct positive integers in one residue class modulo $L$, so the largest is at least $1+(n^2-1)L$.

Step 4: the construction, read off from Step 1. Step 1 with $k=n$ says that modulo $n$ the grid has the form $r_i+c_j$; then an admissible $n$-set (one cell in each row and each column) has sum $\equiv\sum_i r_i+\sum_j c_j\pmod n$, the same for all such sets. So it is natural to try $x_{ij}=1+L\,\big(n(i-1)+(j-1)\big)$, which uses the $n^2$ numbers $1,\,1+L,\,\dots,\,1+(n^2-1)L$. For an admissible $k$-set with $k\le n-1$ the sum is $k+L\cdot(\text{integer})$ and $k\mid L$, so the mean is a positive integer. For $k=n$ the cells are $(i,\sigma(i))$ for a permutation $\sigma$, and \[\sum_{i=1}^n\big(n(i-1)+\sigma(i)-1\big)=n\cdot\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n(n^2-1)}{2},\] so the mean is $1+L\,\dfrac{n^2-1}{2}$, an integer because $L$ is even. Therefore the minimum of the largest entry is \[\boxed{1+(n^2-1)\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n-1)}.\]

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a scalene triangle, and let $D\in(BC)$, $M\in(AD)$. Denote by $\omega$, $\omega_1$, and $\omega_2$ the circumcircles of triangles $ABC$, $BDM$, and $CDM$, respectively. The circles $\omega$ and $\omega_1$ intersect again at $E\ne B$, while $\omega$ and $\omega_2$ intersect again at $F\ne C$. Line $AB$ meets $\omega_1$ again at $S$, and line $AC$ meets $\omega_2$ again at $R$. Let $Q$ and $P$ be the second intersection points of line $EF$ with $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, respectively (different from $E$ and $F$). Prove that the points $P$, $Q$, $R$, $S$ are concyclic.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of $A$, then parallels and the tangent at $A$)

We use directed angles between lines, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$, taken modulo $180^\circ$; then four points $W,X,Y,Z$ (no three collinear) are concyclic if and only if $\measuredangle(XW,XZ)=\measuredangle(YW,YZ)$, and this works in every configuration. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. Recall the tangent-chord rule: for $X,Y$ on $\omega$, $\measuredangle(t,AY)=\measuredangle(XA,XY)$.

Step 1: $SR\parallel t$. The circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ meet at $D$ and $M$, and $A$ lies on line $DM$. By the power of the point $A$, \[AS\cdot AB=AM\cdot AD=AR\cdot AC .\] By the converse of the power of a point, $B,S,R,C$ are concyclic. Hence \[\measuredangle(SR,AC)=\measuredangle(RS,RC)=\measuredangle(BS,BC)=\measuredangle(BA,BC)=\measuredangle(t,AC),\] the last equality by the tangent-chord rule. So $SR\parallel t$.

Step 2: $SQ\parallel AF$ and $RP\parallel AE$. The points $B,S,E,Q$ lie on $\omega_1$, the points $B,A,E,F$ on $\omega$, the line $BS$ is line $BA$, and the line $QE$ is line $EF$. Hence \[\measuredangle(QS,EF)=\measuredangle(QS,QE)=\measuredangle(BS,BE)=\measuredangle(BA,BE)=\measuredangle(FA,FE),\] so $QS\parallel FA$. In the same way, from $C,R,F,P\in\omega_2$ and $C,A,F,E\in\omega$, \[\measuredangle(PR,EF)=\measuredangle(PR,PF)=\measuredangle(CR,CF)=\measuredangle(CA,CF)=\measuredangle(EA,EF),\] so $PR\parallel EA$. (This is Reim's theorem, used twice.)

Step 3: conclusion. Since $QS\parallel FA$ and $QP$ is the line $EF$, \[\measuredangle(QS,QP)=\measuredangle(FA,FE).\] Since $RS\parallel t$ and $RP\parallel AE$, and by the tangent-chord rule with $F\in\omega$, \[\measuredangle(RS,RP)=\measuredangle(t,AE)=\measuredangle(FA,FE).\] Thus $\measuredangle(QS,QP)=\measuredangle(RS,RP)$, and the points $P,Q,R,S$ are concyclic.

Solution 2 (Inversion at $A$ and the power of the point $Y=SR\cap EF$)

Let $k=AM\cdot AD>0$ (the point $M$ lies between $A$ and $D$). As in Solution 1, $k$ is the power of $A$ with respect to both $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, because $A$ lies on their common chord line $DM$.

The inversion. Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\sqrt k$. If a line through $A$ meets $\omega_1$ at $U$ and $V$, then $AU\cdot AV=k$ with $U,V$ on the same side of $A$, so $\Phi(U)=V$; thus $\Phi$ maps $\omega_1$ onto itself, and likewise $\omega_2$. In particular $\Phi(B)=S$ and $\Phi(C)=R$. The circle $\omega$ passes through $A$, so $\Phi(\omega)$ is a line; it contains $\Phi(B)=S$ and $\Phi(C)=R$, so $\Phi(\omega)$ is the line $SR$.

Let $E^*=\Phi(E)$ and $F^*=\Phi(F)$. Since $E\in\omega\cap\omega_1$, the point $E^*$ lies on line $SR$ and on $\omega_1$; it is the second intersection of line $AE$ with $\omega_1$, and $E^*\neq S$ because $E\neq B$. Likewise $F^*$ lies on line $SR$ and on $\omega_2$, and $F^*\ne R$. Moreover \[AE\cdot AE^*=k=AF\cdot AF^* .\] Note that $E^*=E$ exactly when $AE^2=k$, i.e. when $AE$ is tangent to $\omega_1$, and then $E$ lies on line $SR$; similarly for $F$. Also $E\ne S$ (the only points of line $AB$ on $\omega$ are $A$ and $B$, and $A\notin\omega_1$, $E\ne B$), and likewise $F\ne R$. The cases $E^*=E$ and $F^*=F$ cannot occur together: then $E,F$ would both lie on $SR$, so line $EF$ would be line $SR$ and $Q\in\omega_1\cap SR=\{S,E\}$ would give $Q=S$, a degenerate figure. We distinguish three cases.

Case 1: $E^*\ne E$, $F^*\ne F$, and lines $SR$, $EF$ meet at a point $Y$. As $A,E,F$ are not collinear (three points of $\omega$), the converse of the power of a point shows that $E,F,E^*,F^*$ lie on a circle $\Gamma$. Also $Y\ne E$: otherwise $E\in SR\cap\omega_1=\{S,E^*\}$, impossible; likewise $Y\ne F$. Using signed lengths along each line, the power of $Y$ gives \begin{align*} \text{w.r.t. }\omega_1:&\quad YS\cdot YE^*=YE\cdot YQ,\\ \text{w.r.t. }\omega_2:&\quad YR\cdot YF^*=YF\cdot YP,\\ \text{w.r.t. }\Gamma:&\quad YE^*\cdot YF^*=YE\cdot YF\ne0 . \end{align*} Multiplying the first two equalities and dividing by the third gives \[YS\cdot YR=YQ\cdot YP .\] The points $S,R$ lie on one line through $Y$ and $Q,P$ on another, so by the converse of the power of a point, $P,Q,R,S$ are concyclic.

Case 2: $SR\parallel EF$. Then $E,F\notin SR$, so $E^*\ne E$, $F^*\ne F$, and $\Gamma$ exists as in Case 1. Project every point orthogonally onto line $SR$ and write $x(\cdot)$ for the coordinate of the projection. Two parallel chords of a circle have the same perpendicular bisector (the perpendicular from the centre), so their midpoints have equal projections; this also holds if a chord shrinks to a point of tangency. Applying this to the chords $SE^*,EQ$ of $\omega_1$, $RF^*,FP$ of $\omega_2$, and $E^*F^*,EF$ of $\Gamma$: \begin{align*} x(S)+x(E^*)&=x(E)+x(Q),\\ x(R)+x(F^*)&=x(F)+x(P),\\ x(E^*)+x(F^*)&=x(E)+x(F). \end{align*} Adding the first two and subtracting the third gives $x(S)+x(R)=x(Q)+x(P)$. So the parallel segments $SR$ and $QP$ have the same perpendicular bisector; $SRPQ$ is an isosceles trapezoid (or a rectangle), hence cyclic.

Case 3: $E^*=E$ (the case $F^*=F$ is the same with the roles of $\omega_1,\omega_2$ exchanged). Then $F^*\ne F$, $E$ lies on line $SR$, and $SR\ne EF$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle through $E,F,F^*$ (not collinear, as $F\notin SR$). The power of $A$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $AF\cdot AF^*=k=AE^2$; if line $AE$ met $\Gamma$ again at $E'$, then $AE\cdot AE'=AE^2$ gives $E'=E$, so $AE$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $E$. It is also tangent to $\omega_1$ at $E$ (as $AE^2$ is the power of $A$). So $\omega_1$ and $\Gamma$ are tangent at $E$, and they are different circles ($F\in\Gamma$, but $F\notin\omega_1$ since $\omega\cap\omega_1=\{B,E\}$, $F\ne E$, and $F\ne B$ because line $BC$ meets $\omega_2$ only at $C$ and $D$). Let $h$ be the homothety with centre $E$ mapping $\omega_1$ onto $\Gamma$, with signed ratio $\lambda$. It maps every line through $E$ to itself and the second intersection with $\omega_1$ to the second intersection with $\Gamma$: on line $SR$ it sends $S$ to $F^*$, and on line $EF$ it sends $Q$ to $F$. Hence, in signed lengths, \[ES=\lambda\,EF^*,\qquad EQ=\lambda\,EF .\] The power of $E$ with respect to $\omega_2$ along the lines $SR$ and $EF$ gives $ER\cdot EF^*=EF\cdot EP$. Therefore \[ES\cdot ER=\lambda\,EF^*\cdot ER=\lambda\,EF\cdot EP=EQ\cdot EP,\] and the converse of the power of a point (with lines $SR$ and $QP$ through $E$) shows again that $P,Q,R,S$ are concyclic.

Solution 3 (A distance ratio on circles through $D$ and $M$)

Both $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ pass through $D$ and $M$, and line $DM$ is line $AD$. We assume the figure is non-degenerate, as the statement describes it: $S\ne B$, $R\ne C$, $P\ne Q$, $S\ne Q$ and $R\ne P$ (otherwise fewer than four distinct points are named, or a second intersection is missing). Note that $A\notin\omega_1$ and $A\notin\omega_2$, because line $AD$ meets these circles only at $D$ and $M$; also $E\ne S$ (line $AB$ meets $\omega$ only at $A$ and $B$) and $F\ne R$.

The ratio. Fix one of the two open half-planes bounded by line $DM$ and call it positive. For a point $X$ of $\omega_1$ or $\omega_2$ other than $D,M$ (so $X\notin DM$) put \[u(X)=\pm\frac{DX}{MX},\] with the sign $+$ if $X$ is in the positive half-plane and $-$ otherwise. For a point $T$ of line $DM$ other than $M$ write $\rho(T)=\overline{DT}/\overline{MT}$ (signed lengths along $DM$). With the coordinate $x$ on line $DM$ such that $D=0$, $M=1$, we have $\rho=x/(x-1)$; this takes every real value except $1$ exactly once, so $\rho(T)$ determines $T$.

Lemma. Let $\Gamma$ be a circle through $D$ and $M$, and let $X\ne Y$ be points of $\Gamma$ other than $D,M$.

  • If line $XY$ meets line $DM$ at $T$, then $u(X)\,u(Y)=\rho(T)$.

  • If $XY\parallel DM$, then $u(X)\,u(Y)=1$.

Proof. In the first case $T\ne D,M$ (a line through $D$ meets $\Gamma$ at $D$, which is neither $X$ nor $Y$). By the power of the point $T$, $TX\cdot TY=TD\cdot TM$. Triangles $TDX$ and $TYM$ have the same angle at $T$ (the rays $TD,TM$ point the same way exactly when $TX,TY$ do, since both products have the same sign) and $TD/TY=TX/TM$; hence they are similar, and $DX/YM=TD/TY$. Exchanging $X$ and $Y$, $DY/XM=TD/TX$. Multiplying, \[\frac{DX\cdot DY}{MX\cdot MY}=\frac{TD^2}{TX\cdot TY}=\frac{TD^2}{TD\cdot TM}=\frac{TD}{TM}.\] For the signs: $u(X)u(Y)<0$ if and only if $X,Y$ are on opposite sides of $DM$, that is, $T$ lies between $X$ and $Y$, that is, $T$ is inside $\Gamma$, that is, $T$ lies between $D$ and $M$ (the points of line $DM$ inside $\Gamma$), that is, $\rho(T)<0$. In the second case, the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $DM$ maps $\Gamma$ to itself (its centre lies on that bisector) and also maps the chord $XY$ to itself (a chord parallel to $DM$ has the same perpendicular bisector). So it swaps $D,M$ and swaps $X,Y$, giving $DX=MY$ and $DY=MX$; as $X,Y$ are on the same side of $DM$, $u(X)u(Y)=1$. $\square$

Three relations.

  • 1. The chord $BS$ of $\omega_1$ and the chord $CR$ of $\omega_2$ lie on lines $AB$ and $AC$, which both meet $DM$ at $A$. So $u(B)u(S)=\rho(A)=u(C)u(R)$.

  • 2. Line $BE$ is the radical axis of $\omega,\omega_1$, line $CF$ that of $\omega,\omega_2$, and line $DM$ that of $\omega_1,\omega_2$. If $BE$ meets $DM$ at $Z$, then $Z$ has equal powers with respect to all three circles, so $Z\in CF$; if $BE\parallel DM$, then also $CF\parallel DM$, since a common point of $CF$ and $DM$ would lie on $BE$. In both cases the lemma gives $u(B)u(E)=u(C)u(F)$.

  • 3. The chords $EQ$ of $\omega_1$ and $FP$ of $\omega_2$ lie on the same line $EF$, so they meet $DM$ at the same point or are both parallel to it, and $u(E)u(Q)=u(F)u(P)$. (Here $Q,P\notin\{D,M\}$: if line $EF$ passed through $D$, then both second intersections would be $D$, and $P=Q$; likewise for $M$.)

All these values of $u$ are nonzero. Dividing the product of relations 1 and 3 by relation 2, \[u(S)\,u(Q)=u(R)\,u(P).\]

Conclusion. By the lemma, the chords $SQ$ of $\omega_1$ and $RP$ of $\omega_2$ either meet $DM$ at the same point $T$, or are both parallel to $DM$. They are different lines: a common line would contain $Q\ne P$, so it would be line $EF$, and then $S\in EF\cap\omega_1=\{E,Q\}$, giving $S=Q$.

  • If they meet $DM$ at $T$, then $T\ne D,M$ and $T$ lies on the radical axis of $\omega_1,\omega_2$, so in signed lengths $\overline{TS}\cdot\overline{TQ}=\overline{TR}\cdot\overline{TP}\ne0$. By the converse of the power of a point (two different lines through $T$), $P,Q,R,S$ are concyclic.

  • If both are parallel to $DM$, then each has as perpendicular bisector the perpendicular through the centre of its circle, and both centres lie on the perpendicular bisector $m$ of the common chord $DM$. So the reflection in $m$ swaps $S$ with $Q$ and $R$ with $P$; the two different parallel segments $SQ$ and $RP$ have the common perpendicular bisector $m$, so $SQPR$ is an isosceles trapezoid (or a rectangle), which is cyclic.

In every case $P$, $Q$, $R$, $S$ are concyclic.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

We say that two positive integers $a$ and $b$ are interlaced if they have the same number of proper divisors, at least two, denoted $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ and $b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n$, such that $a_1<b_1<a_2<b_2<\cdots<a_n<b_n$.

  • (a) Give an example of interlaced numbers $a$ and $b$ that each have exactly $2026$ divisors.

  • (b) If $a$ and $b$ are interlaced, is it possible that $a+b$ and $ab$ are also interlaced?

Show the answer

(a) for example $a=2027^{2025}$, $b=2029^{2025}$; (b) no

Show the solution

Solution (Prime powers for (a); the largest proper divisors for (b))

Here a proper divisor of $N$ is a divisor different from $1$ and $N$ (if $1$ were counted, we would always have $a_1=b_1=1$ and no two numbers could be interlaced). If $N$ has proper divisors $d_1<d_2<\dots<d_n$, then $d_1$ is the smallest prime factor of $N$ and $d_i\,d_{n+1-i}=N$ for all $i$ (the map $d\mapsto N/d$ reverses the order). In particular $d_n=N/d_1$.

A first observation. If $a$ and $b$ are interlaced, then $a<b$, because $a=a_1a_n<b_1b_n=b$.

(a) The numbers $p=2027$ and $q=2029$ are primes (neither is divisible by a prime up to $43$, and the next prime satisfies $47^2=2209>2029$). Take \[a=2027^{2025},\qquad b=2029^{2025}.\] Each has exactly $2026$ divisors, and the proper divisors are $p^k$ and $q^k$ for $k=1,2,\dots,2024$. We need \[p<q<p^2<q^2<\dots<p^{2024}<q^{2024},\] that is, $p^k<q^k<p^{k+1}$ for $1\le k\le 2024$. The left inequality is clear. For the right one, $q^k<p^{k+1}$ means $(q/p)^k<p$, and, using $1+x<e^x$, \[\Big(\frac qp\Big)^{k}\le\Big(1+\frac2{2027}\Big)^{2024}<e^{2\cdot2024/2027}<e^2<8<2027 .\] So $a$ and $b$ are interlaced. (Any primes $p<q$ with $q^{2024}<p^{2025}$ work in the same way, e.g. $1019$ and $1021$.)

(b) No. Suppose $a,b$ are interlaced (so $a<b$, with $n\ge2$ proper divisors each) and that $X=a+b$ and $Y=ab$ are also interlaced, with proper divisors $x_1<\dots<x_m$ and $y_1<\dots<y_m$, $m\ge2$.

The smallest prime factor of $Y=ab$ is $\min(a_1,b_1)=a_1$, so $y_1=a_1$. The number $b_1$ is a proper divisor of $Y$ different from $a_1$, hence $y_2\le b_1$, and therefore \[y_{m-1}=\frac{Y}{y_2}\ge\frac{ab}{b_1}.\] On the other hand $x_m=X/x_1\le X/2=(a+b)/2<b$. Interlacing gives $y_{m-1}<x_m$, so \[\frac{ab}{b_1}<b,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a<b_1 .\] But interlacing of $a$ and $b$ gives $b_1<a_2\le a_n<a$ (here $a_2\le a_n$ since $n\ge2$). This contradiction shows that $a+b$ and $ab$ cannot be interlaced.

The reverse order is impossible too: if $ab$ and $a+b$ were interlaced (with $ab$ in the role of the first number), the first observation would give $ab<a+b$, which is false because $ab-a-b=(a-1)(b-1)-1>0$ for $a,b\ge 3$ (numbers with at least two proper divisors are at least $6$).

So the answer to (b) is no.

Remark. Parity gives a little more information. If $u,v$ are interlaced, then $v$ is odd, since its smallest prime factor $v_1$ exceeds $u_1\ge2$. So $b$ is odd; and if $a+b$, $ab$ were interlaced, $ab$ would be odd as well, so $a,b$ are both odd, $a+b$ is even and $x_m=(a+b)/2$ exactly. This is not needed in the proof above.
