Problem 1

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ be a sequence of positive real numbers. For each natural number $k$, define the sums of odd-indexed and even-indexed terms by \[O_k=a_1+a_3+\dots+a_{2k-1},\qquad E_k=a_2+a_4+\dots+a_{2k},\] with the convention that $E_0=0$. We call the sequence good if for every natural number $k$ the following conditions hold: \[a_{2k}\ge 2026\,(O_k-E_{k-1}),\qquad a_{2k+1}\ge 2026\,(E_k-O_k).\] Find the largest real number $M$ such that for every good sequence and for all natural numbers $n$, $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\ge M$.

Show the answer

$M=2025$

Show the solution – Alternating partial sums

Let $c=2026$. Define the alternating sums \[D_{2k-1}=O_k-E_{k-1},\qquad D_{2k}=E_k-O_k\qquad(k\ge1).\] Then $D_1=a_1$ and, for every $n\ge2$, $D_n=a_n-D_{n-1}$ (adding $a_n$ to the sum of the other parity and subtracting). The two conditions of a good sequence say exactly \[a_{n+1}\ge c\,D_n\qquad\text{for all }n\ge1.\]

All $D_n$ are positive. $D_1=a_1>0$, and if $D_n>0$ then $D_{n+1}=a_{n+1}-D_n\ge(c-1)D_n>0$.

Lower bound. For $n=1$: $a_2\ge cD_1=c\,a_1$. For $n\ge2$: from $a_n\ge cD_{n-1}$ we get $D_{n-1}\le a_n/c$, so $D_n=a_n-D_{n-1}\ge\frac{c-1}{c}\,a_n$, and therefore \[a_{n+1}\ge c\,D_n\ge(c-1)\,a_n.\] So $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\ge c-1=2025$ for every good sequence and every $n$.

Sharpness. Take $a_1=1$ and choose every term with equality, $a_{n+1}=cD_n$. Then $D_{n+1}=(c-1)D_n$, so $D_n=(c-1)^{n-1}$ and $a_{n+1}=c(c-1)^{n-1}$; all terms are positive and the sequence is good. For $n\ge2$, \[\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{c(c-1)^{n-1}}{c(c-1)^{n-2}}=c-1=2025.\] Hence the largest such $M$ is $\boxed{2025}$.

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Homothety Centers and Image Tracking

In an isosceles triangle $ABC$ with $AB=AC$, let $I$ be the incenter. Consider the circle $\omega$ centered at $I$ with radius $IB$. Let $X$ be an arbitrary point on $\omega$, distinct from $B$ and $C$. Let $Y$ be the point on $\omega$ such that $CX\parallel BY$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $XY$. Prove that the lines $BX$, $CY$, and $AM$ are concurrent.

Show solution 1 – Complex numbers with $\omega$ as the unit circle

Let $\omega$ be the unit circle, so $I=0$, and use lower-case letters for complex coordinates. Rotate the picture so that the axis of symmetry $AI$ is the imaginary axis; then $C$ is the reflection of $B$ in it, $c=-\bar b=-1/b$, and $a$ lies on the imaginary axis.

The point $A$. Line $BA$ is the reflection of line $BC$ in the bisector $BI$. The reflection of $c$ in the line through $0$ and $b$ is $b^2\bar c=-b^3$, a point of $\omega$; so line $AB$ is the chord through $b$ and $-b^3$, and symmetrically line $AC$ is the chord through $c$ and $-c^3$. For chords $pq$ and $rs$ of the unit circle the intersection is $\dfrac{pq(r+s)-rs(p+q)}{pq-rs}$; with $pq=-b^4$, $rs=-c^4=-b^{-4}$ this gives, after multiplying through by $b^4$, \[a=\frac{(b^3-b)(b^4-1)}{1-b^8}=\frac{b(1-b^2)}{1+b^4}.\]

The point $Y$ and the midpoint $M$. Two chords $pq$, $rs$ of the unit circle are parallel iff $pq=rs$. So $CX\parallel BY$ means $by=cx$, i.e. $y=cx/b=-x/b^2$, and \[m=\frac{x+y}{2}=\frac{x(b^2-1)}{2b^2}.\]

The point $Z=BX\cap CY$. By the chord formula, \[z=\frac{bx(c+y)-cy(b+x)}{bx-cy}=\frac{c\,(b+x)}{b+c}=-\frac{b+x}{b^2-1},\] where we used $y=cx/b$ (the numerator equals $\frac{xc(b-c)(b+x)}{b}$ and the denominator $\frac{x(b^2-c^2)}{b}$; note $b\ne\pm c$).

Collinearity. Put $\lambda=-\dfrac{2b^2}{(b^2-1)^2}=-\dfrac{2}{(b-1/b)^2}$. Since $b-1/b=b-\bar b=2i\operatorname{Im}b$, we have $\lambda=\dfrac{1}{2(\operatorname{Im}b)^2}$, a real number. Now \[\lambda m=-\frac{x}{b^2-1},\qquad (1-\lambda)a=\frac{(b^2-1)^2+2b^2}{(b^2-1)^2}\cdot\frac{b(1-b^2)}{1+b^4}=-\frac{b}{b^2-1},\] so $(1-\lambda)a+\lambda m=-\dfrac{b+x}{b^2-1}=z$. Thus $z-a=\lambda(m-a)$ with $\lambda$ real: the point $Z=BX\cap CY$ lies on line $AM$, and the three lines are concurrent.

Show solution 2 – Rotations about $I$ and a homothety from $A$

Two circles inscribed in angle $A$. Let $N_0$ be the midpoint of $BC$; as the triangle is isosceles, $N_0$ is the point where the incircle (centre $I$, radius $r=IN_0$) touches $BC$. Let $\kappa$ be the circle tangent to $AB$ at $B$ and to $AC$ at $C$; its centre $N$ lies on the axis $AI$. It passes through $I$: with $\angle A+2\angle B=180^\circ$, we get $\angle NBI=90^\circ-\tfrac{\angle B}{2}$ and $\angle NIB=180^\circ-\angle AIB=\tfrac{\angle A}{2}+\tfrac{\angle B}{2}=90^\circ-\tfrac{\angle B}{2}$, so $NI=NB$. So $\kappa$ is the circle through $B$, $I$, $C$. The incircle and $\kappa$ are both tangent to the two sides of angle $A$, so the homothety $h$ with centre $A$ and ratio $NB/r$ maps the incircle onto $\kappa$ and $I$ to $N$. Let $J$ be the point of $\kappa$ diametrically opposite $I$; $h(N_0)=J$, because $IN_0$ and $NJ$ both point along the axis away from $A$.

A rotation. The chords $BY$ and $CX$ of $\omega$ are parallel, so they have a common perpendicular bisector $s$, which passes through $I$. Let $\tau_0$ and $\tau$ be the reflections in $AI$ and in $s$. Then $\tau_0$ swaps $B,C$ and $\tau$ swaps $B,Y$ and $C,X$, so $\mu=\tau\circ\tau_0$ is the rotation about $I$ through $\theta=2\measuredangle(AI,s)$ with $\mu(B)=X$ and $\mu(C)=Y$. Consequently $\mu(N_0)=M$: $M$ lies on the incircle and $\overrightarrow{IM}$ is $\overrightarrow{IN_0}$ rotated through $\theta$.

The point $Z=BX\cap CY$. (i) $\tau$ maps line $BX$ to line $YC$ and vice versa, so it fixes their intersection: $Z\in s$. (ii) The rotation $\nu$ about $I$ with $\nu(B)=C$ commutes with $\mu$, so $\nu(X)=\nu\mu(B)=\mu\nu(B)=\mu(C)=Y$; thus $\nu$ maps line $BX$ to line $CY$, and the angle between a line and its image under a rotation is the rotation angle: $\measuredangle BZC=\measuredangle BIC$. Hence $Z\in\kappa$. So $Z$ is the second intersection of $s$ with $\kappa$ (if $s$ is tangent to $\kappa$, then $Z=I$ and below $IZ$ means the tangent $s$). By the inscribed angle theorem in $\kappa$ (directed form: central angle equals twice the inscribed angle), \[\measuredangle(\overrightarrow{NJ},\overrightarrow{NZ})=2\measuredangle(IJ,IZ)=2\measuredangle(AI,s)=\theta .\] So $\overrightarrow{NZ}$ is $\overrightarrow{NJ}$ rotated through $\theta$.

Conclusion. The homothety $h$ multiplies vectors by $NB/r$ and keeps directions, so \[h(M)=N+\tfrac{NB}{r}\,\overrightarrow{IM}=N+\big(\tfrac{NB}{r}\overrightarrow{IN_0}\big)\text{ rotated through }\theta=N+\overrightarrow{NJ}\text{ rotated through }\theta=Z.\] Hence $A$, $M$ and $Z=h(M)$ are collinear: the line $AM$ passes through the common point of $BX$ and $CY$.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

Let $n\ge 2$ be a natural number. Suppose $P_1(x),P_2(x),\dots,P_{2026}(x)$ are pairwise distinct polynomials with real coefficients, all of degree $n$. We say that two polynomials $P_i$ and $P_j$ are friends if and only if the polynomial $Q(x)=P_i(x)+P_j(x)$ has at least one real root. Determine all possible values of the number of unordered pairs $(i,j)$ with $i\ne j$ such that $P_i$ and $P_j$ are friends.

Show the answer

If $n$ is even: every integer from $0$ to $\binom{2026}{2}=2051325$. If $n$ is odd: every integer from $\binom{2026}{2}-1013^2=1013\cdot1012=1025156$ to $2051325$

Show the solution – Threshold constructions and a bound for odd degree

Let $N=2026$ and $T=\binom N2=2051325$. The number $F$ of friendly pairs always satisfies $0\le F\le T$.

Even $n$: every value $0\le F\le T$ occurs. Let $0\le m\le T$. Every such $m$ is a sum of distinct elements of $\{1,2,\dots,N-1\}$ (take the largest element not exceeding what remains, repeatedly; since $1+2+\dots+(N-1)=T$ this always ends at $0$). Fix such a set $S$ with sum $m$, and for $k=0,1,\dots,N-1$ let \[c_k=-3^k\ \text{ if }k\in S,\qquad c_k=3^k\ \text{ if }k\notin S,\qquad P_k(x)=x^n+c_k .\] These are distinct polynomials of degree $n$. Since $n$ is even, $P_j+P_k=2x^n+c_j+c_k$ has a real root iff $c_j+c_k\le0$. For $j<k$, $|c_k|=3^k>3^j=|c_j|$, so $c_j+c_k$ has the sign of $c_k$: the pair $\{j,k\}$ is friendly iff $k\in S$. Hence $F=\sum_{k\in S}k=m$.

Odd $n$: $F\ge T-1013^2$. Let $a_i\ne0$ be the leading coefficient of $P_i$. If $a_i+a_j\ne0$, then $P_i+P_j$ has odd degree $n$ and therefore a real root. So a non-friendly pair must have $a_j=-a_i$. For $t>0$ let $p_t$ and $q_t$ be the numbers of polynomials with leading coefficient $t$ and $-t$. The number of non-friendly pairs is at most \[\sum_{t>0}p_tq_t\le\sum_{t>0}\frac{(p_t+q_t)^2}{4}\le\frac14\Big(\sum_{t>0}(p_t+q_t)\Big)^2\le\frac{N^2}{4}=1013^2 .\] So $F\ge T-1013^2=1013\cdot2025-1013^2=1013\cdot1012$.

Odd $n$: every value $T-1013^2\le F\le T$ occurs. Let $0\le m\le1013^2$ and write $m=d_1+\dots+d_{1013}$ with integers $0\le d_j\le1013$. Put, for $i,j=1,\dots,1013$, \[P_i(x)=x^n+x^{n-1}+i,\qquad R_j(x)=-x^n+x^{n-1}+r_j,\] where $r_j=-(1013-d_j)-\tfrac12+\tfrac{j}{4\cdot1013}$; these are $2026$ distinct polynomials of degree $n$ (the $r_j$ are distinct and not integers). Two $P$'s, or two $R$'s, have a sum with leading coefficient $\pm2$ and odd degree, so they are friends. $P_i+R_j=2x^{n-1}+i+r_j$, and $n-1$ is even, so this has a real root iff $i+r_j\le0$. For fixed $j$, the inequality $i+r_j>0$ holds exactly for \[i>1013-d_j+\tfrac12-\tfrac{j}{4\cdot1013},\] i.e. for the $d_j$ largest values $i=1014-d_j,\dots,1013$. So exactly $\sum d_j=m$ pairs are not friends, and $F=T-m$.

Answer. For even $n$, $F$ can be any integer with $\boxed{0\le F\le 2051325}$; for odd $n$, any integer with $\boxed{1025156\le F\le2051325}$.

Day 2

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Strategy Stealing and Symmetry

Let $n$ be a fixed even natural number. Two players, Alborz and Zagros, play a game on an $n\times n$ grid, with Alborz moving first. Initially, a token is placed in the bottom-left corner of the board. On each move, the current player must move the token to a new square. Alborz may move the token only to a diagonally adjacent square, while Zagros may move it only to an edge-adjacent square. The token may never visit the same square twice. The player who cannot make a move loses. Determine which player has a winning strategy.

Show the answer

Alborz (the first player) wins

Show the solution – Pairing the squares diagonally

Since $n$ is even, the board splits into $(n/2)^2$ blocks of $2\times2$ squares. In each block pair the two squares on each diagonal; this partitions the board into pairs of diagonally adjacent squares (see the figure). The starting corner is paired with the square diagonally next to it in the corner block.

Strategy for Alborz. On his first move Alborz moves to the partner of the starting square. Afterwards, whenever Zagros moves the token to a square $u$, Alborz moves it to the partner $u'$ of $u$.

The strategy is always legal. We claim that after each move of Alborz every pair is either completely visited or completely unvisited. This is true after the first move. If it holds and Zagros moves to an unvisited square $u$, then the pair of $u$ was completely unvisited, so its partner $u'$ is unvisited, and it is diagonally adjacent to $u$; so Alborz can move to $u'$, and the claim holds again.

So Alborz always has a legal move. The game ends after finitely many moves (no square is visited twice), so the player who gets stuck is Zagros. Hence Alborz has a winning strategy.

Problem 2

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Function Equations on Integers

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ and all non-constant polynomials $P$ with integer coefficients such that $P(n)>0$ for all natural numbers $n$, and $f(n)=P(n)$ holds for infinitely many natural numbers $n$. Moreover, for all natural numbers $n$ and $m$, we have \[f(n)+P(m)\mid f(n^2)+P(n)f(m).\]

Show the answer

$P(x)=x^k$ and $f(n)=n^k$ for a positive integer $k$

Show the solution – Let the other variable run through the infinite set

Let $S$ be the infinite set of $n$ with $f(n)=P(n)$. Since $P$ is non-constant and positive on $\mathbb N$, $P(m)\to\infty$ as $m\to\infty$.

Step 1: $f(n^2)=P(n)f(n)$ for all $n$. Fix $n$ and take $m\in S$. Modulo $f(n)+P(m)$ we have $f(m)=P(m)\equiv-f(n)$, so \[f(n^2)+P(n)f(m)\equiv f(n^2)-P(n)f(n)\pmod{f(n)+P(m)},\] and the condition gives $f(n)+P(m)\mid f(n^2)-P(n)f(n)$. The left side is unbounded as $m$ runs through $S$, while the right side is a fixed integer; hence $f(n^2)=P(n)f(n)$.

Step 2: $f=P$. Substituting Step 1, the condition becomes $f(n)+P(m)\mid P(n)\big(f(n)+f(m)\big)$. Fix $m$ and take $n\in S$: then $P(n)+P(m)\mid P(n)\big(P(n)+f(m)\big)$. Modulo $P(n)+P(m)$ we have $P(n)\equiv-P(m)$, so \[P(n)+P(m)\ \big|\ \big(-P(m)\big)\big(f(m)-P(m)\big).\] The right side does not depend on $n$, and the left side is unbounded as $n$ runs through $S$; so $P(m)\big(f(m)-P(m)\big)=0$, and since $P(m)>0$, $f(m)=P(m)$. This holds for every $m$.

Step 3: $P(x)=x^k$. By Steps 1 and 2, $P(n^2)=P(n)^2$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$, hence $P(x^2)=P(x)^2$ as polynomials. Write $P(x)=ax^k+bx^j+(\text{lower terms})$ with $a\ne0$, where $bx^j$ ($j<k$, $b\ne0$) is the next non-zero term, if there is one. In $P(x)^2$ the coefficient of $x^{k+j}$ is $2ab\ne0$. In $P(x^2)$ only even powers $x^{2i}$ with $a_i\ne0$ occur, and $x^{k+j}$ would need $i=\frac{k+j}2$ strictly between $j$ and $k$, where all coefficients vanish. This contradiction shows $P(x)=ax^k$, and then $a^2=a$ gives $a=1$; $k\ge1$ as $P$ is non-constant.

Check. For $f(n)=n^k$, $P(x)=x^k$: $f(n^2)+P(n)f(m)=n^{2k}+n^km^k=n^k(n^k+m^k)$ is divisible by $f(n)+P(m)=n^k+m^k$, $P$ is positive on $\mathbb N$, and $f=P$ everywhere.

So the solutions are $\boxed{P(x)=x^k,\ f(n)=n^k\ (k\ge1)}$.

Problem 3

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle, and let $O$ be its circumcenter. Let $D$ be the intersection of the altitude from $A$ to $BC$ with the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. The extensions of $DM$ and $AO$ meet the circumcircle again at points $X$ and $A'$, respectively. Let $E$ be the intersection point of $AO$ and $BC$. Let the line $MO$ intersect $AB$ and $AC$ at $C_0$ and $B_0$, respectively, and let $T$ be the midpoint of $B_0C_0$. Denote by $\omega$ the circumcircle of triangle $A'EX$. Let $P$ be the second intersection point of $MA'$ with $\omega$, and let $Q$ be the second intersection point of $TP$ with $\omega$. Prove that $EQ$ is the angle bisector of $\angle AQX$.

Show solution 1 – Identify $Q$ on the circle $AOX$ and on the circle with diameter $OE$

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle and $H$ the orthocenter; $\measuredangle(m,n)$ denotes the directed angle between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. We use the standard facts: $D$ is the reflection of $H$ in $BC$; $A'$ is the antipode of $A$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $HA'$ (as $BHCA'$ is a parallelogram), so $H,M,A'$ are collinear; $DA'\parallel BC$ (since $\angle ADA'=90^\circ$ and $AD\perp BC$). Let $K$ be the second intersection of line $A'M$ with $\Gamma$; as $AA'$ is a diameter, $AK\perp A'K$, i.e. $AK\perp HM$. Note $P$ lies on line $A'K$.

Step 1: $AT\perp HM$, so $A,T,K$ are collinear. The triangles $AB_0C_0$ and $HBC$ have pairwise perpendicular sides: $AB_0=AC\perp BH$, $AC_0=AB\perp CH$, $B_0C_0=OM\perp BC$. Rotate triangle $AB_0C_0$ through $90^\circ$: its sides become parallel to $HB$, $HC$, $BC$, so the rotated triangle and triangle $HBC$ have corresponding sides parallel, with $A\leftrightarrow H$, $B_0\leftrightarrow B$, $C_0\leftrightarrow C$. Two such triangles are related by a homothety or a translation, which maps the midpoint of the rotated $B_0C_0$ to the midpoint $M$ of $BC$ and preserves directions. Hence the rotated median $AT$ is parallel to $HM$, i.e. $AT\perp HM$.

Step 2: $A,O,X,T$ are concyclic. As $TO\perp BC$ and $TA\perp HM$, $\measuredangle(TO,TA)=\measuredangle(BC,HM)$. On the other side, $OA=OX$ gives $\measuredangle(XO,XA)=\measuredangle(AX,AO)$, and \[\measuredangle(AX,AO)=\measuredangle(AX,AA')=\measuredangle(DX,DA')=\measuredangle(DM,BC)=\measuredangle(BC,HM),\] using $\Gamma$, then $DA'\parallel BC$, and finally the reflection in $BC$, which maps $D\mapsto H$, $M\mapsto M$ and reverses directed angles. So $\measuredangle(TO,TA)=\measuredangle(XO,XA)$. Call this circle $\Omega$.

Step 3: $Q$ lies on $\Omega$. The points $T,P,Q$ are collinear by the definition of $Q$. Using this, then $\omega$ (chord $XP$), the fact that $P\in A'K$, $\Gamma$ (chord $XK$), and Step 1: \[\measuredangle(QX,QT)=\measuredangle(QX,QP)=\measuredangle(A'X,A'P)=\measuredangle(A'X,A'K)=\measuredangle(AX,AK)=\measuredangle(AX,AT).\] Hence $Q$ lies on the circle through $A,X,T$, which is $\Omega$ by Step 2.

Step 4: $\angle OQE=90^\circ$. Using $\Omega$ (chord $OX$) and $\omega$ (chord $XE$, with $A'E$ the line $AA'$): \[\begin{aligned}\measuredangle(QO,QE)&=\measuredangle(QO,QX)+\measuredangle(QX,QE)=\measuredangle(AO,AX)+\measuredangle(A'X,A'E)\\&=\measuredangle(A'X,AA')+\measuredangle(AA',AX)=\measuredangle(A'X,AX)=90^\circ .\end{aligned}\] So $Q$ lies on the circle $\gamma$ with diameter $OE$, which also passes through $M$ ($\angle OME=90^\circ$).

Step 5: $QE$ is the internal bisector. Since $OA=OX$, $O$ is the midpoint of one of the arcs $AX$ of $\Omega$, so $QO$ bisects one of the two angles formed by $QA$ and $QX$; by Step 4, $QE\perp QO$ is the other bisector. It remains to see that $QO$ is the external one. First, $A,M,X,E$ are concyclic: $\measuredangle(XA,XM)=\measuredangle(XA,XD)=\measuredangle(A'A,A'D)=\measuredangle(AA',BC)=\measuredangle(EA,EM)$. Line $AX$ is not parallel to $BC$: otherwise $X$ would be the reflection $A^*$ of $A$ in $OM$; as $\angle A^*AD=90^\circ$, $A^*D$ is a diameter, so line $DX$ would contain $O$ and $M$, hence equal $OM\parallel AD$; sharing $D$, the lines $DX$ and $AD$ would coincide and $M\in AD$, i.e. $AB=AC$, which is excluded. So let $S=AX\cap BC$. Then $S$ has power $SA\cdot SX$ with respect to $\Omega$ and, via the circle $AMXE$, the same power $SA\cdot SX=SM\cdot SE$ with respect to $\gamma$. So $S$ lies on the radical axis $OQ$ of $\Omega$ and $\gamma$. Finally $D$ lies on the arc $BC$ opposite to $A$ and the chord $DX$ crosses $BC$ at $M$, so $X$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$; hence $S$ lies outside the segment $AX$. A bisector meeting line $AX$ outside the segment $AX$ is the external bisector of $\angle AQX$. Therefore $QO$ is the external bisector, and $EQ$ is the internal bisector of $\angle AQX$.

Show solution 2 – Cartesian coordinates and a common rotation

Setting up. Take $M$ as the origin, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and $A=(u,v)$ with $v>0$. The triangle is acute, so $|u|<1$, and it is scalene, so $u\ne0$. Put \[g=1-u^2,\qquad h=\frac gv,\qquad r=u^2+h^2 .\] The orthocenter is $H=(u,h)$ (it lies on the altitude $x=u$, and $\overrightarrow{BH}\cdot\overrightarrow{CA}=(u+1)(u-1)+hv=0$). As the triangle is acute, $H$ lies inside it, so $0<h<v$; equivalently $h^2<hv=g$, that is, $r<1$.

The points on $\Gamma$. A direct check (using $v=g/h$) shows that $\Gamma$ is \[F_0(x,y)=x^2+y^2-2oy-1=0,\qquad o=\frac{1-r}{2h}>0,\] since $F_0(B)=F_0(C)=0$ and $F_0(A)=u^2+v^2-\frac{(1-r)v}{h}-1=0$. The second point of the altitude $x=u$ on $\Gamma$ is $D=(u,-h)$, and the antipode of $A$ is $A'=2O-A=(-u,2o-v)=(-u,-h)$. The power of $M$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $F_0(M)=-1$ and $MD^2=r$, so the second point of line $DM$ on $\Gamma$ is \[X=-\frac{D}{r}=\Bigl(-\frac ur,\ \frac hr\Bigr).\] The points $E$ and $T$. The line $AO$ is the line $AA'$; it meets $y=0$ at \[E=(-uc,\,0),\qquad c=\frac{v-h}{v+h}\in(0,1),\] because the point of segment $AA'$ at height $0$ divides it in the ratio $v:h$. (Equivalently $c=\frac{1-r}{g+h^2}$.) The line $MO$ is the $y$-axis; it meets $AB$ and $AC$ at heights $\frac{v}{1+u}$ and $\frac v{1-u}$, so their midpoint is $T=\bigl(0,\frac v{1-u^2}\bigr)=\bigl(0,\frac1h\bigr)$.

The circle $\omega$. Let $s=g+h^2$ and \[F(x,y)=x^2+y^2+\frac{g(r+1)}{us}\,x-hc\,y+c .\] The line $A'X$ is $L(x,y)=(r+1)hx-(r-1)uy+2uh=0$ (substitute $A'$ and $X$), and a direct expansion shows $F=F_0+\frac{g}{hus}L$. So $F$ vanishes at $A'$ and $X$, which lie on both $\Gamma$ and $A'X$; also $F(E)=u^2c^2-\frac{g(r+1)}{s}c+c=0$, by the same expansion. Hence $F=0$ is the circle $\omega$ through $A',E,X$.

The points $P$ and $Q$. The points of line $MA'$ are $tA'=(-ut,-ht)$, and $F(tA')$ is a quadratic in $t$ with leading coefficient $r$ and constant term $c$. One root is $t=1$ (the point $A'$), so the other is $t=\frac cr$: \[P=\frac cr\,A'=\Bigl(-\frac{uc}r,\,-\frac{hc}r\Bigr).\] The power of $T$ with respect to $\omega$ is $F(T)=\frac1{h^2}$, so the second intersection of line $TP$ with $\omega$ is $Q=T+\frac{F(T)}{|P-T|^2}(P-T)$. Simplifying, \[Q=\Bigl(-\frac{uc}{W},\ \frac{hc(1+c)}{W}\Bigr),\qquad W=u^2+h^2(1+c)^2>0 .\] The directions at $Q$. Identify vectors with complex numbers and put \[p=\frac{uhc^2}{1-c},\qquad \zeta=1+ip\ne0 .\] A direct expansion, using $v=\frac{h(1+c)}{1-c}$ (which is the definition of $c$), gives \[\overrightarrow{QX}=\frac{1-c}{rW}\,(-u+ih)\,\zeta,\qquad \overrightarrow{QA}=\frac{1+c}{W}\,(u+ih)\,\zeta,\qquad \overrightarrow{QE}=\frac{ch(1+c)}{W}\,(-i)\,\zeta .\] All the real factors in front are positive, because $0<c<1$, $h>0$, $r>0$ and $W>0$. Multiplying by $\zeta^{-1}$ (a rotation followed by a scaling, which preserves angles), the rays $QA$, $QX$, $QE$ point in the directions of $u+ih$, $-u+ih$ and $-i$. The first two are mirror images of each other in the imaginary axis, so the internal bisector of the angle between them points along $(u+ih)+(-u+ih)=2ih$, that is, along $i$; the ray $QE$ points along $-i$, the opposite direction. Hence the line $EQ$ is the internal bisector of $\angle AQX$ (with $E$ on its extension beyond $Q$).
