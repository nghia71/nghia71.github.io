Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Determine all positive integers $n$ with the following property: the set $\{1,2,\ldots,2n-1,2n\}$ can be partitioned into two disjoint sets $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ with $n$ elements each, such that the sum of the elements of $\mathcal{A}$ divides the sum of the elements of $\mathcal{B}$.

Show the answer

all $n$ with $n\not\equiv 5\pmod 6$ (that is, $n$ even or $n\not\equiv2\pmod3$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The quotient is $1$ or $2$, and all sums occur)

Let $S=1+2+\dots+2n=n(2n+1)$, and let $a,b$ be the sums of $\mathcal A,\mathcal B$, so $a+b=S$.

Necessity. If $a\mid b$, write $b=ka$ with $k\ge1$; then $S=(k+1)a$. Since $\mathcal A$ has $n$ distinct elements, $a\ge1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, hence \[k+1=\frac Sa\le\frac{2n(2n+1)}{n(n+1)}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}<4 ,\] so $k+1\in\{2,3\}$. If $k+1=2$, then $S=2a$ is even, so $n$ is even ($2n+1$ is odd). If $k+1=3$, then $3\mid n(2n+1)$; for $n\equiv2\pmod3$ both factors are $\equiv2\pmod 3$, so $n\equiv0$ or $1\pmod3$. Thus $n$ is even or $n\not\equiv2\pmod3$, i.e. $n\not\equiv5\pmod6$.

Lemma. Every integer from $\frac{n(n+1)}2$ to $\frac{n(3n+1)}2$ is the sum of some $n$-element subset of $\{1,\dots,2n\}$. Start with $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (sum $\frac{n(n+1)}2$). As long as the current set $X$ is not $\{n+1,\dots,2n\}$, it contains an element $x<2n$ with $x+1\notin X$ (otherwise $X$ would be a block of consecutive numbers ending at $2n$); replacing $x$ by $x+1$ raises the sum by exactly $1$. We stop at $\{n+1,\dots,2n\}$, whose sum is $\frac{n(3n+1)}2$, so every value in between is reached.

Sufficiency. If $n$ is even, take $a=\frac S2$; if $n\equiv0,1\pmod 3$, take $a=\frac S3$ (an integer, as $3\mid n(2n+1)$). In both cases $\frac{n(n+1)}2\le a\le\frac S2\le\frac{n(3n+1)}2$ (for $a=\frac S3$ the first inequality is $2(2n+1)\ge3(n+1)$, true for $n\ge1$). By the lemma some $n$-element $\mathcal A$ has sum $a$; its complement $\mathcal B$ has $n$ elements and sum $S-a=a$ or $2a$, a multiple of $a$.

So the answer is: all positive integers $n$ with $\boxed{n\not\equiv 5\pmod 6}$.

Solution 2 (Bounding $\mathcal B$ and explicit partitions)

Let $S=n(2n+1)$ and let $a,b$ be the sums of $\mathcal A,\mathcal B$.

Necessity. Suppose $b=ka$ with $k$ a positive integer. The set $\mathcal A$ has sum at least $1+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, and $\mathcal B$ has sum at most $(n+1)+\dots+2n=\frac{n(3n+1)}2$. Hence \[k=\frac ba\le\frac{3n+1}{n+1}<3,\] so $k\in\{1,2\}$. For $k=1$: $S=2a$, so $n$ is even. For $k=2$: $S=3a$, so $3\mid n(2n+1)$, which fails for $n\equiv2\pmod3$. Hence $n\not\equiv5\pmod6$.

Constructions.

  • $n$ even: $\mathcal A=\{i,\ 2n+1-i:\ 1\le i\le\frac n2\}$ consists of $\frac n2$ pairs with sum $2n+1$, so $a=\frac S2=b$.

  • $n\equiv0$ or $1\pmod3$: we want $a=\frac S3$. The excess over $1+\dots+n$ is $E=\frac{n(2n+1)}3-\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n(n-1)}6$, an integer. Write $E=un+r$ with $0\le r<n$ (then $u\le\frac{n-1}6<n$) and put \[\mathcal A=\{1,2,\dots,n-u-1\}\cup\{n-u+r\}\cup\{2n-u+1,\dots,2n\}.\] Compared with $\{1,\dots,n\}$, the $u$ largest elements were raised by $n$ each and the element $n-u$ by $r$, so $a=\frac{n(n+1)}2+un+r=\frac S3$. The three blocks are disjoint because $n-u-1<n-u+r\le 2n-u-1<2n-u+1$, so $|\mathcal A|=n$. Then $b=S-a=2a$.

(For instance $n=4$: $\mathcal A=\{1,2,7,8\}$, $a=b=18$; $n=6$: $E=5$, $u=0$, $r=5$, $\mathcal A=\{1,2,3,4,5,11\}$, $a=26$, $b=52$.)

Hence the property holds exactly for $\boxed{n\not\equiv5\pmod 6}$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Given a triangle $ABC$, let $K$ and $L$ be distinct points on side $AC$ such that $\angle ABK=\angle CBL$. Rays $BK$ and $BL$ are not orthogonal to $AC$, and intersect the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$ for the second time at points $K_1$ and $L_1$, respectively. Points $K_2$ and $L_2$ lie on the tangents to the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$ at points $K_1$ and $L_1$, respectively, such that $\angle BKK_2=\angle BLL_2=90^\circ$.

Prove that the points $A,C,K_2$, and $L_2$ lie on a circle.

Show the solution

Solution (An auxiliary point on both perpendiculars)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$, and signed products of collinear segments. If $K=A$ then $\angle ABK=0$, so $L=C$, $K_1=K_2=A$ and $L_1=L_2=C$, and there is nothing to prove; so $K,L$ are interior points of the side $AC$, and $K\ne L$.

Let $\ell_K$ and $\ell_L$ be the lines through $K$ and $L$ perpendicular to $BK$ and $BL$; $K_2\in\ell_K$, $L_2\in\ell_L$. As $BK\ne BL$, these lines meet at a point $V$. $V\ne K$: otherwise $K\in\ell_L$, i.e. $LK\perp BL$, i.e. $BL\perp AC$, which is excluded; similarly $V\ne L$. Also $V\notin AC$, since $\ell_K$ meets $AC$ only at $K$. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$.

Step 1. Since $\measuredangle(KB,KV)=90^\circ=\measuredangle(LB,LV)$, the points $B,K,V,L$ are concyclic, so \[\measuredangle(VB,VK)=\measuredangle(LB,LK)=\measuredangle(BL,AC). \tag{1}\]

Step 2. By the tangent–chord angle, $\measuredangle(K_1K_2,K_1B)=\measuredangle(CK_1,CB)$, i.e. $\measuredangle(K_1B,K_1K_2)=\measuredangle(CB,CK_1)$. By inscribed angles in $\omega$, $\measuredangle(CA,CK_1)=\measuredangle(BA,BK_1)=\measuredangle(BA,BK)$, and since $BK,BL$ are symmetric in the bisector of angle $B$ (both inside the angle), $\measuredangle(BA,BK)=\measuredangle(BL,BC)$. Therefore \[\measuredangle(K_1B,K_1K_2)=\measuredangle(CB,CA)+\measuredangle(CA,CK_1)=\measuredangle(BC,CA)+\measuredangle(BL,BC)=\measuredangle(BL,AC). \tag{2}\]

Step 3: $KB\cdot KK_1=KV\cdot KK_2$. The points $B,K_1$ lie on line $BK$ and $V$ lies on $\ell_K\perp BK$ with $V\ne K$, so $B,K_1,V$ are not collinear; let their circle meet $\ell_K$ again at $W$. Then $\measuredangle(K_1B,K_1W)=\measuredangle(VB,VW)=\measuredangle(VB,VK)$, which by (1) and (2) equals $\measuredangle(K_1B,K_1K_2)$. So the lines $K_1W$ and $K_1K_2$ coincide, and as both $W$ and $K_2$ lie on $\ell_K$ (which does not pass through $K_1$), $W=K_2$. The power of $K$ with respect to this circle gives $KB\cdot KK_1=KV\cdot KW=KV\cdot KK_2$ (signed). (Here $W\ne V$: the product $KB\cdot KK_1$ is negative because $K$ lies inside $\omega$, between $B$ and $K_1$, while $W=V$ would give $KV^2>0$.)

Step 4. The power of $K$ with respect to $\omega$ gives $KA\cdot KC=KB\cdot KK_1$. Hence $KA\cdot KC=KV\cdot KK_2$, a negative number (as $K$ is between $A$ and $C$). By the converse of the power of a point (the circle through $A,C,V$ meets $\ell_K$ a second time at the point $X$ with $KV\cdot KX=KA\cdot KC$), the point $K_2$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ through $A,C,V$.

Step 5. The hypothesis is symmetric under exchanging $A\leftrightarrow C$ and $K\leftrightarrow L$, and $V$ is defined symmetrically. Repeating Steps 2–4 for $L$ shows that $L_2$ also lies on the circle through $A,C,V$, which is $\Gamma$. Hence $A,C,K_2,L_2$ lie on one circle.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n\geq 2$ be an integer. Euroland has $n$ cities, with direct flights connecting every pair of cities in both directions. For each pair of cities, the emperor assigns a positive price, which is the same in each direction. For two distinct cities $A$ and $B$, let $D(A,B)$ be the number of flights in the cheapest journey between them; if there are multiple such journeys, then $D(A,B)$ is defined by the longest one.

For each value of $n$, find the largest average value of $D(A,B)$ over all pairs of distinct cities $(A,B)$, that the emperor can achieve.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{n+1}{3}$

Show the solution

Solution (Count triples with a city in the middle)

For cities $X\ne Y$ let $d(X,Y)$ be the price of the cheapest journey from $X$ to $Y$, and for each pair fix the journey that defines $D(X,Y)$ (cheapest, and with the largest number of flights among cheapest ones). A cheapest journey never visits a city twice (cutting out the loop would make it strictly cheaper, as prices are positive), so it has $D(X,Y)-1$ intermediate cities. Call $Z$ inner for the pair $\{X,Y\}$ if $Z$ is an intermediate city of the fixed journey between $X$ and $Y$.

Fact. If $Z$ is inner for $\{X,Y\}$, then $d(X,Y)=d(X,Z)+d(Z,Y)$. Indeed, the journey splits at $Z$ into a journey $X\to Z$ and a journey $Z\to Y$ costing at least $d(X,Z)$ and $d(Z,Y)$, so $d(X,Y)\ge d(X,Z)+d(Z,Y)$; and joining cheapest journeys $X\to Z\to Y$ shows $d(X,Y)\le d(X,Z)+d(Z,Y)$.

Claim. Among three cities $X,Y,Z$, at most one is inner for the pair formed by the other two. If $Z$ were inner for $\{X,Y\}$ and $Y$ inner for $\{X,Z\}$, then $d(X,Y)=d(X,Z)+d(Z,Y)$ and $d(X,Z)=d(X,Y)+d(Y,Z)$; adding gives $2d(Y,Z)=0$, impossible.

Upper bound. Counting pairs (pair of cities, inner city) by the triple they form, \[\sum_{\{X,Y\}}\bigl(D(X,Y)-1\bigr)\le\binom n3,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \sum_{\{X,Y\}}D(X,Y)\le\binom n2+\binom n3=\binom{n+1}3 .\] The average over the $\binom n2$ pairs is at most $\binom{n+1}{3}\big/\binom n2=\frac{n+1}3$.

Construction. Number the cities $c_1,\dots,c_n$; let the price be $1$ for $\{c_i,c_{i+1}\}$ and $n$ for all other pairs. For $i<j$, the journey $c_i,c_{i+1},\dots,c_j$ costs $j-i\le n-1$, while any journey using a price-$n$ flight costs at least $n$. A journey using only price-$1$ flights moves one step along the chain each time, so it costs at least $j-i$, with equality only for the direct walk. So the cheapest journey is unique and $D(c_i,c_j)=j-i$. Then \[\sum_{i<j}(j-i)=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}t(n-t)=\binom{n+1}3,\] and the average is $\frac{n+1}3$. Hence the largest achievable average is $\boxed{\dfrac{n+1}{3}}$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Let $\mathbb{N}$ be the set of positive integers. Find all functions $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ that simultaneously satisfy the following properties:

  • (i) $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ for all positive integers $m$ and $n$;

  • (ii) There exists a positive integer $c$ such that $f(n)\leq n^c$ for all positive integers $n$;

  • (iii) The numbers $f(n)+m$ and $f(m)+n+1$ are coprime for all positive integers $m$ and $n$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=n$ for all $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Primes in progressions kill every exponent but one)

The identity works. For $f(n)=n$, (i) holds, (ii) holds with $c=1$, and $n+m$ and $m+n+1$ are consecutive integers, hence coprime.

Conversely, let $f$ satisfy (i)–(iii). From (i), $f(1)=f(1)^2$, so $f(1)=1$. Condition (iii) with $m=1$, $n=2$ says that $f(2)+1$ and $f(1)+3=4$ are coprime, so $f(2)+1$ is odd and $f(2)$ is even. We use one theorem without proof:

Dirichlet's theorem. If $Q\ge1$ and $b$ are coprime integers, then there are infinitely many primes $r$ with $r\equiv b\pmod Q$.

Step 1: $f(p)=p^{a_p}$ with $0\le a_p\le c$ for every prime $p$. Suppose a prime $r\ne p$ divides $f(p)$. As $p$ is invertible modulo $r$, there is $t\in\{1,\dots,r\}$ with $pt\equiv-1-f(r)\pmod r$. Take $n=pt$ and $m=r$. Then $f(n)+m=f(p)f(t)+r$ and $f(m)+n+1=f(r)+pt+1$ are both divisible by $r$, contradicting (iii). So $f(p)$ has no prime factor other than $p$, i.e. $f(p)=p^{a_p}$ with $a_p\ge0$, and (ii) gives $p^{a_p}\le p^c$, so $a_p\le c$.

Step 2: a key observation. Let $s\ge1$ and $l\ge0$ be integers, and let $q$ be a prime with $q\nmid f(s)$ and $q\mid(-f(s))^l+s+1$. Then no prime $r\equiv-f(s)\pmod q$ has $a_r=l$. Indeed, suppose $r$ is such a prime with $f(r)=r^l$, and take $n=s$, $m=r$. Then modulo $q$, \[f(n)+m=f(s)+r\equiv0,\qquad f(m)+n+1=r^l+s+1\equiv(-f(s))^l+s+1\equiv0,\] so $q$ divides both numbers, contradicting (iii). (The condition $q\nmid f(s)$ makes $-f(s)$ invertible modulo $q$, so Dirichlet's theorem applies to this residue class.)

Step 3: $a_p\ge1$ for every prime $p$. Suppose $f(p)=1$ for some prime $p$. Then $p\ne2$, because $f(2)$ is even. Let $q$ be a prime factor of $p+2$; $q\ne p$, since $p\mid p+2$ would force $p\mid2$. Apply Step 2 with $s=p$, $f(s)=1$ (so $p\nmid f(s)$ and $q\nmid f(s)$):

  • for odd $l$, $(-1)^l+p+1=p$ is divisible by $p$, so no prime $r\equiv-1\pmod p$ has odd $a_r$;

  • for even $l$, $(-1)^l+p+1=p+2$ is divisible by $q$, so no prime $r\equiv-1\pmod q$ has even $a_r$.

By Dirichlet's theorem (with $Q=pq$, $b=-1$) there is a prime $r\equiv-1\pmod{pq}$. Its exponent $a_r$ can be neither odd nor even, a contradiction.

Step 4: $a_p=1$ for every prime $p$. Suppose $a_p=k\ge2$ for some prime $p$ (then $c\ge k\ge2$). We choose distinct primes $q_1,\dots,q_c$, all different from $p$, one after another. Let $1\le l\le c$ and suppose $q_1,\dots,q_{l-1}$ are already chosen. Put \[j_l=6\prod_{i<l}(q_i-1),\qquad s_l=p^{j_l},\qquad P_l=(-1)^l s_l^{\,kl}+s_l+1 .\]

  • For $i<l$ we have $q_i\ne p$ and $(q_i-1)\mid j_l$, so Fermat's little theorem gives $s_l\equiv1\pmod{q_i}$ and $P_l\equiv(-1)^l+2\pmod{q_i}$, which is $1$ or $3$. So $q_i\nmid P_l$, except possibly when $q_i=3$ and $l$ is even. In that case $p\ne3$, so $p^6\equiv1\pmod 9$ (as $\varphi(9)=6$), and $6\mid j_l$ gives $s_l\equiv1\pmod9$ and $P_l\equiv1+1+1=3\pmod 9$: then $3\mid P_l$ but $9\nmid P_l$.

  • $p\mid s_l$, so $P_l\equiv1\pmod p$ and $p\nmid P_l$.

  • Since $kl\ge2$ and $s_l\ge2^6$, we have $|P_l|\ge s_l^{\,kl}-s_l-1\ge s_l^2-s_l-1>3$.

So the primes $p,q_1,\dots,q_{l-1}$ contribute at most a factor $3$ to $|P_l|>3$, and $P_l$ has a prime factor $q_l\notin\{p,q_1,\dots,q_{l-1}\}$. This defines $q_l$.

Now $f(s_l)=f(p)^{j_l}=p^{kj_l}=s_l^{\,k}$, so $q_l\nmid f(s_l)$ and $(-f(s_l))^l+s_l+1=P_l$ is divisible by $q_l$. By Step 2 (with $s=s_l$, $q=q_l$): no prime $r\equiv-s_l^{\,k}\pmod{q_l}$ has $a_r=l$. The residues $-s_l^{\,k}$ are non-zero modulo the distinct primes $q_l$, so by the Chinese remainder theorem there is an integer $b$, coprime to $Q=q_1q_2\cdots q_c$, with $b\equiv-s_l^{\,k}\pmod{q_l}$ for $l=1,\dots,c$. By Dirichlet's theorem there is a prime $r\equiv b\pmod Q$. Then $a_r\ne l$ for every $l\in\{1,\dots,c\}$; but $1\le a_r\le c$ by Steps 1 and 3. This contradiction proves Step 4.

Conclusion. By Steps 1–4, $f(p)=p$ for every prime $p$. As $f(1)=1$ and $f$ is completely multiplicative by (i), $f(n)=n$ for every positive integer $n$. So the only function is $\boxed{f(n)=n}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Let $n\geqslant 4$ be a positive integer. Find all positive real numbers $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ such that \[\begin{cases} x_1+x_2=x_2x_3+1\\ x_2+x_3=x_3x_4+1\\ \qquad\vdots\\ x_{n-1}+x_n=x_nx_1+1\\ x_n+x_1=x_1x_2+1. \end{cases}\]

Show the answer

$n$ odd: only $x_1=\dots=x_n=1$. $n$ even: $x_1=x_3=\dots=x_{n-1}=1,\ x_2=x_4=\dots=x_n=t$, or $x_2=x_4=\dots=x_n=1,\ x_1=x_3=\dots=x_{n-1}=t$, with any $t>0$.

Show the solution

Solution (Factor out $x-1$)

Indices are taken modulo $n$. The $i$-th equation $x_i+x_{i+1}=x_{i+1}x_{i+2}+1$ can be rewritten as \[x_i-1=x_{i+1}\,(x_{i+2}-1).\] Put $y_i=x_i-1$; then $y_i=x_{i+1}y_{i+2}$ with $x_{i+1}>0$. Consequently:

  • $y_i=0\iff y_{i+2}=0$, and $y_i,y_{i+2}$ have the same sign;

  • $|y_i|=x_{i+1}|y_{i+2}|$.

Call the indices $i,i+2,i+4,\dots$ a chain. If $n$ is odd there is one chain containing all indices; if $n$ is even there are two chains, the odd and the even indices. Along a chain all $y$ are zero, or all are non-zero with one common sign.

A non-zero chain forces a product. Suppose the $y_j$ on the chain through $i$ are non-zero. Multiplying $|y_j|=x_{j+1}|y_{j+2}|$ over all $j$ of the chain, the $|y|$'s cancel and $\prod x_{j+1}=1$, the product over the indices $j+1$ of the other parity class (for $n$ odd: over all indices). Moreover, all $x_j$ with $j$ in the chain are $>1$ (if the $y_j$ are positive) or all $<1$ (if negative).

$n$ odd. If the chain is non-zero, all $x_j$ are $>1$ or all are $<1$, so their product is not $1$ – contradiction. So all $y_i=0$: $x_1=\dots=x_n=1$, which indeed works ($1+1=1\cdot1+1$).

$n$ even. If both chains were non-zero, the odd chain would give $\prod_{j\text{ even}}x_j=1$ and the even chain $\prod_{j\text{ odd}}x_j=1$; but the odd $x_j$ are all $>1$ or all $<1$, a contradiction. So one chain is zero, say $x_j=1$ for all odd $j$. Then for even $i$, $y_i=x_{i+1}y_{i+2}=y_{i+2}$, so all even $x_j$ are equal to some $t>0$. Conversely, if $x_{\text{odd}}=1$ and $x_{\text{even}}=t$, each equation reads $1+t=t\cdot1+1$ or $t+1=1\cdot t+1$, true. The case with the even indices equal to $1$ is symmetric.

So for odd $n$ the only solution is $\boxed{x_1=\dots=x_n=1}$, and for even $n$ the solutions are $\boxed{(1,t,1,t,\dots,1,t)\ \text{and}\ (t,1,t,1,\dots,t,1),\ t>0}$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Determine all positive integers $n\geq 2$ with the following property: for every positive divisor $d$ of $n$, the product of all the other positive divisors of $n$ is a perfect power.

A perfect power is a number of the form $a^b$ for some integers $a\geq 1$ and $b\geq 2$.

Show the answer

$n=p^k$ with $p$ prime and $k\ge3$

Show the solution

Solution (A Bezout choice of the divisor)

Let $n=p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_s^{e_s}$ and $\tau=\prod(e_i+1)$, the number of divisors. Pairing $d$ with $n/d$ gives $\bigl(\prod_{d\mid n}d\bigr)^2=n^\tau$, so the product of all divisors is $n^{\tau/2}$, and the product of all divisors except $d=\prod p_i^{f_i}$ is \[N_d=\frac{n^{\tau/2}}{d}=\prod_i p_i^{\,u_i-f_i},\qquad u_i=\frac{e_i\tau}{2}\in\mathbb Z .\] Fact. A positive integer $N>1$ is a perfect power if and only if the exponents in its prime factorisation have a common divisor $g\ge2$ (then $N=(N^{1/g})^g$; conversely $N=a^b$ makes every exponent a multiple of $b$); and $1=1^2$ is a perfect power.

Prime powers. For $n=p^k$ the divisors are $p^j$ ($0\le j\le k$) and $N_{p^j}=p^{k(k+1)/2-j}$. A power $p^e$ is a perfect power exactly when $e\ne1$. The exponents $k(k+1)/2-j$ run over the integers from $\frac{k(k-1)}2$ to $\frac{k(k+1)}2$. For $k=1$ they are $0,1$, for $k=2$ they are $1,2,3$: the value $1$ occurs, so $n=p$ and $n=p^2$ fail. For $k\ge3$ the smallest is $\frac{k(k-1)}2\ge3$, so all $N_d$ are perfect powers and $n=p^k$ works.

Two or more prime factors. Take two primes $p_1,p_2\mid n$, let $g=\gcd(e_1,e_2)$, $w_1=e_1/g$, $w_2=e_2/g$ (coprime). Then $w_2u_1=\frac{e_1e_2\tau}{2g}=w_1u_2$. Choose $x\in\{0,\dots,w_1-1\}$ with $w_2x\equiv-1\pmod{w_1}$ (take $x=0$ if $w_1=1$) and put $y=\frac{1+w_2x}{w_1}$, an integer with $1\le y\le\frac{1+w_2(w_1-1)}{w_1}<w_2+1$. So $0\le x\le e_1$, $1\le y\le e_2$ and $w_1y-w_2x=1$. For $d=p_1^{x}p_2^{y}$ the exponents of $p_1,p_2$ in $N_d$ are $E_1=u_1-x$ and $E_2=u_2-y$, and \[w_2E_1-w_1E_2=(w_2u_1-w_1u_2)+(w_1y-w_2x)=1 .\] Hence $\gcd(E_1,E_2)=1$, so $N_d\ne1$ and the exponents of $N_d$ have no common divisor $\ge2$: $N_d$ is not a perfect power, and $n$ fails.

Therefore the numbers with the property are exactly $\boxed{n=p^k,\ p\text{ prime},\ k\ge3}$.

Example. $n=12$: $u_1=6$, $u_2=3$, $w_1=2$, $w_2=1$, $x=y=1$, $d=6$, and $N_6=12^3/6=288=2^5\cdot3^2$ is not a perfect power.

Problem 7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute angle triangle with $AB<AC$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of segment $BC$. Let $E$ and $F$ be points on segments $AC$ and $AB$, respectively, such that the circumcircle of triangle $MEF$ is tangent to $BC$. The circumcircles of triangles $AEF$ and $ABC$ intersect at a point $P\neq A$. Let $Q$ be a point on the circumcircle of triangle $AEF$ such that $AQ$ is perpendicular to $BC$.

Prove that $PQ$ passes through the circumcenter of triangle $MEF$.

Show the solution

Solution (The spiral similarity at $P$)

Directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ are taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega=(ABC)$, $\omega_1=(AEF)$, let $\sigma=(MEF)$ with centre $O'$, and let $M_1$ be the midpoint of $EF$.

Step 0. The circle $\sigma$ passes through $M\in BC$ and is tangent to $BC$, so it touches $BC$ at $M$ and $O'M\perp BC$. Also $O'M_1\perp EF$. The line $EF$ is not parallel to $BC$: otherwise the triangles $AEF$ and $ACB$ would be homothetic from $A$, the circles $\omega_1,\omega$ would be tangent at $A$, and $P$ would not exist. Let $T=EF\cap BC$; $T\ne M$, since $M,E,F$ are not collinear.

Step 1: the spiral similarity. The circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega$ meet at $A$ and $P$, and $A=FB\cap EC$. Hence $\measuredangle(FP,FB)=\measuredangle(FP,FA)= \measuredangle(EP,EA)=\measuredangle(EP,EC)$ and $\measuredangle(BP,BF)=\measuredangle(BP,BA)=\measuredangle(CP,CA)=\measuredangle(CP,CE)$, so the triangles $PFB$ and $PEC$ are directly similar. Therefore the spiral similarity $\mathcal S$ with centre $P$ that maps $F\mapsto B$ also maps $E\mapsto C$. It maps the line $EF$ to the line $BC$ and the midpoint $M_1$ of $EF$ to the midpoint $M$ of $BC$.

Lemma. If a spiral similarity with centre $P$ maps a line $g$ to a line $h$ meeting it at $T$, then for every $X\in g$ the points $P,X,\mathcal S(X),T$ are concyclic. Indeed, $\mathcal S$ maps the lines $XP$ and $g$ to the lines $\mathcal S(X)P$ and $h$ and preserves directed angles, so $\measuredangle(XP,XT)=\measuredangle(XP,g)=\measuredangle(\mathcal S(X)P,h)=\measuredangle(\mathcal S(X)P,\mathcal S(X)T)$.

Applying it with $g=EF$, $h=BC$: $P,F,B,T$ are concyclic, and $P,M_1,M,T$ are concyclic.

Step 2. Since $\angle O'MT=90^\circ$ and $\angle O'M_1T=90^\circ$, the points $M,M_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $O'T$. This circle is therefore the circle through $M,M_1,T$, which contains $P$ by Step 1. So $P,M,O',T$ are concyclic.

Step 3. Using Step 2, then Step 1, then $\omega_1$: \[\measuredangle(O'P,O'M)=\measuredangle(TP,TM)=\measuredangle(TP,TB)=\measuredangle(FP,FB)=\measuredangle(FP,FA)=\measuredangle(QP,QA).\] (If $Q=A$, the line $QA$ means the tangent to $\omega_1$ at $A$, and the last equality is the tangent–chord angle.) The lines $QA$ and $O'M$ are both perpendicular to $BC$, hence parallel, so $\measuredangle(QP,QA)=\measuredangle(QP,O'M)$. Thus $\measuredangle(O'P,O'M)=\measuredangle(QP,O'M)$: the lines $O'P$ and $QP$ are parallel, and since they share $P$, they coincide. Hence $PQ$ passes through $O'$, the circumcentre of $MEF$.

Remark. Tangency was used only to place $O'$ on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. The proof shows more: for any $E\in AC$, $F\in AB$, the line $PQ$ passes through the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of $EF$ and $BC$.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

For a convex polygon $\mathcal{P}$, let $\mathcal{B}$ be the set of points on the boundary of $\mathcal{P}$. A function $f:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}$ is European if it satisfies the following properties:

  • (i) $f(f(X))=X$ for all points $X\in\mathcal{B}$;

  • (ii) Line segments $Yf(Y)$ and $Zf(Z)$ have a common point strictly inside the polygon, for all points $Y,Z\in\mathcal{B}$.

What is the largest real number $c$ such that for any convex polygon $\mathcal{P}$ and European function $f$, there is a point $W\in\mathcal{B}$ such that the length of line segment $Wf(W)$ is at least $c$ times the perimeter of $\mathcal{P}$?

Show the answer

$c=\dfrac1{\pi+2}$

Show the solution

Solution (A half-disc example and a matching argument)

Let $L$ be the perimeter of $\mathcal P$. We show that $c=\frac1{\pi+2}$ works and that no larger $c$ does.

Upper bound: no $c>\frac1{\pi+2}$ works (a polygon close to a half-disc). Fix $0<\theta<\pi$ and a large $N$. Let $\mathcal P$ be the convex polygon with vertices $O$ and $U_0,\dots,U_N$, where the $U_j$ lie on the unit circle around $O$ at the angles $\frac{\pi-\theta}2+\frac{j\theta}N$; it lies in the angle of size $\theta$ at $O$, and $L=2+2N\sin\frac{\theta}{2N}\to2+\theta$ as $N\to\infty$. Let $Q$ be the point at distance $\varepsilon$ from $O$ on the bisector of this angle, and let $f(X)$ be the second endpoint of the chord through $X$ and $Q$. Then $f(f(X))=X$, and any two chords meet at the interior point $Q$, so $f$ is European. On a line through $Q$, one of the two rays from $Q$ makes an angle $\psi\ge90^\circ$ with the ray $OQ$ and leaves the angle at $O$ (hence $\mathcal P$) after a distance $\varepsilon\sin\frac\theta2/\sin(\psi-\frac\theta2)\le\varepsilon\kappa$, where $\kappa=1/\min(\sin\frac\theta2,\cos\frac\theta2)$ (law of sines in the triangle formed by $O$, $Q$ and the exit point); the other ray stays within distance $1+\varepsilon$ of $Q$. So every chord has length at most $1+\varepsilon(1+\kappa)$, and \[\frac{\max_W|Wf(W)|}{L}\le\frac{1+\varepsilon(1+\kappa)}{2+2N\sin\frac\theta{2N}}\longrightarrow\frac1{2+\theta}\qquad(\varepsilon\to0,\ N\to\infty).\] Given $c>\frac1{\pi+2}$, choose $\theta$ so close to $\pi$ that $\frac1{2+\theta}<c$, and then $\varepsilon$ small and $N$ large: no chord reaches $cL$.

Lower bound: $c=\frac1{\pi+2}$ works. Let $f$ be European and $\rho=\frac{L}{\pi+2}$. We use two standard facts about a convex polygon: a line through an interior point meets the boundary in exactly two points; and a convex set contained in another one has no larger perimeter. We say that two segments with endpoints on the boundary cross if their four endpoints are distinct and alternate in the cyclic order along the boundary.

(a) Any two chords $Yf(Y)$, $Zf(Z)$ with $Z\notin\{Y,f(Y)\}$ cross. Taking $Z=Y$ in (ii), the segment $Yf(Y)$ contains an interior point; so $f(Y)\ne Y$, and the line $Yf(Y)$ meets the boundary only at $Y$ and $f(Y)$. The four points $Y,f(Y),Z,f(Z)$ are distinct ($f(Z)=Y$ would give $Z=f(Y)$, and $f$ is injective by (i)). The points $Y,f(Y)$ cut the boundary into two open arcs; each arc is connected and misses the line $Yf(Y)$, so it lies in one open half-plane of this line. If $Z$ and $f(Z)$ were on the same arc, the segment $Zf(Z)$ would lie in that open half-plane and miss the segment $Yf(Y)$, contradicting (ii). So $Z$ and $f(Z)$ are on different arcs.

(b) Farthest vertices. For a boundary point $B$ let $g(B)$ be a vertex farthest from $B$ (the distance from $B$ is a convex function, so its maximum over the polygon is attained at a vertex), and $R(B)=|Bg(B)|>0$. Then $R(B)\ge\rho$: $\mathcal P$ lies in the closed half-plane bounded by a supporting line through $B$ and in the disc of radius $R(B)$ about $B$, hence in a half-disc of perimeter $\pi R(B)+2R(B)$, so $L\le(\pi+2)R(B)$.

(c) Matching lemma. Let $2k$ distinct boundary points be given, and let $\mathcal C$ be a perfect matching of them (a partition into $k$ pairs, each joined by a segment) in which every two segments cross. Then no perfect matching of these points has a larger total length than $\mathcal C$.

First, a fact: if $W,X,Y,Z$ are distinct boundary points in this cyclic order, the segments $WY$ and $XZ$ have a common point $O$. If the four points are collinear, they lie on one side of $\mathcal P$ (by the first standard fact, their line contains no interior point), in an order along that side which is a cyclic shift of $W,X,Y,Z$ or of its reverse, say $a,b,c,d$; then $WY$ and $XZ$ are $ac$ and $bd$, which share the segment $bc$. Otherwise the lines $WY$ and $XZ$ are different. The points $X$ and $Z$ lie in opposite closed half-planes of the line $WY$: if this line contains an interior point, the two open arcs cut off by $W,Y$ lie in opposite open half-planes (by the argument in (a), and they cannot lie on the same side, since then the whole polygon would); if not, the polygon lies in one closed half-plane, but then $W,Y$ lie on one side of $\mathcal P$ and the boundary arc between them along this side lies on the line, so one of $X,Z$ is on the line. Hence the segment $XZ$ meets the line $WY$; in the same way the segment $WY$ meets the line $XZ$. Both meeting points equal the unique common point $O$ of the two lines, so $O$ lies on both segments. Consequently, by the triangle inequality, \[|WX|+|YZ|\le|WO|+|OX|+|YO|+|OZ|=|WY|+|XZ|,\] and likewise $|WZ|+|XY|\le|WY|+|XZ|$.

Now among the perfect matchings of maximal total length take one, $\mathcal D$, with the largest number of crossing pairs of segments. Suppose two segments of $\mathcal D$ do not cross; their endpoints are four distinct points $W,X,Y,Z$ in cyclic order, and the two segments are $WX,YZ$ or $WZ,XY$. Replace them by $WY,XZ$. By the fact, the total length does not decrease, so the new matching is also of maximal length. The new two segments cross each other, the old two did not; and every other segment $UV$ crosses at least as many of the new two as of the old two. (The points $W,X,Y,Z$ cut the boundary into four open arcs; if $U,V$ are on the same arc, $UV$ crosses none of the four segments, and checking the six possible pairs of arcs for $U,V$ shows that $UV$ crosses equally many old and new segments, except in one case where it crosses none of the old and both of the new.) So the number of crossing pairs grows, contradicting the choice of $\mathcal D$. Hence every two segments of $\mathcal D$ cross.

Finally, a perfect matching in which every two segments cross is unique: number the points $P_1,\dots,P_{2k}$ in cyclic order; for a segment $P_iP_j$, each of the other $k-1$ segments has one endpoint on each of the two arcs cut off by $P_i,P_j$, so each arc contains exactly $k-1$ of the points, and $j\equiv i+k\pmod{2k}$. So $\mathcal C=\mathcal D=\{P_iP_{i+k}\}$, and $\mathcal C$ has maximal total length.

(d) A cycle. The map $V\mapsto g(f(V))$ sends vertices to vertices; as there are finitely many vertices, iterating it from any vertex eventually repeats, so there is a cycle of distinct vertices $A_1,\dots,A_k$ with $A_{i+1}=g(f(A_i))$ (indices modulo $k$). Put $B_i=f(A_i)$. The $B_i$ are distinct ($f$ is injective), $B_i\ne A_i$ by (a), and $B_i\ne A_{i+1}$ because $|B_iA_{i+1}|=R(B_i)>0$. We claim \[\sum_{i=1}^k|A_iB_i|\ \ge\ \sum_{i=1}^k|B_iA_{i+1}| .\tag{$*$}\] For $k=1$ both sides equal $|A_1B_1|$. Let $k\ge2$.

  • If the $2k$ points $A_i,B_i$ are distinct, then by (a) every two of the segments $A_iB_i$ cross. The segments $B_iA_{i+1}$ form another perfect matching of the same points, so (c) gives $(*)$.

  • Otherwise some $B_i$ equals some $A_j$; then $j\ne i,i+1$ and $B_j=f(A_j)=f(B_i)=A_i$, so the segments $A_iB_i$ and $A_jB_j$ coincide. Such coincidences come in disjoint pairs of indices $\{i,j\}$. For each such pair, move $A_j$ and $B_j$ forward along the boundary by the same small arclength $\delta>0$, and call the new points $A'_m,B'_m$ (the other points are unchanged). For $\delta$ small enough, all $2k$ points are distinct; any two segments that are not twins crossed before by (a) (four distinct alternating endpoints), so they still cross; and each moved segment crosses its twin: the cyclic order becomes $A_i,\,B'_j,\,\dots,\,B_i,\,A'_j$, which alternates. So every two segments $A'_mB'_m$ cross, the segments $B'_mA'_{m+1}$ form a perfect matching of the same $2k$ points, and (c) gives $\sum|A'_mB'_m|\ge\sum|B'_mA'_{m+1}|$. Letting $\delta\to0$ gives $(*)$.

By (b), the right side of $(*)$ is $\sum_i|B_ig(B_i)|=\sum_i R(B_i)\ge k\rho$. Hence some vertex $W=A_i$, a boundary point, has $|Wf(W)|\ge\rho=\frac{L}{\pi+2}$.

Together with the upper bound, the answer is $\boxed{c=\dfrac1{\pi+2}}$.
