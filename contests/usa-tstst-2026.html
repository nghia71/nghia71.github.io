Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle. Let $O$ be the circumcenter of $ABC$, $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ to $BC$, and $P$ be a point on line $AO$ not lying on the circumcircle of $ABC$. The circle with diameter $AP$ intersects the circumcircle of $BCP$ at $E\ne P$, and the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$ at $F\ne A$. Prove that $D$, $E$, and $F$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Radical center, then Pascal)

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, $\omega$ the circle with diameter $AP$ and $\gamma$ the circle $BCP$. Let $X$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to line $AD$; since $\angle AXP=90^\circ$, $X$ lies on $\omega$ (and $X\ne A$, because $\angle(AO,AD)=|\angle B-\angle C|<90^\circ$). Also $\angle AFP=90^\circ$, so $PF\perp AF$.

Step 1: line $PE$ passes through $R$, the intersection of $BC$ with the tangent $t$ to $\Gamma$ at $A$. The centres of $\Gamma$ and $\omega$ both lie on line $AO$ and both circles pass through $A$, so they are tangent at $A$ and their radical axis is $t$. The radical axis of $\Gamma$ and $\gamma$ is $BC$ (they are different circles, as $P\notin\Gamma$), and that of $\omega$ and $\gamma$ is $PE$. Since $AB\ne AC$, $t$ is not parallel to $BC$, so the three radical axes meet at the radical centre $R=t\cap BC$; in particular $R\in PE$.

Step 2: Pascal. Apply Pascal's theorem to the hexagon $AAFEPX$ inscribed in $\omega$ (the side $AA$ is the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$, which is $t$). The three intersection points \[t\cap EP=R,\qquad AF\cap PX,\qquad FE\cap XA\] are collinear. Now $AF\parallel BC$ by definition, and $PX\parallel BC$ because both are perpendicular to $AD$; so $AF\cap PX$ is the point at infinity of the direction $BC$. Hence $FE\cap XA$ lies on the line through $R$ parallel to $BC$, which is $BC$ itself. As $XA$ is the line $AD$, the point $FE\cap XA$ is $AD\cap BC=D$. Therefore $D$ lies on line $FE$: the points $D$, $E$, $F$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Place $D=(0,0)$, $BC$ on the $x$-axis, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a>0$. The circumcentre is $O=\bigl(\frac{b+c}2,\frac{a^2+bc}{2a}\bigr)$, and $P=(p,q)$ lies on line $AO$, which means \[p\,(bc-a^2)=a\,(b+c)\,(q-a).\tag{1}\] The foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to the line $y=a$ is $F=(p,a)$.

The circle with diameter $AP$ is $x(x-p)+(y-a)(y-q)=0$. Points of line $DF$ are $s(p,a)$; substituting gives \[s^2(p^2+a^2)-s\bigl(p^2+a^2+aq\bigr)+aq=0 .\] The root $s=1$ is $F$; the other root is $s_0=\frac{aq}{p^2+a^2}$, giving the second intersection $E'=s_0(p,a)$ of line $DF$ with the circle.

The circle $BCP$ is $x^2+y^2-(b+c)x+vy+bc=0$, where $v$ is fixed by $P$: $vq=-(p^2+q^2-(b+c)p+bc)$. At $E'=(s_0p,s_0a)$ the left side equals \[s_0^2(p^2+a^2)-s_0(b+c)p+s_0av+bc=s_0\Bigl(aq-(b+c)p-\frac aq\bigl(p^2+q^2-(b+c)p+bc\bigr)\Bigr)+bc,\] using $s_0(p^2+a^2)=aq$. Multiplying out with $s_0=\frac{aq}{p^2+a^2}$, the first term is \[\frac{a}{p^2+a^2}\Bigl(aq^2-(b+c)pq-a(p^2+q^2)+a(b+c)p-abc\Bigr)=\frac{a}{p^2+a^2}\Bigl(-ap^2+(b+c)p(a-q)-abc\Bigr).\] By (1), $(b+c)(a-q)=\frac{p(a^2-bc)}{a}$, so the last bracket is \[-ap^2+\frac{p^2(a^2-bc)}{a}-abc=-\frac{bc\,(p^2+a^2)}{a},\] and the value at $E'$ is $-bc+bc=0$. So $E'$ lies on both circles. It is not $P$: otherwise $P$ would lie on line $DF$, forcing $P=F$ (then $P$ is on the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$ and on $AO$, so $P=A\in\Gamma$) or $p=0$ (then $AO=AD$ and $AB=AC$), both excluded. Hence $E'=E$, and $E$ lies on line $DF$.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Graph Coloring and Labeling

Given a graph $G$ and a positive integer $\ell$, an $\ell$-coloring of $G$ is a function $\sigma\colon V(G)\to\{1,2,3,\dots,\ell\}$ such that for any edge $uv$ in $G$, $\sigma(u)\ne\sigma(v)$. Two $\ell$-colorings $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ are close if $\sigma(u)\ne\sigma'(u)$ for at most one $u\in V(G)$.

Find all pairs of positive integers $(k,m)$ with $k\le m$ such that for all graphs $G$ and pairs $(\tau,\tau')$ of $k$-colorings of $G$, there exists a sequence $\pi_0,\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n$ of $m$-colorings of $G$ such that $\pi_0=\tau$, $\pi_n=\tau'$, and $\pi_i$ and $\pi_{i+1}$ are close for all $0\le i<n$.

Note: $V(G)$ denotes the vertices of $G$. Since $k\le m$, $\tau$ and $\tau'$ are also $m$-colorings of $G$.

Show the answer

all pairs with $m\ge2k-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Shift into fresh colours; an invariant on $K_k\times K_k$)

Answer: exactly the pairs with $m\ge2k-1$.

A move changes the colour of one vertex and keeps the colouring proper; moves are reversible.

$m\ge2k-1$ suffices. Let $\tau,\tau'$ be $k$-colourings and define $\sigma(v)=\tau'(v)+k-1\in\{k,\dots,2k-1\}$; $\sigma$ is a proper colouring with at most $m$ colours. We show that any $k$-colouring $\gamma$ can be moved to $\sigma$; applying this to $\gamma=\tau$ and $\gamma=\tau'$ and reversing the second sequence gives a path from $\tau$ to $\tau'$.

First recolour, one at a time, every vertex $v$ with $\gamma(v)=k$ from $k$ to $\sigma(v)$; then recolour all the other vertices, one at a time, to $\sigma(v)$. Consider the move at $v$ and a neighbour $u$. If $u$ was already recoloured, its colour is $\sigma(u)\ne\sigma(v)$, as $\tau'$ is proper. If not, its colour is $\gamma(u)$. In the first phase $\gamma(u)\ne k=\gamma(v)$, so $\gamma(u)\le k-1<\sigma(v)$. In the second phase all vertices of colour $k$ are already recoloured, so again $\gamma(u)\le k-1<k\le\sigma(v)$. Every move is legal.

$m\le2k-2$ fails (for $k\ge2$). Let $G$ have vertex set $\{1,\dots,k\}^2$, with $(i,j)$ adjacent to $(i',j')$ exactly when $i\ne i'$ and $j\ne j'$. Call $\{(i,1),\dots,(i,k)\}$ row $i$. The colourings $\tau(i,j)=i$ and $\tau'(i,j)=j$ are $k$-colourings.

Two vertices of the same colour are non-adjacent, so they share a coordinate. Hence if two vertices of row $i$ have the same colour $c$, every vertex of colour $c$ shares a coordinate with both of them, and since they lie in different columns it must lie in row $i$: the colour $c$ occurs only in row $i$.

Say a colouring has property $(\ast)$ if every row contains two vertices of the same colour. Suppose $(\ast)$ holds and a move at a vertex of row $i$ destroys it. After the move, row $i$ shows $k$ different colours, while each other row $i'$ still contains a repeated colour $c_{i'}$, which occurs only in row $i'$. The colours $c_{i'}$ ($i'\ne i$) are distinct and different from the $k$ colours of row $i$, so at least $k+(k-1)=2k-1>m$ colours are in use, which is impossible. Thus $(\ast)$ is preserved by every move. Since $\tau$ has $(\ast)$ (each row is monochromatic and $k\ge2$) and $\tau'$ does not (each row shows $k$ distinct colours), no sequence of moves leads from $\tau$ to $\tau'$.

For $k=1$, every $m\ge1=2k-1$ works. So the answer is all pairs with $m\ge2k-1$.

Problem 3

SAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Hybrid Olympiad Algebra

Suppose $f_1,f_2,\dots,f_{1000}\in\mathbb{C}[x]$ are single-variable polynomials with complex coefficients such that there do not exist distinct $i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,1000\}$ and $a\in\mathbb{C}$ with $f_i=af_j$. Given that the degree of $f_1$ is $10$, find the minimum possible total degree of \[\sum_{k=1}^{1000}f_k(x_1)f_k(x_2)\cdots f_k(x_{2026}).\]

Note: For example, the total degree of the polynomial $g(x,y)=20x^3y^4+26x^6$ is $7$ because $7=3+4>6$.

Show the answer

$10270$

Show the solution

Solution (Independence of tensor powers, and a two-term construction)

Write $d=2026$, $r=1000$, $F=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f_k(x_i)$, and let $c_{k,e}$ denote the coefficient of $x^e$ in $f_k$. All $f_k$ are nonzero (a zero polynomial would equal $0\cdot f_j$).

Lemma. If $g_1,\dots,g_r$ are pairwise non-proportional nonzero polynomials and $s\ge r-1$, then the polynomials $G_k=\prod_{i=1}^{s}g_k(x_i)$ are linearly independent.

Proof. Let $V$ be the space of polynomials of degree $\le N$ (large). For a linear functional $\varphi$ on $V$, applying $\varphi$ in the variable $x_i$ is a linear map, and applying functionals $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_s$ in the variables $x_1,\dots,x_s$ sends $\prod_ig(x_i)$ to $\prod_i\varphi_i(g)$. Suppose $\sum_k\lambda_kG_k=0$ and fix $k$. For each $l\ne k$, since $g_k,g_l$ are linearly independent, there is a functional $\varphi_l$ with $\varphi_l(g_l)=0\ne\varphi_l(g_k)$; also choose $\psi$ with $\psi(g_k)\ne0$. Apply the $r-1$ functionals $\varphi_l$ in $r-1$ of the variables and $\psi$ in the remaining $s-r+1$ ones: $G_j$ goes to $0$ for $j\ne k$ (the factor $\varphi_j(g_j)=0$ occurs) and $G_k$ goes to a nonzero number. Hence $\lambda_k=0$. $\square$

Lower bound. Put $s=r-1=999$ and $t=d-s=1027$. The coefficient of $x_1^{10}x_2^{10}\cdots x_t^{10}$ in $F$, viewed as a polynomial in $x_{t+1},\dots,x_d$, is \[\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{k,10}^{\,t}\prod_{i=t+1}^{d}f_k(x_i).\] Here $c_{1,10}\ne0$ because $\deg f_1=10$, so by the Lemma this polynomial is nonzero; it contains some monomial $M$, and then $x_1^{10}\cdots x_t^{10}M$ is a monomial of $F$ with nonzero coefficient. So the total degree of $F$ is at least $10t=10270$.

Construction. Choose distinct nonzero numbers $u_1,\dots,u_r$. The $999$ linear equations $\sum_kw_ku_k^{\,j}=0$ ($1028\le j\le2026$) in the $1000$ unknowns $w_k$ have the solution \[w_k=\frac{u_k^{-1028}}{\prod_{l\ne k}(u_k-u_l)},\] because $\sum_k\frac{u_k^{\,i}}{\prod_{l\ne k}(u_k-u_l)}=0$ for $0\le i\le r-2$ (Lagrange interpolation of $x^i$ at the $u_k$, leading coefficient). All $w_k\ne0$. Moreover $\sum_kw_ku_k^{1027}=\sum_k\frac{u_k^{-1}}{\prod_{l\ne k}(u_k-u_l)}=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{\prod_lu_l}\ne0$ (partial fractions of $\frac1{x\prod_l(x-u_l)}$ evaluated through its residues).

Now let $a_k$ be any $d$-th root of $w_k$ and $f_k(x)=a_k+a_ku_kx^{10}$. These have degree $10$ and are pairwise non-proportional (the ratios $u_k$ of their coefficients are distinct). Expanding, \[F=\sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}\Bigl(\sum_ka_k^{d}u_k^{|T|}\Bigr)\prod_{i\in T}x_i^{10}=\sum_{T}\Bigl(\sum_kw_ku_k^{|T|}\Bigr)\prod_{i\in T}x_i^{10}.\] The coefficient vanishes for $|T|\ge1028$ and not for $|T|=1027$, so the total degree is exactly $10\cdot1027$.

The minimum is $\boxed{10270}$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

A beehive is a polygon with all internal angles being multiples of $60^\circ$. Find the least positive integer $k$ for which every $2026$-sided beehive can be expressed as the union of at most $k$ convex beehives.

Note: A polygon contains its interior and boundary. Its boundary must be a single loop that does not intersect itself. Internal angles of $180^\circ$ are not allowed.

Show the answer

$k=1349$

Show the solution

Solution (Cutting at reflex corners; a staircase roof with 1349 marks)

Angle bookkeeping. At every vertex the two edges meet at a multiple of $60^\circ$, so going around the boundary every edge is parallel to one of three directions at $60^\circ$ to each other, and the angles are $60^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$ or $300^\circ$. Let $n_\theta$ be the number of angles equal to $\theta$. The exterior turns, in units of $60^\circ$, are $+2,+1,-1,-2$ and add up to one full turn, so \[2n_{60}+n_{120}-n_{240}-2n_{300}=6 .\] Let $c=n_{60}+n_{120}$ and $r=n_{240}+n_{300}$ be the numbers of convex and reflex corners. Then $6\le 2c-r=2(2026-r)-r$, so $3r\le4046$ and $r\le1348$.

Every beehive with $r$ reflex corners is a union of at most $r+1$ convex beehives. We use induction on $r$; if $r=0$ the beehive is convex. Otherwise take a reflex corner $V$. If its angle is $240^\circ$, take the ray from $V$ that splits it into $120^\circ+120^\circ$; if it is $300^\circ$, take the extension of one of its edges, which splits it into $180^\circ+120^\circ$. In both cases the ray is parallel to an edge direction and starts into the interior. Follow it to the first boundary point $Q$; the segment $VQ$ lies inside the polygon and meets the boundary only at $V$ and $Q$, so it cuts the polygon into two polygons whose boundaries are simple loops. At $V$ both new angles are at most $180^\circ$. At $Q$ the old angle $\theta$ ($180^\circ$ if $Q$ is inside an edge) is split into two positive multiples of $60^\circ$, because the cut and all edges lie in the three directions and the cut does not run along an edge. Since the two parts add up to $\theta<360^\circ$, at most one of them is reflex, and none is if $\theta\le180^\circ$. Angles of $180^\circ$ are simply not vertices of the new polygons. So both pieces are beehives, all other corners are unchanged, and the total number of reflex corners of the two pieces is at most $r-1$. By induction the pieces are unions of at most $r_1+1$ and $r_2+1$ convex beehives with $r_1+r_2\le r-1$, together at most $r+1$. Hence every $2026$-sided beehive is a union of at most $1348+1=1349$ convex beehives.

A beehive that needs $1349$ pieces. Use horizontal edges $e_0,e_1,\dots,e_{674}$ of length $1$ at the increasing heights $h_i=i/1350$, placed from left to right with horizontal gaps $2$ between consecutive ones. Join the right end of $e_i$ to the left end of $e_{i+1}$ by a “tooth”: a segment of slope $\sqrt3$ followed by a segment of slope $-\sqrt3$. A short computation shows that they meet at the tip $T_{i+1}$ at height $\tfrac{h_i+h_{i+1}}2+\sqrt3>1$, above every horizontal edge. Close the polygon by going from the right end of $e_{674}$ down with slope $-\sqrt3$ to height $-1$, then left along the line $y=-1$, and up with slope $\sqrt3$ to the left end of $e_0$ (figure, with the heights exaggerated). The upper boundary (the “roof”) is the graph of a function and lies above the line $y=-1$, so the boundary is a simple loop and every vertical line meets the polygon in one segment from height $-1$ up to the roof. The angles are: $674$ tips of $60^\circ$; $2\cdot674=1348$ feet of teeth of $240^\circ$; two corners of $120^\circ$ at the ends of the roof; two bottom corners of $60^\circ$. None is $180^\circ$, so this is a beehive with $675+2\cdot674+3=2026$ sides.

Mark the midpoints $M_0,\dots,M_{674}$ of the horizontal edges and the tips $T_1,\dots,T_{674}$: $1349$ marks. We claim that no convex set inside the polygon contains two marks. A convex set containing two points contains the segment joining them, so it suffices to show that each such segment leaves the polygon.

  • One mark is some $M_i$ and the other is higher (every other mark is higher than $M_i$ or is an $M_j$ at a different height; take $M_i$ to be the lower one). The segment leaves $M_i$ going strictly upward. Since $M_i$ is an inner point of the horizontal edge $e_i$ and the polygon lies below $e_i$ near $M_i$, the points of the segment just above $M_i$ are outside the polygon.

  • Both marks are tips $T_i,T_j$ with $i<j$. The edge $e_i$ lies horizontally strictly between them, so the segment $T_iT_j$ crosses the vertical line through $M_i$ at a height above $1$. On that vertical line the polygon only reaches height $h_i<1$, so the segment leaves the polygon.

Therefore any union of convex beehives equal to this polygon uses a different piece for each of the $1349$ marks (overlapping pieces are allowed in this argument). So $k\ge1349$, and together with the upper bound the answer is $\boxed{1349}$.

Problem 5

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Olympiad-Level Synthesis

Let $\Omega(k)$ denote the number of prime factors of $k$, counted with multiplicity. For example, $\Omega(20)=3$, because $20=2\cdot2\cdot5$. Prove that for all positive integers $n$, there exists a positive integer $k$ strictly between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ such that $\Omega(k)$ is odd.

Show the solution

Solution (Adjacent products force a constant sign)

Write $\lambda(k)=(-1)^{\Omega(k)}$. Since $\Omega(ab)=\Omega(a)+\Omega(b)$, we have $\lambda(ab)=\lambda(a)\lambda(b)$; in particular $\lambda(u^2)=1$ and $\lambda(2u^2)=-1$ for every positive integer $u$.

Suppose that for some $n$ every $k$ with $n^2<k<(n+1)^2$ has $\Omega(k)$ even, and put $t=n+1\ge2$. The endpoints are squares, so $\lambda=1$ on all integers of the closed interval $I=[(t-1)^2,t^2]$. Let $r=\lfloor\sqrt{2t-1}\rfloor$; then $1\le r<t$, so all integers of $[t-r,t+r]$ are positive.

Step 1: $\lambda$ is constant on $[t-r,t]$. Fix $1\le j\le r$ (so $j^2\le 2t-1$) and put $a=t-j$. We find $b$ with $ab$ and $(a+1)b$ both in $I$:

  • if $j(j-1)\le t-1$, take $b=t+j-1$: then $ab=t^2-t-j(j-1)\ge (t-1)^2$ and $(a+1)b=t^2-(j-1)^2\le t^2$;

  • if $j(j-1)\ge t$, take $b=t+j$: then $ab=t^2-j^2\ge t^2-(2t-1)=(t-1)^2$ and $(a+1)b=t^2+t-j(j-1)\le t^2$.

In both cases $ab<(a+1)b$, so both lie in $I$ and $\lambda(a)\lambda(b)=\lambda(a+1)\lambda(b)=1$, whence $\lambda(t-j)=\lambda(t-j+1)$. Letting $j$ run from $1$ to $r$ gives the claim.

Step 2: $\lambda$ is constant on $[t-r,t+r]$. For $1\le j\le r$ we have $(t-j)(t+j)=t^2-j^2\in I$, so $\lambda(t+j)=\lambda(t-j)$.

Step 3: a square and twice a square in $[t-r,t+r]$. Let $u=\lfloor\sqrt t\rfloor$. If $t\le u^2+u$ then $0\le t-u^2\le u$; otherwise $t\ge u^2+u+1$ and $0<(u+1)^2-t\le u$. As $u^2\le t\le 2t-1$, we have $u\le r$, so a square lies within distance $r$ of $t$.

Let $v=\lfloor\sqrt{t/2}\rfloor\ge1$ and $d=t-2v^2\ge0$. If $d=0$, then $t=2v^2$. If $1\le d\le 2v$, then $d^2\le4v^2=2(t-d)<2t-1$, so $d\le r$ and $2v^2$ is within distance $r$ of $t$. If $d\ge2v+1$, then $t\ge 2v^2+2v+1$, so $2t-1\ge(2v+1)^2$, i.e. $r\ge 2v+1$; and $0<2(v+1)^2-t=4v+2-d\le 2v+1\le r$ (positive because $v+1>\sqrt{t/2}$). So twice a square lies within distance $r$ of $t$.

By Steps 1 and 2, $\lambda$ takes the same value at this square and at this twice-square; but these values are $1$ and $-1$. This contradiction shows that every interval $(n^2,(n+1)^2)$ contains a $k$ with $\Omega(k)$ odd.

Problem 6

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Miquel and Rotational Circle Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$ and let $S$ be a point inside triangle $ABC$. Let $P\ne S$ be the second intersection of line $AS$ with the circumcircle of $BCS$.

Let $T\ne A$ be a variable point on $\Gamma$, and let $Q\ne A$ and $R\ne A$ be the second intersections of line $AT$ with the circumcircles of $ABP$ and $ACP$, respectively. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $PQR$. Show that, as $T$ varies along $\Gamma$, the intersection(s) of line $ST$ with $\omega$ lie on two fixed circles (excluding cases where $\omega$ is undefined).

Show the solution

Solution (Inversion at $A$ and two Miquel points)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\kappa$ be the circle $BCS$; it passes through $P$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\kappa$ at $S$ and let $m$ be the line through $A$ parallel to $t$. Let $Y_B\ne A$ and $Y_C\ne A$ be the second intersections of $m$ with the circles $ABP$ and $ACP$. We prove that the two intersections of line $ST$ with $\omega$ lie on the fixed circles \[\kappa=(BCS)\qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma'=(PY_BY_C).\] (At the end we also show that $Y_B$ lies on line $BS$ and $Y_C$ on line $CS$.) We argue for $T$ in general position (no two of the lines used below parallel, no three of them concurrent); the finitely many remaining positions follow by continuity.

The inversion. Since $S$ is inside triangle $ABC$, $\angle BSC>\angle BAC$, so $A$ lies outside $\kappa$ and its power $k=AS\cdot AP$ with respect to $\kappa$ is positive ($S,P$ on the same ray from $A$). Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\sqrt k$. It maps $\kappa$ to itself and swaps $S$ and $P$. Let $B'=\Phi(B)$ and $C'=\Phi(C)$; these are the second intersections of lines $AB$, $AC$ with $\kappa$. Let $\ell$ be line $AT$ (mapped to itself). Then

  • $\Phi(\Gamma)$ is line $B'C'$, $\Phi(ABP)$ is line $B'S$, $\Phi(ACP)$ is line $C'S$;

  • so $T^*=\Phi(T)=\ell\cap B'C'$, $Q^*=\Phi(Q)=\ell\cap B'S$, $R^*=\Phi(R)=\ell\cap C'S$;

  • $\Phi(\omega)$ is the circle $\omega^*=(SQ^*R^*)$, and $\Phi(\text{line }ST)$ is the circle $\gamma=(APT^*)$.

($\omega$ does not pass through $A$, since $A,Q,R$ are collinear; so $\omega^*$ is a circle not through $A$, and $\omega^*\ne\gamma$.) The intersections of line $ST$ with $\omega$ are the images of the intersections of $\gamma$ and $\omega^*$. Note that $m$ is mapped to itself; let $U=m\cap B'S$ and $V=m\cap C'S$, so $\Phi(U)=m\cap\Phi(B'S)=Y_B$ and $\Phi(V)=Y_C$. Let $\sigma=(SUV)$; then $\Phi(\sigma)=(PY_BY_C)=\sigma'$.

First common point: the Miquel point $M$ of the lines $\ell, B'C', B'S, C'S$. By Miquel's theorem for four lines, $M$ lies on the circumcircles of the four triangles they form: $\kappa=(B'C'S)$, $\omega^*=(Q^*R^*S)$ and $(B'Q^*T^*)$ among them. Using $(B'Q^*T^*)$ and then $\kappa$ (which contains $M,B',S,P$), \[\angle(MT^*,MP)=\angle(MT^*,MB')+\angle(MB',MP)=\angle(Q^*T^*,Q^*B')+\angle(SB',SP)=\angle(\ell,B'S)+\angle(B'S,AS)=\angle(\ell,AS).\] The right side is $\angle(AT^*,AP)$, so $M$ lies on $\gamma=(APT^*)$. Thus $M\in\gamma\cap\omega^*$ and $M\in\kappa$.

Second common point: the Miquel point $Z$ of the lines $\ell, B'S, C'S, m$. Again $Z$ lies on the circumcircles of the four triangles: $\omega^*=(Q^*R^*S)$, $\sigma=(SUV)$ and $(AQ^*U)$ among them (for the last, $A=\ell\cap m$, $Q^*=\ell\cap B'S$, $U=B'S\cap m$). Since $\gamma$ is the circle through $A,P,M$, it suffices to show that $Z,A,P,M$ are concyclic. Using $(AQ^*U)$, then $\omega^*$ (which contains $Z,Q^*,S,M$), then $m\parallel t$ and the tangent–chord angle in $\kappa$: \[\angle(ZA,ZM)=\angle(ZA,ZQ^*)+\angle(ZQ^*,ZM)=\angle(UA,UQ^*)+\angle(SQ^*,SM)=\angle(m,B'S)+\angle(B'S,SM)=\angle(m,SM)\] \[=\angle(t,SM)=\angle(PS,PM)=\angle(PA,PM).\] Hence $Z\in\gamma$. So $Z\in\gamma\cap\omega^*$ and $Z\in\sigma$.

Conclusion. The distinct circles $\gamma$ and $\omega^*$ have at most two common points, and $M,Z$ are common points; so their intersection points are $M$ and $Z$ (when $M=Z$ this still holds by continuity in $T$). Applying $\Phi$: line $ST$ meets $\omega$ at $\Phi(M)$ and $\Phi(Z)$. As $M\in\kappa$, $\Phi(M)\in\Phi(\kappa)=\kappa=(BCS)$; as $Z\in\sigma$, $\Phi(Z)\in\sigma'=(PY_BY_C)$. Both circles depend only on $A,B,C,S$, not on $T$, which proves the statement.

Remark (where $Y_B$, $Y_C$ are). Since $U\in B'S$ and $AU\parallel t$, the tangent–chord angle in $\kappa$ gives $\angle(AU,AP)=\angle(t,SP)=\angle(B'S,B'P)=\angle(B'U,B'P)$, so $U$ lies on circle $APB'$. Applying $\Phi$, $Y_B$ lies on $\Phi(APB')$, which is line $SB$. So $Y_B$ is the second intersection of line $BS$ with circle $ABP$; likewise $Y_C$ is the second intersection of line $CS$ with circle $ACP$; and $A,Y_B,Y_C$ are collinear (on $m$). So the second circle can be described as the circle through $P$ and the second intersections of $BS$ with $(ABP)$ and $CS$ with $(ACP)$.

Problem 7

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

Determine all ordered pairs of positive integers $(a,b)$ such that $n+1$ divides $\binom{an+b}{n}$ for all positive integers $n$.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(a,a)$ with $a\ge1$, and $(a,a-2)$ with $a\ge3$

Show the solution

Solution (A gcd identity and Lucas's theorem)

Put $N=an+b$ and $c=b-a+1$; then $N+1=a(n+1)+c$.

Sufficiency for $c=\pm1$, i.e. $b=a$ or $b=a-2$. From $\binom{N+1}{n+1}=\frac{N+1}{n+1}\binom Nn$, \[(n+1)\binom{N+1}{n+1}=(N+1)\binom{N}{n}.\] So $n+1$ divides $(N+1)\binom Nn$, and $\gcd(n+1,N+1)=\gcd(n+1,c)=1$. Hence $n+1\mid\binom Nn$ for every $n$.

Necessity. Suppose $c\ne\pm1$. Then some prime $p$ divides $c$ (if $c=0$ take any prime). Let $n=p-1\ge1$. Then \[N=a(p-1)+b\equiv b-a=c-1\equiv-1\pmod p,\] so the last base-$p$ digit of $N$ is $p-1$. By Lucas's theorem, \[\binom{N}{p-1}\equiv\binom{p-1}{p-1}\binom{\lfloor N/p\rfloor}{0}=1\pmod p,\] so $n+1=p$ does not divide $\binom Nn$.

Therefore the condition holds exactly when $b-a+1=\pm1$, i.e. $b=a$ (any $a\ge1$) or $b=a-2$ (which needs $a\ge3$ since $b\ge1$).

Problem 8

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

Let $A$ be a finite set of positive real numbers. Define $a_k$ to be the number of distinct positive real numbers that can be expressed as a sum of at most $k$ not necessarily distinct elements of $A$. Prove that $2025a_{2026}\ge2026a_{2025}$.

Show the solution

Solution (Chains with difference $\max A$)

We prove $k\,a_{k+1}\ge(k+1)\,a_k$ for every $k\ge1$; the case $k=2025$ is the claim.

Let $S_k$ be the set of sums of at least one and at most $k$ elements of $A$, so $a_k=|S_k|$, and let $M=\max A$. Then:

  • every element of $S_k$ is positive and at most $kM$;

  • $S_k\subseteq S_{k+1}$ and $S_k+M\subseteq S_{k+1}$ (add one more summand $M$).

Split $S_k$ into maximal chains $s,s+M,s+2M,\dots,s+(L-1)M$ of elements of $S_k$ (each element lies in exactly one maximal chain: extend downwards and upwards by $M$ as long as possible). A chain of length $L$ satisfies $0<s$ and $s+(L-1)M\le kM$, so $(L-1)M<kM$ and $L\le k$. Hence there are at least $a_k/k$ chains.

For the top element $x$ of each chain, $x+M\notin S_k$ but $x+M\in S_{k+1}$. These numbers are distinct for different chains. Therefore \[a_{k+1}\ge a_k+\frac{a_k}{k}=\frac{k+1}{k}\,a_k ,\] which is $k\,a_{k+1}\ge(k+1)a_k$. With $k=2025$: $2025\,a_{2026}\ge2026\,a_{2025}$.

Problem 9

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

In the plane, we say that a set of points $A=\{A_1,A_2,\dots,A_k\}$ demolishes a set of points $B=\{B_1,B_2,\dots,B_\ell\}$ if, for all points $P$, \[PA_1+PA_2+\dots+PA_k\ge PB_1+PB_2+\dots+PB_\ell.\] Let $S$ be a set of $n$ points in the plane. Show that we can partition $S$ into two sets $A$ and $B$ such that $A$ demolishes $B$ and $|A|\le|B|+100\sqrt n$.

Show the solution

Solution (Extreme points pay for short pairs)

Write $\Sigma_X(P)=\sum_{Y\in X}PY$ for a finite set $X$, so $A$ demolishes $B$ means $\Sigma_A(P)\ge\Sigma_B(P)$ for all $P$. We may assume $n\ge1$. Let $s=\lceil\sqrt n\,\rceil$ and $K=4s$.

Small $n$. If $n\le2K$, take $A=S$ and $B=\emptyset$. Then $A$ demolishes $B$, and $|A|=n\le8s<8(\sqrt n+1)\le16\sqrt n$.

Reserved points. Let $n>2K$. Order $S$ by $x$-coordinate, breaking ties by any fixed rule, and let $L$ and $R$ be the first $K$ and the last $K$ points; they are disjoint since $n>2K$. Do the same with the $y$-coordinate to get the $K$ lowest points $D$ and the $K$ highest points $U$. Put all points of $L\cup R\cup D\cup U$ into $A$; there are at most $4K$ of them. Let $S'$ be the set of the other points. If $S'=\emptyset$ we are done as below, so assume not, and let $w$ and $h$ be the width and height of the smallest axis-parallel rectangle containing $S'$, and $d=\max(w,h)$.

Every point of $L$ has $x$-coordinate at most that of every point of $S'$, and every point of $R$ at least; so any $X\in L$ and $Y\in R$ satisfy $XY\ge w$. Match $L$ with $R$ in any way. For each matched pair, the triangle inequality gives $PX+PY\ge XY\ge w$, so the $K$ pairs give $\Sigma_{L\cup R}(P)\ge Kw$. In the same way $\Sigma_{D\cup U}(P)\ge Kh$. The reserved set contains both $L\cup R$ and $D\cup U$, hence its distance sum to $P$ is at least $Kd$ for every point $P$.

Short pairs. Cut the rectangle of $S'$ into $s$ vertical strips of width $w/s$, putting each point of $S'$ into exactly one strip (one strip if $w=0$). In each strip, sort its points by height and pair the first with the second, the third with the fourth, and so on; at most one point per strip is left over. In a strip, the vertical gaps of these pairs are disjoint pieces of an interval of length $h$, so they add up to at most $h$, and each horizontal gap is at most $w/s$. There are at most $n/2$ pairs, so the total length of these pairs (each length is at most horizontal gap plus vertical gap) is at most \[sh+\frac n2\cdot\frac ws\le sh+\frac{sw}2,\] using $n\le s^2$. Now pair up the at most $s$ leftover points arbitrarily, leaving at most one point $Z$; there are at most $s/2$ such pairs, each of length at most $w+h$. The total length $\ell$ of all pairs is therefore \[\ell\le sh+\frac{sw}2+\frac s2(w+h)=sw+\frac32sh\le\frac52sd\le Kd .\]

The partition. From each pair $\{X,Y\}$ put one point into $A$ and the other into $B$; put $Z$ (if it exists) into $A$. For every point $P$ and every pair, $PX-PY\ge-XY$ by the triangle inequality. Adding up, \[\Sigma_A(P)-\Sigma_B(P)\ \ge\ (\text{reserved sum})+PZ-\ell\ \ge\ Kd-\ell\ \ge\ 0,\] so $A$ demolishes $B$. Finally, the pairs contribute equally to $A$ and $B$, so \[|A|-|B|\le4K+1=16s+1\le16(\sqrt n+1)+1\le33\sqrt n\le100\sqrt n .\] This proves the statement (in fact with $33\sqrt n$).
