Problem 5

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

For a finite set of integers $A$, we define the difference set $A-A:=\{a_i-a_j\mid a_i,a_j\in A\}$ to be the set of all differences of two (possibly not distinct) elements of $A$. Let $N$ be the minimum of $|A-A|$ over all subsets $A\subset\mathbb Z$ with $|A|=99$, and let $M$ be the maximum of $|A-A|$ over all subsets $A\subset\mathbb Z$ with $|A|=99$. Find $N+M$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try making the difference set of a set with three elements. Keep a single copy of each result and notice when changing the order of subtraction produces a new entry.
Hint 2: key idea
For a lower bound, fix the smallest element and subtract it from every other element. For an upper bound, count how many ordered pairs of distinct elements could produce different nonzero differences. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Include zero and the negative differences when you work out the lower bound, then look for a set whose differences overlap as much as possible. For the upper bound, look for a set that grows so quickly that each positive difference identifies the two elements that produced it.
Common trap
Two different ordered pairs may produce the same difference, which appears only once in the set.
Show the answer

$9900$

Show the solution – Extreme examples

Let $A=\{a_1<a_2<\dots<a_{99}\}$.

Minimum. The numbers $a_i-a_1$ ($2\le i\le 99$) are $98$ distinct positive differences, their negatives are $98$ distinct negative ones, and $0$ is also a difference: $|A-A|\ge 197$. For $A=\{1,2,\dots,99\}$, $A-A=\{-98,\dots,98\}$ has exactly $197$ elements. So $N=197$.

Maximum. $A-A$ consists of $0$ and the $99\cdot98$ differences $a_i-a_j$ with $i\neq j$, so $|A-A|\le 9703$. For $A=\{2,4,8,\dots,2^{99}\}$ all these differences are distinct: $2^i-2^j$ with $i>j$ has binary form $1\dots10\dots0$ ($i-j$ ones followed by $j$ zeros), which determines $i$ and $j$, and its sign tells which is larger. So $M=9703$ and $N+M=\boxed{9900}$.

Problem 6

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Coordinate Area Methods

$ABCD$ is a square with side length $40$. Points $E,F$ are chosen on $\overline{AB},\overline{CD}$, respectively, such that $AE=1$ and $CF=9$. Point $X_1$ is chosen on $\overline{AB}$, points $X_2$ and $X_3$ are chosen on $\overline{BC}$, and point $X_4$ is chosen on $\overline{CD}$ so that $\angle EX_iF=90^\circ$ for $i=1,2,3,4$. Given that the polygon $X_1X_2X_3X_4$ is non-degenerate and not self-intersecting, find its area.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the square and mark $E$ and $F$ using the given distances. Mark the sides on which the four unknown vertices must lie.
Hint 2: key idea
By Thales' theorem, a point making a right angle with endpoints $E$ and $F$ lies on the circle with diameter $EF$. Put the square on coordinate axes so that you can intersect this circle with its sides. (LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Find the centre and radius from $E$ and $F$, then solve the circle equation along $AB$, $BC$ and $CD$. Discard the diameter endpoints and order the two points on $BC$ so that the polygon does not cross itself. Put the vertices in that order to set up the shoelace calculation.
Common trap
Interchanging the two points on $BC$ can turn a simple quadrilateral into a crossed one.
Show the answer

$648$

Show the solution – Thales' circle in coordinates

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(40,0)$, $C=(40,40)$, $D=(0,40)$, so $E=(1,0)$ and $F=(31,40)$. By Thales' theorem, $\angle EXF=90^\circ$ exactly when $X\ne E,F$ lies on the circle with diameter $EF$: centre $(16,20)$, radius $\frac12\sqrt{30^2+40^2}=25$.

  • On $AB$ ($y=0$): $(x-16)^2=625-400=225$, so $x=1$ (the point $E$) or $x=31$: $X_1=(31,0)$.

  • On $BC$ ($x=40$): $(y-20)^2=625-576=49$, so the two points are $(40,13)$ and $(40,27)$. We take $X_2=(40,13)$, $X_3=(40,27)$: with the other labelling the sides $X_1X_2$ and $X_3X_4$ would cross, and the polygon would not be a simple quadrilateral.

  • On $CD$ ($y=40$): $x=1$ or $31$ (the point $F$): $X_4=(1,40)$.

By the shoelace formula, \begin{align*} 2[X_1X_2X_3X_4]&=(31\cdot13-40\cdot0)+(40\cdot27-40\cdot13)\\ &\quad+(40\cdot40-1\cdot27)+(1\cdot0-31\cdot40)=1296, \end{align*} so the area is $\boxed{648}$. (Check by subtraction: the square minus the triangles $X_1BX_2$ ($58.5$), $X_3CX_4$ ($253.5$) and the quadrilateral $AX_1X_4D$ ($640$) leaves $1600-952=648$.)

Problem 7

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Permutations and Arrangements

Let $N$ be the number of functions $f$ from the integers $\{1,2,\dots,25\}$ to integers in $\{1,2,\dots,25\}$ such that $f(i)\neq f(j)$ for all $i\neq j$ and $f(i)+j\equiv i+f(j)\pmod 5$ for all $1\le i<j\le 25$. How many divisors does $N$ have?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Group the numbers from $1$ to $25$ by their remainders modulo $5$. Keep track of how many possible inputs and outputs lie in each group.
Hint 2: key idea
Rearrange the congruence so that the terms involving $i$ are together and the terms involving $j$ are together. This reveals a uniform shift between the residue class of an input and that of its image. (LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration)
Hint 3: approach
Choose the residue shift and, for that shift, count the bijections between each input class and its assigned output class. Multiply the independent counts and account for every shift, then prime-factorize the resulting number to prepare for counting its divisors.
Common trap
Fixing the shift determines an output residue class, but there can still be multiple choices for each output.
Show the answer

$672$

Show the solution – A constant shift of residues

The congruence says $f(i)-i\equiv f(j)-j\pmod5$ for all $i<j$, so $f(i)\equiv i+c\pmod 5$ for a constant $c$. As $f$ is injective on a $25$-element set, it is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,25\}$; it maps the residue class $r$ (five numbers) into the class $r+c$ (five numbers), hence bijectively onto it. Conversely, any $c$ and any choice of five bijections between the classes $r$ and $r+c$ give a valid $f$. Therefore \[N=5\cdot(5!)^5=5\cdot2^{15}3^55^5=2^{15}\cdot3^5\cdot5^6,\] which has $(15+1)(5+1)(6+1)=\boxed{672}$ divisors.

Problem 8

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Factorization

For a positive integer $n$, let $\Omega(n)$ denote the number of prime factors of $n$, counted with multiplicity. What is the smallest positive integer $n$ for which $2^{\Omega(n)}<n<2.2^{\Omega(n)}$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Factor a small number such as $12$ and count its prime factors with repetitions. Compare the number with the two bounds generated by that count.
Hint 2: key idea
Fix the prime-factor count at $k$. The strict lower bound excludes a product of all twos, so find the smallest product with $k$ prime factors and at least one factor larger than $2$. (LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization)
Hint 3: approach
For positive $k$, compare that smallest product with $(2.2)^k$ by dividing through by $2^k$. Find the first $k$ whose smallest candidate passes the comparison, then use a lower bound for products with more prime factors to check whether they could give a smaller number.
Common trap
Check $n=1$ separately, since it has no prime factors and both bounds are strict.
Show the answer

$48$

Show the solution – Replace one factor 2 by 3

Let $k=\Omega(n)$. For $n=1$ both bounds equal $1$, so $n=1$ fails and $k\ge1$. Every $n$ with $\Omega(n)=k$ is at least $2^k$, with equality only for $n=2^k$; any other such $n$ has a prime factor at least $3$, so $n\ge3\cdot2^{k-1}=1.5\cdot2^k$. Hence a solution with $\Omega(n)=k$ needs $1.5\cdot2^k<2.2^k$, i.e. $1.1^k>1.5$. Since $1.1^4=1.4641<1.5<1.61051=1.1^5$, we need $k\ge5$.

For $k=5$, the smallest candidate is $3\cdot2^4=48$, and indeed $32<48<2.2^5=51.53\ldots$. Every $n$ with $\Omega(n)\ge6$ is at least $2^6=64>48$. So the answer is $\boxed{48}$.

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

Let $S$ be the set of prime numbers less than $2026$. For $p\neq q$, $p$ and $q$ are considered connected if $pq\equiv k\pmod{2026}$, for some integer $k$ with $0<k<2026$. The degree of $p$ is the number of $q\in S$ such that $p$ and $q$ are connected. For what value of $k$ is the maximum degree of an element in $S$ maximized?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Fix a residue $k$ and one prime $p$. Record what a second prime $q$ must satisfy to count as a neighbor of $p$.
Hint 2: key idea
Factor the modulus and separate primes that are invertible modulo $2026$ from its prime divisors. In the invertible case, multiply the congruence by the inverse of $p$ to see how many residues can contain a neighbor. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
For $p=2$, use the parity of $k$ and reduce the congruence to one with half the modulus when division by $2$ is possible. For the odd prime divisor, check even and odd partners separately and record the nonzero residues that occur. Compare the corresponding neighbor counts with the bounds from the other cases.
Common trap
The definition excludes $q=p$, even when its product would give the chosen residue.
Show the answer

$1013$

Show the solution – Only the prime 1013 can have a large degree

Note $2026=2\cdot1013$ with $1013$ prime. Fix $k$ and consider the degree of $p\in S$.

  • If $p\nmid 2026$, then $p$ is invertible mod $2026$ and $pq\equiv k$ forces $q\equiv kp^{-1}\pmod{2026}$: at most one $q<2026$. Degree $\le1$.

  • If $p=2$: $2q\equiv k\pmod{2026}$ has no solution if $k$ is odd, and for even $k$ it means $q\equiv\frac k2\pmod{1013}$: at most two $q<2026$. Degree $\le2$.

  • If $p=1013$: for odd $q$, $1013q\equiv1013\pmod{2026}$, and for $q=2$, $1013q\equiv0$. So $p=1013$ has positive degree only for $k=1013$, and then it is connected to every odd prime $q\neq1013$ below $2026$.

There are $306$ primes below $2026$, so for $k=1013$ the degree of $1013$ is $306-2=304$, far more than any degree possible for another $k$ (at most $2$). The answer is $k=\boxed{1013}$.

Problem 10

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Angle Expressions and Trigonometric Identities

Triangle $ABC$ is inscribed in a circle centered at $O$ with radius $5$. It is given that $BC=6$ and the three distances from $O$ to the sides of $ABC$ form the side lengths of a right triangle. The area of $ABC$ can be expressed as $\frac mn$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. What is $m+n$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Drop perpendiculars from $O$ to the three side lines. Use the midpoint of $BC$ and the given radius to work out the distance to $BC$.
Hint 2: key idea
Express each distance as $R$ times the absolute cosine of the opposite angle. Translate the right-triangle condition into an equation between three squared cosines, allowing each one in turn to represent the hypotenuse. (LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion)
Hint 3: approach
Use $C=180^\circ-(A+B)$ to get an identity involving the sum of the three cosine squares and their product. Combine it with each possible Pythagorean equation to identify the feasible hypotenuse, then use positivity of $\sin B\sin C$ to check the sign of $\cos A$. Use the extended sine rule to set up the area once the sine product is known.
Common trap
Distances are nonnegative, but the cosines before taking absolute values may have different signs.
Show the answer

$79$

Show the solution – Distances are R times cosines

The distance from $O$ to $BC$ is $\sqrt{25-9}=4$. In general the distance from $O$ to side $BC$ is $R|\cos A|$ (the central angle on $BC$ is $2A$ or $360^\circ-2A$), so with $R=5$ the distances are $5|u|,5|v|,5|w|$, where $u=\cos A$, $v=\cos B$, $w=\cos C$ and $u^2=\frac{16}{25}$. We use the identity $u^2+v^2+w^2+2uvw=1$, valid in every triangle: since $w=-\cos(A+B)=-uv+\sin A\sin B$, squaring $w+uv=\sin A\sin B$ gives $w^2+2uvw+u^2v^2=(1-u^2)(1-v^2)$, which simplifies to the identity.

The hypotenuse is the distance $4$. Suppose instead, say, $w^2=u^2+v^2$ (the case $v^2=u^2+w^2$ is the same with $B,C$ exchanged). Put $t=w^2\ge\frac{16}{25}$. The identity gives $2w^2+2uvw=1$, so $(2uvw)^2=(1-2t)^2$, i.e. $4\cdot\frac{16}{25}\left(t-\frac{16}{25}\right)t=(1-2t)^2$, which simplifies to $900t^2-1476t+625=0$. Its discriminant $1476^2-4\cdot900\cdot625=-71424$ is negative: impossible.

So $v^2+w^2=u^2$, and the identity gives $2u^2+2uvw=1$, i.e. $vw=\frac{1-2u^2}{2u}=\frac{-7/25}{2u}$. Also $\cos(B+C)=-u$ gives $\sin B\sin C=vw+u$.

  • $u=-\frac45$: $vw=\frac7{40}$ and $\sin B\sin C=\frac7{40}-\frac45<0$, impossible.

  • $u=\frac45$: $vw=-\frac7{40}$ and $\sin B\sin C=\frac{25}{40}=\frac58$, with $\sin A=\frac35$.

Hence $[ABC]=\frac12\,bc\sin A=2R^2\sin A\sin B\sin C=50\cdot\frac35\cdot\frac58=\frac{75}4$. (Such a triangle exists, e.g. $A=\left(\frac{\sqrt{319}}4,\frac94\right)$, $B=(-3,-4)$, $C=(3,-4)$ with $O$ the origin: the squared distances from $O$ to the sides, computed from the midpoints of the sides, are $16$, $\frac{64-3\sqrt{319}}8$ and $\frac{64+3\sqrt{319}}8$, and the last two add up to $16$.) The answer is $75+4=\boxed{79}$.

Problem 12

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · Expected Value and Fairness

Twenty points are evenly spaced on a unit circle. Three distinct points $A$, $B$, and $C$ are chosen from them uniformly at random. The expected value of $AB^2+BC^2+CA^2$ can be expressed in the form $\frac mn$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. What is $m+n$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch several choices for $B$ while keeping $A$ at one marked point. Compare the chords for different angular separations.
Hint 2: key idea
By symmetry and linearity of expectation, you only need the expected squared length of one side. With one endpoint fixed, average over the remaining equally likely endpoints. (LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value)
Hint 3: approach
Express each squared chord length as $2-2\cos\theta$ for its central angle $\theta$. Use the real parts of the roots of unity to sum the cosines, removing the term for the fixed endpoint before setting up the average.
Common trap
The sampling rule excludes the fixed point from the choices for the other endpoint.
Show the answer

$139$

Show solution 1 – Linearity over the three sides

By linearity, the expectation is $3E[AB^2]$, where $A,B$ is a uniformly random pair of distinct points among the twenty. Fix $A$; then $B$ is equally likely to be any of the other $19$ points, at angle $\frac{2\pi k}{20}$, $1\le k\le19$, and $AB^2=2-2\cos\frac{2\pi k}{20}$. Since the $20$th roots of unity sum to $0$, $\sum_{k=1}^{19}\cos\frac{2\pi k}{20}=-1$, so \[E[AB^2]=\frac{38+2}{19}=\frac{40}{19},\qquad E\left[AB^2+BC^2+CA^2\right]=\frac{120}{19},\] and $m+n=\boxed{139}$.

Show solution 2 – Vectors and the centroid

Treat the points as unit vectors. Then \[AB^2+BC^2+CA^2=6-2(A\cdot B+B\cdot C+C\cdot A)=9-|A+B+C|^2,\] because $|A+B+C|^2=3+2(A\cdot B+B\cdot C+C\cdot A)$. For two distinct random points, $E[A\cdot B]=\frac1{19}\sum_{k=1}^{19}\cos\frac{2\pi k}{20}=-\frac1{19}$ (the twenty points sum to the zero vector). So $E|A+B+C|^2=3-\frac6{19}=\frac{51}{19}$, and the expectation is $9-\frac{51}{19}=\frac{120}{19}$. Hence $m+n=\boxed{139}$.

Problem 5

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\Omega$. Let $D$ and $E$ be the feet of the altitudes from $B$ and $C$, respectively. Extend $\overline{DE}$ to intersect $\Omega$ at distinct points $K$ and $L$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $\overline{KL}$. If $\angle B=40^\circ$ and $\angle C=70^\circ$, what is the measure of $\angle BAM$, in degrees?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Find $\angle A$ and mark the right angles at the two altitude feet. Draw the circumcentre $O$ and mark the midpoint of the chord on line $DE$.
Hint 2: key idea
Find a circle through $B,C,D,E$ by looking at the two right angles. Use that circle to relate the direction of $DE$ to the tangent to $\Omega$ at $A$. (LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing)
Hint 3: approach
Find the angle between $DE$ and $AB$ from the cyclic quadrilateral and compare it with the tangent–chord angle at $A$. Use the perpendicular from $O$ to the chord midpoint and check that $M$ lies on the ray from $A$ through $O$. Set up the angle calculation in isosceles triangle $AOB$.
Common trap
Collinearity of $A,O,M$ alone leaves open which ray from $A$ contains $M$.
Show the answer

$20^\circ$

Show the solution – AO is perpendicular to DE

The angles are $\angle A=70^\circ$, $\angle B=40^\circ$, $\angle C=70^\circ$, so the triangle is acute and $O$, the centre of $\Omega$, lies inside it.

$AO\perp DE$. Since $\angle BDC=\angle BEC=90^\circ$, the points $B,C,D,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$. As the triangle is acute, $D$ lies on segment $AC$ and $E$ on segment $AB$, so $BCDE$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral, and $\angle AED=180^\circ-\angle BED=\angle BCD=\angle ACB$. If $t$ is the tangent to $\Omega$ at $A$, the tangent-chord angle between $t$ and $AB$ also equals $\angle ACB$. Hence $t\parallel DE$, and since $OA\perp t$, $OA\perp DE$.

$M$ lies on ray $AO$. $M$ is the midpoint of the chord $KL$ of $\Omega$, so $OM\perp KL$; as $OA\perp KL$ too, $M$ lies on line $AO$. Moreover the ray $AO$ lies inside $\angle BAC$, so it crosses the segment $DE$ (from ray $AC$ to ray $AB$); that crossing point is $M$. Therefore \[\angle BAM=\angle BAO=90^\circ-\angle ACB=90^\circ-70^\circ=\boxed{20^\circ}.\] (Here $\angle BAO=90^\circ-\angle C$ because triangle $AOB$ is isosceles with apex angle $\angle AOB=2\angle C$.)

Problem 6

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Remainder, Factor, and Evaluation Logic

Define a sequence of polynomials by $F_1(x)=(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-2025)$, $F_2(x)=\left(x-1^2\right)\left(x-2^2\right)\cdots\left(x-2025^2\right)$, and for $n\ge3$, $F_n=F_{n-1}^2+F_1^2$ if $n$ is odd and $F_n=F_{n-1}^2F_2^2$ if $n$ is even. How many distinct real roots does $F_{2025}$ have?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Read the roots of $F_1$ and $F_2$ from their factors. Try tracing a few small real candidates through the rules for $F_3$ and $F_4$.
Hint 2: key idea
For real inputs, a sum of squares is zero only if both squares vanish, whereas a product is zero if either factor vanishes. Use these observations to update the set of real roots at each step. (LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
Form the intersection for $F_3$ and compare it with the root set of $F_2$. Track what the next even and odd steps do to those sets, then identify the bound that a square must satisfy to belong to the relevant set.
Common trap
Count distinct real roots, regardless of how many times a root occurs.
Show the answer

$45$

Show the solution – Zero sets of sums and products of squares

All $F_n$ have real coefficients. For real $x$: a sum of two squares $F_{n-1}(x)^2+F_1(x)^2$ vanishes iff both terms vanish, and a product vanishes iff a factor does. Writing $Z_n$ for the set of real roots of $F_n$, \[Z_n=Z_{n-1}\cap Z_1\ (n\text{ odd}),\qquad Z_n=Z_{n-1}\cup Z_2\ (n\text{ even}),\] with $Z_1=\{1,\dots,2025\}$ and $Z_2=\{1^2,2^2,\dots,2025^2\}$. Then $Z_3=Z_2\cap Z_1=\{1^2,2^2,\dots,45^2\}$ (as $45^2=2025<46^2$), which is contained in $Z_2$, so $Z_4=Z_3\cup Z_2=Z_2$ and $Z_5=Z_2\cap Z_1=Z_3$. By induction $Z_n=Z_3$ for odd $n\ge3$ and $Z_n=Z_2$ for even $n\ge2$. Hence $F_{2025}$ has $\boxed{45}$ distinct real roots.

Problem 8

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 36: Power of a Point and Circle Measurement · Secant-Tangent Power

Equilateral triangle $ADG$, where the vertices are labeled clockwise, has side length $27$. A circle is drawn such that it intersects each side of the equilateral triangle twice. Clockwise from $A$, label these intersections $B,C,E,F,H$, and $I$. If $AI=6$, $GF=7$, $DC=15$, and the other six edges each have integer length, find $BC\cdot EF\cdot HI$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the triangle and mark the intersections in their clockwise order. Along each side, distinguish the segments between intersections from the distances measured from a vertex.
Hint 2: key idea
Apply power of a point at each triangle vertex. The product of the distances to the near and far intersections along one adjoining side equals the corresponding product along the other. (LPS VI, Chapter 36: Power of a Point and Circle Measurement)
Hint 3: approach
Take $AB$, $DE$, and $HI$ as unknowns and express the distances you need in terms of them and the given lengths. Use the three power equations to solve for the unknowns, then recover each requested segment length separately.
Common trap
For the farther intersection, measure all the way from the vertex.
Show the answer

$128$

Show the solution – Power of the three vertices

On side $AD$ the order is $A,B,C,D$; on $DG$ it is $D,E,F,G$; on $GA$ it is $G,H,I,A$. Let $AB=x$ and $HI=h$. Then $AC=27-15=12$, $BC=12-x$, $AH=6+h$, $GH=21-h$, $DF=27-7=20$; let $DE=e$, $EF=20-e$.

The powers of $A$, $D$, $G$ with respect to the circle give \begin{align*} AB\cdot AC=AI\cdot AH&:\quad 12x=6(6+h),\\ DC\cdot DB=DE\cdot DF&:\quad 15(27-x)=20e,\\ GF\cdot GE=GI\cdot GH&:\quad 7(27-e)=21(21-h). \end{align*} From the first, $h=2x-6$; the third becomes $27-e=3(27-2x)$, so $e=6x-54$; the second becomes $15(27-x)=20(6x-54)$, i.e. $405-15x=120x-1080$, so $x=11$. Then $h=16$, $e=12$, and $BC=1$, $EF=8$, $HI=16$ (all nine segments $11,1,15;12,8,7;5,16,6$ are positive integers). Hence $BC\cdot EF\cdot HI=\boxed{128}$.

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Quadratic Residues

Find the remainder when $\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{2026}\left(k^2+1\right)$ is divided by $2027$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the factors for $k$ and $2027-k$ after reducing modulo $2027$. Set aside the term for $k=0$ before looking for pairs.
Hint 2: key idea
Choose one representative from each pair to index the nonzero squares. For $p=2027$, use Fermat's little theorem to connect those squares to the roots of $t^{(p-1)/2}-1$ modulo $p$. (LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences)
Hint 3: approach
Verify that $p$ is prime and that the chosen representatives give as many distinct squares as the degree of the polynomial. Factor the polynomial using these squares, then evaluate it at $t=-1$ and track the signs to recover the product from one representative per pair.
Common trap
The paired indices contribute identical factors twice in the original product.
Show the answer

$4$

Show solution 1 – Quadratic residues

Let $p=2027$, a prime (it has no prime divisor up to $43$, and $47^2>2027$) with $p\equiv3\pmod4$, so $k^2+1\not\equiv0$ for all $k$ (if $k^2\equiv-1$, then $k^{p-1}=(k^2)^{1013}\equiv-1$, against Fermat's little theorem). The term $k=0$ is $1$, and $k$ and $p-k$ give the same factor, so \[\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(k^2+1)\equiv\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{(p-1)/2}(k^2+1)\Bigr)^2=\Bigl(\prod_{q\in Q}(q+1)\Bigr)^2,\] where $Q$ is the set of the $\frac{p-1}2$ nonzero quadratic residues (each is $k^2$ for exactly one $k\in\{1,\dots,\frac{p-1}{2}\}$). Each $q=a^2\in Q$ satisfies $q^{(p-1)/2}=a^{p-1}\equiv1$ by Fermat's little theorem, so the $\frac{p-1}2$ distinct elements of $Q$ are all the roots of the monic polynomial $x^{(p-1)/2}-1$ of degree $\frac{p-1}2$, and $\prod_{q\in Q}(x-q)\equiv x^{(p-1)/2}-1$. Putting $x=-1$ and using that $\frac{p-1}2=1013$ is odd: \[\prod_{q\in Q}(-1-q)\equiv(-1)^{1013}-1=-2,\qquad\prod_{q\in Q}(q+1)\equiv(-1)^{1013}(-2)=2 .\] Hence the product is $\equiv2^2=\boxed{4}\pmod{2027}$.

Show solution 2 – Factor over the field with i

Let $p=2027$, a prime (no prime divisor up to $43$, and $47^2>2027$). Then $-1$ is not a square mod $p$: if $v^2\equiv-1$, then $v^{p-1}=(v^2)^{1013}\equiv-1$, against Fermat's little theorem. So we can adjoin a square root $i$ of $-1$: the numbers $a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb F_p$, computed with $i^2=-1$, form a field $\mathbb F_p[i]$ of $p^2$ elements (a nonzero $a+bi$ has inverse $\frac{a-bi}{a^2+b^2}$, and $a^2+b^2\ne0$ because $-1$ is not a square), where $(a+bi)^p=a-bi$ for $a,b\in\mathbb F_p$ (Frobenius, as $i^p=i\cdot(i^2)^{(p-1)/2}=i(-1)^{1013}=-i$).

The identity $\prod_{k\in\mathbb F_p}(x-k)=x^p-x$ gives, at $x=i$ and $x=-i$, \[\prod_k(i-k)=i^p-i=-2i,\qquad\prod_k(-i-k)=(-i)^p+i=2i .\] Since $k^2+1=(k-i)(k+i)=(i-k)(-i-k)$, \[\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(k^2+1)=(-2i)(2i)=-4i^2=4 .\] The remainder is $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 12

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry · Isosceles and Equilateral Structure

Point $P$ is inside square $ABCD$. Given that $\angle DAP=\angle ADP=15^\circ$, find $\angle CPD$, in degrees.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the two inward rays fixed by the angle data at $A$ and $D$. Their crossing locates $P$.
Hint 2: key idea
Construct an equilateral triangle $BCQ$ inside the square. Compare its side lengths with those of the square to find isosceles triangles that let you check the directions of $AQ$ and $DQ$. (LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the base angles in $ABQ$ and $DCQ$ to decide whether $Q$ lies on both of the rays that determine $P$. Then use the equilateral construction to identify an equal pair of sides in $CPD$ and work out its angle at $C$.
Common trap
Construct the equilateral triangle on the side of $BC$ that lies inside the square.
Show the answer

$75^\circ$

Show solution 1 – The hidden equilateral triangle

Let $Q$ be the point inside the square such that $BCQ$ is equilateral. Then $\angle ABQ=90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ$ and $BA=BC=BQ$, so triangle $ABQ$ is isosceles with apex angle $30^\circ$ and $\angle BAQ=75^\circ$, i.e. $\angle DAQ=15^\circ$. In the same way $\angle ADQ=15^\circ$. The rays from $A$ and $D$ making $15^\circ$ with $AD$ meet in a single point, so $Q=P$.

Hence $CP=CB=CD$ and $\angle PCD=90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ$. Triangle $CPD$ is isosceles with apex angle $30^\circ$, so $\angle CPD=\frac{180^\circ-30^\circ}{2}=\boxed{75^\circ}$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Let the side be $2$, $A=(0,0)$, $B=(2,0)$, $C=(2,2)$, $D=(0,2)$. Triangle $APD$ is isosceles, so $P$ is on the line $y=1$, and $\angle DAP=15^\circ$ gives $P=(\tan15^\circ,1)=(2-\sqrt3,1)$. Then \[CP^2=(\sqrt3)^2+1^2=4=CD^2,\qquad PD^2=(2-\sqrt3)^2+1=8-4\sqrt3=(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)^2 .\] Since $CP=CD$, the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $PD$ is its midpoint, so $\cos\angle CPD=\frac{PD/2}{CP}=\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}=\cos75^\circ$. Hence $\angle CPD=\boxed{75^\circ}$.

Problem 19

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · GCD Logic

For how many nonnegative integers $n$ does $2^n+3^n$ divide $4^n+9^n$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Find the divisor and dividend at a small value of $n$. Notice how $4^n$ and $9^n$ are related to the two summands in the divisor.
Hint 2: key idea
Expand $(2^n+3^n)^2$ to replace the dividend by a simpler expression for testing divisibility. The cross term has only powers of $2$ and $3$ as prime factors. (LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction)
Hint 3: approach
Subtract the square of the divisor and inspect the prime factors of the remaining term. As $n$ ranges over all nonnegative integers, decide when the divisor can share those primes and when it is too large to divide the term.
Common trap
A divisor that shares no prime factor with the leftover term can divide it only if the divisor equals $1$.
Show the answer

$1$

Show the solution – Complete the square

We have $4^n+9^n=(2^n+3^n)^2-2\cdot6^n$, so $2^n+3^n\mid4^n+9^n$ iff $2^n+3^n\mid2\cdot6^n$. For $n\ge1$, $2^n+3^n$ is odd and not divisible by $3$, so it is coprime to $2\cdot6^n$; a divisor of $2\cdot6^n$ coprime to it must be $1$, which is impossible as $2^n+3^n\ge5$. So no $n\ge1$ works, while $n=0$ works ($2\mid2$). The answer is $\boxed{1}$.

Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Geometric Reflection and Isometry

A bee and a flower are a distance of $\frac{14}{15}$ apart on the same side of a unit square, and the bee is a distance of $\frac{1}{150}$ from the nearest vertex of the square. The length of the shortest path that the bee can take to reach the flower that touches all four sides of the square at least once can be expressed as $\frac mn$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. Find $m+n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Place the bee and flower on the bottom side, with the bee near the left corner. List the sides that still have to be touched and sketch a path for one possible visiting order.
Hint 2: key idea
For a fixed visiting order, reflect the flower across the mirrors in reverse order, starting with the last side touched. Each reflection fixes the point of contact on its side, so a straight segment to the final image gives a lower bound on the path length. (LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the images of the flower for the two relative orders of the left and right sides, tracking what the top reflection does separately. For the closer image, trace the straight segment through the reflected copies and check that folding back hits all the required sides.
Common trap
Verify that the proposed straight segment crosses the required reflected sides before treating its length as attainable.
Show the answer

$49$

Show the solution – Unfolding by reflections

Place the square as $[0,1]^2$ with the bee at $B=\left(\frac1{150},0\right)$ and the flower at $F=\left(\frac{141}{150},0\right)$ on the bottom side (the bee is near the vertex $(0,0)$, and $\frac1{150}+\frac{14}{15}=\frac{141}{150}$). The path already touches the bottom side; it must also touch the left side $x=0$, the right side $x=1$ and the top $y=1$. Let $\rho_L,\rho_R,\rho_T$ be the reflections in these lines.

Lower bound. Suppose the path first touches the three sides in the order $s_1,s_2,s_3$, at points $P_1,P_2,P_3$. Since $P_3$ lies on $s_3$, $|P_2P_3|+|P_3F|=|P_2P_3|+|P_3\rho_{s_3}F|\ge|P_2\,\rho_{s_3}F|$; then, as $P_2\in s_2$, $|P_2\,\rho_{s_3}F|=|P_2\,\rho_{s_2}\rho_{s_3}F|$, and so on. Hence the length is at least $|B\,\rho_{s_1}\rho_{s_2}\rho_{s_3}F|$. The reflection $\rho_T$ changes only $y$, giving $y=2$; and $\rho_L\rho_R(x)=x-2$, $\rho_R\rho_L(x)=x+2$. So the image of $F$ is $\left(\frac{141}{150}-2,2\right)=\left(-\frac{159}{150},2\right)$ if $L$ comes before $R$, and $\left(\frac{441}{150},2\right)$ otherwise. The first is closer to $B$: \[\left|B\left(-\tfrac{159}{150},2\right)\right|=\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{160}{150}\right)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{\tfrac{256}{225}+\tfrac{900}{225}}=\tfrac{34}{15}.\]

The bound is attained. The segment from $B$ to $\left(-\frac{159}{150},2\right)$ crosses $x=0$ at height $\frac1{80}$, then $y=1$ at $x=-\frac{79}{150}$, then $x=-1$ at height $\frac{151}{80}$. Folding back gives the path $B\to\left(0,\frac1{80}\right)\to\left(\frac{79}{150},1\right)\to\left(1,\frac{9}{80}\right)\to F$, of length $\frac{34}{15}$, touching all four sides. Hence $m+n=34+15=\boxed{49}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

The polynomial $x^5-4x^4+2x^3-3x^2+x+1$ has $3$ real roots and $2$ pure imaginary roots. If the real roots are $a$, $b$, and $c$, find $abc-(3-a)(3-b)(3-c)$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Look at the coefficients of the polynomial. If one pure imaginary number is a root of a polynomial with real coefficients, what other root must appear with it?
Hint 2: key idea
Split off the quadratic factor belonging to the conjugate pair. The remaining monic cubic lets you use its constant term for one product and evaluate it at a specific input for the other. (LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
Try the simplest pure imaginary conjugate pair and use polynomial division to test the quadratic factor it gives. Write the resulting cubic in factored form using $a$, $b$, and $c$, then work out which evaluations give the two products.
Common trap
In a monic cubic the constant term is the negative of the product of the roots.
Show the answer

$4$

Show the solution – Split off the imaginary pair

The pure imaginary roots come in a conjugate pair $\pm ti$, so $x^2+t^2$ divides the polynomial. Indeed \[x^5-4x^4+2x^3-3x^2+x+1=(x^2+1)(x^3-4x^2+x+1),\] as expanding the right side shows. So $a,b,c$ are the roots of $Q(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+1$, whence $abc=-1$ and $(3-a)(3-b)(3-c)=Q(3)=27-36+3+1=-5$. The answer is $-1-(-5)=\boxed{4}$.
