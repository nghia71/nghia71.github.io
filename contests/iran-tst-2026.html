Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

A Shift operation on a positive integer is defined as deleting its first non-zero digit (from left to right) and placing it on its right side. For example, by applying three Shift operations on $13025$, we will get $5132$. Prove that for all $a,k\in\mathbb N$ with $\gcd(a,10)=1$, there exist $k$ pairwise distinct positive integers $n_1,n_2,\dots,n_k$ such that $\gcd(n_1,n_2,\dots,n_k)=a$ and for any two of them, one is achievable from the other one by a number of Shifts.

Show the solution

Solution (A repunit plus a moving multiple of a)

For $L\ge 1$ let $U_L=\underbrace{11\ldots1}_{L}=\dfrac{10^L-1}{9}$ (a “repunit”). For $k=1$ take $n_1=a$; from now on $k\ge 2$.

Step 1: one Shift of a number close to a repunit. Let $L\ge 3$ and let $x$ be an integer with $0\le x<10^{L-2}$. We claim that \[\text{Shift}(U_L+x)=U_L+10x .\] Indeed, $U_L=10^{L-1}+10^{L-2}+U_{L-2}$ and $0\le U_{L-2}+x<2\cdot 10^{L-2}$, so \[11\cdot 10^{L-2}\le U_L+x<13\cdot 10^{L-2}.\] Hence $n=U_L+x$ has exactly $L$ digits, its first digit is $1$ and its second digit is $1$ or $2$. A Shift deletes the leading $1$; what remains is $n-10^{L-1}$, which begins with the non-zero second digit, so no zeros disappear. Putting the digit $1$ at the right end gives \[10\,(n-10^{L-1})+1=10U_L+10x-(10^L-1)=10U_L+10x-9U_L=U_L+10x .\]

Step 2: the choice of $L$. Write $a=3^e a'$ with $e\ge 0$ and $3\nmid a'$, and let $d$ be the number of digits of $a$. Since $\gcd(9a,10)=1$, Euler's theorem gives $10^{\varphi(9a)}\equiv 1\pmod{9a}$. Choose $L$ to be a multiple of $3^{e+1}\varphi(9a)$ with $L\ge k+d+1$. Then:

  • $9a\mid 10^L-1=9U_L$, so $a\mid U_L$;

  • $3^{e+1}\mid U_L$. Indeed $U_{3m}=U_m\,(10^{2m}+10^m+1)$ and $10^{2m}+10^m+1\equiv 3\equiv 0\pmod 3$, so by induction $3^s\mid U_{3^s}$ for all $s\ge 0$; moreover $U_m\mid U_L$ whenever $m\mid L$, because $U_L=U_m\,(1+10^m+10^{2m}+\dots+10^{L-m})$. Now use $m=3^{e+1}$.

Step 3: the numbers. Put \[n_j=U_L+10^{j}a\qquad (j=0,1,\dots,k-1).\] They are pairwise distinct, since $n_0<n_1<\dots<n_{k-1}$. For $j\le k-2$ we have $10^ja<10^{k-2+d}\le 10^{L-3}<10^{L-2}$, so Step 1 (with $x=10^ja$) gives $\text{Shift}(n_j)=n_{j+1}$. Therefore, for $i<j$, the number $n_j$ is obtained from $n_i$ by $j-i$ Shifts.

Step 4: the greatest common divisor. Let $g=\gcd(n_0,\dots,n_{k-1})$. Each $n_j$ is divisible by $a$ (Step 2), so $a\mid g$. Also $g\mid n_1-n_0=9a$. Hence $g=a$, $3a$ or $9a$. If $g$ were $3a$ or $9a$, then $3^{e+1}\mid g\mid n_0=U_L+a$; as $3^{e+1}\mid U_L$, this would give $3^{e+1}\mid a$, which is false. So $g=a$, and the numbers $n_0,n_1,\dots,n_{k-1}$ have all the required properties.

Example. For $a=1$, $k=3$ we may take $L=6$: the numbers $111112\to111121\to111211$ are consecutive Shifts and their greatest common divisor is $1$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

A Triangular operation is defined on $n$-vertex trees as follows: Starting with a tree $T$, we first add an edge such that there will exist a cycle of length $3$. Then we delete a different edge from that cycle, to obtain a new tree. The new tree is the result of a Triangular operation on $T$. The figure shows a sample of Triangular operation.

Prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that for all integers $n>N$ and any two $n$-vertex trees $T_1,T_2$ we can achieve $T_2$ from $T_1$ by using at most $2n-1405$ Triangular operations.

Show the solution

Solution (Through a star or through a path)

Convention. Trees are considered up to isomorphism: the names of the vertices do not matter, and “achieving $T_2$ from $T_1$” means reaching a tree isomorphic to $T_2$. (The figure in the statement shows trees with unnamed vertices.)

The operation. If $u,v$ are two vertices of a tree with a common neighbour $w$, then adding the edge $uv$ creates the triangle $uvw$, and we may delete $uw$. So one operation replaces an edge $uw$ by $uv$, where $v$ is a neighbour of $w$; every Triangular operation has this form. It can be undone by one operation: in the new tree $u$ and $w$ have the common neighbour $v$, so we may add $uw$ and delete $uv$. Hence, if $T$ can be turned into $T'$ by $s$ operations, then $T'$ can be turned into $T$ by $s$ operations.

For a tree $T$ on $n\ge 3$ vertices let $\Delta(T)$ be its largest degree and $D(T)$ the number of edges of a longest path.

Lemma 1 (to a star). $T$ can be turned into a star in $n-1-\Delta(T)$ operations.

Proof. Let $c$ be a vertex of degree $\Delta(T)$. As long as the tree is not the star with centre $c$, some vertex $u$ is at distance $2$ from $c$; let $w$ be the middle vertex of the path $c,w,u$. Replace the edge $uw$ by $uc$ (figure, part (a)). This is a legal operation and the degree of $c$ grows by $1$. After $n-1-\Delta(T)$ operations $c$ has degree $n-1$. $\square$

Lemma 2 (to a path). $T$ can be turned into a path in at most $n-1-D(T)$ operations.

Proof. We keep a path $Q=p_0p_1\dots p_\ell$ of the current tree; at the start $Q$ is a longest path of $T$, so $\ell=D(T)$. Suppose $Q$ does not contain all vertices. As the tree is connected, some vertex $x\notin Q$ is adjacent to some $p_i$.

  • If $i=0$ or $i=\ell$, put $x$ at the end of $Q$; the path becomes longer and no operation is used.

  • If $0<i<\ell$, add the edge $xp_{i+1}$ (it closes the triangle $x p_i p_{i+1}$) and delete the edge $p_ip_{i+1}$ (figure, part (b)). The new tree contains the path $p_0\dots p_i\,x\,p_{i+1}\dots p_\ell$, which has one more vertex.

Each operation makes $Q$ longer by one vertex, and $Q$ starts with $D(T)+1$ vertices, so after at most $n-1-D(T)$ operations $Q$ contains all $n$ vertices. A tree that contains a path through all its vertices is that path. $\square$

Lemma 3. $n\le 1+\Delta+\Delta^2+\dots+\Delta^{D}$, where $\Delta=\Delta(T)$, $D=D(T)$.

Proof. Fix a vertex $v$. Every vertex is joined to $v$ by a path with at most $D$ edges, and the number of vertices at distance exactly $j$ from $v$ is at most $\Delta^j$ (each vertex at distance $j-1$ has at most $\Delta$ neighbours). $\square$

The bound. Let $N=1402^{1403}$ and $n>N$. Since $1+1402+\dots+1402^{1402}<1402^{1403}$, Lemma 3 shows that every tree $T$ on $n$ vertices has $\Delta(T)\ge 1403$ or $D(T)\ge 1403$. Let $T_1,T_2$ be trees on $n$ vertices.

  • One of them has a vertex of degree at least $1403$. The other tree has a vertex of degree at least $2$, so $\Delta(T_1)+\Delta(T_2)\ge 1405$. By Lemma 1, $T_1$ becomes a star in $n-1-\Delta(T_1)$ operations and $T_2$ becomes a star in $n-1-\Delta(T_2)$ operations. All stars on $n$ vertices are isomorphic, so, reversing the second sequence, we go from $T_1$ to the star and then to (a copy of) $T_2$ in \[2n-2-\Delta(T_1)-\Delta(T_2)\le 2n-1407\] operations.

  • Both have all degrees at most $1402$. Then $D(T_1),D(T_2)\ge1403$. By Lemma 2 each $T_i$ becomes a path in at most $n-1-D(T_i)$ operations; all paths on $n$ vertices are isomorphic, so, reversing the second sequence, we go from $T_1$ to $T_2$ in at most \[2n-2-D(T_1)-D(T_2)\le 2n-2808\] operations.

In both cases at most $2n-1405$ operations are used, which proves the statement with $N=1402^{1403}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

In triangle $ABC$, points $D,E,F$ lie on sides $BC,AC,AB$ such that \[BD=DE=EA\quad\text{and}\quad CD=DF=FA.\] Let $BE$ and $CF$ meet at $P$. Prove that the orthocenter of $\triangle PEF$ lies on $BC$.

Show the solution

Solution (Two circles centred at D and a homothety)

We assume, as usual, that $D,E,F$ are interior points of the sides. All products and ratios of segments lying on one line are taken with signs.

Step 1: a kite. Since $EA=ED$ and $FA=FD$, both $E$ and $F$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$; as $E\ne F$, the line $EF$ is this perpendicular bisector. Let $K$ be the midpoint of $AD$; it is the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $EF$.

Step 2: two circles with centre $D$. Let $\omega_B$ be the circle with centre $D$ and radius $DB=DE$, and $\omega_C$ the circle with centre $D$ and radius $DC=DF$. Let $B^*,C^*$ be the reflections of $B,C$ in $D$; then $BB^*$ is a diameter of $\omega_B$ and $CC^*$ is a diameter of $\omega_C$. As $E$ lies on $\omega_B$ and $F$ on $\omega_C$, \[EB^*\perp BE,\qquad FC^*\perp CF. \tag{1}\] Let $E',F'$ be the reflections of $E,F$ in $K$. They lie on the line $EF$, and since $DK\perp EF$ we get $DE'=DE$, $DF'=DF$: the line $EF$ meets $\omega_B$ at $E,E'$ and $\omega_C$ at $F,F'$. Moreover $AEDE'$ is a parallelogram (its diagonals $AD$ and $EE'$ bisect each other), so $DE'\parallel AE$, that is, \[DE'\parallel CE,\qquad\text{and in the same way}\qquad DF'\parallel BF. \tag{2}\]

Step 3: the case $EF\nparallel BC$. Let $T$ be the common point of the lines $EF$ and $BC$. Then $T\ne D$ ($D$ is not on $EF$), $T\ne E,F$ (these are not on $BC$) and $T\ne B,C$ (if, say, $T=C$, the line $EF$ would be the line $CE=CA$ and $F$ would be $A$, but $FA=CD>0$).

The power of $T$ with respect to $\omega_B$ and to $\omega_C$ gives \[TE\cdot TE'=TB\cdot TB^*,\qquad TF\cdot TF'=TC\cdot TC^*. \tag{3}\] By (2), the homothety with centre $T$ taking $C$ to $D$ takes $E$ to $E'$, and the homothety with centre $T$ taking $B$ to $D$ takes $F$ to $F'$: \[\frac{TE'}{TE}=\frac{TD}{TC},\qquad \frac{TF'}{TF}=\frac{TD}{TB}. \tag{4}\] Substituting (4) in (3): \[TE^2=\frac{TB\cdot TB^*\cdot TC}{TD},\qquad TF^2=\frac{TC\cdot TC^*\cdot TB}{TD},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad \frac{TE^2}{TF^2}=\frac{TB^*}{TC^*}. \tag{5}\] (In particular $TB^*,TC^*\ne0$.) Now let $H$ be the point of the line $BC$ with \[TH=TB^*\cdot\frac{TF}{TE}\ \overset{(5)}{=}\ TC^*\cdot\frac{TE}{TF}.\] The homothety with centre $T$ and ratio $TF/TE$ takes $E$ to $F$ and $B^*$ to $H$, so $FH\parallel EB^*$. The homothety with centre $T$ and ratio $TE/TF$ takes $F$ to $E$ and $C^*$ to $H$, so $EH\parallel FC^*$. By (1), \[FH\perp BE,\qquad EH\perp CF .\] Since $P$ lies on $BE$ and on $CF$, this says $FH\perp PE$ and $EH\perp PF$: the point $H$ lies on two altitudes of triangle $PEF$, so $H$ is its orthocenter, and $H$ lies on $BC$.

Step 4: the case $EF\parallel BC$. Then $AD\perp BC$ and $EF$ is the line through the midpoint $K$ of $AD$ parallel to $BC$, so $E$ and $F$ are the midpoints of $AC$ and $AB$, and $\overrightarrow{FE}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{BC}$. Let $H=B^*+\overrightarrow{EF}$. Because $B^*-C^*=(2D-B)-(2D-C)=\overrightarrow{BC}=2\overrightarrow{FE}$, we also have $H=C^*+\overrightarrow{FE}$. So $H$ lies on $BC$, $EFHB^*$ and $FEHC^*$ are parallelograms, $FH\parallel EB^*$ and $EH\parallel FC^*$, and we finish with (1) exactly as in Step 3.

In both cases the orthocenter of $\triangle PEF$ lies on $BC$.

Remark. Triangles with such points $D,E,F$ form a one-parameter family; the equilateral triangle (with the midpoints of the sides) belongs to it, and so does the right triangle with $AB=3$, $BC=4$, $CA=5$ (there $BD=2\sqrt6-3$).

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Positive reals $a<b$ and a positive integer $n$ are given. Initially Arash writes a polynomial of degree $n$ with real coefficients on the board and then plays the following game with Babak:

In each turn, Arash chooses a number $c$ in the interval $(a,b)$ and gives it to Babak. Then Babak chooses one of the $n+1$ coefficients of the polynomial currently written on the board, adds $c$ to that coefficient and replaces the polynomial written on the board with the new polynomial. Prove that Babak can play in such a way that, after finitely many moves, the polynomial on the board always has $n$ real roots, regardless of Arash's moves.

Show the solution

Solution (Keeping the coefficients in a fixed proportion)

Write the polynomial on the board as $c_0+c_1x+\dots+c_nx^n$. We describe a strategy for Babak and show that from some move on the polynomial on the board always has degree $n$ and $n$ distinct real roots.

The target shape. Let \[w_k=\frac{4^{-k^2}}{W}\quad(k=0,1,\dots,n),\qquad W=\sum_{i=0}^{n}4^{-i^2},\] so that $w_0>w_1>\dots>w_n>0$ and $w_0+\dots+w_n=1$.

Lemma 1. Let $S>0$ and suppose that $|c_k-w_kS|\le \frac14 w_kS$ for all $k=0,\dots,n$. Then $p(x)=c_0+c_1x+\dots+c_nx^n$ has degree $n$ and $n$ distinct real roots.

Proof. Write $c_k=(1+\varepsilon_k)\,w_kS$ with $|\varepsilon_k|\le\frac14$; in particular $c_n>0$. Let $x_j=-16^{\,j}$ for $j=0,1,\dots,n$. The $k$-th term of $p(x_j)$ is \[c_kx_j^k=(-1)^k(1+\varepsilon_k)\frac{S}{W}\,4^{-k^2+2jk}=(-1)^k(1+\varepsilon_k)\frac{S}{W}\,4^{\,j^2}\cdot4^{-(k-j)^2}.\] So the term with $k=j$ has sign $(-1)^j$ and absolute value at least $\frac34\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}$, while the other terms together have absolute value at most \[\frac54\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}\sum_{k\ne j}4^{-(k-j)^2}\le\frac54\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}\cdot2\Bigl(\frac14+\sum_{d\ge2}4^{-2d}\Bigr)=\frac54\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}\cdot2\Bigl(\frac14+\frac1{240}\Bigr)=\frac{61}{96}\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}<\frac34\cdot\frac SW4^{j^2}.\] Hence $p(x_j)$ has the sign $(-1)^j$ for $j=0,\dots,n$. The values of $p$ at $x_0>x_1>\dots>x_n$ alternate in sign, so $p$ has a root in each of the $n$ intervals $(x_{j+1},x_j)$. As $\deg p=n$, these are all its roots, and they are distinct and real. $\square$

Babak's strategy. Let $s_k$ be the total amount that Babak has added so far to the coefficient of $x^k$ (so $s_k=0$ at the start), and let $\rho_k=s_k/w_k$. When Babak receives a number $c$, he adds it to a coefficient for which $\rho_k$ is smallest.

Lemma 2. At every moment $\max_k\rho_k-\min_k\rho_k\le b/w_n$.

Proof. This is true at the start. Suppose it is true before a move, with $m=\min\rho_k$, $M=\max\rho_k$, and Babak adds $c\in(a,b)$ to the coefficient of $x^k$, where $\rho_k=m$. The new value is $\rho_k'=m+c/w_k<m+b/w_n$ (as $w_k\ge w_n$); the other $\rho_i$ do not change. The new minimum $m'$ satisfies $m'\ge m$. If the new maximum is still $M$, then $M-m'\le M-m\le b/w_n$; otherwise the new maximum is $\rho_k'$ and $\rho_k'-m'<m+b/w_n-m=b/w_n$. $\square$

Let $S=s_0+\dots+s_n$ be the total amount added so far. Then $S=\sum_k w_k\rho_k$ is a weighted mean of the numbers $\rho_k$ (the weights $w_k$ have sum $1$), so it lies between their minimum and maximum, and Lemma 2 gives $|\rho_k-S|\le b/w_n$, that is, \[|s_k-w_kS|\le\frac{b\,w_k}{w_n}\qquad\text{for all }k,\text{ at every moment.}\]

Conclusion. Let $c_k^{(0)}$ be the coefficients of Arash's initial polynomial; the current coefficients are $c_k=c_k^{(0)}+s_k$. Put \[S_0=4\max_{0\le k\le n}\Bigl(\frac{|c_k^{(0)}|}{w_k}+\frac{b}{w_n}\Bigr).\] Whenever $S\ge S_0$ we have, for every $k$, \[|c_k-w_kS|\le|c_k^{(0)}|+\frac{b\,w_k}{w_n}\le\frac14w_kS,\] and Lemma 1 applies. Each move increases $S$ by more than $a>0$, so after $t$ moves $S>at$. Therefore after every move number $t\ge S_0/a$ the polynomial on the board has degree $n$ and $n$ distinct real roots, whatever Arash does.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Two circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ intersect at points $A$ and $B$. A fixed point $C$ lies on $\omega_1$ and $P$ is a point varying on the line $AB$ and outside of the segment $AB$. Points $R$ and $S$ are the tangency points of the lines through $P$ tangent to $\omega_2$, and $Q$ is the second intersection of the line $CP$ with $\omega_1$. Let $O$ be the center of $\omega_2$. Prove that as $P$ varies, the circumcircle of $\triangle QRS$ always passes through a fixed point on the line $OC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion about R: two perpendicular mirrors)

Let $r$ be the radius of $\omega_2$. We assume $C\ne A,B$ (otherwise the line $CP$ is the line $AB$ for every $P$) and $C\ne O$ (so that the line $OC$ is defined). Then $C\notin\omega_2$, and we let $C^*$ be the inverse of $C$ in $\omega_2$: the point of the ray $OC$ with $OC\cdot OC^*=r^2$. It lies on the line $OC$ and does not depend on $P$. We prove that $C^*$ lies on the circle $QRS$.

Three inversions hidden in the figure. Since $P$ lies on the line $AB$ outside the segment $AB$, it is outside both circles and its powers are \[PR^2=PS^2=PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PQ .\] Let $\gamma$ be the circle with centre $P$ and radius $PR=PS$.

  • $\gamma\perp\omega_2$: at the common point $R$ the radius $PR$ of $\gamma$ is tangent to $\omega_2$.

  • $C$ and $Q$ are inverse in $\gamma$: they lie on one ray from $P$ and $PC\cdot PQ=PR^2$.

  • $C$ and $C^*$ are inverse in $\omega_2$.

Note that $Q\ne R,S$: a common point of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ is $A$ or $B$, and $Q\in\{A,B\}$ would force $C\in\{A,B\}$.

Inverting about $R$. Recall that an inversion maps circles and lines to circles and lines, preserves angles, and maps a pair of points inverse in a circle $\delta$ to a pair of points inverse in the image of $\delta$ (symmetric in it, if the image is a line).

Apply an inversion $\Psi$ with centre $R$ and write $X'=\Psi(X)$. The circles $\omega_2$ and $\gamma$ pass through $R$ and through $S$, and they are orthogonal; so their images are two perpendicular lines $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\gamma$ through $S'$. By the facts above, \[(C^*)'\ \text{is the reflection of }C'\text{ in }\ell_2,\qquad Q'\ \text{is the reflection of }C'\text{ in }\ell_\gamma .\] Reflecting in two perpendicular lines through $S'$ one after the other is the half-turn about $S'$. Hence $Q'$ is the image of $(C^*)'$ under the half-turn about $S'$: the points $Q'$, $S'$, $(C^*)'$ lie on one line $m$ (and $S'$ is the midpoint of $Q'(C^*)'$).

Now go back. If $R\notin m$, then $\Psi(m)$ is a circle through $R$; it contains $Q$, $S$ and $C^*$, so it is the circle $QRS$ and it passes through $C^*$. If $R\in m$, then $Q,R,S$ are collinear; this happens only when $PC\perp OC$, and for that position of $P$ there is no circle $QRS$, so it is excluded by the statement.

So for every position of $P$ the circle $QRS$ passes through the fixed point $C^*$ of the line $OC$.

Solution 2 (Powers of points and vectors)

We use vectors with origin $O$; write $p,c,q$ for the position vectors of $P,C,Q$ and $r$ for the radius of $\omega_2$. Assume $C\ne A,B,O$ (otherwise $Q$ or the line $OC$ is not defined), and let $C^*$ be the point with position vector $c^*=\dfrac{r^2}{|c|^2}\,c$ (the inverse of $C$ in $\omega_2$). It is a fixed point of the line $OC$; we show that it lies on the circle $\Sigma=(QRS)$.

Step 1: the position of $Q$. The powers of $P$ with respect to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are equal ($P$ is on the common chord line) and positive: $\overrightarrow{PC}\cdot\overrightarrow{PQ}=|p|^2-r^2$. Put \[k=|p|^2-r^2>0,\qquad m=|c-p|^2>0,\qquad t=\frac km,\qquad w=p\cdot(c-p).\] Since $Q$ is on the line $PC$, we get $q=p+t(c-p)$.

Step 2: powers with respect to $\Sigma$. For a point $Y$ with position vector $y$ let $\pi(Y)=|y|^2-r^2$ be its power with respect to $\omega_2$ and $\pi_\Sigma(Y)$ its power with respect to $\Sigma$. The difference $\pi_\Sigma(Y)-\pi(Y)$ is a function of the form $y\cdot u+\text{const}$, and it vanishes at $R$ and $S$. The function $g(Y)=y\cdot p-r^2$ also vanishes at $R$ and $S$ (if $\rho$ is the position vector of $R$, then $OR\perp RP$ means $\rho\cdot(p-\rho)=0$, i.e. $\rho\cdot p=|\rho|^2=r^2$), and it is not constant. Two such functions vanishing on the same line $RS$ are proportional, so there is a constant $\lambda$ with \[\pi_\Sigma(Y)=\pi(Y)+\lambda\,g(Y)\qquad\text{for all }Y .\] As $Q,R,S$ are not collinear, $Q$ is not on the line $RS$, so $g(Q)\ne0$ and $\lambda=-\pi(Q)/g(Q)$. Therefore \[\pi_\Sigma(C^*)=0\iff \pi(C^*)\,g(Q)=\pi(Q)\,g(C^*). \tag{$\ast$}\]

Step 3: the computation. Using $tm=k$ and $|c|^2=|p|^2+2w+m$: \begin{align*} g(Q)&=|p|^2-r^2+t\,p\cdot(c-p)=k+tw, & \pi(Q)&=k+2tw+t^2m=k+2tw+tk,\\ \pi(C^*)&=\frac{r^4}{|c|^2}-r^2=-\frac{r^2}{|c|^2}\,(k+2w+m), & g(C^*)&=\frac{r^2}{|c|^2}\,(c\cdot p-|c|^2)=-\frac{r^2}{|c|^2}\,(w+m). \end{align*} Multiplying by $m$ and using $tm=k$ once more, \[m\,(k+tw)=k(m+w),\qquad m\,(k+2tw+tk)=k(m+2w+k),\] so both sides of $(\ast)$, multiplied by $m$, are equal to $-\dfrac{r^2}{|c|^2}\,k\,(k+2w+m)(m+w)$. Thus $(\ast)$ holds, $\pi_\Sigma(C^*)=0$, and the circle $QRS$ passes through the fixed point $C^*$ of the line $OC$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry

A natural number $n$ is given. Ali is located at a point in the plane where $n$ points have been colored blue, and he can move according to the following rule. His goal is to escape from the blue points!

In each move, Ali can choose an equilateral triangle such that one of its vertices is at his current point and no blue point lies inside it, and then move to an arbitrary point on the perimeter of that triangle. (Note that points on the perimeter are not considered inside the triangle, and Ali's location may coincide with blue points as well.) We say he has escaped if he reaches a point with the following property: For every positive real number $C$, he can make a move to a point whose distance from all blue points is at least $C$.

Find the smallest value of $k$ in terms of $n$ such that, regardless of the initial placement of the $n$ blue points, Ali can escape in at most $k$ moves.

Show the answer

$\left\lfloor n/7\right\rfloor$

Show the solution

Solution (Peeling off the free points; nested heptagons)

Answer. $k=\boxed{\left\lfloor \dfrac n7\right\rfloor}$.

Throughout, a wedge at a point $y$ is an open angle of $60^\circ$ with vertex $y$, and $y$ is called free if some wedge at $y$ contains no blue point. (A blue point placed at $y$ itself is in no wedge at $y$.)

Lemma 1. Ali has escaped at $y$ if and only if $y$ is free.

Proof. Let $y$ be free, with an empty wedge $V$. For $s>0$ the equilateral triangle with vertex $y$, side $s$ and two sides along the sides of $V$ has no blue point inside. The midpoint $z$ of its third side lies on the bisector of $V$ at distance $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s$ from $y$, hence at distance $\frac{\sqrt3}{4}s$ from both sides of $V$; every blue point is outside $V$, so its distance from $z$ is at least $\frac{\sqrt3}4s$, which exceeds any given $C$ when $s$ is large.

Conversely, let Ali have escaped at $y$ (and $n\ge1$). For each $C=1,2,3,\dots$ there is an admissible triangle $\Delta_C$ with vertex $y$ having a point at distance at least $C$ from a fixed blue point; so the side of $\Delta_C$ tends to infinity. The direction of the bisector of $\Delta_C$ at $y$ has a convergent subsequence; let $V$ be the wedge at $y$ whose bisector has the limit direction. A blue point $b\in V$ would be strictly inside $\Delta_C$ for all large $C$ in the subsequence, which is impossible. So $V$ is empty. $\square$

Lemma 2. $y$ is free if and only if the directions from $y$ to the blue points different from $y$ leave a gap of at least $60^\circ$ (or there is no such blue point). In particular, if at most $6$ blue points are different from $y$, then $y$ is free.

Proof. A wedge is empty exactly when no direction of a blue point lies strictly between its sides. Six or fewer directions cut the full angle into at most six gaps, one of which is at least $60^\circ$. $\square$

The upper bound $k\le\lfloor n/7\rfloor$. Let $B$ be the set of blue points. For a finite set $S$ we say that a point $y$ is free with respect to $S$ if some wedge at $y$ contains no point of $S$; so “free” means free with respect to $B$, and a point that is free with respect to $S$ is free with respect to every subset of $S$. For a wedge $K$ with vertex at the origin, $y+K$ is the wedge at $y$ with sides parallel to those of $K$, and $u$ is the unit vector of the bisector of $K$.

Descent. Let $y+K$ contain a point of $S$, and let $b$ be a point of $S$ in $y+K$ with the smallest value of $u\cdot b$. Since $b-y\in K$ and $K$ is a convex cone, $b+K\subset y+K$; and $b\notin b+K$. So $b+K$ contains fewer points of $S$ than $y+K$. Repeating this from $b$, we arrive after finitely many steps at a point $b^*$ of $S$ in $y+K$ such that $b^*+K$ contains no point of $S$: the point $b^*$ is free with respect to $S$.

Lemma 3 (seven free points). If some point $x$ is not free with respect to $S$, then at least $7$ points of $S$ are free with respect to $S$.

Proof. Every wedge at $x$ contains a point of $S$, so by the Descent every wedge at $x$ contains a point of $S$ that is free with respect to $S$. Six or fewer points have directions from $x$ that leave a gap of at least $60^\circ$, and the wedge in that gap would contain none of them. So there are at least $7$ such points. $\square$

Layers. Put $B_0=B$. For $i\ge1$ let $L_i$ be the set of the points of $B_{i-1}$ that are free with respect to $B_{i-1}$, and $B_i=B_{i-1}\setminus L_i$. A non-empty finite set has a point that is free with respect to it (a vertex of its convex hull: the other points lie in a closed half-plane bounded by a line through it), so the sets $B_0,B_1,\dots$ decrease strictly until some $B_s$ is empty. For a point $y$ let the depth $d(y)$ be the least $j\ge0$ such that $y$ is free with respect to $B_j$. Thus $d(y)=0$ exactly when $y$ is free.

(1) $d(y)\le\lfloor n/7\rfloor$ for every point $y$. Let $d(y)=j$. For $i=1,\dots,j$ the point $y$ is not free with respect to $B_{i-1}$, so $|L_i|\ge7$ by Lemma 3. The sets $L_1,\dots,L_j$ are disjoint subsets of $B$, hence $7j\le n$.

(2) If $d(y)=j\ge1$, Ali can move from $y$ to a point $b$ with $d(b)\le j-1$ (figure, part (a)). There is a wedge $y+K$ containing no point of $B_j$; so all blue points in $y+K$ belong to $L_1\cup\dots\cup L_j$, and there is at least one of them, because $y$ is not free. Among the blue points in $y+K$ let $b$ be one with the smallest value of $u\cdot b$. The triangle cut from $y+K$ by the line through $b$ perpendicular to $u$ is equilateral with vertex $y$; a blue point strictly inside it would be a blue point of $y+K$ with a smaller value of $u\cdot{}$, so there is none. Ali moves to $b$, which is on the perimeter of this triangle. Now $b\in L_i$ for some $i\le j$, which means that $b$ is free with respect to $B_{i-1}$; hence $d(b)\le i-1\le j-1$.

By (2), starting from $x$ Ali reaches a point of depth $0$, that is, a free point, after at most $d(x)$ moves, and $d(x)\le\lfloor n/7\rfloor$ by (1). By Lemma 1 he has then escaped. Hence $k\le\lfloor n/7\rfloor$.

The lower bound $k\ge\lfloor n/7\rfloor$. First note that adding blue points never helps Ali (every admissible triangle stays admissible, and every free point stays free, when blue points are removed). So it is enough to treat $n=7t$, $t\ge1$, and to place the remaining points anywhere.

Let $r_i=100^{\,i}$ and colour blue the $7t$ vertices of the regular heptagons \[r_i\,(\cos\tfrac{2\pi j}{7},\ \sin\tfrac{2\pi j}{7}),\qquad j=0,\dots,6,\quad i=1,\dots,t ,\] with centre $O$, where Ali starts. Put $\rho_0=0$ and $\rho_i=3r_i$.

Claim A. If $0\le i\le t-1$ and $|Oy|\le\rho_i$, then every wedge at $y$ contains a vertex of the $(i+1)$-st heptagon; in particular $y$ is not free. Seen from $O$ the vertices of this heptagon have directions $\varphi_j=j\cdot\frac{360^\circ}{7}$. Seen from $y$, the direction of a vertex $P$ differs from $\varphi_j$ by the angle $\angle OPy\le\arcsin\frac{|Oy|}{|OP|}\le\arcsin 0.03<1.8^\circ$. Let the wedge consist of the directions in $(\alpha,\alpha+60^\circ)$. The interval $[\alpha+1.8^\circ,\alpha+58.2^\circ]$ has length $56.4^\circ>\frac{360^\circ}{7}$, so it contains some $\varphi_j$, and then the direction from $y$ to the corresponding vertex lies in $(\alpha,\alpha+60^\circ)$.

Claim B. If $1\le i\le t$ and $|Oy|\le\rho_{i-1}$, then every move from $y$ ends at a point $y'$ with $|Oy'|\le\rho_i$. Let the move use a triangle with vertex $y$ and side $s$, filling the wedge $V$ at $y$ up to distance $\frac{\sqrt3}2s$ along the bisector. By Claim A there is a vertex $P$ of the $i$-th heptagon in $V$, and $|yP|\le r_i+\rho_{i-1}\le1.03\,r_i$. Since $P$ is not strictly inside the triangle, $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s\le1.03\,r_i$, so $s<1.2\,r_i$ and $|Oy'|\le|Oy|+s<0.03r_i+1.2r_i<\rho_i$.

By Claim B and induction, after $i\le t$ moves Ali is within distance $\rho_i$ of $O$; by Claim A his position after $i\le t-1$ moves is not free. So he needs at least $t$ moves, and $k\ge\lfloor n/7\rfloor$.

The two bounds give $k=\boxed{\left\lfloor \dfrac n7\right\rfloor}$.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Given natural numbers $n,k$, Pink Panther and Mr. White play on an $n\times n$ board in turns. Initially, all cells are white. Pink Panther starts. In each turn, Pink Panther chooses a row or column and paints all its cells pink (regardless of whether they were white or pink). In each turn, Mr. White chooses $n-1$ consecutive cells on a row or column and paints them white.

If at any stage after Mr. White's turn, at least $k$ cells are pink, Pink Panther wins. Otherwise, the game continues. Find the largest value of $k$ in terms of $n$ for which Pink Panther can be certain to win in a finite number of turns, regardless of Mr. White's play.

Show the answer

$2n-2$ for $n\ge 2$ (and $1$ for $n=1$)

Show the solution

Solution (An invariant for Mr. White, a two-phase attack for Pink Panther)

Answer. For $n\ge2$ the largest $k$ is $\boxed{2n-2}$. (For $n=1$ Mr. White paints $n-1=0$ cells, the only cell stays pink, and the largest $k$ is $1$.)

Let $n\ge2$. The cell in row $i$ and column $j$ is $(i,j)$; the ends of a row or column are its first and last cells. $P$ always denotes the number of pink cells after a turn of Mr. White. Note that when $n-1$ consecutive cells of a row or column are painted white, exactly one cell of that line, one of its two ends, is left untouched.

Part 1: Mr. White can keep $P\le 2n-2$ for ever. Call a colouring tame if

  • (a) every pink cell lies on the border of the board;

  • (b) in each row at most one of the two ends is pink, and in each column at most one of the two ends is pink.

A tame colouring has at most $2n-2$ pink cells: the border cells that are not corners form $2(n-2)$ pairs (the two ends of a row $2\le i\le n-1$, or of a column $2\le j\le n-1$), each with at most one pink cell, and by (b) no two adjacent corners are pink, so at most $2$ corners are pink (figure).

The empty board is tame. Suppose the colouring is tame and Pink Panther paints a row $i$ (columns are treated in the same way). Mr. White whitens all cells of row $i$ except one end $e$, chosen as follows.

  • If one end of row $i$ was pink before Pink Panther's turn, $e$ is that end.

  • If no end of row $i$ was pink and $2\le i\le n-1$, $e$ is any end.

  • If no end of row $i$ was pink and $i\in\{1,n\}$, let $i'$ be the other of the rows $1,n$. By (b) at most one end of row $i'$ is pink, so one of the columns $1,n$ has no pink cell in row $i'$; $e$ is the end of row $i$ in that column.

The new colouring is the old one with all pink cells of row $i$ other than $e$ removed and $e$ made pink. It is tame: $e$ is a border cell; row $i$ has exactly one pink end; and if $e$ is a corner, the other end of its column is not pink (in the first case because the old colouring was tame, in the third case by the choice of $e$). So Mr. White keeps the colouring tame, $P\le2n-2$ after each of his turns, and Pink Panther cannot win when $k\ge2n-1$.

Part 2: Pink Panther can reach $P\ge 2n-2$. We use one simple fact.

Fact. If Pink Panther paints a line with no pink cell, then $P$ grows by at least $1$: he adds $n$ pink cells and Mr. White removes at most $n-1$. The other lines parallel to it that had no pink cell still have none, because Mr. White only whitens cells.

Phase 1. Pink Panther paints rows $1,2,\dots,n$, in this order (he stops as soon as $P\ge2n-2$). Let $E_t$ be Mr. White's reply after row $t$ is painted; it is a row reply or a column reply, according to the line containing the whitened cells. Let $y$ be the number of column replies among $E_1,\dots,E_n$. After Phase 1 let $a_i$ be the number of pink cells in row $i$, let $w_r$ be the number of rows and $w_c$ the number of columns without pink cells. Then \[P+w_r=\sum_{i}a_i+\#\{i:a_i=0\}=n+\Sigma,\qquad \Sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(a_i-1,0).\]

Claim. After Phase 1, $P+w_r\ge2n-2$ or $P+w_c\ge2n-2$.

Row $i$ is completely pink just after turn $i$, and later its cells are changed only by the replies $E_i,\dots,E_n$: a row reply inside row $i$ whitens $n-1$ of its cells, a row reply in another row none, a column reply at most one. Call row $i$ untouched if none of $E_i,\dots,E_n$ is a row reply inside row $i$. For an untouched row, \[a_i\ \ge\ n-\#\{\text{column replies among }E_i,\dots,E_n\}. \tag{1}\] Different touched rows are touched by different row replies, so at most $n-y$ rows are touched and at least $y$ rows are untouched.

  • $y=0$. If some row is untouched, (1) gives $a_i=n$ and $\Sigma\ge n-1$. Otherwise all $n$ rows are touched; there are only $n$ replies, so each row receives exactly one reply, after it was painted. Then each row is left with exactly one pink cell, at one of its ends: $P=n$, all pink cells are in columns $1$ and $n$, so $w_c\ge n-2$ and $P+w_c\ge2n-2$.

  • $1\le y\le n-2$. At least $y$ rows are untouched, and by (1) each of them has $a_i\ge n-y$. So $\Sigma\ge y(n-y-1)=(n-2)+(y-1)(n-2-y)\ge n-2$.

  • $y=n-1$. There is one row reply, $E_{t_0}$, inside a row $i_0$. If row $n$ is untouched, (1) gives $a_n\ge n-1$ (only $E_n$ comes after row $n$ is painted), so $\Sigma\ge n-2$. Otherwise $i_0=n$ and $t_0=n$; then every row $i\le n-1$ is untouched and the replies $E_i,\dots,E_{n-1}$ are the only column replies after it is painted, so $a_i\ge n-(n-i)=i$ and $\Sigma\ge\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i-1)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}\ge n-2$.

  • $y=n$. All rows are untouched and $a_i\ge n-(n-i+1)=i-1$, so $\Sigma\ge\sum_{i=2}^{n}(i-2)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}2\ge n-2$.

(For $n=2$ the inequality $\Sigma\ge n-2=0$ is trivial.) In all cases except the second half of the first one $P+w_r=n+\Sigma\ge2n-2$, and the claim is proved.

Phase 2. If $P+w_r\ge2n-2$, Pink Panther paints the $w_r$ rows without pink cells, one after another; otherwise he paints the $w_c$ columns without pink cells. By the Fact, each of these turns increases $P$ by at least $1$ and the lines still waiting stay without pink cells. So at the end $P\ge2n-2$ after a turn of Mr. White, and Pink Panther wins for $k=2n-2$ after at most $2n$ turns.

Together with Part 1, the largest $k$ is $\boxed{2n-2}$ for every $n\ge2$.

Problem 8

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

A positive integer $N$ and positive reals $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N$ are given. For indices larger than $N$, we define the sequence $(a_i)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ inductively as follows:

If for $m\ge N$, the numbers $a_1,\dots,a_m$ are defined, we consider all of the $\frac{m(m+1)}{2}$ numbers of the form $a_i+a_j$ with $1\le i\le j\le m$ in decreasing order and denote them by $t_1\ge t_2\ge\dots\ge t_{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}$. Now we define $a_{m+1}=t_m$. Prove that for all reals $c$, there exists a positive integer $M$ such that for all $n>M$ we have $a_{n+1}>a_n+c$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting the sums in a short interval)

For $m\ge N$ let $t_1^{(m)}\ge t_2^{(m)}\ge\dots$ be the ordered sums $a_i+a_j$ $(1\le i\le j\le m)$, so that $a_{m+1}=t_m^{(m)}$. Each sum belongs to a pair $(i,j)$ with $i\le j$; different pairs are counted separately even if the sums are equal. Let $\mu=\min(a_1,\dots,a_N)>0$.

Step 1: the sequence increases by at least $\mu$ after the $N$-th term. Let $m\ge N$, and let $a_r$ be a largest and $a_s$ a smallest of $a_1,\dots,a_m$. The $m$ pairs $\{r,j\}$, $j=1,\dots,m$, have sums $a_r+a_j\ge a_r+a_s$, so \[a_{m+1}=t^{(m)}_m\ \ge\ \max(a_1,\dots,a_m)+\min(a_1,\dots,a_m).\] By induction $\min(a_1,\dots,a_m)=\mu$ for all $m\ge N$ and \[a_{m+1}\ge a_m+\mu\quad(m\ge N),\qquad a_{N+1}>a_i\quad(i\le N).\] So $a_{N+1}<a_{N+2}<\dots$, these terms are larger than $a_1,\dots,a_N$, consecutive ones differ by at least $\mu$, and $a_m\to\infty$. Consequently:

  • (i) for $m>N$ and $1\le j\le m-N$, the $j$-th largest of $a_1,\dots,a_m$ is $a_{m-j+1}$;

  • (ii) every interval of length $c>0$ contains at most $K=N+\frac c\mu+1$ terms of the sequence.

Step 2: the new term is at least twice a fairly large old term. Let $m>N$ and let $k$ be a positive integer with $\frac{k(k+1)}2\ge m$ and $k\le m-N$. The $k$ largest of $a_1,\dots,a_m$ are $a_{m-k+1},\dots,a_m$, and the $\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\ge m$ pairs formed by them have sums at least $2a_{m-k+1}$. Hence \[a_{m+1}\ge 2a_{m-k+1},\qquad\text{so at most $k$ of the terms $a_1,\dots,a_m$ are }\ge\tfrac12a_{m+1}. \tag{1}\] We use this with $k=\lceil\sqrt{2m}\,\rceil$, which satisfies both conditions when $m$ is large.

Step 3: few sums lie in a short interval just above $a_{m+1}$. Fix $c>0$ (for $c\le0$ the statement follows from Step 1). Let $I=[a_{m+1},a_{m+1}+c]$. If $a_i+a_j\in I$, then one of the two terms, say $a_j$, is at least $\frac12a_{m+1}$; by (1) there are at most $k$ choices for $j$, and for each of them $a_i$ lies in the interval $[a_{m+1}-a_j,\ a_{m+1}-a_j+c]$ of length $c$, so by (ii) there are at most $K$ choices for $i$. Thus \[\#\{(i,j):\ i\le j\le m,\ a_i+a_j\in I\}\ \le\ K\lceil\sqrt{2m}\,\rceil. \tag{2}\]

Step 4: conclusion. Let $s$ be the number of indices $i$ with $a_i\le c$; it is finite because $a_m\to\infty$. Take $m$ so large that $m>N$, $\lceil\sqrt{2m}\,\rceil\le m-N$, all terms $a_i\le c$ have $i\le m$, and \[m-s>K\lceil\sqrt{2m}\,\rceil .\] We claim that $t^{(m)}_{s+1}>a_{m+1}+c$. Otherwise the $m-s$ sums $t^{(m)}_{s+1}\ge\dots\ge t^{(m)}_{m}=a_{m+1}$ all lie in $I$, contradicting (2).

Now look at the sums at the next stage, formed from $a_1,\dots,a_{m+1}$. More than $a_{m+1}+c$ are:

  • the $s+1$ old sums $t^{(m)}_1,\dots,t^{(m)}_{s+1}$;

  • the new sums $a_i+a_{m+1}$ with $a_i>c$, $i\le m+1$; there are $m+1-s$ of them.

These are $m+2$ different pairs, so the $(m+1)$-st largest sum exceeds $a_{m+1}+c$: \[a_{m+2}=t^{(m+1)}_{m+1}>a_{m+1}+c .\] This holds for all sufficiently large $m$, which is the statement.

Problem 9

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

For a prime $p>7$ we define: \[S_p=\{1,2,3,\dots,p(p-1)\}.\] Find all functions $f:S_p\to S_p$ such that for all $c\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}$ there exists $a\in S_p$ such that $f(a)\equiv c\pmod p$, and also for all $m,n\in S_p$ we have: \[\bigl(mf(n)\bigr)^{f(n+(p-1)m)}\equiv f(mn)^n\pmod p.\] Note: The input of $f$ is always considered $\pmod{p(p-1)}$. For example if $p=11$, then $f(120)=f(10)$.

Show the answer

$f(x)\equiv cx\pmod p$ with a constant $c\not\equiv0$; $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$ for $p\nmid x$; for $p\mid x$: $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$ if $c$ is a non-residue, $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{\frac{p-1}{2}}$ if $c$ is a quadratic residue

Show the solution

Solution (Orders in the multiplicative group)

Answer. Each element of $S_p$ is determined by its residues modulo $p$ and modulo $p-1$. The solutions are the functions given by a constant $c\in\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}$ and the rules

  • $f(x)\equiv cx\pmod p$ for all $x\in S_p$;

  • $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$ for all $x$ not divisible by $p$;

  • for the $p-1$ multiples $x$ of $p$: $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$ if $c$ is a quadratic non-residue modulo $p$, and $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{\frac{p-1}{2}}$ if $c$ is a quadratic residue (so there are two possible values for each such $x$, chosen independently).

For example $f(x)=x$ is the solution with $c=1$ and the first choice everywhere.

Throughout, congruences without a modulus are modulo $p$; $\mathbb F_p^*$ is the group of non-zero residues, which is cyclic of order $p-1$; $\langle z\rangle$ is the subgroup generated by $z$. All exponents in the equation are positive integers, so a base divisible by $p$ gives $0$. Put $M=p(p-1)$.

Step 1: the residues of $f$ modulo $p$.

(a) $p\mid x\Rightarrow p\mid f(x)$. Take $m=x$, $n=1$: the left side is a positive power of $x f(1)\equiv0$, so $f(x)^1\equiv0$.

(b) $f(m)$ modulo $p$ depends only on $m$ modulo $p$. With $n=1$ the equation reads $(m f(1))^{f(1+(p-1)m)}\equiv f(m)$. If $m\equiv m'$, then $(p-1)m\equiv(p-1)m'\pmod M$, so the left sides for $m$ and $m'$ are congruent.

So there is a map $G:\mathbb F_p\to\mathbb F_p$ with $f(x)\equiv G(x)$, and $G(0)=0$. Let $c=G(1)$.

(c) $c\ne0$, and $G(m)\in\langle cm\rangle$ for $m\ne0$. The equation with $n=1$ says that $G(m)$ is a power of $mc$. If $c=0$, then $G\equiv0$, contradicting the first condition.

(d) $G$ permutes $\mathbb F_p^*$, and $\langle G(m)\rangle=\langle cm\rangle$. By the first condition every non-zero residue is a value of $G$, and by (a),(c) it is a value at a non-zero argument; so $G$ maps $\mathbb F_p^*$ onto itself, bijectively. For $d\mid p-1$ let $A_d=\{z:z^d=1\}$ and $B_d=\{m:(cm)^d=1\}$; both have $d$ elements. By (c), $G(B_d)\subset A_d$, and since $G$ is injective, $G(B_d)=A_d$ and $G(m)\in A_d\iff m\in B_d$. Thus $G(m)$ and $cm$ have the same order, and as $G(m)\in\langle cm\rangle$, they generate the same subgroup.

(e) $G(x)=cx$. Let $m,n$ be non-zero residues. Choose the integer $n$ in its residue class modulo $p$ so that $n\equiv1\pmod{p-1}$. Then $G(mn)^n=G(mn)$ by Fermat's theorem, and the equation shows \[G(mn)\in\langle m\,G(n)\rangle\qquad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb F_p^* .\] Write $G(n)=c\,n\,u(n)$. For $z\in\mathbb F_p^*$ and $m=z/n$ we get, using (d), \[cz\in\langle cz\rangle=\langle G(z)\rangle\subset\langle c z\,u(n)\rangle .\] Fix $n$ and choose $z$ with $cz=u(n)^{-1}$. Then $cz\in\langle1\rangle$, so $cz=1$ and $u(n)=1$. Hence $G(n)=cn$ for all $n$.

Step 2: the residues of $f$ modulo $p-1$. Now $f(x)\equiv cx$ for all $x$, with $c\not\equiv0$. If $p\mid mn$, both sides of the equation are $\equiv0$ (if $p\mid m$ the base on the left is $0$; if $p\mid n$ then $f(n)\equiv0$; and $f(mn)\equiv0$). So only pairs with $p\nmid mn$ matter. For them let $x\in S_p$ be the element congruent to $n+(p-1)m$ modulo $M$; then \[x\equiv n\pmod{p-1},\qquad x\equiv n-m\pmod p,\] and the equation becomes $(cmn)^{f(x)}\equiv(cmn)^{n}\equiv(cmn)^x$, i.e. \[\operatorname{ord}(cmn)\ \big|\ f(x)-x .\] Conversely, for $x\in S_p$ and non-zero residues $\mu,\nu$ with $\nu-\mu\equiv x$, there are $m,n\in S_p$ with $m\equiv\mu$, $n\equiv\nu\pmod p$ and $n\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$, and then $n+(p-1)m\equiv x\pmod M$. So, for a function with $f(x)\equiv cx$, the equation is equivalent to: \[\text{for every }x\in S_p:\qquad f(x)\equiv x\pmod{|D_\delta|},\quad \delta=x\bmod p, \tag{$\ast$}\] where $D_\delta$ is the subgroup of $\mathbb F_p^*$ generated by all $c\mu(\mu+\delta)$ with $\mu\ne0,-\delta$ (a number is divisible by the orders of all generators of a cyclic group exactly when it is divisible by the order of the group).

The case $\delta=0$. The generators are $c\mu^2$. They include $c$, and their quotients give all squares. So $D_0=\mathbb F_p^*$ if $c$ is a non-residue, and $D_0$ is the group of squares, of order $\frac{p-1}2$, if $c$ is a residue.

The case $\delta\ne0$: $D_\delta=\mathbb F_p^*$. With $\mu=\delta u$ the generators are $c\delta^2\,u(u+1)$, $u\ne0,-1$. The map $u\mapsto u(u+1)$ takes each value at most twice and $u$ takes $p-2$ values, so there are at least $\frac{p-1}{2}$ different generators and $|D_\delta|\ge\frac{p-1}2$. If $D_\delta\ne\mathbb F_p^*$, then $D_\delta$ is the subgroup of index $2$, the squares, and $c\,u(u+1)$ is a square for all $u\ne0,-1$. But $u(u+1)=u^2(1+u^{-1})$, and when $u$ runs through the residues different from $0,-1$, the number $v=1+u^{-1}$ runs through all residues different from $1,0$; among these there are $\frac{p-3}{2}\ge1$ squares and $\frac{p-1}2$ non-squares. So $c\,u(u+1)$ is a square for some $u$ and a non-square for others, a contradiction.

So $(\ast)$ says exactly: $f(x)\equiv x\pmod{p-1}$ when $p\nmid x$; and when $p\mid x$, $f(x)\equiv x$ modulo $p-1$ (if $c$ is a non-residue) or modulo $\frac{p-1}2$ (if $c$ is a residue).

Step 3: check. We proved that every solution has the stated form. Conversely, a function of the stated form satisfies $f(x)\equiv cx$, so it takes all residues modulo $p$, and by Step 2 the equation is equivalent to $(\ast)$, which holds. Hence these are exactly the solutions; there are $\frac{p-1}{2}\bigl(1+2^{\,p-1}\bigr)$ of them.

Problem 10

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral with circumcenter $O$. Let $P$ be the intersection of $AC$ and $BD$. Points $X$ and $Y$ are in the plane such that $PX\parallel AB$ and $PY\parallel CD$. It is also known that $AC$ bisects $\angle XCD$ and $BD$ bisects $\angle ABY$. Prove that $XY\perp OP$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Similar triangles and projections of O)

$ABCD$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral, so $P$ is inside the circle; let $\kappa=PA\cdot PC=PB\cdot PD$. If $O=P$ there is nothing to prove, so let $O\ne P$.

Step 1: where $X$ and $Y$ are. The ray $CX$ is the reflection of the ray $CD$ in the line $AC$, so $X$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$; hence $X$ is on the same side of $AC$ as $B$. Since $PX\parallel AB$ and $P,A$ lie on $AC$, the vectors $\overrightarrow{PX}$ and $\overrightarrow{AB}$ have the same direction: \[\overrightarrow{PX}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{AB},\qquad\lambda=\frac{PX}{AB}>0. \tag{1}\] In particular $\angle XPC=\angle BAC$. So in triangles $XPC$ and $PDC$ \[\angle XPC=\angle BAC=\angle BDC=\angle PDC,\qquad\angle XCP=\angle PCD,\] the triangles are similar ($X\to P$, $P\to D$, $C\to C$), and \[\frac{PX}{DP}=\frac{PC}{DC},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \lambda=\frac{PC\cdot PD}{AB\cdot CD}. \tag{2}\] In the same way ($Y$ and $C$ are on the same side of $BD$, and $\triangle YPB\sim\triangle PAB$) \[\overrightarrow{PY}=\mu\,\overrightarrow{DC},\qquad\mu=\frac{PY}{CD}=\frac{PA\cdot PB}{AB\cdot CD}. \tag{3}\]

Step 2: projections of $O$ on the diagonals. The projection of $O$ on the chord $AC$ is its midpoint $M$. Measuring along the line $AC$ in the direction from $P$ to $C$, the points $A$, $C$, $M$ have coordinates $-PA$, $PC$, $\frac{PC-PA}{2}$. Hence \[\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PC}=PC\cdot\frac{PC-PA}2=\frac{PC^2-\kappa}{2},\qquad\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PA}=(-PA)\cdot\frac{PC-PA}{2}=\frac{PA^2-\kappa}{2},\] and in the same way $\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PB}=\frac{PB^2-\kappa}2$, $\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PD}=\frac{PD^2-\kappa}2$.

Step 3: the computation. By (1), (2) and Step 2, \[\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PX}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\bigl(\overrightarrow{PB}-\overrightarrow{PA}\bigr)=\frac{PC\cdot PD\,(PB^2-PA^2)}{2\,AB\cdot CD},\] and by (3) \[\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PY}=\mu\,\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\bigl(\overrightarrow{PC}-\overrightarrow{PD}\bigr)=\frac{PA\cdot PB\,(PC^2-PD^2)}{2\,AB\cdot CD}.\] Dividing both numerators by $PA\cdot PB\cdot PC\cdot PD$ we have to compare \[\frac{PB}{PA}-\frac{PA}{PB}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{PC}{PD}-\frac{PD}{PC},\] and these are equal because $\frac{PB}{PA}=\frac{PC}{PD}$ (from $PA\cdot PC=PB\cdot PD$). Therefore \[\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{XY}=\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PY}-\overrightarrow{PO}\cdot\overrightarrow{PX}=0,\] that is, $XY\perp OP$.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers with origin P)

We use complex numbers with origin $P$ and the real axis along $AC$: \[A=-a,\quad C=c,\quad B=-bu,\quad D=du,\qquad a,b,c,d>0,\ |u|=1,\ u\ne\pm1,\qquad ac=bd\] (the last equality is the power of the point $P$). For complex numbers $z,w$ the scalar product of the corresponding vectors is $\operatorname{Re}(z\overline w)$.

Step 1: the circumcenter. Let $o$ be the complex number of $O$. From $|A-o|=|C-o|$ we get $a^2+2a\operatorname{Re}o=c^2-2c\operatorname{Re}o$, and from $|B-o|=|D-o|$ similarly; hence \[2\operatorname{Re}(o)=c-a,\qquad 2\operatorname{Re}(u\,\overline o)=d-b. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: the points $X$ and $Y$. We claim that \[X=\frac{CD}{D-C}=\frac{cd\,u}{du-c},\qquad Y=\frac{AB}{A-B}=\frac{ab\,u}{bu-a}.\] The line through $P$ parallel to $AB$ and the line obtained by reflecting $CD$ in $AC$ have at most one common point (the second line passes through $C$; if the two lines coincided, the line $PC=AC$ would be parallel to $AB$), so $X$ is determined by the two conditions. Check them for $X=\frac{CD}{D-C}$:

  • $X-C=\dfrac{C^2}{D-C}$, so $\dfrac{X-C}{P-C}=\dfrac{P-C}{D-C}$: the rotation about $C$ taking the ray $CD$ to the ray $CP$ takes the ray $CP$ to the ray $CX$. So $CA$ bisects $\angle XCD$.

  • $B-A=a-bu=\frac{bd}{c}-bu=\frac bc\,(d-cu)=\frac bc\,u\,\overline{(du-c)}$, and $X=\dfrac{cd\,u\,\overline{(du-c)}}{|du-c|^2}$. So $X=\dfrac{c^2d}{b\,|du-c|^2}\,(B-A)$ is a real multiple of $B-A$: $PX\parallel AB$.

The formula for $Y$ follows by exchanging the roles $(A,B,C,D,X)\to(D,C,B,A,Y)$.

Step 3: the scalar products. Write $CD^2=|du-c|^2$ and $AB^2=|bu-a|^2$. By (1), \[\operatorname{Re}(X\overline o)=\frac{cd}{CD^2}\operatorname{Re}\bigl((d-cu)\,\overline o\bigr)=\frac{cd}{CD^2}\cdot\frac{d(c-a)-c(d-b)}{2}=\frac{cd\,(bc-ad)}{2\,CD^2},\] \[\operatorname{Re}(Y\overline o)=\frac{ab}{AB^2}\operatorname{Re}\bigl((b-au)\,\overline o\bigr)=\frac{ab}{AB^2}\cdot\frac{b(c-a)-a(d-b)}{2}=\frac{ab\,(bc-ad)}{2\,AB^2}.\] Finally $bu-a=bu-\frac{bd}c=-\frac bc\,(d-cu)$ and $|d-cu|=|d\overline u-c|=|du-c|$ give $AB^2=\frac{b^2}{c^2}\,CD^2$, hence $\dfrac{ab}{AB^2}=\dfrac{ac^2}{b\,CD^2}=\dfrac{cd}{CD^2}$ (as $ac=bd$). So $\operatorname{Re}(X\overline o)=\operatorname{Re}(Y\overline o)$, i.e. $\operatorname{Re}\bigl((X-Y)\overline o\bigr)=0$: the vector $\overrightarrow{YX}$ is perpendicular to $\overrightarrow{PO}$.

Problem 11

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

For every positive integer $n$ with factorization $n=p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}$ (where $p_1,p_2,\dots,p_k$ are its distinct prime divisors and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_k\in\mathbb N$) we define \[f(n)=\alpha_1\times\alpha_2\times\dots\times\alpha_k.\] We also define $f(n)=f(-n)$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$ and $f(1)=f(0)=1$. Find all polynomials $Q\in\mathbb Z[x]$ such that there exist positive integers $N$ and $a>1$ such that for all integers $n>N$ we have: \[f(a^n+1)\mid f(Q(n)).\]

Show the answer

no such polynomial exists

Show the solution

Solution (A large prime exponent that Q cannot match)

Answer: there is no such polynomial. We fix a polynomial $Q\in\mathbb Z[x]$ and an integer $a>1$ and find arbitrarily large $n$ with $f(a^n+1)\nmid f(Q(n))$. Here $v_q(m)$ denotes the exponent of the prime $q$ in $m$.

If $Q$ is not the zero polynomial, write $Q(x)=x^k Q_1(x)$ with $k\ge0$ and $Q_1(0)\ne0$, let $D=\deg Q$ and let $C$ be the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of $Q$, so that $|Q(n)|\le C\,n^{D}$ for $n\ge1$.

Step 1: a prime $p$ and the exponent of $p$ in $a^n+1$. The numbers $a^{2^i}+1$ $(i\ge1)$ are greater than $2$ and not divisible by $4$, so each has an odd prime factor; and two of them have no common odd prime factor (if $q\mid a^{2^i}+1$ and $i<j$, then $a^{2^j}=(a^{2^i})^{2^{j-i}}\equiv1\pmod q$, so $a^{2^j}+1\equiv2$). So infinitely many odd primes divide numbers of this form, and we can choose $i\ge1$ and an odd prime $p\mid a^{2^i}+1$ with $p\nmid Q_1(0)$ (any such $p$ if $Q=0$). Let \[d=2^i,\qquad v=v_p(a^d+1)\ge1 .\] For every $j\ge0$ and every odd $m$ not divisible by $p$, the lifting-the-exponent lemma (for the odd prime $p\mid a^d+1$ and the odd exponent $p^jm$) gives \[v_p\bigl(a^{\,d\,p^j m}+1\bigr)=v_p(a^d+1)+v_p(p^jm)=v+j. \tag{1}\] So for $n=dp^jm$ the number $v+j$ is one of the exponents in the factorization of $a^n+1$, and \[v+j\ \big|\ f(a^n+1). \tag{2}\]

If $Q=0$ we are done: $f(Q(n))=f(0)=1$, while $f(a^n+1)\ge v+j>1$ for $n=dp^j$, $j\ge1$. From now on $Q\ne0$.

Step 2: keeping the small primes under control. Let $\mathcal Q$ be the set of primes $q\le 2p^{D}$, $q\ne p$. For each $q\in\mathcal Q$ choose an integer $n_q$ with $Q(n_q)\ne0$ and, if $q=2$, also $v_2(n_2)=i$ (possible, since $Q$ has finitely many roots). Put $e_q=v_q(Q(n_q))$. If $n\equiv n_q\pmod{q^{\,e_q+1}}$, then $Q(n)\equiv Q(n_q)\pmod{q^{\,e_q+1}}$ and so \[v_q(Q(n))=e_q. \tag{3}\] Let $M_0=2^{e_2+1}\,p\prod_{q\in\mathcal Q,\ q\ \mathrm{odd}}q^{\,e_q+1}$. For every $j\ge1$ the Chinese remainder theorem gives an integer $m=m_j$ with $1\le m\le M_0$ such that

  • $m\equiv1\pmod p$;

  • $d\,p^j m\equiv n_q\pmod{q^{\,e_q+1}}$ for every odd $q\in\mathcal Q$ (possible because $q\nmid dp^j$);

  • $m\equiv (n_2/2^i)\,p^{-j}\pmod{2^{\,e_2+1}}$; then $m$ is odd and $d\,p^jm\equiv n_2\pmod{2^{\,e_2+1}}$.

Put $n=d\,p^{j}m_j$. Then $m_j$ is odd and prime to $p$, so (1), (2) hold, and (3) holds for all $q\in\mathcal Q$; in particular $Q(n)\ne0$.

Step 3: a prime $r$ dividing $f(a^n+1)$ but not $f(Q(n))$. Choose $j$ so large that \[r=v+j\ \text{is prime},\qquad r>kv,\qquad r>e_q\ \text{for all }q\in\mathcal Q,\qquad 2^{\,j}>C\,(dM_0)^{D}.\] (There are infinitely many such $j$.) Let $n=dp^jm_j$. By (2), $r\mid f(a^n+1)$. Suppose that $r\mid f(Q(n))$. Since $r$ is prime, $r$ divides $v_q(Q(n))$ for some prime $q\mid Q(n)$; in particular $v_q(Q(n))\ge r$.

  • $q=p$ is impossible: $n=dp^jm_j$ with $p\nmid dm_j$ and $Q_1(n)\equiv Q_1(0)\not\equiv0\pmod p$, so $v_p(Q(n))=kj=kr-kv$, which is not a positive multiple of $r$ because $0\le kv<r$ (if $k=0$ it is $0$).

  • $q\in\mathcal Q$ is impossible: $v_q(Q(n))=e_q<r$ by (3).

  • $q>2p^{D}$ is impossible: it would give $q^{\,r}\le|Q(n)|\le C\,n^D\le C(dM_0)^{D}p^{\,jD}<2^{\,j}p^{\,jD}\le(2p^{D})^{r}<q^{\,r}$.

So $r\nmid f(Q(n))$, and therefore $f(a^n+1)\nmid f(Q(n))$. As $j$ can be arbitrarily large, so can $n$; hence no $N$ works. Since $Q$ and $a$ were arbitrary, no polynomial $Q$ has the required property.

Problem 12

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

A function $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ and a real number $c$ are given such that for all $z\in\mathbb C$, the following inequality holds: \[|f(z)-z|<|z|^{0.99}+c.\] We also know that for any four distinct complex numbers $\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\omega_4$ such that the corresponding points on the complex plane are the vertices of a convex quadrilateral (not necessarily in this order), the corresponding points of $f(\omega_1),f(\omega_2),f(\omega_3),f(\omega_4)$ on the complex plane are the vertices of a convex quadrilateral (not necessarily in this order) as well.

Prove that there exists a complex number $a\in\mathbb C$ such that for all $z\in\mathbb C$ we have $f(z)=z+a$.

Show the solution

Solution (Far points detect the directions of the sides)

We identify complex numbers with points of the plane. Four points are in convex position if they are the vertices of a convex quadrilateral, i.e. no three of them are collinear and none lies inside the triangle formed by the other three. A direction is a unit vector $e^{i\theta}$.

Step 1: far points keep their direction. Fix $\theta$ and let $w=Re^{i\theta}$. From $|f(w)-w|<R^{0.99}+c$ we get $\left|\dfrac{f(w)}{R}-e^{i\theta}\right|<R^{-0.01}+\dfrac{c}{R}\to0$ as $R\to\infty$. So for every fixed point $q$: $|f(w)-q|\to\infty$ and the direction of $f(w)-q$ tends to $e^{i\theta}$; the same holds for $w-q$.

Step 2: good and bad directions of a triangle. Let $x,y,z$ be three non-collinear points. The directions of the six vectors $\pm(y-x)$, $\pm(z-y)$, $\pm(x-z)$ cut the circle of directions into six open arcs. Three of them are the sets of directions pointing from a vertex into the angle of the triangle at that vertex; call these directions good. The other three arcs are opposite to them; call their directions bad (figure). Let $w$ be a point far away, $|w|\to\infty$, such that the direction of $w-x$ tends to a direction $e^{i\theta}$.

  • If $e^{i\theta}$ is good and points into the angle at $x$, then for $|w|$ large the ray from $x$ through $w$ lies inside the angle $yxz$ and $w$ is beyond the line $yz$; so the segments $xw$ and $yz$ cross, and $x,y,w,z$ are in convex position.

  • If $e^{i\theta}$ is bad and opposite to the angle at $x$, then for $|w|$ large $w=x-\alpha(y-x)-\beta(z-x)$ with $\alpha,\beta>0$, so $x=\frac{w+\alpha y+\beta z}{1+\alpha+\beta}$ lies inside the triangle $wyz$, and $x,y,z,w$ are not in convex position.

Step 3: $f$ preserves the directions of the sides of a triangle. Let $x,y,z$ be non-collinear and $X=f(x)$, $Y=f(y)$, $Z=f(z)$. Take a good direction $e^{i\theta}$ of $xyz$ and $w=Re^{i\theta}$ with $R$ large. By Step 2, for $R$ large $x,y,z,w$ are in convex position, hence so are $X,Y,Z,f(w)$. In particular $X,Y,Z$ are distinct and not collinear. Moreover, $e^{i\theta}$ cannot be a bad direction of the triangle $XYZ$: by Step 1, $|f(w)|\to\infty$ and the directions of $f(w)$ from $X,Y,Z$ tend to $e^{i\theta}$, and Step 2 (applied to the triangle $XYZ$ and the far point $f(w)$) would show that $X,Y,Z,f(w)$ are not in convex position for large $R$.

So the set $\mathcal G$ of good directions of $xyz$ contains no bad direction of $XYZ$. Now $\mathcal G$ is open, so it also avoids the six endpoints of the arcs of $XYZ$; hence $\mathcal G\subset\mathcal G'$, the set of good directions of $XYZ$. Both $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal G'$ consist of three open arcs of total length $\pi$ (the angles of a triangle add up to $\pi$), and the arcs of $\mathcal G$ are separated by arcs of positive length. Each arc of $\mathcal G$ lies in one arc of $\mathcal G'$; if two of them were in the same arc of $\mathcal G'$, or if one of them were shorter than the arc of $\mathcal G'$ containing it, the total length of $\mathcal G'$ would exceed $\pi$. Hence $\mathcal G=\mathcal G'$, and the endpoints of the arcs are the same six directions: \[\text{each of }X-Y,\ Y-Z,\ Z-X\ \text{ is parallel to one of }x-y,\ y-z,\ z-x. \tag{1}\]

Step 4: $f(x)-f(y)$ is parallel to $x-y$. Let $x\ne y$. Choose $z$ off the line $xy$, and then $z'$ off the line $xy$ such that neither of the lines $yz'$, $z'x$ is parallel to $yz$ or to $zx$ (this excludes finitely many lines for $z'$). By (1) for the triangles $xyz$ and $xyz'$, the vector $f(x)-f(y)$ is parallel to one of $x-y,y-z,z-x$ and to one of $x-y,y-z',z'-x$. The only common possibility is that it is parallel to $x-y$. So for all $x\ne y$ \[f(x)-f(y)=\lambda(x,y)\,(x-y)\qquad\text{with a real number }\lambda(x,y)=\lambda(y,x)\ne0 .\]

Step 5: $\lambda$ is constant. For non-collinear $x,y,z$, adding the three relations gives \[\lambda(x,y)(x-y)+\lambda(y,z)(y-z)+\lambda(z,x)(z-x)=0 .\] Since $z-x=-(x-y)-(y-z)$ and $x-y$, $y-z$ are not parallel, this forces $\lambda(x,y)=\lambda(z,x)=\lambda(y,z)$. Now let $\{x,y\}$ and $\{x',y'\}$ be any two pairs of distinct points. Choose $z$ on neither of the lines $xy$, $x'y'$, $x'y$. Using the triangles $xyz$, $yzx'$ and $zx'y'$ in turn, \[\lambda(x,y)=\lambda(y,z)=\lambda(z,x')=\lambda(x',y').\] (If $x'=y$ the middle step is not needed.) So $\lambda(x,y)=\lambda$ is one real constant, and $f(z)-f(0)=\lambda z$ for all $z$: \[f(z)=\lambda z+a,\qquad a=f(0).\]

Step 6: $\lambda=1$. The hypothesis gives $|(\lambda-1)z+a|<|z|^{0.99}+c$ for all $z$. For real $z=R\to\infty$ the left side is at least $|\lambda-1|R-|a|$, so $|\lambda-1|\le R^{-0.01}+\frac{c+|a|}{R}\to0$. Hence $\lambda=1$ and $f(z)=z+a$ for all $z\in\mathbb C$.
