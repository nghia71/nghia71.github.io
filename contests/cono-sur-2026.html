Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle. Let $M$ be the midpoint of segment $AC$ and $N$ the midpoint of segment $BM$. If $\angle BAN=\angle ACN$, prove that $AN=AM$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Completing a parallelogram and the power of $A$)

Let $D$ be the point such that $N$ is the midpoint of $AD$. The diagonals $AD$ and $BM$ of the quadrilateral $ABDM$ bisect each other (both have midpoint $N$), so $ABDM$ is a parallelogram; it is not degenerate because $B$ does not lie on line $AC$. Hence $DM\parallel AB$ and $AD=2AN$.

Step 1: $N,M,C,D$ are concyclic. In the parallelogram $ABDM$ the vertices $B$ and $M$ lie on opposite sides of the diagonal $AD$, so $\angle ADM$ and $\angle DAB$ are alternate angles for the parallel lines $AB$, $DM$ and the transversal $AD$. Therefore \[ \angle NDM=\angle ADM=\angle DAB=\angle NAB=\angle ACN=\angle MCN . \] Next, $D$ and $C$ lie on the same side of line $BM$: the point $N$ of this line lies strictly between $A$ and $D$, and the point $M$ lies strictly between $A$ and $C$, so both $D$ and $C$ are on the side opposite to $A$. Also $D\ne C$ (otherwise $B=A+C-M=M$). So the segment $NM$ is seen from $D$ and from $C$, on the same side of it, under equal angles; hence $N$, $M$, $C$, $D$ lie on one circle $\omega$.

Step 2: the power of $A$. The line $AD$ meets $\omega$ at $N$ and $D$, both on the same ray from $A$, and the line $AC$ meets $\omega$ at $M$ and $C$, again on one ray from $A$. So $A$ is outside $\omega$ and the power of the point $A$ gives \[ AN\cdot AD=AM\cdot AC . \] With $AD=2AN$ and $AC=2AM$ this reads $2AN^2=2AM^2$, so $AN=AM$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Choose coordinates with $A=(0,0)$, $M=(1,0)$, $C=(2,0)$ and $B=(p,q)$ with $q>0$. Then $N=\left(\tfrac{p+1}{2},\tfrac q2\right)$.

For an angle $\theta\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ between two vectors $u=(u_1,u_2)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2)$ we have $\cot\theta=\dfrac{u\cdot v}{|u_1v_2-u_2v_1|}$, and $\cot$ is one-to-one on $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$. So two such angles are equal exactly when their cotangents are equal.

For $\angle BAN$ take $u=\overrightarrow{AB}=(p,q)$ and $v=\overrightarrow{AN}=\left(\tfrac{p+1}{2},\tfrac q2\right)$: \[ u\cdot v=\frac{p^2+p+q^2}{2},\qquad u_1v_2-u_2v_1=\frac{pq}{2}-\frac{q(p+1)}{2}=-\frac q2, \qquad\text{so}\quad \cot\angle BAN=\frac{p^2+p+q^2}{q}. \] For $\angle ACN$ take $u=\overrightarrow{CA}=(-2,0)$ and $v=\overrightarrow{CN}=\left(\tfrac{p-3}{2},\tfrac q2\right)$: \[ u\cdot v=3-p,\qquad u_1v_2-u_2v_1=-q,\qquad\text{so}\quad \cot\angle ACN=\frac{3-p}{q}. \] Hence $\angle BAN=\angle ACN$ is equivalent to $p^2+p+q^2=3-p$, that is, to \[ p^2+2p+q^2=3 . \] (Geometrically: $B$ lies on the circle with centre $(-1,0)$ and radius $2=AC$.) Then \[ AN^2=\frac{(p+1)^2+q^2}{4}=\frac{(p^2+2p+q^2)+1}{4}=\frac{3+1}{4}=1=AM^2, \] so $AN=AM$.

Solution 3 (Areas, the cosine rule and the median formula)

Write $s=AM=MC$, $u=AN$, $v=CN$, $b=AB$ and $w=BN=NM$, and let $\theta=\angle BAN=\angle ACN$. The triangles $ABN$, $ACN$ and $ABM$ are not degenerate, because $B$ does not lie on line $AC$ and $N$ lies strictly between $B$ and $M$.

Step 1: an area relation. Let $h$ be the distance from $B$ to line $AC$. Since $N$ is the midpoint of $BM$ and $M$ lies on $AC$, the distance from $N$ to line $AC$ is $h/2$. Hence \[ [ACN]=\tfrac12\cdot AC\cdot\tfrac h2=\tfrac12\cdot 2s\cdot\tfrac h2=\tfrac12\,s\,h=[ABM]. \] Also $[ABN]=\tfrac12[ABM]$, since the triangles $ABN$ and $ABM$ have the same height from $A$ and the base $BN$ is half of $BM$. So $[ACN]=2[ABN]$. The angle $\theta$ lies between the sides $AB$, $AN$ of triangle $ABN$ and between the sides $CA$, $CN$ of triangle $ACN$, so \[ [ABN]=\tfrac12\,b\,u\sin\theta,\qquad [ACN]=\tfrac12\cdot 2s\cdot v\sin\theta . \] Since $\sin\theta>0$, the relation $[ACN]=2[ABN]$ gives \[ sv=bu. \tag{1} \]

Step 2: the cosine rule. In triangle $ABN$ the side opposite $\theta$ is $BN=w$, and in triangle $ACN$ the side opposite $\theta$ is $AN=u$. So \[ 2bu\cos\theta=b^2+u^2-w^2,\qquad 4sv\cos\theta=4s^2+v^2-u^2 . \] By (1) the second left side is twice the first, hence \[ 4s^2+v^2-u^2=2\left(b^2+u^2-w^2\right). \tag{2} \] (We never divide by $\cos\theta$, which may be $0$.)

Step 3: the median formula. If $W$ is the midpoint of $YZ$, then $XY^2+XZ^2=2XW^2+2WY^2$ for every point $X$: with vectors from $W$, write $\vec x=\overrightarrow{WX}$ and $\vec y=\overrightarrow{WY}$, so that $\overrightarrow{WZ}=-\vec y$, and then $|\vec x-\vec y|^2+|\vec x+\vec y|^2=2|\vec x|^2+2|\vec y|^2$. Applying this to the midpoint $N$ of $BM$ with $X=A$, and to the midpoint $M$ of $AC$ with $X=N$, gives \[ b^2+s^2=2u^2+2w^2,\qquad u^2+v^2=2w^2+2s^2 . \] So $b^2=2u^2+2w^2-s^2$ and $v^2=2s^2+2w^2-u^2$. Substituting into (2): \[ 6s^2+2w^2-2u^2=2\left(3u^2+w^2-s^2\right)=6u^2+2w^2-2s^2 , \] that is, $8s^2=8u^2$. Hence $AN=u=s=AM$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Find all composite positive integers $n$ such that, for all integers $a>1$, $b>1$ with $ab=n$, it is true that: \[(a+b)^2\mid a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}.\]

Show the answer

$n\in\{4,\,6,\,16\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Coprime factor pairs and a division lemma)

Lemma. Let $x,y$ be coprime positive integers and $N$ an odd positive integer. Then $x+y\mid x^N+y^N$, and \[ (x+y)^2\mid x^N+y^N\iff x+y\mid N . \] Proof. $x^N+y^N=(x+y)Q$ with $Q=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x^{N-1-j}(-y)^j$. Modulo $x+y$ we have $-y\equiv x$, so every one of the $N$ terms of $Q$ is $\equiv x^{N-1}$ and $Q\equiv Nx^{N-1}\pmod{x+y}$. Thus $(x+y)^2\mid x^N+y^N$ iff $x+y\mid Q$ iff $x+y\mid Nx^{N-1}$, and since $\gcd(x+y,x)=\gcd(y,x)=1$ this holds iff $x+y\mid N$. $\square$

Call a composite $n$ good if it has the property. We split by the power of $2$ in $n$.

$n$ odd. Take any factorization $n=ab$ with $a,b>1$ (it exists as $n$ is composite). Then $a,b$ are odd, so $4\mid (a+b)^2$. But $n-1$ is even, so $a^{n-1}$ and $b^{n-1}$ are odd squares, each $\equiv 1\pmod 4$, and $a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}\equiv 2\pmod 4$. So no odd $n$ is good.

$n=2m$ with $m$ odd. As $n$ is composite, $m\ge 3$. The pair $(2,m)$ is coprime and $N=n-1$ is odd, so the Lemma requires $m+2\mid 2m-1=2(m+2)-5$, i.e. $m+2\mid 5$, so $m=3$ and $n=6$. Conversely $6=2\cdot 3$ is the only factorization, and $2^5+3^5=275=25\cdot 11$ is divisible by $(2+3)^2$. So $6$ is good.

$n=4m$ with $m$ odd. If $m=1$, then $n=4$, the only factorization is $2\cdot 2$, and $(2+2)^2=16=2^3+2^3$: good. If $m\ge 3$, the coprime pair $(4,m)$ and the Lemma give $m+4\mid 4m-1=4(m+4)-17$, so $m+4\mid 17$, $m=13$, $n=52$. Now use the pair $(2,26)$: we need $28^2\mid 2^{51}+26^{51}=2^{51}(1+13^{51})$, and since $7\mid 28$ this needs $49\mid 1+13^{51}$. By the proof of the Lemma with $x=1$, $y=13$, $N=51$: $1+13^{51}=14Q$ with $Q\equiv 51\pmod{14}$, so $Q\equiv 51\equiv 2\pmod 7$ and $7\nmid Q$. Hence $49\nmid 1+13^{51}$, and $52$ is not good.

$8\mid n$. Put $t=n/4$, an even number. Use the pair $(2,2t)$: $a+b=2(1+t)$ and $a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}=2^{n-1}(1+t^{n-1})$. Since $1+t$ is odd and $n-1\ge 2$, the condition $4(1+t)^2\mid 2^{n-1}(1+t^{n-1})$ is equivalent to $(1+t)^2\mid 1+t^{n-1}$, and by the Lemma (with $x=1$, $y=t$, $N=n-1$) to $1+t\mid n-1=4t-1=4(1+t)-5$. So $1+t\mid 5$, $t=4$, $n=16$. Conversely, for $n=16$: the pairs $(2,8)$, $(8,2)$ need $100\mid 2^{15}(1+2^{30})$, which holds because $2^{10}=1024\equiv -1\pmod{25}$ gives $2^{30}\equiv -1\pmod{25}$; the pair $(4,4)$ needs $64\mid 2\cdot 4^{15}=2^{31}$, which holds. So $16$ is good.

Therefore the good numbers are exactly $\boxed{n\in\{4,\ 6,\ 16\}}$.

Remark (lifting the exponent). For an odd prime $q$ the Lemma amounts to $v_q(x^N+y^N)=v_q(x+y)+v_q(N)$ (for $q\mid x+y$, $q\nmid xy$, $N$ odd). In this language the case analysis can be organised around the single pair $(2,n/2)$: it forces the odd part of $\frac n2+2$ to be $1$ or $5$, which leaves $n=6$, $n=16$ and $n=4m$ with $m$ odd; the pair $(4,m)$ then leaves only $m=1$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 35: Generating Functions and Algebraic Encoding

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer. In each cell of a $1\times n$ grid, one of the numbers $1,2,3$ or $4$ is written on it. Let $a_n$ be the number of ways to do this labeling for which there exists a cut between two consecutive cells such that the sum of the numbers on the left equals the sum of the numbers on the right.

Find an explicit formula of $a_n$ in terms of $n$.

Show the answer

$a_n=\dfrac{4^n+4(-1)^n}{5}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Generating functions and a roots-of-unity filter)

Write the labels as $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\{1,2,3,4\}$, with prefix sums $S_k=x_1+\dots+x_k$ and total $T=S_n$. A cut after cell $k$ works iff $S_k=T-S_k$. Since $S_1<S_2<\dots<S_{n-1}$, at most one cut works, so \[ a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\#\{\text{labelings with } S_k=T-S_k\}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{s}N_k(s)\,N_{n-k}(s), \] where $N_j(s)$ is the number of sequences of length $j$ with entries in $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and sum $s$. Let $f(x)=x+x^2+x^3+x^4$ and $g(x)=1+x+x^2+x^3$, so $f(x)=x\,g(x)$ and $f(1/x)=x^{-4}g(x)$. Since $N_j(s)$ is the coefficient of $x^s$ in $f(x)^j$, the inner sum is the constant term of \[ f(x)^k f(1/x)^{\,n-k}=x^{\,k-4(n-k)}\,g(x)^n=x^{\,5k-4n}\,g(x)^n , \] i.e. the coefficient $c_{4n-5k}$ in $g(x)^n=\sum_{j=0}^{3n}c_jx^j$. So $a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_{4n-5k}$.

This is exactly the sum of all $c_j$ with $j\equiv 4n\pmod 5$: for $0\le j\le 3n$ with $j\equiv 4n\pmod 5$, the number $k=(4n-j)/5$ satisfies $n/5\le k\le 4n/5$, hence $1\le k\le n-1$; and the terms with $j<0$ or $j>3n$ are zero.

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/5}$. The roots-of-unity filter gives \[ \sum_{j\equiv 4n \ (\mathrm{mod}\ 5)}c_j=\frac15\sum_{t=0}^{4}\omega^{-4nt}g(\omega^t)^n=\frac15\sum_{t=0}^{4}\big(\omega^t g(\omega^t)\big)^n =\frac15\sum_{t=0}^{4}f(\omega^t)^n , \] using $\omega^{-4nt}=\omega^{nt}$. Now $f(1)=4$, and for $t=1,2,3,4$ the numbers $\omega^t,\omega^{2t},\omega^{3t},\omega^{4t}$ are the four fifth roots of unity other than $1$, whose sum is $-1$; so $f(\omega^t)=-1$. Therefore \[ a_n=\boxed{\dfrac{4^n+4(-1)^n}{5}} . \] (Check: $a_2=4$, from $11,22,33,44$.)

Solution 2 (A bijection with sums divisible by $5$)

Write a labeling as $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ and call it balanced if some cut works. The prefix sums $S_k=x_1+\dots+x_k$ strictly increase, so a balanced labeling has exactly one working cut; call its position $k(x)$.

The bijection. For a balanced $x$ with $k=k(x)$ define \[ \varphi(x)=(x_1,\dots,x_k,\ 5-x_{k+1},\dots,5-x_n). \] Its entries are again in $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and its sum is $S_k+5(n-k)-(T-S_k)=5(n-k)$, where $T=2S_k$ is the total of $x$. So $\varphi(x)$ lies in the set $Z_n$ of labelings whose sum is divisible by $5$.

Conversely, let $y\in Z_n$ have sum $U$. Then $n\le U\le 4n$ and $5\mid U$, so $k:=n-U/5$ is an integer with $n/5\le k\le 4n/5$, hence $1\le k\le n-1$. Put $\psi(y)=(y_1,\dots,y_k,5-y_{k+1},\dots,5-y_n)$. Its left part sums to $L=y_1+\dots+y_k$ and its right part to $5(n-k)-(U-L)=U-U+L=L$, so $\psi(y)$ is balanced with $k(\psi(y))=k$, and $\varphi(\psi(y))=y$. Also, for balanced $x$, the sum of $\varphi(x)$ is $5(n-k(x))$, so $\psi$ uses the same $k$ and $\psi(\varphi(x))=x$. Hence $\varphi$ is a bijection and $a_n=|Z_n|$.

Counting $Z_n$. Let $z_n=|Z_n|$; clearly $z_1=0$. Fix the first $n-1$ entries (in $4^{n-1}$ ways) and let $s$ be their sum. The last entry must be $\equiv -s\pmod 5$; exactly one of $1,2,3,4$ works if $s\not\equiv 0$, and none works if $s\equiv 0$. Hence $z_n=4^{n-1}-z_{n-1}$. The formula $z_n=\frac{4^n+4(-1)^n}{5}$ holds for $n=1$ and is preserved by this recursion, because $4^{n-1}-\frac{4^{n-1}+4(-1)^{n-1}}{5}=\frac{4^n+4(-1)^n}{5}$. Therefore \[ a_n=\boxed{\dfrac{4^n+4(-1)^n}{5}} . \]

Day 2

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n\ge 3$ be an integer. In an $n\times n$ grid $k$ dominoes are placed without overlaps. Each domino covers two grid cells that share a side and in those two cells the numbers $1$ and $2$ are written (one in each cell). Determine in terms of $n$, the minimal value of $k$ for which it is possible to perform a domino tiling such that in every row and column the sum of numbers is at least $4$.

Show the answer

$k_{\min}=\left\lceil \dfrac{4n}{3}\right\rceil$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting the total, then diagonal blocks)

Lower bound. Every domino carries the numbers $1$ and $2$, so it adds $3$ to the total of all the numbers on the board. The total is also the sum of the $n$ row sums, each at least $4$. Hence $3k\ge 4n$ and $k\ge\lceil 4n/3\rceil$.

Three building blocks (see the figure; in each domino the cell with $1$ and the cell with $2$ are marked).

  • A $3\times3$ block with $4$ dominoes (a “pinwheel”): its rows read $1\,2\,1$, $2\,0\,2$, $1\,2\,1$, and so do its columns; every row and column sum is $4$.

  • A $4\times 4$ block with $6$ dominoes, with rows $1\,1\,2\,0$; $2\,0\,1\,2$; $2\,1\,0\,2$; $0\,2\,1\,1$. Row sums $4,5,5,4$; column sums $5,4,4,5$.

  • A $5\times 5$ block with $7$ dominoes, with rows $1\,2\,1\,0\,0$; $2\,0\,0\,2\,1$; $0\,0\,1\,2\,1$; $0\,0\,2\,0\,2$; $1\,2\,0\,0\,1$. Row sums $4,5,4,4,4$; column sums $4,4,4,4,5$.

Construction. Place blocks along the main diagonal of the board, so that every row and every column of the board meets exactly one block; then each row and column sum of the board equals a row or column sum of a block, which is at least $4$.

  • $n=3t$: $t$ pinwheels, using $4t=4n/3$ dominoes.

  • $n=3t+1$ ($t\ge1$): $t-1$ pinwheels and the $4\times4$ block, using $4(t-1)+6=4t+2=\lceil 4n/3\rceil$ dominoes.

  • $n=3t+2$ ($t\ge1$): $t-1$ pinwheels and the $5\times5$ block, using $4(t-1)+7=4t+3=\lceil 4n/3\rceil$ dominoes.

So the lower bound is attained, and the minimum is $\boxed{\left\lceil \dfrac{4n}{3}\right\rceil}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle and let $P$ be a point in the plane (distinct from the vertices of the triangle). Denote by $A_1$, $B_1$, $C_1$ the midpoints of sides $BC$, $CA$ and $AB$, respectively. We will also denote by $A_2$, $B_2$ and $C_2$ the midpoints of $AP$, $BP$ and $CP$, respectively. If the segments $A_1A_2$, $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$ have the same length then prove that $P$ is the orthocenter of triangle $ABC$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Put the origin at the circumcentre $O$ of $ABC$ and write $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c,\vec p$ for the position vectors of $A,B,C,P$; then $|\vec a|=|\vec b|=|\vec c|=R$, the circumradius. Let $\vec u=\vec p-(\vec a+\vec b+\vec c)$. Since $A_1=\frac{\vec b+\vec c}{2}$ and $A_2=\frac{\vec a+\vec p}{2}$, \[ \overrightarrow{A_1A_2}=\frac{\vec a+\vec p-\vec b-\vec c}{2}=\frac{2\vec a+\vec u}{2},\qquad A_1A_2^2=\frac{4R^2+4\,\vec a\cdot\vec u+|\vec u|^2}{4}, \] and in the same way $B_1B_2^2=\frac14\big(4R^2+4\,\vec b\cdot\vec u+|\vec u|^2\big)$ and $C_1C_2^2=\frac14\big(4R^2+4\,\vec c\cdot\vec u+|\vec u|^2\big)$.

So the three lengths are equal iff $\vec a\cdot\vec u=\vec b\cdot\vec u=\vec c\cdot\vec u$, i.e. iff $(\vec a-\vec b)\cdot\vec u=0$ and $(\vec b-\vec c)\cdot\vec u=0$. Then $\vec u$ is perpendicular to both $AB$ and $BC$, which are not parallel, so $\vec u=\vec 0$ and $\vec p=\vec a+\vec b+\vec c$.

Finally, the point $H$ with position vector $\vec h=\vec a+\vec b+\vec c$ is the orthocentre: $(\vec h-\vec a)\cdot(\vec b-\vec c)= (\vec b+\vec c)\cdot(\vec b-\vec c)=|\vec b|^2-|\vec c|^2=0$, so $AH\perp BC$, and similarly $BH\perp CA$. Hence $P=H$ is the orthocentre of $ABC$.

Solution 2 (Two parallelograms of midpoints)

Look at the four midpoints $C_1,B_2,C_2,B_1$. In triangle $ABP$, $C_1B_2$ is a midline, so $\overrightarrow{C_1B_2}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{AP}$; in triangle $ACP$, $\overrightarrow{B_1C_2}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{AP}$; in triangles $ABC$ and $PBC$, $\overrightarrow{C_1B_1}=\overrightarrow{B_2C_2}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{BC}$. So $C_1B_2C_2B_1$ is a parallelogram with sides parallel to $AP$ and $BC$, and $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$ are its diagonals. Precisely, with $\vec u=\overrightarrow{AP}$ and $\vec v=\overrightarrow{CB}$, \[ \overrightarrow{B_1B_2}=\frac{(B+P)-(C+A)}{2}=\frac{\vec u+\vec v}{2},\qquad \overrightarrow{C_1C_2}=\frac{(C+P)-(A+B)}{2}=\frac{\vec u-\vec v}{2}, \] so \[ B_1B_2^2-C_1C_2^2=\frac{|\vec u+\vec v|^2-|\vec u-\vec v|^2}{4}=\vec u\cdot\vec v=\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{CB}. \] (The diagonals of a parallelogram are equal exactly when it is a rectangle.) Hence $B_1B_2=C_1C_2$ gives $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{CB}=0$. As $P\ne A$, this means $AP\perp BC$: $P$ lies on the altitude from $A$.

In exactly the same way, the parallelogram $A_1C_2A_2C_1$ (sides parallel to $BP$ and $CA$, diagonals $C_1C_2$ and $A_1A_2$) gives $C_1C_2^2-A_1A_2^2=\overrightarrow{BP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}$, so $A_1A_2=C_1C_2$ and $P\ne B$ give $BP\perp CA$: $P$ lies on the altitude from $B$. Two altitudes meet only at the orthocentre, so $P$ is the orthocentre of $ABC$.

Solution 3 (The common midpoint of the three segments and a homothety)

Let $G$ be the centroid and $O$ the circumcentre of $ABC$. Put the origin at $G$ and write $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c,\vec p,\vec o$ for the position vectors of $A,B,C,P,O$; then $\vec a+\vec b+\vec c=\vec 0$.

Step 1: the three segments have a common midpoint. The midpoint $A_1$ of $BC$ has position vector $\frac{\vec b+\vec c}{2}=-\frac{\vec a}2$, and $A_2$ has position vector $\frac{\vec a+\vec p}{2}$. So the midpoint of $A_1A_2$ has position vector \[ \frac12\left(-\frac{\vec a}{2}+\frac{\vec a+\vec p}{2}\right)=\frac{\vec p}{4}. \] In the same way the midpoints of $B_1B_2$ and $C_1C_2$ have position vector $\frac{\vec p}{4}$. Call this common midpoint $K$. Then $KA_1=\frac12A_1A_2$, $KB_1=\frac12B_1B_2$ and $KC_1=\frac12C_1C_2$, so the hypothesis says $KA_1=KB_1=KC_1$. The points $A_1,B_1,C_1$ are not collinear (see the homothety below), so $K$ is the circumcentre of triangle $A_1B_1C_1$.

Step 2: a homothety. Let $\eta$ be the homothety with centre $G$ and ratio $-\frac12$, that is, $\vec x\mapsto-\frac12\vec x$. It sends $A,B,C$ to $A_1,B_1,C_1$ (for instance $-\frac12\vec a=A_1$), so $A_1B_1C_1$ is a triangle. It multiplies all distances by $\frac12$, so the point $\eta(O)$ is equidistant from $A_1,B_1,C_1$; hence $\eta(O)$ is the circumcentre $K$ of $A_1B_1C_1$. Thus $-\frac12\vec o=\frac14\vec p$, i.e. $\vec p=-2\vec o$, and therefore $\eta(P)=O$.

Step 3: $AP$ is perpendicular to $BC$. Since $\eta$ maps $A\mapsto A_1$ and $P\mapsto O$, it multiplies vectors by $-\frac12$: \[ \overrightarrow{A_1O}=-\tfrac12\,\overrightarrow{AP}. \] As $P\ne A$, the vector $\overrightarrow{AP}$ is not zero, so $O\ne A_1$ and the line $A_1O$ is parallel to $AP$. The point $O$ is equidistant from $B$ and $C$, and $A_1$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so the line $A_1O$ is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. Hence $AP\perp BC$, and $P$ lies on the altitude from $A$.

In the same way $\eta$ maps $B\mapsto B_1$ and $P\mapsto O$, so $\overrightarrow{B_1O}=-\frac12\overrightarrow{BP}\ne\vec 0$ (as $P\ne B$), the line $B_1O$ is the perpendicular bisector of $CA$, and $BP\perp CA$. The altitudes from $A$ and $B$ are not parallel and meet only at the orthocentre, so $P$ is the orthocentre of $ABC$. Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Given a nonnegative integer, an operation over this integer allows us to add to it $2026$ or to delete any two of its equal adjacent digits in base $10$ if there exist any. Let $a$ and $b$ be two arbitrary nonnegative integers. Prove that if we start with the number $a$ then it will be possible to perform a finite amount of operations to attain number $b$.

Show the solution

Solution (Appending erasable pairs, with Fermat and the Chinese remainder theorem)

We show that from $a$ we can reach $b$ by first adding $2026$ several times, reaching a suitable number $N$, and then deleting pairs of digits at the right end of $N$.

Deleting at the right end. If an integer $x\ge 10$ has equal last two digits, deleting them turns $x$ into $\lfloor x/100\rfloor$. (When all digits are deleted, or leading zeros appear, we read the result as an ordinary integer; in particular deleting both digits of $11$ gives $0$. Some such convention is needed in any case to reach $b=0$ from a positive number.)

Arithmetic facts. $2026=2\cdot 1013$ and $1013$ is prime, so by Fermat's little theorem $100^{1012}\equiv 1\pmod{1013}$. Put $e=1012$. Also $\gcd(11,1013)=1$, so $11$ is invertible modulo $1013$.

The number $N$. Choose an integer $L\ge1$ with $100^{Le}>a$, and let $r=(L+1013)e+1$. For $d\in\{0,1\}$ and $c\in\{1,2,\dots,1013\}$ let \[ Y=11d+11\sum_{t=1}^{c}100^{(L+t)e},\qquad N=b\cdot 100^{\,r}+Y . \] Written with exactly $2r$ digits, $Y$ consists of $r$ two-digit blocks: counting blocks from the right as block $0,1,\dots,r-1$, block $j$ is $11$ if $j=0$ and $d=1$, or if $j=(L+t)e$ for some $1\le t\le c$; every other block is $00$. (All these exponents are distinct and less than $r$.) So the decimal form of $N$ is the decimal form of $b$ followed by these $2r$ digits (if $b=0$, it is just $Y$ without leading zeros).

Choosing $d$ and $c$. The last digit of $N$ is $d$, so $N\equiv d\pmod 2$. Modulo $1013$, each $100^{(L+t)e}\equiv 1$, so $N\equiv b\cdot 100^{r}+11(d+c)\pmod{1013}$. Take $d\equiv a\pmod 2$, and then take $c\in\{1,\dots,1013\}$ with $11(d+c)\equiv a-b\cdot 100^r\pmod{1013}$ (possible because $c$ runs through a complete residue system and $11$ is invertible). Then $N\equiv a$ modulo $2$ and modulo $1013$, hence modulo $2026$. Moreover $N\ge 11\cdot 100^{(L+1)e}>100^{Le}>a$. So $N=a+2026s$ with $s\ge1$.

The operations. Starting from $a$, add $2026$ exactly $s$ times to reach $N$. Then delete at the right end as long as the current number is not $b$. After $i<r$ deletions the current number is $x=\lfloor N/100^i\rfloor=b\cdot 100^{r-i}+\lfloor Y/100^i\rfloor$, and $x\bmod 100$ is block $i$ of $Y$, which is $00$ or $11$. If $x\ge 10$, its last two digits are these two equal digits, so the deletion is allowed. If $x<10$, then $b=0$ (otherwise $x\ge 100$) and $\lfloor Y/100^i\rfloor<10$; but this number is a sum of terms $11\cdot 100^{j}$, so it is $0$, and $x=0=b$ has already been reached. Thus after at most $r$ deletions we reach $\lfloor N/100^r\rfloor=b$.

Hence from any $a$ we can reach any $b$ with finitely many operations.
