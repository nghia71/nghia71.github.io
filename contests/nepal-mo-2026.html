Problem 1

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Remainder, Factor, and Evaluation Logic

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{2n+2}$ be positive real numbers. Prove that they can be permuted into numbers $u_0,v_0,u_1,v_1,\dots,u_n,v_n$ such that the polynomial \[P(x)=u_0-v_0x+u_1x^2-v_1x^3+\dots+u_nx^{2n}-v_nx^{2n+1}\] has at least one real root, and every real root of $P$ is greater than or equal to $1$.

Show the solution – Pair each number with a smaller one

Split the $2n+2$ numbers into $n+1$ pairs in any way, and in the $i$-th pair ($0\le i\le n$) call the larger number $u_i$ and the smaller one $v_i$ (if they are equal, either choice). So $u_i\ge v_i>0$ for every $i$, and \[P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x^{2i}\,(u_i-v_ix).\]

No root in $(-\infty,0]$. For $x\le0$ every term $u_ix^{2i}$ is $\ge0$ and every term $-v_ix^{2i+1}$ is $\ge0$, while the constant term is $u_0>0$. So $P(x)\ge u_0>0$.

No root in $(0,1)$. For $0<x<1$ we have $u_i-v_ix>u_i-v_i\ge0$ and $x^{2i}>0$, so every summand is positive and $P(x)>0$.

A real root exists. $P$ has odd degree $2n+1$ with leading coefficient $-v_n<0$, so $P(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$. Also $P(1)=\sum_i(u_i-v_i)\ge0$. If $P(1)=0$, then $1$ is a root; otherwise $P(1)>0$ and, by the intermediate value theorem, $P$ has a root in $(1,\infty)$.

Hence $P$ has a real root, and every real root of $P$ is at least $1$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Function Equations on Integers

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ such that for all $m\in\mathbb{N}$, \[f(m-1)\mid m\qquad\text{and}\qquad m+1\mid T_{2026}(m),\] where $T_1(m)=f(m)$ and $T_{k+1}(m)=f\big(m+T_k(m)\big)$ for all $k\ge 1$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=n+1$ for all $n\ge0$

Show the solution – Divisibility travels backwards along the chain

The function $f(n)=n+1$ works. Then $f(m-1)=m\mid m$. Also $T_1(m)=m+1$, and if $T_k(m)=k(m+1)$ then $T_{k+1}(m)=f\big(m+k(m+1)\big)=(k+1)(m+1)$; so $T_{2026}(m)=2026(m+1)$, which is divisible by $m+1$.

It is the only one. The first condition, applied to $m=j+1\ge1$, says \[f(j)\mid j+1\qquad\text{for every } j\ge0. \tag{$*$}\] In particular $f(j)\ne0$ (zero divides only zero), so $f(j)\ge1$. Now fix $m\ge1$ and write $t_k=T_k(m)$; so $t_1=f(m)$ and $t_{k+1}=f(m+t_k)$. By $(*)$ with $j=m+t_k$, \[t_{k+1}\mid m+t_k+1=(m+1)+t_k .\] Claim: if $m+1\mid t_{k+1}$, then $m+1\mid t_k$. Indeed, $m+1\mid t_{k+1}$ and $t_{k+1}\mid(m+1)+t_k$ give $m+1\mid(m+1)+t_k$, hence $m+1\mid t_k$.

We are given $m+1\mid t_{2026}$. Applying the claim for $k=2025,2024,\dots,1$ gives $m+1\mid t_1=f(m)$. Together with $f(m)\mid m+1$ from $(*)$ and $f(m)\ge1$, this forces $f(m)=m+1$. This holds for every $m\ge1$; and for $m=0$, $(*)$ gives $f(0)\mid1$, so $f(0)=1$.

Therefore the only function is $\boxed{f(n)=n+1}$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with $\angle BAC=60^\circ$. Denote by $O$ and $H$ the circumcenter and orthocenter of $\triangle ABC$ respectively. Let $M$ be the midpoint of arc $BC$ on $(ABC)$ not containing $A$, and let ray $MH$ intersect $(ABC)$ a second time at $P$. Show that the lines $AP$, $OH$ and $BC$ pass through a common point.

Show solution 1 – A rhombus and a radical centre

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle and $R$ its radius.

Step 1: $AOMH$ is a rhombus with side $R$. Since $\angle BOC=2\angle A=120^\circ$ and $OM$ bisects it, triangle $BOM$ has $OB=OM$ and $\angle BOM=60^\circ$, so it is equilateral: $MB=R$; likewise $MC=R$. Let $\omega$ be the circle with centre $M$ and radius $R$; it passes through $B$, $C$, $O$. The triangle is acute, so $H$ lies inside it, on the same side of $BC$ as $A$, and $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A=120^\circ$. The arc of $\omega$ on that side of $BC$ is the minor arc $BC$ (the centre $M$ is on the other side), and from its points the chord $BC$ is seen at $180^\circ-\tfrac12\angle BMC=180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ$; the locus of points on that side of $BC$ seeing $BC$ at $120^\circ$ is exactly this arc, so $H\in\omega$ and $MH=R$. Finally $AO=R$, and $AH=2R\cos A=R$ (the well-known formula $AH=2R\cos A$).

So both $O$ and $H$ lie on the circle with centre $A$ and on the circle with centre $M$, both of radius $R$. Two circles meet in at most two points, symmetric in the line of centres $AM$, and $O\ne H$ (the triangle is not equilateral). Hence $AOMH$ is a rhombus: $\overrightarrow{MH}=\overrightarrow{OA}$ and $OH\perp AM$.

Step 2: $A,O,H,P$ are concyclic. Let $\tau$ be the reflection in the perpendicular bisector $s$ of $OA$; it swaps $O$ and $A$. The line $MH$ is parallel to $OA$, hence perpendicular to $s$, so $\tau$ maps line $MH$ onto itself. The point $\tau(H)$ is on line $MH$ and $O\tau(H)=\tau(A)\tau(H)=AH=R$, so $\tau(H)\in\Gamma\cap MH=\{M,P\}$. If $\tau(H)=M$, then $OH=\tau(O)\tau(H)=AM$, so the rhombus would have equal diagonals, i.e. be a square; then $MH\parallel OA\perp OM$ would be tangent to $\Gamma$ at $M$ and could not meet $\Gamma$ again. Hence $\tau(H)=P$.

$A,O,H$ are not collinear (otherwise $MH\parallel AO$ would force $M$ onto line $AO$, i.e. $AB=AC$). The circle $\kappa$ through $A,O,H$ has its centre on $s$, so $\tau(\kappa)=\kappa$ and $P=\tau(H)\in\kappa$. Also $P\ne A$ (else $H=\tau(A)=O$).

Step 3: radical centre. Consider $\Gamma$ (through $A,P,B,C$), $\omega$ (through $B,C,O,H$) and $\kappa$ (through $A,O,H,P$); they are distinct ($A\notin\omega$ because $A$ sees $BC$ at $60^\circ$, not $120^\circ$). Their pairwise radical axes are the common chords \[\Gamma,\omega:\ BC,\qquad \omega,\kappa:\ OH,\qquad \Gamma,\kappa:\ AP.\] $BC\perp OM$ and $OH\perp AM$, and $OM\ne AM$ as lines (the triangle is scalene), so $BC$ and $OH$ meet at a point $T$. $T$ has equal power with respect to all three circles, so it lies on the third radical axis $AP$. Hence $AP$, $OH$ and $BC$ pass through $T$.

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers on the unit circle

Take $\Gamma$ as the unit circle. The arc $BC$ not containing $A$ measures $2\angle A=120^\circ$ and $M$ is its midpoint, so we may put \[m=1,\qquad b=e^{i\pi/3},\qquad c=e^{-i\pi/3},\qquad a=e^{i\alpha},\] with $a\ne -1$ ($a=-1$ would give $AB=AC$). Then $o=0$ and $h=a+b+c=a+1$, because $b+c=2\cos60^\circ=1$.

The point $P$. The ray $MH$ has direction $h-m=a$, so its points are $1+sa$ ($s\ge0$). From $|1+sa|^2=1+s(a+\bar a)+s^2=1$ we get $s=0$ (the point $M$) or $s=-(a+\bar a)$; hence \[p=1-(a+\bar a)a=1-a^2-1=-a^2\qquad(\text{using }a\bar a=1).\]

The point $T=OH\cap BC$. $B$ and $C$ have real part $\tfrac12$, so line $BC$ is $\operatorname{Re}z=\tfrac12$. Line $OH$ consists of the points $\lambda(1+a)$, $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, and $\operatorname{Re}\big(\lambda(1+a)\big)=\lambda(1+\operatorname{Re}a)$. Since $(1+a)(1+\bar a)=2+a+\bar a=2(1+\operatorname{Re}a)\ne0$, the intersection is at $\lambda=\dfrac{1}{2(1+\operatorname{Re}a)}=\dfrac{1}{(1+a)(1+\bar a)}$: \[t=\frac{1+a}{(1+a)(1+\bar a)}=\frac{1}{1+\bar a}=\frac{a}{a+1}.\]

Collinearity of $A,T,P$. We have \[t-a=\frac{a-a^2-a}{a+1}=-\frac{a^2}{a+1},\qquad p-a=-a^2-a=-a(a+1),\qquad \frac{t-a}{p-a}=\frac{a}{(a+1)^2}.\] This number is real: its conjugate is $\dfrac{\bar a}{(\bar a+1)^2}=\dfrac{1/a}{(1+a)^2/a^2}=\dfrac{a}{(1+a)^2}$. Therefore $T$ lies on line $AP$ (note $p\ne a$, since $-a^2=a$ would mean $a^3=-1$, i.e. $a\in\{-1,b,c\}$). So $AP$, $OH$, $BC$ all pass through $T$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

Consider the standard triangular grid of unit equilateral triangles forming a larger equilateral triangle of side length $n$. Initially, only the top unit triangle is colored black, and all other unit triangles are colored white.

A move consists of choosing a black unit triangle and simultaneously flipping the colors of all unit triangles that share an edge with it; that is, each such neighboring triangle changes from white to black or from black to white. Determine all positive integers $n$ for which it is possible, after a sequence of moves, for all unit triangles in the grid to be black at the same time.

Show the answer

$n=1$ only

Show the solution – Two parity invariants

For $n=1$ the only triangle is black from the start, so $n=1$ works. Let $n\ge2$.

Call the unit triangles pointing up upward and the others downward. Every neighbour of an upward triangle is downward and vice versa. Number the rows $1,\dots,n$ from the top; row $r$ has $r$ upward and $r-1$ downward triangles, alternating and starting and ending with upward ones. A downward triangle in row $r$ has exactly three neighbours: the two upward triangles beside it in row $r$ and the upward triangle directly above it in row $r-1$.

Consider two sets of upward triangles (see the figure for $n=5$):

  • $L$: the first (leftmost) triangle of each row, $n$ triangles, including the top one;

  • $B$: the $n$ upward triangles of the bottom row (not including the top one, as $n\ge2$).

Key fact: every unit triangle has an even number of neighbours in $L$, and an even number in $B$. An upward triangle has no neighbours in $L$ or $B$ at all. A downward triangle that is first in its row has, as neighbours, the first triangle of its row and the first triangle of the row above: two members of $L$; any other downward triangle has none. A downward triangle in the bottom row has its two side neighbours in $B$ and its upper neighbour outside $B$; downward triangles in other rows have no neighbour in $B$.

A move flips exactly the neighbours of one triangle, so it changes the colour of an even number of triangles of $L$, and of an even number of triangles of $B$. Hence the parity of the number of black triangles in $L$, and the parity of the number of black triangles in $B$, never change.

At the start $L$ contains exactly $1$ black triangle and $B$ contains $0$. If all triangles were black, $L$ would contain $n$ and $B$ would contain $n$ black triangles. For even $n$ this contradicts the invariant for $L$ ($1$ odd, $n$ even); for odd $n\ge3$ it contradicts the invariant for $B$ ($0$ even, $n$ odd).

So the task is possible only for $\boxed{n=1}$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

Let $\sigma(n)$ denote the sum of the divisors of $n$, and let $d(n)$ denote the number of divisors of $n$. Prove that for any nonnegative integer $k$, the equation $\sigma(n)=d(n)^k$ has finitely many solutions.

Show solution 1 – The divisor function grows slower than every root

Fix $k\ge0$. For a prime $p$ and $a\ge0$ put $g(p,a)=\dfrac{(a+1)^{2k}}{p^a}$.

  • If $p>4^k$, then, since $a+1\le2^a$, $g(p,a)\le\dfrac{(2^a)^{2k}}{p^a}=\Big(\dfrac{4^k}{p}\Big)^a\le1$.

  • If $p\le4^k$, then $g(p,a)\to0$ as $a\to\infty$, so $C_p=\max_{a\ge0}g(p,a)$ exists; $C_p\ge g(p,0)=1$.

Let $C=\prod_{p\le4^k}C_p$, a constant depending only on $k$. For $n=\prod p_i^{a_i}$ we have $d(n)=\prod(a_i+1)$, so \[\frac{d(n)^{2k}}{n}=\prod_i g(p_i,a_i)\le C,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad d(n)^k\le\sqrt{Cn}.\] Now suppose $\sigma(n)=d(n)^k$ with $n\ge2$. As $1$ and $n$ are divisors, $\sigma(n)\ge n+1>n$, so $n<\sqrt{Cn}$, i.e. $n<C$. Thus every solution satisfies $n\le\max(1,C)$, and there are only finitely many solutions.

Show solution 2 – Multiplicativity and finitely many admissible prime powers

Fix $k\ge0$. Both $\sigma$ and $d$ are multiplicative, so for $n=\prod_i p_i^{a_i}$ (distinct primes, $a_i\ge1$) the equation $\sigma(n)=d(n)^k$ reads \[\prod_i h(p_i,a_i)=1,\qquad h(p,a)=\frac{\sigma(p^a)}{(a+1)^k}=\frac{1+p+\dots+p^a}{(a+1)^k}>\frac{p^a}{(a+1)^k}.\] Large prime powers give large factors. Since $a\le\log_2(p^a)$, we get $h(p,a)>\dfrac{q}{(1+\log_2q)^k}$ with $q=p^a$, which tends to infinity with $q$. So for every bound $\beta$ only finitely many prime powers $p^a$ have $h(p,a)\le\beta$.

Small factors are bounded below. For each prime $p$ let $\mu_p=\min_{a\ge1}h(p,a)$ (it exists by the previous remark). If $p\ge2^k$, then $(a+1)^k\le2^{ak}\le p^a$, so $h(p,a)>1$ and $\mu_p>1$. Hence $\delta=\prod_p\min(1,\mu_p)$ is a product of finitely many positive numbers, so $\delta>0$, and for every $n$ the product of any subfamily of the factors $h(p_i,a_i)$ is at least $\delta$.

Let $F$ be the finite set of prime powers $p^a$ with $h(p,a)\le1/\delta$. If $n$ had a prime-power factor $p_j^{a_j}\notin F$ (with $p_j^{a_j}\,\|\,n$), then \[\prod_i h(p_i,a_i)=h(p_j,a_j)\prod_{i\ne j}h(p_i,a_i)>\frac1\delta\cdot\delta=1,\] so $n$ is not a solution. Hence every solution is a product of prime powers from the finite set $F$, with distinct primes, and there are only finitely many such numbers.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition

Let $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ be two circles intersecting at distinct points $X$ and $Y$. Let $\ell$ be their common tangent closer to $Y$, intersecting $\omega_1$ at $A$ and $\omega_2$ at $B$. The tangent to $\omega_1$ at $Y$ meets $\omega_2$ again at $C$ with $C\ne Y$. Let the line $XB$ meet $\omega_1$ again at $K$ with $K\ne X$. Let $D=BC\cap AY$, and let $E=YK\cap CX$.

Prove that $D,Y,X,E$ are concyclic. (Four points are concyclic if there exists a circle containing all four points.)

Show solution 1 – Two auxiliary facts: $YK\parallel BC$ and $A,B,X,D$ concyclic

We use directed angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$, written $\measuredangle(m,n)$, so that the argument does not depend on the picture. Recall: four points $P,Q,R,S$ (no three collinear) are concyclic iff $\measuredangle(RP,RQ)=\measuredangle(SP,SQ)$; and the tangent-chord theorem: if $t$ is tangent at $U$ to a circle through $U,V,W$, then $\measuredangle(t,UV)=\measuredangle(WU,WV)$.

Fact 1: $YK\parallel BC$. In $\omega_1$, the line $YC$ is tangent at $Y$, so by the tangent-chord theorem $\measuredangle(YC,YX)=\measuredangle(KY,KX)$. In $\omega_2$, which contains $B,C,X,Y$, we have $\measuredangle(BC,BX)=\measuredangle(YC,YX)$. Hence $\measuredangle(KY,KX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)$, and since $KX$ and $BX$ are the same line, $KY\parallel BC$.

Fact 2: $A,B,X,D$ are concyclic. Lines $DA=AY$ and $DB=BC\parallel YK$, so, using $\omega_1$ (through $A,X,Y,K$) and the fact that $K$ lies on line $XB$, \[\measuredangle(DA,DB)=\measuredangle(YA,YK)=\measuredangle(XA,XK)=\measuredangle(XA,XB).\] Hence $A,B,X,D$ are concyclic.

Conclusion. The line $AB=\ell$ is tangent to $\omega_2$ at $B$, so $\measuredangle(BX,BA)=\measuredangle(YX,YB)$ by the tangent-chord theorem. Then \[\measuredangle(DX,DY)=\measuredangle(DX,DA)=\measuredangle(BX,BA)=\measuredangle(YX,YB)=\measuredangle(CX,CB),\] using the circle $ABXD$ and then the circle $\omega_2$ (through $B,C,X,Y$). On the other side, $EX$ is the line $CX$ and $EY$ is the line $YK\parallel CB$, so \[\measuredangle(EX,EY)=\measuredangle(CX,CB).\] Therefore $\measuredangle(DX,DY)=\measuredangle(EX,EY)$, and $D,Y,X,E$ are concyclic.

Show solution 2 – Inversion at Y and oblique coordinates

We use an inversion centred at $Y$ and write $Z'$ for the image of a point $Z\ne Y$. Recall: a circle through $Y$ becomes a line not through $Y$, a line not through $Y$ becomes a circle through $Y$, a line through $Y$ is mapped to itself, and tangency is preserved. In the image we call the centre $O$ (it is the point $Y$).

The inverted picture. The circles $\omega_1\ni X,A,K$ and $\omega_2\ni X,B,C$ pass through $Y$, so they become two lines through $X'$: the line $X'A'$ (containing $K'$) and the line $X'B'$ (containing $C'$). The line $\ell$ does not pass through $Y$ and touches $\omega_1$ at $A$ and $\omega_2$ at $B$, so $\ell'$ is a circle through $O$ touching the line $X'A'$ at $A'$ and the line $X'B'$ at $B'$. The two tangent segments from $X'$ to this circle are equal: $X'A'=X'B'$. We choose the radius of inversion so that $X'A'=X'B'=1$.

Oblique coordinates. Let $\vec e_1=\overrightarrow{X'B'}$, $\vec e_2=\overrightarrow{X'A'}$ (unit vectors, not parallel) and $g=\vec e_1\cdot\vec e_2$, so $|g|<1$. Write $(x,y)$ for the point $X'+x\vec e_1+y\vec e_2$. Then $X'=(0,0)$, $B'=(1,0)$, $A'=(0,1)$, the squared distance from $X'$ is $Q(x,y)=x^2+y^2+2gxy$, and every circle has an equation $Q(x,y)+\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma=0$ (and conversely such an equation with at least two solutions is a circle). Let $O=(u,v)$. Here $u\ne0$ and $v\ne0$, since $O$ is on neither line $X'A'$ ($x=0$) nor $X'B'$ ($y=0$).

The circle $\ell'$ is $Q(x,y)-2x-2y+1=0$: on $y=0$ it becomes $(x-1)^2=0$ and on $x=0$ it becomes $(y-1)^2=0$, so it touches both lines at $B'$ and $A'$, and a circle tangent to two given lines at two given points is unique. As $O\in\ell'$, $Q(u,v)=2u+2v-1$, which can be rewritten as \[(u+v-1)^2=2(1-g)\,uv. \tag{1}\] In particular $u+v-1\ne0$.

The points $C'$ and $K'$. The tangent to $\omega_1$ at $Y$ is a line through $Y$, so it is mapped to itself; it meets $\omega_1$ only at $Y$, so it does not meet the line $\omega_1'=X'A'$, i.e. it is the line through $O$ parallel to $\vec e_2$, namely $x=u$. Since $C$ lies on it and on $\omega_2$, $C'$ is on $x=u$ and $y=0$: $C'=(u,0)$.

The line $XB$ does not pass through $Y$, so it becomes the circle through $O,X',B'$, of the form $Q(x,y)-x-ky=0$ (it passes through $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$). Putting in $O$ and using $Q(u,v)=2u+2v-1$ gives $k=\frac{u+2v-1}{v}$. The point $K$ is on $XB$ and $\omega_1$, so $K'$ is the second common point of this circle with the line $x=0$, which is $K'=(0,k)$. Note that \[k-1=\frac{u+v-1}{v}\ne0 .\]

The points $D'$ and $E'$. The line $AY$ goes through $Y$, so it is the line $OA'$; its points satisfy $y-v=-\frac{1-v}{u}(x-u)$. The line $BC$ becomes the circle through $O,B',C'$, whose equation is \[(x-u)(x-1+2gy)+y(y-v)=0,\] since this has the form $Q+\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma=0$ and vanishes at $(u,v)$, $(1,0)$, $(u,0)$. On the line $OA'$ we replace $y(y-v)$ by $-\frac{1-v}{u}y(x-u)$; the equation becomes $(x-u)\bigl(x-1+(2g-\frac{1-v}{u})y\bigr)=0$. The factor $x-u$ vanishes at $O$, so $D'$, the second common point, satisfies \[x-1+\Bigl(2g-\frac{1-v}u\Bigr)y=0. \tag{2}\] In the same way, the line $YK$ is the line $OK'$, with points satisfying $y-v=-\frac{k-v}{u}(x-u)$, and the line $CX$ becomes the circle through $O,C',X'$, with equation $(x-u)(x+2gy)+y(y-v)=0$. So $E'$ satisfies \[x+\Bigl(2g-\frac{k-v}u\Bigr)y=0. \tag{3}\] Equation (3) is a line $m$ through $X'$, and $E'$ lies on it. We show that $D'$ lies on $m$ too.

Solving (2) together with the equation of line $OA'$ gives \[y_{D'}=\frac{u(u+v-1)}{u^2-2gu(1-v)+(1-v)^2}.\] The denominator equals $|\overrightarrow{A'O}|^2=Q(u,v-1)$; expanding and using $Q(u,v)=2u+2v-1$ it equals $2(1-g)u$, so by (1), $y_{D'}=\frac{u+v-1}{2(1-g)}=\frac{uv}{u+v-1}=\frac{u}{k-1}$. Subtracting (2) from the left side of (3), at $D'$ the left side of (3) equals \[1-\frac{k-1}{u}\,y_{D'}=0 .\] So $X',D',E'$ lie on the line $m$.

Back to the original picture. The line $m$ does not pass through $O$: otherwise $m$ and the line $OA'$ would both contain $O$ and $D'\ne O$ (note $D\ne Y$, as the line $BC$ meets $\omega_2$ only at $B$ and $C$), so they would coincide and $X'\in m$ would lie on $OA'$; then $O$ would be on the line $X'A'$, which is false. A line not through the centre of inversion is mapped to a circle through $Y$. Hence $X,D,E$ lie on a circle through $Y$: the points $D,Y,X,E$ are concyclic.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

For each positive integer $n$, let $\kappa(n)$ denote the unique squarefree positive integer such that $\dfrac{n}{\kappa(n)}$ is a perfect square. Let $G$ be a connected simple graph whose edges are labeled by positive integers $w_e$. Assume that for every cycle $C$ in $G$, the product $\prod_{e\in C}w_e$ is a perfect square. Prove that there exist squarefree positive integers $s_v$ assigned to the vertices $v\in V(G)$ such that for every edge $uv\in E(G)$ there exists a positive integer $t_{uv}$ with $w_{uv}=\kappa(s_us_v)\,t_{uv}^2$.

Show solution 1 – Potentials along a spanning tree

For positive integers write $x\sim y$ if $xy$ is a perfect square. This is an equivalence relation (if $xy$ and $yz$ are squares, so is $xy\cdot yz/y^2=xz$, a rational square that is an integer, hence a square), $x\sim\kappa(x)$, and $\kappa(x)=\kappa(y)$ if and only if $x\sim y$ (two squarefree numbers whose product is a square are equal). Note also $x^2y\sim y$.

What we must find is squarefree $s_v$ with $\kappa(w_{uv})=\kappa(s_us_v)$, i.e. $w_{uv}\sim s_us_v$, for every edge: then $w_{uv}/\kappa(s_us_v)=w_{uv}/\kappa(w_{uv})$ is a perfect square $t_{uv}^2$ with $t_{uv}$ a positive integer.

$G$ is connected; fix a vertex $r$ and a spanning tree $T$ of $G$. For a vertex $v$ let $\Pi_v$ be the product of the labels on the path from $r$ to $v$ in $T$ ($\Pi_r=1$), and set $s_v=\kappa(\Pi_v)$. So $s_v\sim\Pi_v$.

Tree edges. If $uv\in T$ with $u$ closer to $r$, then $\Pi_v=\Pi_uw_{uv}$, so $s_us_v\sim\Pi_u\Pi_v=\Pi_u^2w_{uv}\sim w_{uv}$.

Other edges. Let $uv\notin T$, and let $z$ be the last common vertex of the tree paths from $r$ to $u$ and to $v$. The tree path from $u$ to $v$ goes through $z$, and together with the edge $uv$ it forms a cycle $C$ of $G$ (it is a simple path that does not use the edge $uv$). Writing $Q$ for the product of the labels on the tree path from $u$ to $v$, the hypothesis says $Qw_{uv}$ is a square, i.e. $Q\sim w_{uv}$. On the other hand $\Pi_u\Pi_v=\Pi_z^2\,Q$, so $s_us_v\sim\Pi_u\Pi_v\sim Q\sim w_{uv}$.

So $w_{uv}\sim s_us_v$ for every edge, as required.

Show solution 2 – One bipartition for each prime

For a prime $p$ call an edge $e$ $p$-odd if the exponent $v_p(w_e)$ is odd. Since $v_p$ of a product is the sum of the $v_p$'s, the hypothesis says: every cycle contains an even number of $p$-odd edges, for every prime $p$. Only the finitely many primes dividing some label have $p$-odd edges at all.

Claim: every closed walk contains an even number of $p$-odd edges (counted with multiplicity). Take a closed walk with an odd number and minimal length. If some vertex appears twice in it (apart from start = end), the walk splits there into two shorter closed walks, one of which has an odd number of $p$-odd edges, contradicting minimality. So no vertex repeats: the walk is either a cycle (excluded by hypothesis) or goes along one edge and back (an even count). Contradiction.

Colouring. Fix a vertex $r$. For a vertex $v$ let $c_p(v)\in\{0,1\}$ be the parity of the number of $p$-odd edges on a walk from $r$ to $v$; by the claim this does not depend on the walk (two walks from $r$ to $v$ form a closed walk). For an edge $uv$, a walk to $u$ followed by the edge $uv$ is a walk to $v$, so $c_p(u)\ne c_p(v)$ exactly when $uv$ is $p$-odd.

Definition of $s_v$. Let $s_v$ be the product of the primes $p$ (among those dividing some label) with $c_p(v)=1$; it is squarefree. For an edge $uv$ and any prime $p$: $v_p(s_us_v)=c_p(u)+c_p(v)$ is odd iff $c_p(u)\ne c_p(v)$ iff $v_p(w_{uv})$ is odd (for primes dividing no label both sides are even). Since $\kappa(x)$ is the product of the primes with odd exponent in $x$, we get $\kappa(s_us_v)=\kappa(w_{uv})$, and $w_{uv}=\kappa(s_us_v)\,t_{uv}^2$ with the positive integer $t_{uv}=\sqrt{w_{uv}/\kappa(w_{uv})}$.

Problem 8

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure · Symmetric and Cyclic Inequalities

For all positive reals $a,b,c$, prove the inequality \[\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ \ge\ \frac32+\frac{(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2}{2(a+b+c)^2}.\] Determine when equality holds.

Show the answer

Equality holds if and only if $a=b=c$

Show solution 1 – Sum of squares form of Nesbitt

First, \[\sum\frac{a}{b+c}-\frac32=\sum\Big(\frac{a}{b+c}-\frac12\Big)=\sum\frac{(a-b)+(a-c)}{2(b+c)},\] where the sums are cyclic over $(a,b,c)$. Collect the two terms containing $a-b$: $\dfrac{a-b}{2(b+c)}+\dfrac{b-a}{2(c+a)}=\dfrac{(a-b)^2}{2(b+c)(c+a)}$. Doing the same for $b-c$ and $c-a$, \[\sum\frac{a}{b+c}-\frac32=\frac{(a-b)^2}{2(b+c)(c+a)}+\frac{(b-c)^2}{2(c+a)(a+b)}+\frac{(c-a)^2}{2(a+b)(b+c)}.\] Since $0<b+c<a+b+c$ and $0<c+a<a+b+c$, we have $(b+c)(c+a)<(a+b+c)^2$, hence $\dfrac{(a-b)^2}{2(b+c)(c+a)}\ge\dfrac{(a-b)^2}{2(a+b+c)^2}$, with equality iff $a=b$; similarly for the other two terms. Adding gives the inequality, and equality holds iff $a=b$, $b=c$ and $c=a$, that is, iff $a=b=c$.

Show solution 2 – Cauchy-Schwarz and the ratio $p^2/q$

Let $p=a+b+c$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in Engel form, \[\sum\frac{a}{b+c}=\sum\frac{a^2}{ab+ac}\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(ab+bc+ca)}=\frac{p^2}{2q}.\] The right-hand side of the problem is $\dfrac32+\dfrac{2p^2-6q}{2p^2}=\dfrac52-\dfrac{3q}{p^2}$, because $(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2=2(p^2-3q)$. With $t=p^2/q$ it suffices to show $\dfrac t2\ge\dfrac52-\dfrac3t$, i.e. \[t^2-5t+6=(t-2)(t-3)\ge0.\] This holds because $t\ge3$, which is $p^2\ge3q$, i.e. $(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\ge0$.

Equality requires $t=3$, i.e. $a=b=c$; conversely, for $a=b=c$ both sides equal $\frac32$. So equality holds iff $a=b=c$.

Show solution 3 – A tangent line at the equality point

Put $s=a+b+c$ and $x=\frac as$, $y=\frac bs$, $z=\frac cs$, so $x,y,z\in(0,1)$, $x+y+z=1$ and $\frac{a}{b+c}=\frac{x}{1-x}$, etc. For $0<x<1$ we have the exact identity \[\frac{x}{1-x}=\frac12+\frac94\Bigl(x-\frac13\Bigr)+\frac94\cdot\frac{(x-\frac13)^2}{1-x},\] which is checked by multiplying by $1-x$: the right side becomes $\frac12(1-x)+\frac94(x-\frac13)(1-x)+\frac94(x-\frac13)^2=\frac12-\frac x2+\frac94(x-\frac13)\cdot\frac23=x$. (The first two terms are the tangent line of $\frac{x}{1-x}$ at $x=\frac13$.) Adding the three identities, the linear terms cancel because $x+y+z=1$, and since $\frac1{1-x}>1$, \[\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ \ge\ \frac32+\frac94\sum\Bigl(x-\frac13\Bigr)^2,\] with equality only if $x=y=z=\frac13$. On the other side, $(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2=3(x^2+y^2+z^2)-(x+y+z)^2=3\sum\bigl(x-\frac13\bigr)^2$, because $\sum(x-\frac13)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2-\frac13$. So the right side of the problem is \[\frac32+\frac{(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2}{2}=\frac32+\frac32\sum\Bigl(x-\frac13\Bigr)^2\le\frac32+\frac94\sum\Bigl(x-\frac13\Bigr)^2 .\] This proves the inequality. Equality forces $x=y=z$, and for $a=b=c$ both sides equal $\frac32$. So equality holds iff $a=b=c$.
