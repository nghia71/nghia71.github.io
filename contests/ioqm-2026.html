Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Coloring Arguments

The central square of a $9\times 9$ chessboard is white. How many white squares are there on the board? (The squares of the chessboard are coloured alternately black and white.)

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a small checkerboard with a white center. Look at the white squares in neighboring rows and see how their positions shift.
Hint 2: key idea
Number the rows and columns from $1$. Moving to a neighboring square changes the parity of the sum of its row and column numbers, so use the white center to decide which parity represents white. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Count which column numbers give white squares in an odd row and which give white squares in an even row. Count the rows of each kind and set up their contributions separately.
Common trap
The two kinds of rows need separate counts on an odd-sized board.
Show the answer

$41$

Show the solution – Colour by parity

Number the rows and columns $1$ to $9$. Two squares have the same colour exactly when the sums row$+$column have the same parity. The central square $(5,5)$ has an even sum and is white, so the white squares are those with an even sum. In each odd row ($5$ of them) the even sums occur in the $5$ odd columns, and in each even row ($4$ of them) in the $4$ even columns. Hence there are $5\cdot5+4\cdot4=\boxed{41}$ white squares.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking · Congruence and Residue Classes

An integer $M$ is divisible by $4$ but not by $256$. What is the number of distinct possible remainders when $M$ is divided by $256$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write an expression for $M$ in terms of its quotient and remainder upon division by $256$. What range must that remainder lie in?
Hint 2: key idea
The quotient times $256$ is a multiple of $4$, as is $M$. Use their difference to restrict the possible remainders. (LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking)
Hint 3: approach
Count the multiples of $4$ in the full remainder range. Use the condition on divisibility by $256$ to decide what to remove, then check that each remaining residue can occur.
Common trap
A remainder of zero means the original integer is divisible by $256$.
Show the answer

$63$

Show the solution – Multiples of 4 modulo 256

Since $4\mid M$ and $4\mid 256$, the remainder $r$ of $M$ modulo $256$ is also a multiple of $4$, so $r\in\{0,4,8,\dots,252\}$, which has $64$ elements. The remainder $r=0$ is impossible because $256\nmid M$, and every other value $r=4k$ ($1\le k\le 63$) occurs for $M=r$. The answer is $\boxed{63}$.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Parity Arguments

If $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{49}$ are non-zero integers such that $\sum_{i=1}^{49}x_i=0$, then what is the minimum possible value of $\sum_{i=1}^{49}x_i^2$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Find the smallest square a nonzero integer can contribute. Try to make a short list of that kind with an odd number of entries and sum zero.
Hint 2: key idea
If every entry has the smallest possible absolute value, the sum of an odd number of such entries is odd. Use this to see why at least one square must contribute more. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Use one entry of absolute value at least two and the smallest square for each other entry to set up a bound. Test whether the bound can be attained by an assignment in which every entry meets its own lower bound with equality while the sum is still zero.
Common trap
The entries may be negative, which matters when making their sum zero.
Show the answer

$52$

Show the solution – Parity bound and construction

If all $|x_i|=1$, then $\sum x_i$ is a sum of $49$ odd numbers, hence odd, not $0$. So some $|x_j|\ge 2$, and \[\sum_{i=1}^{49}x_i^2\ge 4+48\cdot1=52.\] Equality: take $x_1=2$, twenty-three of the numbers equal to $1$ and twenty-five equal to $-1$; the sum is $2+23-25=0$ and the sum of squares is $4+48=52$. The minimum is $\boxed{52}$.

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

All six digits of three $2$-digit numbers are different. If $N$ is the largest possible sum of three such numbers, what is the sum of digits of $N$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Use letters for the tens and units digits in the three numbers. See how the total changes if a larger digit moves from a units place to a tens place through a swap.
Hint 2: key idea
Express the total as ten times the sum of the tens digits plus the sum of the units digits. Bound the tens sum using distinctness, then use the unused digits to bound the units sum in that case. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the best total with the largest tens sum against an upper bound when the tens sum is smaller. In the smaller case, use the largest possible sum of any three distinct units digits. Check that the better bound can be realized by arranging the six digits into three numbers.
Common trap
After maximizing the total, the requested output is its digit sum.
Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution – Tens digits as large as possible

If the tens digits are $t_1,t_2,t_3$ and the units digits $u_1,u_2,u_3$, the sum is $10(t_1+t_2+t_3)+(u_1+u_2+u_3)$, with six distinct digits. The tens digits are distinct, so $t_1+t_2+t_3\le9+8+7=24$. If the tens sum is $24$, the tens digits are $9,8,7$ and the units digits are three of $0,1,\dots,6$, so the sum is at most $240+(6+5+4)=255$. If the tens sum is at most $23$, the sum is at most $230+(9+8+7)=254$. Since $96+85+74=255$, we get $N=255$, and its digit sum is $\boxed{12}$.

Problem 5

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Arcs, Central Angles, and Inscribed Angles

In triangle $ABC$, we are given that $\angle CAB=80^\circ$. Let the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ meet the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$ in $N$, where we assume that $A$ and $N$ lie on the same side of the chord $BC$. Then what is the measure of $\angle NBC$ in degrees?

Hint 1: understand the problem
On a sketch, mark the intersections of the perpendicular bisector with the circle. Identify the one on $A$’s side of $BC$ and draw its segments to $B$ and $C$.
Hint 2: key idea
The perpendicular bisector cuts the arcs between $B$ and $C$ at their midpoints. Compare the inscribed angles subtended by $BC$ at $A$ and at the indicated arc midpoint. (LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the perpendicular bisector to relate $NB$ and $NC$. Transfer the known angle to the vertex at $N$ and set up the angle sum in the resulting isosceles triangle.
Common trap
The side of chord $BC$ determines which arc midpoint to use.
Show the answer

$50$

Show the solution – Midpoint of the arc

The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ passes through the centre and meets the circle at the midpoints of the two arcs $BC$. Since $N$ is on the same side as $A$, $N$ is the midpoint of arc $BAC$. Then $NB=NC$ and, as $A$ and $N$ lie on the same arc, $\angle BNC=\angle BAC=80^\circ$. In the isosceles triangle $NBC$, \[\angle NBC=\tfrac12(180^\circ-80^\circ)=\boxed{50^\circ}.\]

Problem 6

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

Find the number of $2$-digit positive integers $n$ such that $n=26+(a\times b)$, where $a$ and $b$ are the two digits of $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Let $a$ be the tens digit and $b$ the units digit. Write the number in terms of them and record the allowed values for each digit.
Hint 2: key idea
Substitute the place-value expression into the condition and rearrange. Look for a product with factors $a-1$ and $10-b$, matching the terms involving the digits first. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Determine the constant that the product must equal. List the ordered factor pairs within the digit bounds, and translate each admissible pair back into tens and units digits.
Common trap
The tens digit starts at $1$, while the units digit may be zero.
Show the answer

$3$

Show the solution – Factor the condition

Write $n=10a+b$ with $1\le a\le9$, $0\le b\le 9$. The condition $10a+b=26+ab$ becomes \[10a+b-ab-10=16\iff (a-1)(10-b)=16.\] Here $0\le a-1\le 8$ and $1\le 10-b\le 10$, so $(a-1,10-b)\in\{(2,8),(4,4),(8,2)\}$, giving $n=32,56,98$. There are $\boxed{3}$ such numbers.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · Recursive Counting Rules

Find the number of positive integers $n$ satisfying all the following conditions:

  • a) The digits of $n$ lie in the set $\{1,2,4,8\}$. (Digits may be repeated.)

  • b) The sum of the digits is $14$.

  • c) If $1$ occurs as a digit, then it can occur only immediately to the right of $8$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try writing valid and invalid short strings from the allowed digits. Mark what must sit directly before any occurrence of $1$.
Hint 2: key idea
Treat each consecutive pair $81$ as one block. Compare the digit sum of that block with the sums of the other blocks, using both parity and the total available. (LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting)
Hint 3: approach
Test how many $81$ blocks the target could allow. After ruling out impossible blocks, simplify the target and the remaining block sums by their common factor. Set up a recurrence by choosing the last block and counting strings that reach the remaining sum.
Common trap
Give the empty string one way to reach a remaining sum of zero.
Show the answer

$31$

Show the solution – The digit 1 cannot occur

Every $1$ sits right after an $8$, so we may glue it to that $8$ into a block $81$ of digit sum $9$; every $n$ is then a word in the blocks $2,4,8,81$ (sums $2,4,8,9$), and different words give different $n$. A block $81$ is impossible: one block leaves $14-9=5$ for blocks of even sums, and two blocks exceed $14$. So $n$ uses only the digits $2,4,8$, and halving them, we count ordered sums of $1,2,4$ equal to $7$. With $g(m)=g(m-1)+g(m-2)+g(m-4)$, $g(0)=1$ and $g(m)=0$ for $m<0$: \[g(1),\dots,g(7)=1,2,3,6,10,18,31.\] The answer is $\boxed{31}$.

Problem 8

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · Quadrilateral Families

In trapezium $ABCD$, it is given that $AB$ is parallel to $CD$. Assume that $AB=3CD$, $CD=DA$ and $\angle CDA=120^\circ$. If the largest angle of $ABCD$ is $x^\circ$ and the smallest angle is $y^\circ$, what is the value of $x/y$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the longer parallel side below the shorter one. Mark the equal side lengths and the given angle inside the trapezium.
Hint 2: key idea
Use parallelism to relate the angles at $A$ and $D$. Drop perpendiculars from $D$ and $C$ to $AB$ to create two right triangles with the same height. (LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Choose $CD$ as the unit of length and use the right triangle at $A$ to find its horizontal leg and height. Subtract the known horizontal lengths from $AB$ to find the horizontal leg next to $B$, then use the ratio of height to that leg to identify the angle there. Use parallelism to complete the list of angles before choosing the extremes.
Common trap
Compare the interior angles of the trapezium, including the angles at the ends of the shorter base.
Show the answer

$5$

Show the solution – Angles of a trapezium

Since $AB\parallel CD$, $\angle DAB=180^\circ-120^\circ=60^\circ$. Take $CD=DA=1$, $AB=3$, and let the perpendiculars from $D$ and $C$ meet $AB$ at $D'$, $C'$. In the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle $ADD'$, $AD'=\tfrac12$ and $DD'=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. Then $C'B=3-\tfrac12-1=\tfrac32$, so in the right triangle $BCC'$, $\tan\angle B=\frac{\sqrt3/2}{3/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ and $\angle B=30^\circ$, $\angle C=150^\circ$. The angles are $60^\circ,30^\circ,150^\circ,120^\circ$, so $x/y=150/30=\boxed{5}$.

Problem 9

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Leading-Digit Constraints

Let $N$ be the smallest positive integer whose digits add up to $2026$. What is the leading digit of $N+1$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try finding the smallest positive integer with digit sum $26$. First compare numbers with different lengths, then compare numbers with the same length from left to right.
Hint 2: key idea
A digit can contribute at most $9$ to the sum. Bound the number of digits first, then decide how small the leading digit can be when the other digits are allowed their largest contributions. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Divide the target digit sum by $9$ to find the least possible length and the sum that must be left for the first digit when all later digits are maximal. Write the resulting digit pattern and trace the carry from the units position when adding one.
Common trap
The carry can pass through an entire run of trailing nines.
Show the answer

$2$

Show the solution – Fewest digits first

A number with $k$ digits has digit sum at most $9k$, so $N$ needs at least $\lceil2026/9\rceil=226$ digits. Among $226$-digit numbers with digit sum $2026=9\cdot225+1$, the smallest has the smallest possible leading digit: the other $225$ digits add up to at most $2025$, so the leading digit is at least $1$, and $N=1\underbrace{99\dots9}_{225}$. Then $N+1=2\underbrace{00\dots0}_{225}$, whose leading digit is $\boxed{2}$.

Problem 10

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 39: Perfect Powers and Prime Exponents · Squares, Cubes, and Higher Powers

What is the number of integers in the set $\{0,\dots,20\}$ which can be expressed as the sum of two square integers?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try forming a few values near the bottom of the range by adding two squares. Include an example in which both squares come from the same integer.
Hint 2: key idea
Both squares are nonnegative, so each must lie within the given range. Make a short list of those squares before considering their pairwise sums. (LPS VI, Chapter 39: Perfect Powers and Prime Exponents)
Hint 3: approach
For each possible smaller square, pair it with every square at least as large, stopping when the sum exceeds the bound. Collect the resulting integers without repeats.
Common trap
Count distinct sums, since different pairs of squares can produce the same integer.
Show the answer

$13$

Show the solution – List them

Using the squares $0,1,4,9,16$, the sums $u^2+v^2\le 20$ are \[0,1,2,4,5,8,9,10,13,16,17,18,20.\] (The other integers $3,6,7,11,12,14,15,19$ are not of this form, as a quick check of the squares below them shows.) There are $\boxed{13}$.

Problem 11

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Reconstruction from Functional Conditions

Find the number of non-constant polynomials $P(x)$, with real coefficients, such that $P(x^2)=P(P(x))$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
In each side of the equation, circle the entire input to the outer $P$. Follow what happens to the leading term when each input is substituted.
Hint 2: key idea
If $P$ has degree $d$, find the degree of each composition and compare them. Use the resulting restriction before introducing coefficients. (LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation)
Hint 3: approach
Write a general polynomial of the degree you found and subtract the two compositions. Factor out the difference of their inputs, then consider which factor can vanish identically. Use coefficient comparison in each case and check the candidates in the original equation.
Common trap
A factor must vanish as a polynomial identity, meaning all of its coefficients must vanish.
Show the answer

$2$

Show the solution – Degree, then factor

If $\deg P=d\ge1$, comparing degrees gives $2d=d^2$, so $d=2$; write $P(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ with $a\ne0$. Then \[P(P(x))-P(x^2)=a\bigl(P(x)^2-x^4\bigr)+b\bigl(P(x)-x^2\bigr)=\bigl(P(x)-x^2\bigr)\bigl(a(P(x)+x^2)+b\bigr).\] A product of polynomials is $0$ only if a factor is $0$. Either $P(x)=x^2$, or $a(P(x)+x^2)+b=0$, i.e. $P(x)=-x^2-\tfrac ba$ is constant plus $-x^2$; then $a=-1$, the $x$-coefficient gives $b=0$, so $P(x)=-x^2$. Both $x^2$ and $-x^2$ work, so there are $\boxed{2}$ polynomials.

Problem 12

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Parity Arguments

A $7\times 7$ board is divided into $49$ unit squares. We place checkers on the board, at most one per square. Find the largest number of checkers that can be placed on the unit squares so that each row, as well as each column, contains an even number of checkers.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Imagine filling every square, then check the checker totals in one row and one column. What has to change in both directions?
Hint 2: key idea
Each row has odd length but needs an even checker count. Work out the minimum number of empty squares forced in each row to get an upper bound. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Use the row bound across the whole board. Then look for a placement of the forced empty squares in which every column also receives exactly the number of empty squares it needs, and check the resulting parities.
Common trap
An arrangement achieving the row bound must also satisfy the column condition.
Show the answer

$42$

Show the solution – One empty square per row

A row has $7$ squares and must contain an even number of checkers, so at most $6$; hence at most $7\cdot6=42$ checkers. Leaving exactly the main diagonal empty gives $6$ checkers in every row and every column, so $\boxed{42}$ is attained.

Problem 13

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Special Right Triangles

In an isosceles triangle $ABC$, with $\angle ACB=90^\circ$. The point $D$ is on the side $BC$ such that $\angle ADC=75^\circ$. If the area of triangle $ADC$ is $81$, what is the length of segment $BD$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the two equal legs of $ABC$. On $BC$, identify which part is a leg of the smaller right triangle and which part you are asked to find.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the angle at $D$ in right triangle $ACD$ to express $CD$ in terms of $AC$. You can find the needed trigonometric ratio by writing the angle as $45^\circ+30^\circ$. (LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework)
Hint 3: approach
Call the common leg length $a$ and use the area of $ACD$ to find an expression for $a^2$. Express $BD$ as the difference between the full leg and $CD$. Square that difference and substitute the expression for $a^2$ before simplifying.
Common trap
The given area uses $CD$ as a leg, while the requested length is $BD$.
Show the answer

$18$

Show solution 1 – Using $\tan 75^\circ=2+\sqrt3$

Let $AC=BC=a$. In the right triangle $ACD$, $CD=AC\cot75^\circ=a(2-\sqrt3)$. The area condition gives $\tfrac12a\cdot a(2-\sqrt3)=81$, so $a^2=162(2+\sqrt3)$. Then $BD=a-CD=a(\sqrt3-1)$ and \[BD^2=162(2+\sqrt3)(4-2\sqrt3)=324(2+\sqrt3)(2-\sqrt3)=324,\] so $BD=\boxed{18}$.

Show solution 2 – An isosceles triangle with a $30^\circ$ angle

$\angle CAD=15^\circ$ and $\angle DAB=45^\circ-15^\circ=30^\circ$, $\angle ADB=105^\circ$, $\angle B=45^\circ$. Drop the perpendicular $DH$ from $D$ to $AB$; then $DH=BD/\sqrt2$, and in triangle $ADH$ with $\angle DAH=30^\circ$, $AD=2DH=\sqrt2\,BD$. The area of $ADC$ is $\tfrac12AC\cdot CD=81$. Since $\angle CAD=15^\circ$, $AC\cdot CD=AD^2\sin15^\circ\cos15^\circ=\tfrac14AD^2$. Hence $AD^2=4\cdot162=648$, and $BD^2=AD^2/2=324$, $BD=\boxed{18}$.

Problem 14

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Rearrangement

Let $A$ be a $3$-digit number with distinct nonzero digits and $B$ be the number obtained by reversing the digits of $A$. Determine the largest possible prime factor of $|A-B|$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a sample three-digit number and its reversal one below the other. Which digit stays in the same position?
Hint 2: key idea
Expand both numbers by place value and subtract. Collect the terms involving the first and last digits so that all the digit dependence is in a single difference. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Factor the constant multiplier in the difference. Bound the nonzero absolute difference of the two digits and compare its possible prime divisors with those of the multiplier.
Common trap
The first and last digits are distinct, so their difference is nonzero.
Show the answer

$11$

Show the solution – $|A-B|=99|a-c|$

If $A=\overline{abc}$ then $B=\overline{cba}$ and $|A-B|=99|a-c|=3^2\cdot11\cdot|a-c|$ with $1\le|a-c|\le 8$. The prime factors of $|a-c|$ are at most $7$, so the largest possible prime factor is $\boxed{11}$ (it always occurs).

Problem 15

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Let $a,b,c,d$ be positive integers such that $a^2+b^2-cd^2=2026$. Find the minimum possible value of $a+b+c+d$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Rearrange the equation so that the difference between the sum of two squares and $2026$ is visible. What does positivity say about that difference?
Hint 2: key idea
Fix $t=a+b$ and ask how large $a^2+b^2$ can be for that $t$. Use $a^2+b^2=t^2-2ab$ and think about when the product $ab$ is smallest. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Use the bound for fixed $t$ to start the search near the smallest eligible sums, then test positive excesses above $2026$ by looking for square divisors that could serve as $d^2$. Use the best total found to restrict $c+d$ for larger $t$. In the boundary case with $c=d=1$, check which remainders are possible for a sum of two squares modulo $4$.
Common trap
Even when an excess can be written as $cd^2$ in several ways, you need the one that gives the smallest $c+d$.
Show the answer

$51$

Show the solution – Bound $a+b$ first

Since $a^2+b^2=2026+cd^2>2026$ and $a^2+b^2\le(a+b)^2$, we need $a+b\ge46$. Also $(a+b)^2-2ab=a^2+b^2$, and for fixed $a+b=t$ the largest value of $a^2+b^2$ is $1+(t-1)^2$.

  • $t=46$: $a^2+b^2\le1+45^2=2026$, impossible.

  • $t=47$: $a^2+b^2\ge2027$ forces $\{a,b\}=\{1,46\}$ ($cd^2=91$, so $c+d\ge 92$) or $\{2,45\}$ ($cd^2=3$, so $c=3$, $d=1$), the best being $47+4=51$.

  • $t=48$: only $\{1,47\},\{2,46\},\{3,45\}$ give at least $2027$, with $cd^2=184,94,8$; the smallest $c+d$ is $4$ (from $8=2\cdot2^2$), total $52$.

  • $t\ge 49$: a total of at most $51$ needs $c+d\le 2$, i.e. $c=d=1$ and $t=49$; then $a^2+b^2=2027\equiv3\pmod4$, impossible for a sum of two squares.

So the minimum is $\boxed{51}$, attained by $2^2+45^2-3\cdot1^2=2026$.

Problem 16

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

A sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ of real numbers satisfies \[\frac{a_{n+3}-a_{n+2}}{a_n-a_{n+1}}=\frac{a_{n+3}+a_{n+2}}{a_n+a_{n+1}}\] for all $n\ge 1$. Suppose $a_{55}=6$, $a_{66}=2$ and $a_{77}=1$. Let $N$ denote the sum $a_1^2+a_2^2+\dots+a_{2026}^2$. What is the sum of the digits of $N$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the sums and differences in the two fractions. Write down the condition on neighbouring terms that keeps both denominators defined.
Hint 2: key idea
Cross-multiply and cancel matching terms to obtain an equality between two products. Look for a product of terms two indices apart that stays fixed as the index moves. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Rule out a zero product using a known nonzero term and the impossibility of consecutive zeros. Compare two equal products with a shared term to get a repetition rule, then use the supplied indices to fill one period. Split the square sum into whole periods and its leftover terms.
Common trap
Establish that the shared term is nonzero before cancelling it.
Show the answer

$15$

Show the solution – The sequence has period $4$

Cross-multiplying and expanding, \[(a_{n+3}-a_{n+2})(a_n+a_{n+1})=(a_{n+3}+a_{n+2})(a_n-a_{n+1})\iff a_{n+3}a_{n+1}=a_{n+2}a_n.\] So $a_{n+2}a_n$ is a constant $K$ for all $n\ge1$. The denominators $a_n\pm a_{n+1}$ are nonzero, so two consecutive terms are never both $0$. If $K=0$, then $a_{55}a_{57}=0$ gives $a_{57}=0$, hence $a_{56},a_{58}\ne0$, contradicting $a_{56}a_{58}=0$. So $K\ne0$, every term is nonzero, and $a_{n+4}a_{n+2}=K=a_{n+2}a_n$ gives $a_{n+4}=a_n$. Since $55\equiv3$, $66\equiv2$, $77\equiv1\pmod4$: $a_1=1$, $a_2=2$, $a_3=6$, $K=a_1a_3=6$, $a_4=K/a_2=3$. The sequence $1,2,6,3,1,2,6,3,\dots$ indeed satisfies the condition (all denominators are nonzero). As $2026=4\cdot506+2$, \[N=506(1+4+36+9)+1+4=25305,\] whose digit sum is $\boxed{15}$.

Problem 17

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control · Basic Inclusion-Exclusion

Find the number of ordered triples $(x,y,z)$ of positive integers such that $1\le x,y,z\le 8$ and \[|x-y|+|y-z|+|z-x|=8.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Put three sample values in increasing order on a number line. Mark the three distances and notice how the longest one relates to the shorter ones.
Hint 2: key idea

Problem 18

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

Let $P$ be a regular polygon with $8$ vertices. By a labelling of $P$ we mean an assignment of integers $1,2,\dots,8$ to the vertices in some order. A labelling is considered good if the path consisting of line segments $1$ to $2$, $2$ to $3$, $\dots$ and $7$ to $8$ does not self-intersect. If $N$ is the number of good labellings, what is the remainder when $N$ is divided by $100$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a few possible first steps on an octagon. For each one, mark where the unused vertices lie relative to the segment just drawn.
Hint 2: key idea
Track whether the visited vertices form one consecutive block around the boundary. If a new segment puts unused vertices on both sides of its chord, ask how the rest of the path can visit both sides. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Starting with label $1$, show by induction that a noncrossing path must extend the current block at one of its ends. Conversely, for each such extension, check whether any other path segment could have endpoints on opposite open arcs of its chord. Count the choices for the first vertex, the intermediate extensions, and the last vertex separately.
Common trap
A path segment may be a diagonal even when the new vertex is next to the visited block.
Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution – The visited vertices always form an arc

Claim: a labelling is good exactly when, for every $k$, the vertices labelled $1,\dots,k$ form a block of consecutive vertices of the octagon. Then label $1$ can be anywhere ($8$ ways), each of the labels $2,\dots,7$ goes to one of the two vertices next to the block ($2$ ways each), and label $8$ to the last vertex: $N=8\cdot2^6=512$.

Necessity. Suppose labels $1,\dots,k$ form a block, with $k$ at one of its ends (true for $k=1$, and kept by induction), and label $k+1$ goes to a vertex $v$ not next to the block. Then the unvisited vertices other than $v$ lie on both sides of the chord from $k$ to $v$. The path must later go from one side to the other, along a segment joining two vertices on opposite sides of that chord; in a convex polygon such a segment crosses the chord, and it does not share an endpoint with segment $k\,(k+1)$. So the path self-intersects.

Sufficiency. Suppose each new vertex is next to the block; then the newest label is always at an end of the block. Consider a segment $k\,(k+1)$. If $k+1$ is next to $k$, this segment is a side of the octagon and cannot be crossed. Otherwise $k+1$ is attached at the other end of the block, so the arc from $k$ to $k+1$ through the block contains exactly the vertices labelled below $k$, and the other arc exactly those labelled above $k+1$. A segment $j\,(j+1)$ crossing $k\,(k+1)$ would need one endpoint on each arc, i.e. $j<k$ and $j+1>k+1$, which is impossible. So the path does not self-intersect.

Hence $N=512$ and the remainder is $\boxed{12}$.

Problem 19

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

Four points $A,B,C$ and $D$ lie on a straight line, in this order. A point $E$, not on the line, satisfies $\angle AEB=\angle BEC=\angle CED=45^\circ$. Let $F$ and $G$ be the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$, respectively. If $\angle FEG=x^\circ$, what is the value of $x$?

Draw the rays from $E$ in the order in which they meet the line, and mark their equal angular gaps. Place the two midpoints and compare their order along the line.
Hint 2: key idea

Hint 3: approach
Use the equal lengths from the midpoints to relate $\angle FEC$ and $\angle GEB$ to the base angles of triangle $EBC$. Find the sum of those base angles from the angle at $E$. Measure both rays $EF$ and $EG$ from $EA$ so you can compare their directions.
Common trap
The rays $EF$ and $EG$ need not sit in separate gaps between the original rays.
Show the answer

$90$

Show solution 1 – $F$ and $G$ are circumcentres

$\angle AEC=\angle BED=90^\circ$, so $F$, the midpoint of the hypotenuse $AC$, is the circumcentre of the right triangle $AEC$, and $G$ is the circumcentre of $BED$. Hence $FE=FC$ and $GE=GB$, which give \[\angle FEC=\angle ECB,\qquad \angle GEB=\angle EBC.\] In triangle $EBC$, $\angle EBC+\angle ECB=180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ$. The ray $EF$ lies between $EA$ and $EC$ and the ray $EG$ between $EB$ and $ED$; measuring angles at $E$ from the ray $EA$, the ray $EF$ is at $90^\circ-\angle FEC$ and the ray $EG$ at $45^\circ+\angle GEB$. Therefore \[\angle FEG=45^\circ+\angle EBC-(90^\circ-\angle ECB)=135^\circ-45^\circ=90^\circ,\] so $x=\boxed{90}$.

Show solution 2 – Points on a line as tangents of angles

Choose coordinates with $E=(0,1)$ and the line $y=0$, the points $A,B,C,D$ having increasing abscissas. For a point $X=(x,0)$ let $\varphi_X$ be the signed angle from the downward vertical ray of $E$ to the ray $EX$, measured towards the positive $x$-axis; then $-90^\circ<\varphi_X<90^\circ$ and $x=\tan\varphi_X$, and $\varphi_X$ increases with $x$.

Put $\beta=\varphi_B$ and $t=\tan\beta$. The three given $45^\circ$ angles give \[\varphi_A=\beta-45^\circ,\qquad \varphi_C=\beta+45^\circ,\qquad \varphi_D=\beta+90^\circ.\] All four angles lie in $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$, so $-45^\circ<\beta<0^\circ$ and $-1<t<0$; in particular $t\ne0,\pm1$. (In angle terms: $x_F=\tan2\beta$ and $x_G=-\cot2\beta=\tan(2\beta+90^\circ)$, so the rays $EF$ and $EG$ make angles $2\beta$ and $2\beta+90^\circ$ with the vertical.) Thus $\angle FEG=90^\circ$ and $x=\boxed{90}$.

Problem 21

Hint 1: understand the problem

Hint 2: key idea

Hint 3: approach

Common trap
The problem asks for distinct expression values, so several solutions yielding the same absolute value contribute only once.
Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution – Subtract the equations

Write the first few terms of each sequence using its starting values. Mark the conditions that restrict the initial values or the common tenth term.
Hint 2: key idea
Track the coefficients of $a_1$ and $a_2$ as the first sequence grows, and use repeated doubling for the second sequence. Equating the tenth terms gives a linear equation in the starting values. (LPS VII, Chapter 16: Diophantine Equations: Linear and Structural Foundations)
Hint 3: approach
Use the bound on the common tenth term to list possible values of $b_1$. For each value, reduce the linear equation modulo the coefficient of $a_1$ to restrict $a_2$, then recover $a_1$ and check positivity and strict increase.
Common trap
Solving the equation for positive starting values is insufficient unless $a_1<a_2$.
Show the answer

$33$

Show the solution – Fibonacci coefficients

By induction $a_n=F_{n-2}a_1+F_{n-1}a_2$ (with $F_1=F_2=1$), so $a_{10}=21a_1+34a_2$; also $b_{10}=512b_1$. Since $a_{10}<2026$, $b_1\in\{1,2,3\}$. Strict increase of $(a_n)$ means $a_1<a_2$ (later terms then increase automatically).

  • $b_1=1$: $21a_1+34a_2=512$; modulo $21$, $13a_2\equiv8$; since $13\cdot13=169\equiv1$, this gives $a_2\equiv13\cdot8\equiv20\pmod{21}$. But $34a_2<512$ forces $a_2\le15$. No solution.

  • $b_1=2$: $21a_1+34a_2=1024$; $a_2\equiv 19\pmod{21}$ (since $13a_2\equiv 16$), $a_2=19$, $a_1=18$. Valid.

  • $b_1=3$: $21a_1+34a_2=1536$; $a_2\equiv 18\pmod{21}$ (since $13a_2\equiv 3$), and $a_2=18$ gives $a_1=44>a_2$ (invalid), $a_2=39$ gives $a_1=10$. Valid.

The values of $a_1+b_1$ are $20$ and $13$, with sum $\boxed{33}$.

Problem 29

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Orders and Primitive Behavior

Let $n=\dfrac{4^{31}-1}{3}$. Find the remainder when $2^{n-1}$ is divided by $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Rewrite the defining equation with no fraction. Think about how the power of $4$ in it is related to powers of the base $2$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use $3n$ to find a power of $2$ with a very simple residue modulo $n$. Then reduce the exponent $n-1$ modulo the exponent of that power. (LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Rewrite $n-1$ as $4(2^{60}-1)/3$ and find its remainders modulo $2$ and modulo $31$, using the cycle of powers of $2$ modulo $31$. Combine them to find $n-1$ modulo the exponent from $3n$, then reduce $2^{n-1}$ modulo $n$.
Common trap
Division by $3$ is valid modulo $31$ because $3$ is invertible there, but track it carefully when reducing $n-1$.
Show the answer

$1$

Show the solution – $62$ divides $n-1$

We have $3n=4^{31}-1=2^{62}-1$, so $n\mid 2^{62}-1$, i.e. $2^{62}\equiv1\pmod n$. Next, \[n-1=\frac{2^{62}-4}{3}=\frac{4(2^{60}-1)}{3}.\] $2^{60}-1$ is divisible by $3$ ($2^2\equiv1$) and by $31$ ($2^5\equiv1\pmod{31}$), so $n-1=4\cdot\frac{2^{60}-1}{3}$ is divisible by $2\cdot31=62$. Hence $2^{n-1}=(2^{62})^{(n-1)/62}\equiv1\pmod n$, and the remainder is $\boxed{1}$.

Problem 30

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

In triangle $ABC$, it is given that $\angle CAB=50^\circ$ and $\angle ABC=70^\circ$. Points $D$ and $E$ are chosen on sides $BC$ and $AC$ respectively such that $\angle ABE=\angle DAB=30^\circ$. If $\angle DEB=x^\circ$, what is the value of $x$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $AD$ and $BE$ inside the triangle and mark their intersection $P$. Transfer the given angle markings to the smaller triangles around $P$.
Hint 2: key idea
Triangle $ABP$ gives a common length you can use as a unit. Apply the sine rule in $APE$ and $BPD$ to express $PE$ and $PD$ in that unit. (LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion)
Hint 3: approach
Find the angles needed for the two sine-rule calculations and simplify the ratio $PD/PE$ using supplementary angles. In $PDE$, express its other unknown angle in terms of $x$ and write the sine-rule equation for the same ratio. Check whether this ratio can take a given value more than once on the allowed interval for $x$.
Common trap
At $P$, choose the angle belonging to the triangle being used, since adjacent angles there are supplementary.
Show the answer

$40$

Show the solution – Law of sines in triangle $PDE$

$\angle C=60^\circ$. Let $P=AD\cap BE$. In triangle $ABP$ both base angles are $30^\circ$, so $PA=PB$ (take it as $1$) and $\angle APB=120^\circ$. In triangle $APE$: $\angle PAE=50^\circ-30^\circ=20^\circ$, $\angle APE=60^\circ$, so $\angle AEP=100^\circ$ and $PE=\frac{\sin20^\circ}{\sin100^\circ}$. In triangle $BPD$: $\angle PBD=70^\circ-30^\circ=40^\circ$, $\angle BPD=60^\circ$, $\angle BDP=80^\circ$, so $PD=\frac{\sin40^\circ}{\sin80^\circ}$. Since $\sin100^\circ=\sin80^\circ$, \[\frac{PD}{PE}=\frac{\sin40^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}=2\cos20^\circ.\] In triangle $PDE$, $\angle DPE=120^\circ$; let $x=\angle PED$, so $\angle PDE=60^\circ-x$, and by the law of sines $\frac{\sin x}{\sin(60^\circ-x)}=\frac{PD}{PE}=\frac{\sin 40^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}$. The left side increases with $x$ on $(0^\circ,60^\circ)$ and equals the right side at $x=40^\circ$. Hence $\angle DEB=40^\circ$, so $x=\boxed{40}$.
