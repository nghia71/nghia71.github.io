Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Constructive Counting

A building has $n\ge2$ doors numbered from $1$ to $n$. Two doors are connected if their numbers share at least one digit, regardless of position. Daniel is going to explore the building. On the first move, he will enter a door and exit through any connected door (possibly the same one). On each subsequent move, he will enter the door he just exited in the previous move and then exit through any connected door (again, possibly the same one). Daniel must enter the $i$-th door at least $i$ times during his tour, for every $1\le i\le n$.

  • a) If $n=100$, what is the smallest number of moves he needs to achieve his goal?

  • b) Find the smallest value of $n$ for which he can achieve his goal.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Record a tour as a list of doors entered. Check which neighbouring entries are allowed and whether Daniel can repeat a door immediately.
Hint 2: key idea
Each move supplies one entry, so the required frequencies give a lower bound. For a construction, find doors that can serve as hubs for short return trips and leave room to repeat each door up to its quota. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
For part a), choose a few hub doors that together connect to every door, and plan the tour so that each hub's re-entries fit within its own quota. For the smallest building, note that all doors must lie in one connected piece. Track which single-digit doors have no other door sharing a digit as $n$ grows, and test the first $n$ at which this obstruction disappears.
Common trap
Returning to a hub counts as another entry and uses part of its quota.
Show the answer

a) $5050$; b) $n=19$

Show solution 1 – Count the entries, then build a tour around hub doors

In each move Daniel enters exactly one door, so in $k$ moves he makes exactly $k$ entries. If $w_1,w_2,\dots,w_k$ are the doors he enters, then $w_{j+1}$ is the door through which he left in move $j$, so consecutive doors $w_j,w_{j+1}$ are connected (or equal). Conversely, any such sequence, followed by one more exit through $w_k$ itself, is a valid tour of $k$ moves. So a tour is just a sequence of doors in which consecutive doors are equal or connected; and since a door shares its digits with itself, he may always repeat the current door.

a) Door $i$ must be entered at least $i$ times, so the number of moves is at least $1+2+\cdots+100=5050$.

To reach $5050$ we build a sequence in which each door $v$ appears at most $v$ times, and then repeat doors in place. An excursion from $u$ to $v$ (with $u$, $v$ connected) means going $u\to v\to u$. Start at door $100$.

  • From $100$, make an excursion to each of the other $26$ doors containing the digit $0$ or $1$, except $91$: these are $1$, $10,\dots,19$, $20,30,\dots,90$ and $21,31,\dots,81$; each shares the digit $0$ or $1$ with $100$.

  • Go $100\to91$. From $91$, make an excursion to $9$, $29,39,\dots,89$ and $99$ (they share the digit $9$).

  • For $d=2,3,\dots,8$: go $91\to9d$; from $9d$ make an excursion to every door all of whose digits lie in $\{2,\dots,8\}$ and whose smallest digit is $d$ (they share the digit $d$ with $9d$); then go back $9d\to91$.

Every door from $1$ to $100$ occurs: a door containing $0$ or $1$ is reached in the first item (or is $91$ or $100$), a door containing $9$ and neither $0$ nor $1$ is $9$, $29,\dots,89$, $92,\dots,99$, and every other door has all digits in $\{2,\dots,8\}$. Each door other than $100,91,92,\dots,98$ appears exactly once. Door $100$ appears $1+26=27$ times (the start and one return after each excursion), door $91$ appears $1+9+7=17$ times (one arrival, returns from the $9$ doors $9,29,\dots,89,99$, and returns from $92,\dots,98$), and door $9d$ appears at most $1+14=15$ times, because at most $14$ doors have all digits in $\{2,\ldots,8\}$ and smallest digit $d$. All these numbers are at most the door numbers.

Finally, at the first occurrence of each door $v$, insert extra repetitions of $v$ (moves “enter $v$, exit through $v$”) until $v$ occurs exactly $v$ times. The sequence now has exactly $5050$ entries, so Daniel needs exactly $\boxed{5050}$ moves.

b) All doors entered in a tour form a chain of connected doors, so all $n$ doors must lie in one “connected piece” of the building.

  • If $2\le n\le 9$: the doors $1$ and $2$ have one digit each and share no digit with any door, so they cannot both be entered.

  • If $10\le n\le18$: door $9$ shares a digit only with doors containing a $9$, and the only such door up to $18$ is $9$ itself. Door $9$ cannot be entered together with door $1$.

For $n=19$ the goal is reachable: each of the doors $10,\dots,19$ is connected to door $1$, and each door $d\in\{2,\dots,9\}$ is connected to door $1d$. The sequence $1,12,2,12,1,13,3,13,1,\dots,1,19,9,19,1,10,1,11$ enters every door; then repeat doors in place until door $i$ has been entered $i$ times. The answer is $\boxed{n=19}$.

Show solution 2 – A path through every door once

A tour of $k$ moves is a list $w_1,w_2,\dots,w_k$ of the doors Daniel enters, in which any two consecutive doors are equal or share a digit (after entering $w_j$ he leaves through $w_{j+1}$, and in the last move he leaves through $w_k$ itself). Conversely, every such list gives a tour.

a) Each move contains exactly one entry, and door $i$ needs at least $i$ entries, so at least $1+2+\cdots+100=5050$ moves are needed.

For the construction, group the two-digit doors into decades: for $x=1,\dots,9$, the decade $D_x$ consists of the doors $10x$, $10x+1$, …, $10x+9$. Any two doors of $D_x$ share the tens digit $x$, so the doors of a decade can be listed in any order. List the doors as \[1,\ \underbrace{10,\dots,12}_{D_1},\ 2,\ \underbrace{21,\dots,23}_{D_2},\ 3,\ \underbrace{32,\dots,34}_{D_3},\ 4,\ \dots\]\[\dots,\ 8,\ \underbrace{87,\dots,89}_{D_8},\ 9,\ \underbrace{98,\dots,90}_{D_9},\ 100,\] where for $x\le8$ the block $D_x$ starts with $10x+(x-1)$, ends with $10x+(x+1)$ and has the other eight doors of $D_x$ in between, and the block $D_9$ starts with $98$ and ends with $90$. Every junction shares a digit: door $x$ and the first door $10x+(x-1)$ of $D_x$ share $x$; the last door $10x+(x+1)$ of $D_x$ and door $x+1$ share $x+1$; and $90$ and $100$ share $0$. So this is a list of all $100$ doors, each exactly once, with consecutive doors connected.

Now replace each door $v$ in the list by $v$ consecutive copies of $v$ (“enter $v$, leave through $v$”). Consecutive entries are still equal or connected, and door $v$ is entered exactly $v$ times. This tour has exactly $5050$ moves, so the minimum is $\boxed{5050}$.

b) Consecutive doors of a tour share a digit. So if the doors can be split into two nonempty groups such that no door of one group shares a digit with a door of the other, a tour can never pass from one group to the other, and the goal is impossible.

  • For $2\le n\le9$, door $1$ shares no digit with any other door: take the groups $\{1\}$ and $\{2,\dots,n\}$.

  • For $10\le n\le18$, no door other than $9$ contains the digit $9$: take the groups $\{9\}$ and $\{1,\dots,8,10,\dots,n\}$.

For $n=19$ consider the list \[12,2,12,\ 13,3,13,\ 14,4,14,\ \dots,\ 19,9,19,\ 10,1,10,\ 11 .\] Consecutive doors are connected, because the doors $10,\dots,19$ all contain the digit $1$ and door $d$ stands only next to $1d$. The list contains every door from $1$ to $19$, each door $v$ at most $v$ times, so adding repetitions as in a) gives a tour in which door $i$ is entered exactly $i$ times. Hence the smallest $n$ is $\boxed{n=19}$.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Angle Bisectors and Incenter

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. A circle $\Gamma$ with center on the segment $AC$ is tangent to $AB$ at $D$ and to $BC$ at $E$. Let $F$ and $G$ be the points where $\Gamma$ intersects the line $AC$ such that $A$, $F$, $G$ and $C$ lie in that order. Let $P$ be the point on the segment $AD$ such that $PD=AF$. Let $Q$ be the point on the segment $CE$ such that $QE=CG$. Prove that the line $PQ$ is parallel to the line $AC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the centre and draw the radii to $D$ and $E$. Identify their right angles with the sides through $B$.
Hint 2: key idea
The centre is equally far from both sides of the right angle, so its line to $B$ is an angle bisector. Use the quadrilateral formed by $B$, the centre, and the contact points to express $BD$ and $BE$ using the radius. (LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers)
Hint 3: approach
Use the diameter on $AC$ and the definitions of $P$ and $Q$ to express $BP$ and $BQ$ as distances along $AC$. Then apply the angle bisector theorem and compare the fractions of $BA$ and $BC$ cut off by $P$ and $Q$.
Common trap
Use the given order on $AC$ to decide when to subtract the radius from a distance to the centre.
Show solution 1 – The angle bisector theorem

Let $O$ be the center and $r$ the radius of $\Gamma$. Since $OD\perp AB$, $OE\perp BC$ and $\angle B=90^\circ$, the quadrilateral $BDOE$ is a rectangle with $OD=OE=r$, so it is a square and $BD=BE=r$.

The line $AC$ passes through the center, so $FG$ is a diameter and $OF=OG=r$; as $A,F,G,C$ lie in this order, $AF=AO-r$ and $CG=CO-r$. Since $D$ lies between $B$ and $A$ and $P$ lies on segment $DA$, \[BP=BD+DP=r+AF=AO,\] and similarly $BQ=BE+EQ=r+CG=CO$. The center $O$ is at the same distance $r$ from the lines $BA$ and $BC$, so $BO$ is the bisector of $\angle ABC$. By the angle bisector theorem in triangle $ABC$, $\dfrac{AO}{OC}=\dfrac{BA}{BC}$, i.e. \[\frac{BP}{BA}=\frac{AO}{BA}=\frac{CO}{BC}=\frac{BQ}{BC}.\] Since $P$ lies on side $BA$ and $Q$ on side $BC$, the converse of the intercept (Thales) theorem gives $PQ\parallel AC$.

Show solution 2 – Two triangles similar to ABC

Let $O$ be the center and $r$ the radius of $\Gamma$. As $OD\perp AB$, $OE\perp BC$ and $\angle ABC=90^\circ$, $BDOE$ is a square of side $r$; in particular $BD=BE=r$, $DO\parallel BC$ and $OE\parallel AB$. The points $F$, $G$ lie on the line $AC$ at distance $r$ from $O$, so $AF=AO-r$, $CG=CO-r$, and \[BP=BD+DP=r+(AO-r)=AO,\qquad BQ=r+(CO-r)=CO .\] Because $DO\parallel BC$, triangle $ADO$ is similar to triangle $ABC$, so $\dfrac{AO}{AC}=\dfrac{DO}{BC}=\dfrac r{BC}$. Because $OE\parallel AB$, triangle $OEC$ is similar to triangle $ABC$, so $\dfrac{CO}{CA}=\dfrac{OE}{AB}=\dfrac r{AB}$. Hence \[\frac{BP}{BA}=\frac{AO}{AB}=\frac{r\cdot AC}{AB\cdot BC}=\frac{CO}{CB}=\frac{BQ}{BC}.\] So triangle $BPQ$ is similar to triangle $BAC$ (common angle at $B$, proportional sides), whence $\angle BPQ=\angle BAC$ and the lines $PQ$ and $AC$ are parallel (corresponding angles).

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Guaranteed Coincidence

Let $\mathcal{S}=\{2,4,6,\ldots,100\}$ be the set of even numbers between $2$ and $100$. We say that the value of a permutation $\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{50}\}$ of the set $\mathcal{S}$ is the number \[1x_1+3x_2+5x_3+\cdots+97x_{49}+99x_{50}.\]

  • a) Find the smallest value that a permutation of $\mathcal{S}$ can take.

  • b) Prove that there are $2026$ trillion distinct permutations, all with the same value.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick two positions and write their contributions before and after you exchange their entries. Notice that all permutations have the same total of unweighted entries.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the sign of the change under an exchange to constrain a minimizing order. For the coincidence claim, group the permutations by their integer values and compare the numbers of permutations and groups. (LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence)
Hint 3: approach
Find which pairs admit a swap that lowers the value, then set up the weighted sum for an order with no such pairs. Bound the number of possible values using the total of the entries and the largest coefficient. Lower-bound the factorial count using only its larger factors so the two bounds are ready for comparison.
Common trap
The groups are indexed by distinct possible values, with several permutations allowed in each group.
Show the answer

a) $85850$; b) proof

Show solution 1 – Exchange argument and pigeonhole

a) Let $c_k=2k-1$ be the coefficient of $x_k$. If $i<j$ and $x_i<x_j$, swapping $x_i$ and $x_j$ changes the value by \[(c_ix_j+c_jx_i)-(c_ix_i+c_jx_j)=-(c_j-c_i)(x_j-x_i)<0,\] so the value decreases. Hence in a permutation of smallest value (one exists, as there are finitely many) the numbers decrease: $x_k=102-2k$. Its value is \[\sum_{k=1}^{50}(2k-1)(102-2k)=\sum_{k=1}^{50}\bigl(-4k^2+206k-102\bigr),\] which equals $-4\cdot42925+206\cdot1275-5100=\boxed{85850}$.

b) Every value is a positive integer smaller than $100(x_1+\cdots+x_{50})=100\cdot2550<10^6$, so there are fewer than $10^6$ possible values. The number of permutations is \[50!\ \ge\ 10\cdot11\cdots50\ \ge\ 10^{41}.\] By the pigeonhole principle some value is taken by at least $10^{41}/10^6=10^{35}$ permutations, which is far more than $2026$ trillion, whether a trillion is read as $10^{12}$ or as $10^{18}$.

Show solution 2 – Tail sums

a) Write $T_j=x_j+x_{j+1}+\cdots+x_{50}$ for $j=1,\dots,50$. The number $x_k$ appears in $T_1,\dots,T_k$, that is, $k$ times, so \[\sum_{k=1}^{50}(2k-1)x_k=2\sum_{k=1}^{50}kx_k-\sum_{k=1}^{50}x_k=2\sum_{j=1}^{50}T_j-2550 .\] $T_j$ is a sum of $51-j$ different elements of $\mathcal S$, so it is at least the sum of the $51-j$ smallest ones: \[T_j\ge2\bigl(1+2+\cdots+(51-j)\bigr)=(51-j)(52-j).\] All these bounds are attained together by the decreasing order $x_k=102-2k$ (then each tail consists of the smallest numbers). Hence the minimum is \[2\sum_{m=1}^{50}m(m+1)-2550=2\,(42925+1275)-2550=\boxed{85850}.\]

b) The value of every permutation is an even number between $85850$ and the value of the increasing order, $\sum_{k=1}^{50}(2k-1)\cdot2k=169150$. So there are at most $\frac{169150-85850}2+1=41651$ possible values, while there are $50!>10^{41}$ permutations (the $41$ factors $10,11,\dots,50$ already exceed $10$ each). Some value is therefore shared by more than $10^{41}/41651>10^{36}$ permutations, which exceeds $2026$ trillion ($2026\cdot10^{12}$, or even $2026\cdot10^{18}$).

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 47: Combinatorial Number Theory · Pigeonhole in Number Systems

Alberto is going to choose some composite numbers from $1$ to $50$ inclusive and arrange them in a row. The row must satisfy the following property: if two numbers in the row have exactly $n$ numbers strictly between them (not counting the numbers themselves), then at least one of the two numbers is relatively prime to $n+1$.

What is the maximum number of numbers Alberto can choose?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Number the positions in the row. Express the number of entries strictly between a pair using their position numbers.
Hint 2: key idea
Entries divisible by the same prime must occupy distinct position classes modulo that prime. Apply this restriction to the even entries and to the multiples of three, then inspect the composites with neither prime factor. (LPS VI, Chapter 47: Combinatorial Number Theory)
Hint 3: approach
Count the contributions from the even entries, the odd multiples of three, and the remaining composites. For a construction, arrange the groups so that any two entries sharing a prime stand at a gap coprime to that prime. For each possible gap, check which factors of the gap could interfere at its two endpoints.
Common trap
Two entries can violate the rule by sharing different prime factors with their gap.
Show the answer

$8$

Show the solution – Three classes of composites

If two numbers have exactly $n$ numbers between them, their positions differ by $d=n+1$. So the rule says: two entries at distance $d$ may not both have a common factor with $d$.

Upper bound.

  • At most $2$ even numbers. Two even numbers at an even distance $d$ both share the factor $2$ with $d$, which is forbidden; so any two even numbers stand at an odd distance, i.e. at positions of different parity. Among three positions two have the same parity, so there are at most two even numbers.

  • At most $3$ multiples of $3$. Two multiples of $3$ cannot stand at a distance divisible by $3$, so their positions have different remainders modulo $3$; there are only three remainders.

  • The rest. A composite number up to $50$ that is divisible by neither $2$ nor $3$ has all prime factors at least $5$, hence is $25=5^2$, $35=5\cdot7$ or $49=7^2$ ($5\cdot11=55>50$).

So at most $2+3+3=8$ numbers can be chosen.

Construction. The row \[4,\ 8,\ 9,\ 27,\ 33,\ 25,\ 35,\ 49\] works. Its distances are at most $7$, so the factor $11$ of $33$ never matters, and the relevant prime factors in positions $1,\dots,8$ are \[\{2\},\ \{2\},\ \{3\},\ \{3\},\ \{3\},\ \{5\},\ \{5,7\},\ \{7\}.\] A pair at distance $d$ is forbidden only if each of the two numbers has a prime factor dividing $d$:

  • $d=2$ or $4$: both would contain $2$, but only positions $1,2$ do (distance $1$);

  • $d=3$: both would contain $3$, but positions $3,4,5$ are at distances $1$, $2$;

  • $d=5$: both would contain $5$, but positions $6,7$ are at distance $1$;

  • $d=6$: both would contain $2$ or $3$, but these are positions $1$ to $5$ (distances at most $4$);

  • $d=7$: both would contain $7$, but positions $7,8$ are at distance $1$.

So no pair is forbidden, and the maximum is $\boxed{8}$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

Consider a $101\times101$ board with an alien in the center square. Every minute, the following two events occur in that order:

  • Each alien on the grid splits into two identical aliens that remain in the same square as the original alien.

  • Each alien on the board moves to a square that shares a side with the square it is in, or stays in the same cell.

Determine the minimum number of minutes that must pass for it to be possible for there to be at least one alien in every square of the board.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Give the centre square coordinates $(0,0)$. Compare the grid steps needed to reach a corner with the distance one alien can move per minute.
Hint 2: key idea
Assign an alien near the edges of a rectangle to fill that rectangle. On its next split, try sending a copy towards the farther edge while the copy that stays handles the other piece. (LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Track the larger offset to an edge in each direction and check that both copies have enough time in their assigned rectangles after a split. Find a splitting rule that keeps this condition true at every step, starting from the single alien at the centre.
Common trap
The two copies start in the same square and can each take at most one grid step that minute.
Show the answer

$100$

Show solution 1 – Distance bound and a rectangle-splitting lemma

Use coordinates with the center square at $(0,0)$; the board consists of the squares $(x,y)$ with $|x|,|y|\le50$.

At least $100$ minutes. In one minute every alien moves to a neighbouring square or stays, so it changes $|x|+|y|$ by at most $1$. The corner $(50,50)$ has $|x|+|y|=100$, so no alien can be there before minute $100$.

$100$ minutes suffice. For a square $p=(p_1,p_2)$ inside a rectangle $R=[x_1,x_2]\times[y_1,y_2]$ of squares, call $(p_1-x_1,\,x_2-p_1)$ its horizontal offsets and $(p_2-y_1,\,y_2-p_2)$ its vertical offsets.

Lemma. Let an alien stand at $p\in R$ with $r$ minutes still to go, where in each direction the two offsets $(s,t)$ satisfy $\min(s,t)\le1$ and $(s,t)\ne(1,1)$, and $r$ is at least the sum of the larger horizontal offset and the larger vertical offset. Then this alien's descendants can move so that after $r$ minutes every square of $R$ holds an alien.

Proof (induction on the number of squares of $R$). If $R=\{p\}$, all descendants simply stay. Otherwise some direction has a larger offset at least $1$; say it is horizontal with offsets $(L,M)$, $L\ge M$ (the other cases are symmetric). As $\min(L,M)\le1$ and $(L,M)\ne(1,1)$, we have $L\ge1$ and $M\le L-1$. Let $V$ be the larger vertical offset, so $L+V\le r$. In the next minute the alien splits:

  • the first copy moves one square left and takes care of the columns $x_1,\dots,p_1-1$ of $R$; its horizontal offsets are $(L-1,0)$, and $(L-1)+V\le r-1$;

  • the second copy stays at $p$ and takes care of the columns $p_1,\dots,x_2$; its horizontal offsets are $(0,M)$, and $M+V\le L-1+V\le r-1$.

Both smaller rectangles satisfy the hypotheses with $r-1$ minutes left, and together they cover $R$. $\square$

Construction. In minute $1$ the two aliens go to $(-1,0)$ and $(1,0)$. In minute $2$ the alien at $(-1,0)$ sends its copies to $(-1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, and the alien at $(1,0)$ sends its copies to $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. Assign to them the rectangles \[[-50,-1]\times[1,50],\quad [-50,-1]\times[-50,0],\quad [0,50]\times[1,50],\quad [0,50]\times[-50,0],\] which together form the whole board. Their offsets are $(49,0),(0,49)$; $(49,0),(49,1)$; $(1,49),(0,49)$; $(1,49),(49,1)$, so each satisfies the lemma with $49+49=98$ minutes left. After $2+98=100$ minutes every square holds an alien. The answer is $\boxed{100}$.

Show solution 2 – Fill the middle row, then every column

Use coordinates with the center square at $(0,0)$; the board consists of the squares $(x,y)$ with $|x|,|y|\le50$.

At least $100$ minutes. In one minute every alien moves to a neighbouring square or stays, so its value of $|x|+|y|$ changes by at most $1$; every alien descends from the original one, which started with $|x|+|y|=0$. The corner $(50,50)$ has $|x|+|y|=100$, so no alien can be there before minute $100$.

A one-line lemma. Consider one line of the board (a row or a column) with positions $-50,\dots,50$ and one alien at position $0$. Its descendants can move inside this line so that, for every $t$ with $3\le t\le50$, after minute $t$ every position from $-t$ to $t$ holds an alien:

  • minute $1$: the two copies go to $-1$ and $1$;

  • minute $2$: each alien at $\pm1$ sends one copy outward (to $\pm2$) and one copy inward (to $0$); now $\pm2$ hold one alien each and $0$ holds two;

  • minute $3$: the alien at $2$ sends its copies to $3$ and $2$, the alien at $-2$ to $-3$ and $-2$, and the four copies made at $0$ go to $-1$, $1$, $0$, $0$; now every position from $-3$ to $3$ is occupied;

  • minute $t\ge4$: positions $\pm(t-1)$ are occupied; one copy of an alien at $t-1$ moves to $t$, one copy of an alien at $-(t-1)$ moves to $-t$, and every other alien stays. Then every position from $-t$ to $t$ is occupied.

$100$ minutes suffice. During minutes $1$ to $50$ apply the lemma to the middle row $y=0$: after minute $50$ every square $(x,0)$, $|x|\le50$, holds an alien. During minutes $51$ to $100$, in every column $x$ pick one alien at $(x,0)$ and apply the lemma to column $x$ for its descendants (they move only up and down this column); all other aliens and their descendants simply stay where they are. After minute $100$ every column is completely occupied, so every square of the board holds an alien.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{100}$ minutes.

Problem 6

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Equation-Based Figure Analysis

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$ and circumcenter $O$. Let $D$ be the point on $\Gamma$ diametrically opposite to $C$. Let $X$ be a point on $\Gamma$ such that $AX\parallel CD$. Let $Y$ be a point on the line $BC$ such that $\angle CDY=90^\circ$. If the points $X$, $O$, $Y$ are collinear, prove that the lines $YD$, $AB$ and $CX$ concur.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Identify the tangent line at the point opposite $C$. Make that line one coordinate axis, with the diameter as the other axis.
Hint 2: key idea
Parametrize a point on the circle by where its line to $C$ crosses the tangent. Express $A$ and $B$ this way and use the vertical chord $AX$ to find the parameter of $X$. (LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Write the equation of chord $AB$ in terms of the two point parameters and find its intercept with the tangent. Use the collinearity of $X,O,Y$ to obtain a relation between the parameters and simplify the intercept. Keep the parameter of $X$ ready to compare with that intercept.
Common trap
Check that the chord is not parallel to the tangent before dividing by the coefficient of the intercept.
Show solution 1 – Projecting the circle from C onto the tangent at D

Since $\angle CDY=90^\circ$ and $CD$ is a diameter, the line $YD$ is the tangent $\ell$ to $\Gamma$ at $D$. Take coordinates with $D=(0,0)$, $C=(0,2)$, so $\Gamma$ is $x^2+(y-1)^2=1$, $O=(0,1)$ and $\ell$ is the $x$-axis. We assume the configuration is non-degenerate: $A\ne D$, and $A$ does not lie on the line $y=1$ (otherwise there is no second point $X$ with $AX\parallel CD$).

Parametrization. For a real number $u$, the line through $C$ and $(u,0)$ meets $\Gamma$ again at \[P(u)=\Bigl(\frac{4u}{u^2+4},\ \frac{2u^2}{u^2+4}\Bigr),\] and every point of $\Gamma$ other than $C$ is $P(u)$ for exactly one $u$. Thus the line $CP(u)$ meets $\ell$ at $(u,0)$.

Chord lemma. For $u\ne v$ the line through $P(u)$ and $P(v)$ is \[2(u+v)\,x+(uv-4)\,y=2uv .\] Indeed, at $P(u)$ the left side is $\dfrac{8u(u+v)+2u^2(uv-4)}{u^2+4}=\dfrac{2uv(u^2+4)}{u^2+4}=2uv$, and the equation is symmetric in $u,v$. If $u+v\ne0$, this line meets $\ell$ at $\Bigl(\dfrac{uv}{u+v},0\Bigr)$.

The points. Let $A=P(\alpha)$ and $B=P(\beta)$. By the remark above, $Y=CB\cap\ell=(\beta,0)$. Since $AX\parallel CD$ is vertical, $X$ is the reflection of $A$ in the line $y=1$: \[X=\Bigl(\frac{4\alpha}{\alpha^2+4},\ \frac{8}{\alpha^2+4}\Bigr)=P\Bigl(\frac4\alpha\Bigr),\] so the line $CX$ meets $\ell$ at $T=\bigl(\frac4\alpha,0\bigr)$.

The hypothesis. The vectors $\overrightarrow{OX}=\frac{1}{\alpha^2+4}\,(4\alpha,\ 4-\alpha^2)$ and $\overrightarrow{OY}=(\beta,-1)$ are parallel, so $-4\alpha-(4-\alpha^2)\beta=0$, i.e. $\beta=\dfrac{4\alpha}{\alpha^2-4}$.

Conclusion. Then $\dfrac1\alpha+\dfrac1\beta=\dfrac1\alpha+\dfrac{\alpha^2-4}{4\alpha}=\dfrac\alpha4\ne0$, so $\alpha+\beta\ne0$ and, by the chord lemma, $AB$ meets $\ell$ at \[\Bigl(\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta},0\Bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{1}{1/\alpha+1/\beta},0\Bigr)=\Bigl(\frac4\alpha,0\Bigr)=T .\] So the lines $YD$, $AB$ and $CX$ all pass through $T$.

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers

Since $\angle CDY=90^\circ$ and $CD$ is a diameter, $YD$ is the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $D$. Let $\Gamma$ be the unit circle, $D=1$, $C=-1$, and let $a,b,x$ be the complex coordinates of $A,B,X$ ($|a|=|b|=1$). We assume $a\neq\pm1$, $a\ne\pm i$ and $b\neq-1$ (non-degenerate configuration). Recall that the line through two points $p,q$ of the unit circle is $z+pq\,\overline z=p+q$.

The tangent at $D$ is the line $\operatorname{Re}z=1$, i.e. $\overline z=2-z$. Since $AX$ is parallel to the real axis $CD$, $X$ is the reflection of $A$ in the imaginary axis: $x=-\overline a=-\frac1a$.

The point $Y$. On line $BC$: $z-b\overline z=b-1$. With $\overline z=2-z$ this gives $z(1+b)=3b-1$, so $y=\dfrac{3b-1}{b+1}$, and $\overline y=\dfrac{3/b-1}{1/b+1}=\dfrac{3-b}{1+b}$.

The hypothesis. $X$, $O$, $Y$ are collinear iff $\dfrac yx=-ay$ is real, i.e. $ay=\overline{ay}=\dfrac{\overline y}a$, i.e. $a^2y=\overline y$: \[a^2(3b-1)=3-b\iff b\,(3a^2+1)=a^2+3. \tag{$*$}\]

The point $T=CX\cap(\text{tangent})$. The line $CX$ is $z+\frac1a\overline z=-1-\frac1a$. With $\overline z=2-z$ we get $z(a-1)=-(a+3)$, so \[t=\frac{a+3}{1-a},\qquad \overline t=\frac{1+3a}{a-1},\qquad t+\overline t=\frac{(a+3)-(1+3a)}{1-a}=2,\] confirming that $t$ is on the tangent and on $CX$.

$T$ lies on $AB$. The line $AB$ is $z+ab\,\overline z=a+b$. For $z=t$, $\overline t=2-t$, this reads $t(1-ab)=a+b-2ab$, i.e. \[(a+3)(1-ab)=(1-a)(a+b-2ab).\] Expanding, the difference of the two sides is $(a^2+3)-b(3a^2+1)$, which is $0$ by $(*)$. Hence $T$ lies on $AB$, $CX$ and $YD$: the three lines concur.
