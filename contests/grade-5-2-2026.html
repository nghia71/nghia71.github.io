Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · Tiling and Planar Fit

Ms. Souza's fourth grade class is going to start a garden project. The field of land the class has to work with is $75$ feet by $40$ feet. They plan to break the field into $2$ foot by $3$ foot plots, with no plots overlapping. What is the maximum number of plots of these dimensions that they will be able to create out of the field?

Show the answer

$500$ plots

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Area gives a limit, then fit the plots)

The field has area $75\times 40=3000$ square feet and each plot has area $2\times 3=6$ square feet. Plots do not overlap, so there are at most $3000\div 6=500$ plots.

Can we really fit $500$? Turn every plot so that its $3$-foot side runs along the $75$-foot side and its $2$-foot side runs along the $40$-foot side. Since $75=3\times 25$ and $40=2\times 20$, the plots fill the field exactly in $25$ columns and $20$ rows, with nothing left over: $25\times 20=500$ plots. So the maximum is $\boxed{500}$.

Solution 2 (Count rows and columns for both ways of turning the plots)

If all plots are turned the same way, there are two choices.

  • $3$-foot side along the $75$-foot side: $75\div 3=25$ plots in a row and $40\div 2=20$ rows, so $25\times 20=500$ plots, and the field is covered with no gaps.

  • $2$-foot side along the $75$-foot side: only $37$ plots fit in a row ($37\times 2=74$) and $13$ rows ($13\times 3=39$), so $37\times 13=481$ plots, with strips of land wasted.

The first way covers the whole field, and no arrangement can do better than covering the whole field: every plot uses $6$ of the $3000$ square feet, so $500$ plots is the most possible. The answer is $\boxed{500}$.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition · Cut-and-Reassemble Area

The diagrams show four squares of the same size and a circle and parts of a circle, all of which have the same radius. In which diagram does the shaded part have the largest area?

The options (A)–(D) are the four diagrams shown below, and (E) is: All shaded parts have the same area.

Show the answer

(E) All shaded parts have the same area

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the pieces of one circle)

All the curved pieces have the same radius as the circle in B. Measure every shaded area in “whole circles”.

  • A: four quarter circles, $4\times\frac14=1$ circle.

  • B: one whole circle.

  • C: two half circles, $2\times\frac12=1$ circle.

  • D: two quarter circles and one half circle, $\frac14+\frac14+\frac12=1$ circle.

Every shaded part has the area of one circle, so the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}}$: all shaded parts have the same area.

Solution 2 (Move the pieces together)

Imagine cutting out the shaded pieces and sliding them together. In A, the four quarter circles have their right angles at the four corners; pushed together so that the four corners meet at one point, they make a full circle of the same radius. In C, the two half circles fit together along their straight edges into a full circle. In D, the two quarter circles fit together into a half circle, and with the bottom half circle they make a full circle. So each diagram has exactly one circle of shading, the same as B. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}}$.

Problem 3

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

Sritha has four copies of this shape: a strip of three unit squares in a row. Which of the following could she not make from her four copies?

The options (A)–(E) are the five shapes shown below.

Show the answer

(E)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Build A to D, then get stuck in E)

Every shape has $12$ small squares, which is the area of four strips, so area alone does not decide. We look at how the strips can be placed.

Shapes A, B, C and D can be made, as the picture shows: in A the four strips go around the ring like the blades of a windmill; in B the four strips stand upright side by side; in C one upright strip joins the three rows at the corner; in D the two columns are each part of an upright strip, and the rest of the bottom row is two lying strips.

Now try E. The left square of the top row has no square below it, so its strip must lie flat and covers the first three squares of the top row. In the same way the first three squares of the bottom row form a strip. The right square of the middle row also has no square above or below it, so its strip covers the last three squares of the middle row. Three squares are left (marked ?): the last square of the top row, the first square of the middle row and the last square of the bottom row. No two of them touch, so the fourth strip cannot cover them. Shape E is impossible, and the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}}$.

Solution 2 (Colour the columns)

First, A–D can be made. A: go around the ring with four strips like the blades of a windmill (top row from the left corner, right column from the top corner, bottom row from the right corner, left column from the bottom corner). B: four upright strips side by side. C: an upright strip through the column where the three rows meet, then one lying strip for the rest of each row. D: an upright strip in each of the two columns (the shorter one uses a square of the bottom row), then two lying strips for the rest of the bottom row.

Now shape E. Number its columns $1$ to $6$ from the left and paint columns $1$ and $4$ red, columns $2$ and $5$ blue, columns $3$ and $6$ green. A lying strip covers three neighbouring columns, so it covers one red, one blue and one green square. An upright strip covers three squares of one colour. So whenever a strip is placed, the difference between the numbers of squares of two colours changes by $0$ or by $3$. If the four strips made E, the difference between any two colour counts of E would be a multiple of $3$.

Count the colours of E. The top and bottom rows use columns $1$–$4$ and the middle row uses columns $3$–$6$. Adding (top and bottom rows) $+$ (middle row): \[\text{red: } 4+1=5,\qquad \text{blue: } 2+1=3,\qquad \text{green: } 2+2=4.\] Red minus green is $1$, which is not a multiple of $3$. So E cannot be made, and the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}}$.

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Factorization

What is the least positive integer whose digits have a product of $100$?

Show the answer

$455$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Use as few digits as possible)

Write $100=2\times2\times5\times5$. No digit can be $0$ (the product would be $0$), and a digit equal to $1$ only makes the number longer, so we leave out $1$s. Each digit is a product of some of the factors $2,2,5,5$, and must be at most $9$. A digit containing a $5$ cannot contain anything else ($2\times5=10$ is too big), so the two $5$s are separate digits. The two $2$s can be combined into one digit $4$.

So the fewest digits we can use is three: $4,5,5$. A number with fewer digits is smaller, and with the digits $4,5,5$ the smallest order is with the smallest digit first: $455$. The other possible digit sets ($2,2,5,5$ or ones with extra $1$s) give numbers with four or more digits, which are bigger. The answer is $\boxed{455}$.

Solution 2 (Check the small numbers)

The product of digits of a one-digit or two-digit number is at most $9\times 9=81<100$, so the answer has at least three digits.

For a three-digit number with digit product $100$, the three digits multiply to $100=4\times25$. Every digit divides $100$, so it is one of $1,2,4,5$. Since $100$ has two factors $5$, two of the digits are $5$, and the third is $100\div25=4$. The three-digit numbers made from $4,5,5$ are $455$, $545$, $554$; the smallest is $\boxed{455}$.

Problem 5

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

Eva is looking at four seats in a row, numbered $1$ to $4$ from left to right, which are occupied by Andi, Budi, Citra and Dira, but not in that order. The following statements are true: Andi is not in seat $1$; Budi is seated directly to the right of Andi; Dira is not at either end. In what order, from left to right, do they sit?

(A) Budi, Dira, Andi, Citra (B) Citra, Andi, Dira, Budi (C) Citra, Dira, Andi, Budi
(D) Citra, Dira, Budi, Andi (E) Dira, Citra, Budi, Andi

Show the answer

(C) Citra, Dira, Andi, Budi

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Place Andi first)

Andi is not in seat $1$, and Budi sits directly to Andi's right, so Andi is not in seat $4$. Andi is in seat $2$ or seat $3$.

  • If Andi is in seat $2$, Budi is in seat $3$. Then Dira must be in seat $1$ or $4$, which are the ends. Not allowed.

  • If Andi is in seat $3$, Budi is in seat $4$. Dira is not at an end, so Dira is in seat $2$, and Citra is in seat $1$.

The order is Citra, Dira, Andi, Budi: $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}}$.

Solution 2 (Test the options)

Check each option against the three facts. (A) Budi, Dira, Andi, Citra: Budi is not to the right of Andi. No. (B) Citra, Andi, Dira, Budi: Budi is not directly to the right of Andi. No. (C) Citra, Dira, Andi, Budi: Andi is in seat $3$, Budi is next to him on the right, Dira is in seat $2$. Yes. (D) Citra, Dira, Budi, Andi: Budi is to the left of Andi. No. (E) Dira, Citra, Budi, Andi: Dira is at an end. No. Only $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}}$ fits.

Problem 6

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Structured Two-Variable Models

A furniture store is selling sofas, love seats and chairs made from two types of identical modular pieces plus a back piece, as shown viewed from above.

Including the armrests, the width of the sofa is $220$ cm and the width of the love seat is $160$ cm.

What is the width, in cm, of the chair?

(A) $60$ (B) $80$ (C) $90$ (D) $100$ (E) $120$

Show the answer

(D) $100$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Compare the sofa and the love seat)

The sofa is two armrests and three seat pieces; the love seat is two armrests and two seat pieces (the back piece just runs along the whole width). So the sofa is exactly one seat piece wider than the love seat: one seat piece is $220-160=60$ cm wide.

The chair is the love seat with one seat piece removed, so its width is $160-60=\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 100}$ cm.

Solution 2 (Find each piece)

Let one seat piece be $s$ cm wide and the two armrests together be $a$ cm wide. Then \[a+3s=220,\qquad a+2s=160.\] Subtracting, $s=60$, and then $a=160-120=40$ (each armrest is $20$ cm). The chair is two armrests and one seat piece: $a+s=40+60=\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 100}$ cm.

Problem 7

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Partial Totals and Cyclic Sums

When Katarina added the two numbers in each row and each column of the grid, she obtained the results shown in the diagram. Which of the statements below is true?

(A) $a$ is less than $d$ (B) $b$ is equal to $c$ (C) $a$ is greater than $d$
(D) $c$ is greater than $b$ (E) $a$ is equal to $d$

Show the answer

(A) $a$ is less than $d$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Compare two sums that share a number)

The diagram tells us \[a+b=2,\qquad c+d=3,\qquad a+c=1,\qquad b+d=4.\] The top row and the right column both contain $b$. The right column is $2$ more than the top row: \[(b+d)-(a+b)=4-2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad d-a=2.\] So $d$ is $2$ more than $a$, and $a$ is less than $d$.

In the same way, the top row and the left column share $a$: $(a+b)-(a+c)=2-1$, so $b-c=1$ and $b$ is greater than $c$. This rules out (B) and (D), and $d-a=2$ rules out (C) and (E). The true statement is $\boxed{\textbf{(A)}}$.

Solution 2 (Try one filling, then explain why it is enough)

One filling that works is $a=0$, $b=2$, $c=1$, $d=2$: rows $0+2=2$ and $1+2=3$, columns $0+1=1$ and $2+2=4$. Here $a<d$, $b\ne c$, $c<b$, so only (A) is true for this filling.

But could another filling make a different statement true? Change $a$ by some amount: to keep the top row equal to $2$, $b$ must change by the opposite amount; to keep the left column equal to $1$, $c$ also changes by the opposite amount; then to keep the bottom row equal to $3$, $d$ changes by the same amount as $a$ (and the right column $b+d$ stays $4$ automatically). So $a$ and $d$ always move together, keeping $d-a=2$; and $b$ and $c$ always move together, keeping $b-c=1$. Every filling behaves like the one above, and the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A)}}$.

Problem 8

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

The five keys shown fit the five padlocks. The numbers on the keys are coded by letters on the padlocks. Which number is written on the remaining key?

(A) $282$ (B) $284$ (C) $382$ (D) $823$ (E) $824$

Show the answer

(B) $284$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Start with the repeated letter)

The shown keys use the four different digits $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, and the padlocks use only the four letters A, B, D, H. So each letter stands for one of these digits, and different letters stand for different digits. The key $414$ has the same first and last digit, so its padlock has the same first and last letter. The only such padlock is DAD, so DAD $=414$: D $=4$ and A $=1$.

The padlocks starting with A are AHD and ABD; they become $1\,?\,4$. The keys of this form are $184$ and $124$. So H and B are $8$ and $2$ in some order. The remaining shown key is $812$, which must be HAB or BHD. BHD ends in D $=4$, so $812$ is HAB: H $=8$, A $=1$, B $=2$.

The key left over belongs to BHD $=\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 284}$.

Solution 2 (Pair the two keys that look alike)

The shown keys use the four different digits $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, and the padlocks use only the four letters A, B, D, H, so different letters stand for different digits. The keys $184$ and $124$ both start with $1$ and end with $4$. The padlocks AHD and ABD are the only two padlocks with the same first letter and the same last letter, so these two keys open them. Hence A $=1$ and D $=4$, and H, B are $8$ and $2$ in some order.

The key $812$ has the digit $1=\,$A in the middle. The padlocks with A in the middle are DAD and HAB; DAD would start with $4$, so $812$ opens HAB. Then H $=8$ and B $=2$ (and DAD $=414$, which is the last shown key).

The remaining padlock BHD needs the key $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 284}$.

Problem 9

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

How many two-digit positive integers are $36$ more than the sum of their digits?

Show the answer

$10$ integers

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Place value)

A two-digit number with tens digit $a$ and units digit $b$ equals $10a+b$. The condition says \[10a+b=a+b+36.\] Take $a+b$ away from both sides: $9a=36$, so $a=4$. The units digit $b$ cancelled, so it can be any digit $0$ to $9$. The numbers are $40,41,\dots,49$, which is $\boxed{10}$ integers.

Solution 2 (What a number minus its digit sum is)

Take a two-digit number, for example $47$. Subtracting its digit sum, $47-4-7=36$. In general, the number is “tens digit times $10$ plus units digit”, and when we take away both digits, the units digit disappears and the tens digit times $10$ becomes the tens digit times $9$. So (number) $-$ (digit sum) $=9\times$(tens digit). This equals $36$ exactly when the tens digit is $4$, and every units digit works. There are $\boxed{10}$ such numbers, $40$ to $49$.

Problem 10

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

Ada likes adding numbers! On Monday she added all the three-digit numbers each of whose digits is either $0$ or $1$. On Tuesday she added all the two-digit numbers each of whose digits is either $2$ or $3$. On Wednesday she added her answers from Monday and Tuesday. What was the result of Ada's final addition?

(A) $310$ (B) $421$ (C) $532$ (D) $643$ (E) $754$

Show the answer

(C) $532$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (List and add)

A three-digit number cannot start with $0$, so Monday's numbers start with $1$: $100,101,110,111$. Their sum is $422$. Tuesday's numbers are $22,23,32,33$, with sum $110$. Wednesday's result is $422+110=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 532}$.

Solution 2 (Add column by column)

Monday: the four numbers all have hundreds digit $1$; in the tens place two of them have $1$, and in the units place two of them have $1$. So the sum is $4\times100+2\times10+2\times1=422$. Tuesday: the four numbers have tens digits $2,2,3,3$ and units digits $2,3,2,3$. Each place adds to $10$, so the sum is $10\times10+10=110$. Total: $422+110=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 532}$.

Problem 11

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Complementary Counting

There are three different routes from Exe to Wye. There are five different routes from Wye to Zed. Ahmed travels from Exe to Zed via Wye. All the routes can be travelled in either direction. He wants to travel back from Zed to Exe via Wye using a route that is not completely the same as the route he used from Exe to Zed. How many different routes could he use for his return trip?

(A) $5$ (B) $6$ (C) $10$ (D) $12$ (E) $14$

Show the answer

(E) $14$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (All routes minus one)

A trip from Zed to Exe via Wye is a choice of one of the $5$ Zed–Wye routes and one of the $3$ Wye–Exe routes, so there are $5\times3=15$ trips back. Exactly one of them uses both the same routes as the trip there. Ahmed can use any of the other $15-1=\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 14}$.

Solution 2 (Count the different trips directly)

A return trip is “not completely the same” if it changes the first part, the second part, or both.

  • Same Zed–Wye route, different Wye–Exe route: $1\times2=2$ trips.

  • Different Zed–Wye route, same Wye–Exe route: $4\times1=4$ trips.

  • Both different: $4\times2=8$ trips.

Total $2+4+8=\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 14}$.

Problem 12

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Divisibility Tests

How many positive integers between $10$ and $2026$ have all their digits the same and are divisible by $3$?

(A) $8$ (B) $10$ (C) $12$ (D) $14$ (E) $16$

Show the answer

(C) $12$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Digit sum test, by length)

A number is divisible by $3$ exactly when its digit sum is. Sort the numbers by how many digits they have (all digits equal to $d$, with $d$ from $1$ to $9$).

  • Two digits, $dd$: digit sum $2d$, a multiple of $3$ when $d=3,6,9$. That is $33,66,99$: $3$ numbers.

  • Three digits, $ddd$: digit sum $3d$, always a multiple of $3$. All of $111,222,\dots,999$: $9$ numbers.

  • Four digits: only $1111$ is below $2026$, with digit sum $4$, not a multiple of $3$.

The total is $3+9=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 12}$.

Solution 2 (Factor out the repeated digit)

A number with all digits $d$ is $d\times 11$, $d\times 111$ or $d\times1111$. Now $111=3\times37$ is a multiple of $3$, so all nine numbers $d\times111$ count. But $11$ and $1111$ are not multiples of $3$ (their digit sums are $2$ and $4$), so $d\times11$ is a multiple of $3$ only when $d$ is ($d=3,6,9$), and the only four-digit candidate below $2026$ is $1111=1\times1111$, which does not count. Total: $9+3=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 12}$.

Problem 13

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Bounding Integer Solutions

Four players each scored goals in a handball match. Each of them scored a different number of goals. Of the four, Will scored the fewest goals. The other three scored twenty goals in total. What is the largest number of goals Will could have scored?

(A) $2$ (B) $3$ (C) $4$ (D) $5$ (E) $6$

Show the answer

(C) $4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Make the other three as small as possible)

Suppose Will scored $w$ goals. The other three players scored different numbers, each more than $w$, so at least $w+1$, $w+2$ and $w+3$. Their total is at least \[(w+1)+(w+2)+(w+3)=3w+6.\] This total is $20$, so $3w+6\le 20$, $3w\le14$ and $w\le 4$. And $w=4$ really happens: the others score $5$, $6$ and $9$ (total $20$, all different, all more than $4$). The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 4}$.

Solution 2 (Test from the top)

Try the options from the largest. If Will scored $6$, the others scored at least $7+8+9=24>20$. Impossible. If Will scored $5$, the others scored at least $6+7+8=21>20$. Impossible. If Will scored $4$, the others can score $5,6,9$, which add up to $20$. Possible. So the largest number is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 4}$.

Problem 14

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

Beads numbered $1$ to $n$, where $n>1$, are threaded onto a bracelet. What is the smallest value of $n$ for which this can be done in such a way that no two consecutive numbers are next to each other?

(A) $3$ (B) $4$ (C) $5$ (D) $6$ (E) $7$

Show the answer

(C) $5$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Rule out small cases, then build one)

On a bracelet with $3$ or more beads, every bead has two different neighbours.

  • $n=2$ or $n=3$: every bead is next to every other bead, so $1$ is next to $2$. Impossible.

  • $n=4$: bead $2$ needs two neighbours that are not $1$ and not $3$. Only bead $4$ is left, which is just one bead. Impossible.

  • $n=5$: the order $1,3,5,2,4$ around the bracelet works. The neighbouring pairs are $1\text{--}3$, $3\text{--}5$, $5\text{--}2$, $2\text{--}4$ and (closing the circle) $4\text{--}1$; none of these are consecutive numbers.

The smallest value is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 5}$.

Solution 2 (Count the forbidden neighbours)

For $n=2$ the two beads $1$ and $2$ are next to each other, so $n\ge3$. Each bead has $2$ neighbours on the bracelet, chosen from the other $n-1$ beads. A bead in the middle of the list (not $1$ or $n$) has two consecutive numbers that are forbidden as neighbours, so it needs at least $2$ allowed beads among the remaining $n-3$. So $n-3\ge 2$, which means $n\ge5$.

For $n=5$ the forbidden pairs are only $1\text{--}2$, $2\text{--}3$, $3\text{--}4$, $4\text{--}5$. Bead $3$ must sit between $1$ and $5$, and bead $2$ between $4$ and $5$, which gives the circle $1,3,5,2,4$. Its last pair $4\text{--}1$ is also allowed, so the bracelet works and the answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 5}$.

Problem 15

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Invariants

The integers $1$ to $20$ are written on a board. Patricia erases three of the integers and replaces them with their sum. She repeats this process until only two integers remain. What is the sum of these two remaining integers?

(A) $100$ (B) $150$ (C) $180$ (D) $200$ (E) $210$

Show the answer

(E) $210$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The total never changes)

When Patricia replaces three numbers by their sum, the total of all the numbers on the board stays the same: she takes away three numbers and puts back exactly the same amount. At the start the total is \[1+2+\dots+20=\frac{20\times21}{2}=210.\] (Pair $1$ with $20$, $2$ with $19$, and so on: $10$ pairs, each adding to $21$.) So at the end the two numbers also add up to $\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 210}$.

(She can really reach two numbers: each step lowers the count by $2$, so $20\to18\to\dots\to2$ after $9$ steps.)

Solution 2 (Every number ends up in one of the two)

Follow any starting number. Each time it is erased, it becomes part of a new sum, and that sum may later be part of a bigger sum, and so on. It never disappears, and it is never counted twice. So each of the two final numbers is the sum of some of the original numbers $1,\dots,20$, and every original number is in exactly one of them. Therefore the two final numbers add up to $1+2+\dots+20$. Adding in pairs $1+20=2+19=\dots=10+11=21$ gives $10\times21=\boxed{\textbf{(E)}\ 210}$.

Problem 16

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · Blocked and Restricted Paths

The diagram shows a lake containing $10$ islands linked with $12$ bridges. What is the smallest number of bridges that would need to be closed to prevent anyone walking from island P to island Q?

(A) $1$ (B) $2$ (C) $3$ (D) $4$ (E) $5$

Show the answer

(B) $2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two separate walks, and a cut of two)

Name the eight small islands $A$ to $H$ as in the picture.

One bridge is not enough. There are two walks from P to Q that use no bridge in common: the top walk P–$A$–$B$–$C$–Q and the bottom walk P–$E$–$F$–$G$–Q. Closing one bridge blocks at most one of these walks, so the other is still open.

Two bridges are enough. Close the bridge $B$–$C$ and the bridge P–$E$ (the dashed ones). From P one can now reach only $A$, $D$ and $B$: the bridges from these islands go only to each other, to P, or to $C$, and $B$–$C$ is closed. So Q cannot be reached.

The smallest number is $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 2}$.

Solution 2 (Look at the islands near P)

Name the eight small islands $A$ to $H$ as in the picture. From P there are three bridges: to $A$, to $D$ and to $E$. Islands $A$ and $D$ are dead ends except for one way on: both lead only to island $B$, and from $B$ the only way on is the single bridge to $C$. So the only bridges that lead out of the group of islands P, $A$, $B$, $D$ are $B$–$C$ and P–$E$, and every walk from P to Q must cross one of these two bridges. Closing these two bridges therefore blocks every walk, and $2$ bridges are enough.

One bridge cannot be enough: whichever bridge is closed, at least one of the walks P–$A$–$B$–$C$–Q and P–$E$–$F$–$H$–Q stays open, because these two walks share no bridge. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 2}$.

Problem 17

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Worst-Case Thresholds

A professor asks her students to add up the two numbers in their birthday (month $+$ day). For example, Ana's birthday is May 4th, so she would add $5+4=9$. Ana says, “At least two people in this room are going to have the same sum”. What is the minimum number of people that must be in the room to guarantee Ana's statement is correct?

Show the answer

$43$ people

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the possible sums, then pigeonhole)

The smallest possible sum is January 1st: $1+1=2$. The largest is December 31st: $12+31=43$. Every whole number from $2$ to $43$ really is a sum: the sums $2$ to $32$ come from January ($1+1$ to $1+31$), and the sums $33$ to $43$ come from December ($12+21$ to $12+31$). So there are $43-2+1=42$ different sums.

With $42$ people it is possible that all sums are different (one person for each sum), so Ana could be wrong. With $43$ people there are more people than possible sums, so two of them must share a sum. The minimum is $\boxed{43}$.

Solution 2 (Build the worst case)

Ana's statement fails only if everyone in the room has a different sum. Let us try to fill the room with people who all have different sums, for as long as possible. A birthday sum is at least $1+1=2$ and at most $12+31=43$, so there are at most $42$ different sums, and we can find people for all of them: birthdays January 1 to January 31 give $2$ to $32$, and December 21 to December 31 give $33$ to $43$. So a room of $42$ people can have all sums different.

When the $43$rd person comes in, his sum is one of the $42$ values, already taken. So $43$ people guarantee a repeated sum, and $42$ do not: the answer is $\boxed{43}$.

Problem 18

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Truth and Lies

Ali said Dele ate the cake. Dele said Billi ate the cake. Billi said Callie ate the cake. Callie said she didn't eat the cake and Eddie said that he did. I know Ali didn't eat the cake, that one of the others did and that only one of them is telling the truth. Who ate the cake?

(A) Ali (B) Billi (C) Callie (D) Dele (E) Eddie

Show the answer

(C) Callie

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Try each possible eater)

The eater is Billi, Callie, Dele or Eddie. For each one, count the true statements.

  • Billi ate it: Dele (“Billi”) is right and Callie (“not me”) is right. Two true. No.

  • Callie ate it: Billi (“Callie”) is right. Ali (“Dele”), Dele (“Billi”), Callie (“not me”) and Eddie (“me”) are wrong. One true. Yes.

  • Dele ate it: Ali and Callie are right. Two true. No.

  • Eddie ate it: Eddie and Callie are right. Two true. No.

Only one case gives exactly one true statement: $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ \text{Callie}}$.

Solution 2 (Callie's statement cannot be true)

Look at Callie's statement “I didn't eat it”. If it is true, then Callie did not eat the cake, and someone else did: Billi, Dele or Eddie. But then one more statement is true as well: Dele's (if Billi ate it), Ali's (if Dele ate it) or Eddie's (if Eddie ate it). That makes two true statements, which is not allowed. So Callie's statement is false: Callie ate the cake. Check: then only Billi's statement is true. The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ \text{Callie}}$.

Problem 19

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Implication Chains

Freda likes even numbers, Greta likes numbers divisible by $3$ and Hattie likes numbers divisible by $5$. A basket contains eight balls, each with a number written on. The three girls went in turn to the basket and took all the balls with a number on that they like. Freda collected balls with numbers $32$ and $52$ on, Greta collected balls with numbers $24$, $33$ and $45$ on and Hattie collected balls with numbers $20$, $25$ and $35$ on. In what order did the girls go to the basket?

(A) Freda, Hattie, Greta (B) Hattie, Greta, Freda (C) Greta, Freda, Hattie
(D) Greta, Hattie, Freda (E) Hattie, Freda, Greta

Show the answer

(D) Greta, Hattie, Freda

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Find the balls a girl liked but did not get)

  • Freda likes even numbers, and $24$ and $20$ are even, but Greta took $24$ and Hattie took $20$. So both had already gone when Freda came: Freda was last.

  • Hattie likes multiples of $5$, and $45$ is one, but Greta took it. So Greta went before Hattie.

The order is Greta, Hattie, Freda: $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}}$.

Check: Greta takes the multiples of $3$: $24,33,45$. Hattie then takes the multiples of $5$ left: $20,25,35$. Freda takes the rest, $32$ and $52$, both even.

Solution 2 (Who could go first?)

The first girl takes every ball she likes, because all eight balls are still there. Freda first would take $20$, $24$, $32$, $52$ (all even), but she got only $32$ and $52$. Hattie first would take $20,25,35,45$, but $45$ went to Greta. Greta first takes $24,33,45$ (the multiples of $3$), which is exactly what she got. So Greta went first.

Then $20,25,32,35,52$ are left. If Freda went second she would take $20$, $32$, $52$, but $20$ went to Hattie. So Hattie went second and took $20,25,35$, and Freda took $32,52$ last. The order is Greta, Hattie, Freda: $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}}$.

Problem 20

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

Pierre writes all the numbers from $1$ to $20$ in a row and obtains the $31$-digit number \[1234567891011121314151617181920.\] He then deletes $24$ of the $31$ digits so that the remaining number is as large as possible. Which number does he get?

(A) $9671819$ (B) $9567892$ (C) $9781920$ (D) $9912345$ (E) $9818192$

Show the answer

(C) $9781920$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Choose the digits one at a time, greedily)

Pierre keeps $31-24=7$ digits, in their original order. A larger first digit always gives a larger number, so we make each digit as large as possible, from left to right, while leaving enough digits after it to finish.

Write the digits as \[1\,2\,3\,4\,5\,6\,7\,8\,9\ \ 1\,0\ \ 1\,1\ \ 1\,2\ \ 1\,3\ \ 1\,4\ \ 1\,5\ \ 1\,6\ \ 1\,7\ \ 1\,8\ \ 1\,9\ \ 2\,0.\]

  • First digit: we can keep a $9$ (the $9$th digit), since $22$ digits come after it. So the number starts with $9$.

  • Second digit: after it we still need $5$ more digits. The digits after the first $9$ are those of $10,11,\dots,20$. A $9$ or an $8$ cannot be chosen: after the $9$ of $19$ only $2$ digits remain, and after the $8$ of $18$ only $4$. The $7$ of $17$ leaves $6$ digits after it, $1\,8\,1\,9\,2\,0$, which is enough. So the second digit is $7$.

  • Last five digits: from $1\,8\,1\,9\,2\,0$ we keep $5$, so we delete one. Deleting the first $1$ gives $81920$, the largest choice.

Pierre gets $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 9781920}$.

Solution 2 (Compare the options)

The answer is one of the five options, so it is enough to find the largest option that Pierre can make. All options start with $9$, and the second digit decides most of them. The second digit cannot be $9$: the only other $9$ is in $19$, and after it there are only the digits $2$ and $0$, too few for the $5$ more digits needed. So (D) is impossible. The second digit cannot be $8$ either: after the $8$ of $18$ only $1,9,2,0$ remain, but $5$ more digits are needed. So (E) is impossible. Option (C) $9781920$ can be made: keep the $9$ of the first nine digits, the $7$ of $17$, and the digits $8$, $1$, $9$, $2$, $0$ from $18$ (only its $8$), $19$ and $20$. Its second digit $7$ is larger than the second digits of (A) ($6$) and (B) ($5$), so it beats them. The largest is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 9781920}$.
