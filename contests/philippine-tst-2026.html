Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 6, Problem 1; 2026-thailand-tst: TSTST 2 Day 1, Problem 8; 2026-romania-team-selection-tests: Day 2, Problem 4; 2026-azerbaijan-tsts: IMO TST Day 1, Problem 1; 2026-brazil-cono-sur-tst: Test 3, Problem 4

The sunshine cost of a sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of integers is the largest possible value of \[\left|(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_i)-a_j\right|\] as $i$ and $j$ vary over all integers $1,2,\ldots,100$.

Determine the smallest possible sunshine cost over all sequences $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of pairwise distinct integers.

Show the answer

$75$

Show the solution

Solution (Prefix sums are trapped in a short interval)

Write $P_i=a_1+\cdots+a_i$, let $A=\max a_j$, $B=\min a_j$, $D=A-B$, and let $M$ be the sunshine cost. As the $a_j$ are $100$ distinct integers, $D\ge99$.

Construction with cost $75$. Take $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, followed by the pairs $j,-j$ for $j=1,2,\dots,50$, $j\ne25$ (that is $2+2\cdot49=100$ distinct integers). The prefix sums are $-25$ after $a_1,a_2$ and after every complete pair, and $-25+j$ in the middle of the pair $j,-j$; so they range over $[-25,25]$, while the terms range over $[-50,50]$. Hence $M=\max(25-(-50),\,50-(-25))=75$.

Lower bound. For every $i$ and $j$, $|P_i-a_j|\le M$; with $j$ giving $A$ and $B$ this means \[P_i\in I:=[A-M,\ B+M]\qquad(1\le i\le100),\] an interval of length $L=2M-D$ (so $L\ge0$). For $i\ge2$, $a_i=P_i-P_{i-1}$ is a difference of two points of $I$, so $|a_i|\le L$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-L,L]$, which forces $2L+1\ge99$, i.e. $L\ge49$.

Suppose $M\le74$. Then $D=2M-L\le148-49=99$, so $D=99$, and then $L=2M-99\ge49$ forces $M=74$, $L=49$. So $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ are exactly the integers $-49,\dots,49$, and since all $100$ terms are consecutive integers ($D=99$), $a_1=50$ or $a_1=-50$. But then $|P_1-a_j|=|a_1-a_j|=99>74$ for $a_j=\mp49$. Contradiction. So $M\ge75$.

The smallest sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 3, Problem 2; 2026-azerbaijan-tsts: IMO TST Day 4, Problem 12; 2026-taiwan-tst: Round 3, Mock IMO Day 1, Problem 2

Bluey is placing lollies on a $1000\times 1000$ grid. Initially, every cell of the grid is empty. At each step, Bluey chooses a row or column in which the total number of lollies is a multiple of $3$, chooses an empty cell in this row or column, and places a lolly in it. If there is no such row or column, Bluey stops.

Determine the maximum number of lollies that Bluey can place.

Show the answer

$3995$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting visits to multiples of 3)

Call rows and columns lines; $n=1000$. A line is open if its number of lollies is a multiple of $3$. Every lolly raises the count of one row and one column; the line Bluey chose was open just before. Call a placement wasteful if both its row and its column were open just before it. Let $T$ be the total number of lollies and $W$ the number of wasteful placements.

When does Bluey stop? An open line has a multiple of $3$ lollies, so at most $999<1000$, hence an empty cell. So Bluey stops exactly when no line is open. In the final position let $n_L$ be the count of line $L$; then $n_L\equiv1$ or $2\pmod 3$ for all $2n$ lines. Let $k$ be the number of lines with $n_L\equiv2$, of which $x$ are rows and $y$ are columns.

The identity $T=4n-3W-k$. For a line $L$, the number of placements made in $L$ while $L$ was open equals the number of multiples of $3$ among $0,1,\dots,n_L-1$, which is $\lceil n_L/3\rceil$. A non-wasteful placement is made while exactly one of its two lines is open, a wasteful one while both are. Hence \[\sum_L\Bigl\lceil\frac{n_L}{3}\Bigr\rceil=T+W.\] If $n_L\equiv\rho_L\in\{1,2\}\pmod3$ then $\lceil n_L/3\rceil=\frac{n_L-\rho_L}3+1$. Using $\sum_Ln_L=2T$ (each lolly is counted in one row and one column) and $\sum_L\rho_L=2n+k$, \[\frac{2T-2n-k}{3}+2n=T+W\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=4n-3W-k.\]

Upper bound. The first placement is wasteful (everything is empty), so $W\ge1$.

  • If the last placement is also wasteful (and $T\ge2$), then $W\ge2$ and $T\le4n-6$.

  • Otherwise, in the last placement the chosen line goes to a count $\equiv1$, and the other line was not open, so it had count $\equiv1$ or $\equiv2$. In the second case it would end with count $\equiv0$, i.e. open, which is impossible at the end. So it goes from $\equiv1$ to $\equiv2$, and $k\ge1$. Moreover the sum of the row counts is $T$ and so is the sum of the column counts; modulo $3$ these sums are $n+x$ and $n+y$, so $x\equiv y\pmod3$. With $x+y=k\ge1$ this rules out $k=1$, so $k\ge2$ and $T\le4n-3-2=4n-5$.

In all cases $T\le4n-5=3995$.

Construction with $3995$ lollies. Number rows $r_1,\dots,r_n$ and columns $c_1,\dots,c_n$ and write $(i,j)$ for the cell in $r_i$ and $c_j$.

Start: place $15$ lollies in the corner $5\times5$ in the order shown in the figure, namely at \[(1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(2,1),(2,4),(1,4),(3,1),(4,2),(5,2),(2,2),(2,3),(5,1),(5,5),(5,3),(4,3).\] One checks move by move that each time the row or the column of the new cell is open; the open lines used are, in order, $r_1,c_2,c_3,r_2,c_4,r_1,r_3,r_4,r_5,c_2,r_2,c_1,c_5,r_5,c_3$. Afterwards rows $r_1,\dots,r_5$ have $4,4,1,2,4$ lollies and columns $c_1,\dots,c_5$ have $4,4,4,2,1$ lollies, and the cells $(5,4)$ and $(4,5)$ are empty.

Step $k$ ($k=6,7,\dots,n$): place lollies at \[(k,\,k-2),\quad(k-1,\,k-2),\quad(k-2,\,k),\quad(k-2,\,k-1)\quad\text{in this order.}\] Claim: just before step $k$, row $r_{k-2}$ and column $c_{k-2}$ have counts $\equiv2\pmod3$; row $r_{k-1}$ and column $c_{k-1}$ have counts $\equiv1\pmod3$; the cells $(k-1,k-2)$ and $(k-2,k-1)$ are empty; and all rows and columns with index $\ge k$ are empty. For $k=6$ this is the situation after the start. Given the claim, the four moves of step $k$ are legal:

  • 1. $(k,k-2)$: row $r_k$ is empty (open); $c_{k-2}$ goes from $\equiv2$ to $\equiv0$.

  • 2. $(k-1,k-2)$: column $c_{k-2}$ is open now; the cell is empty; $r_{k-1}$ goes from $\equiv1$ to $\equiv2$.

  • 3. $(k-2,k)$: column $c_k$ is empty (open); $r_{k-2}$ goes from $\equiv2$ to $\equiv0$.

  • 4. $(k-2,k-1)$: row $r_{k-2}$ is open now; the cell is empty; $c_{k-1}$ goes from $\equiv1$ to $\equiv2$.

After step $k$: $r_{k-1}$ and $c_{k-1}$ have counts $\equiv2$; $r_k$ and $c_k$ have one lolly each, at $(k,k-2)$ and $(k-2,k)$, so the cells $(k,k-1)$ and $(k-1,k)$ are empty; lines with index $\ge k+1$ are untouched. So the claim holds for $k+1$. Also $r_{k-2}$ and $c_{k-2}$ now have counts $\equiv2+2\equiv1\pmod 3$, and no other line changed.

After step $n$ every line has count $\equiv1$ or $2\pmod3$ (only $r_{n-1}$ and $c_{n-1}$ are $\equiv2$), so Bluey stops, having placed $15+4(n-5)=4n-5=3995$ lollies.

So the maximum is $\boxed{3995}$.

Problem 3

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Counting Integer Configurations · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 6, Problem 3; 2026-azerbaijan-tsts: IMO TST Day 1, Problem 3; 2026-thailand-tst: TST 1 Day 2, Problem 6 (part (a) only)

For positive integers $n$ and $k$, let $f_k(n)$ denote the remainder when $n$ is divided by $2^k-1$. A positive integer $n$ is grouse if \[f_1(n)\leq f_2(n)\leq f_3(n)\leq f_4(n)\leq\cdots\]

  • (a) Prove that there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • (b) Prove that there exists a positive integer $M$ such that there are at most $\dfrac{M}{2025^{2025}}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A greedy chain of multiples; grouse numbers hug the powers of two)

Write $d_i=2^i-1$. Always $f_1(n)=0$, and $f_i(n)=n$ as soon as $d_i>n$.

The greedy chain. Fix $k\ge1$. Put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,k-2,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$. Then $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_k$ and $x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. More generally, starting the chain at any $x_i$, summing these steps gives \[0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i. \tag{1}\]

(a) We show that $x_1$ is grouse; since $x_1\ge x_k=2^k-1$ and $k$ is arbitrary, this gives infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • For $i\le k$: $x_i$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and $0\le x_1-x_i<d_i$ by (1), so $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$. As $x_i$ does not increase with $i$, the values $f_1(x_1)\le f_2(x_1)\le\dots\le f_k(x_1)$ are non-decreasing.

  • For $i>k$: by (1), $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge x_1-x_k=f_k(x_1)$.

(b) Claim. If $2^k\le n<2^{k+1}$ and $n$ is grouse, then $n>2^{k+1}-3\cdot2^{k/2}$. (For $k=0$ this is trivial, so let $k\ge1$.)

Step 1: $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$ for $1\le i\le k$. The number $n-f_i(n)$ is the largest multiple of $d_i$ not exceeding $n$. For $i=k$: $d_k\le n$, so $n-f_k(n)\ge d_k=x_k$. If $n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, then, since $n$ is grouse, $n-f_i(n)\ge n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, and as $n-f_i(n)$ is a multiple of $d_i$, the definition of $x_i$ gives $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$.

Step 2: $x_i=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ for $k/2\le i\le k$. For $i=k$ both sides equal $d_k$. Suppose it holds for $i+1$, where $k/2\le i<k$. The number $y=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and \[y-x_{i+1}=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)-(2^{i+1}-1)(2^{k-i}-1)=2^i-2^{k-i},\] which lies in $[0,d_i)$ because $i\ge k-i$ and $k-i\ge1$. So $y$ is the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is at least $x_{i+1}$, i.e. $y=x_i$.

Step 3. Take $i=\lceil k/2\rceil$. Then $n\ge n-f_i(n)\ge x_i=2^{k+1}-\bigl(2^i+2^{k+1-i}\bigr)+1$. If $k=2t$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=3\cdot2^t=3\cdot2^{k/2}$; if $k=2t-1$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=2^{t+1}=2\sqrt2\cdot2^{k/2}<3\cdot2^{k/2}$. This proves the claim.

Counting. By the claim, the block $[2^k,2^{k+1})$ contains at most $3\cdot2^{k/2}$ grouse numbers. Let $M=2^{b+1}$. The grouse numbers below $M$ lie in the blocks with $k=0,1,\dots,b$, so there are at most \[\sum_{k=0}^{b}3\cdot2^{k/2}<\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\,2^{(b+1)/2}=\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\sqrt M\] of them. This is at most $M/2025^{2025}$ as soon as $\sqrt M\ge3\cdot2025^{2025}/(\sqrt2-1)$, which holds for $b$ large enough. Such an $M$ proves (b).

Solution 2 (Part (b) by forbidden residue classes and the Chinese remainder theorem)

(a) Write $d_i=2^i-1$; then $f_i(n)=n$ whenever $d_i>n$. Fix $k\ge1$, put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$; so $x_{i+1}\le x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. Summing these steps, $0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j<i}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i$. Hence for $i\le k$ we get $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$, which does not decrease as $i$ grows; and for $i>k$ we get $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge f_k(x_1)$. Thus $x_1\ge2^k-1$ is grouse, and as $k$ is arbitrary there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

(b) We only use the inequality $f_{2k-1}(n)\le f_{2k+1}(n)$, which every grouse number satisfies.

Forbidden classes. Let $k\ge2$, $a=2^{2k-1}-1$ and $b=2^{2k+1}-1$. Recall $\gcd(2^u-1,2^v-1)=2^{\gcd(u,v)}-1$; since $\gcd(2k-1,2k+1)=1$, the numbers $a,b$ are coprime. For each pair of integers $0\le j<i\le a-1$ (there are $\binom a2$ pairs; note $j<a<b$) the Chinese remainder theorem gives one residue class modulo $ab$ consisting of the $n$ with $n\equiv i\pmod a$ and $n\equiv j\pmod b$, that is, $f_{2k-1}(n)=i>j=f_{2k+1}(n)$. No grouse number lies in these $\binom a2$ classes. So the fraction of classes modulo $ab$ that may contain grouse numbers is at most \[1-\frac{\binom a2}{ab}=1-\frac{a-1}{2b}=1-\frac{2^{2k-2}-1}{2^{2k+1}-1}=1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8(2^{2k+1}-1)}\le1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8\cdot31}=\frac{28}{31},\] using $2^{2k+1}-1\ge31$.

Many independent moduli. Choose $k_1=2$ and $k_j=\prod_{i<j}(2k_i-1)(2k_i+1)$ for $j\ge2$. Each $k_j$ is at least $2$, and every earlier number $2k_i\pm1$ divides $k_j$, so it is coprime to $2k_j\pm1$; also $\gcd(2k_j-1,2k_j+1)=1$. Hence the exponents $2k_j\pm1$ $(j=1,\dots,s)$ are pairwise coprime, and so are the numbers $a_j=2^{2k_j-1}-1$, $b_j=2^{2k_j+1}-1$. Put $M=\prod_{j=1}^{s}a_jb_j$.

Counting. The numbers $0,1,\dots,M-1$ represent each class modulo $M$ once. If such a number is grouse, its class modulo each $a_jb_j$ is not forbidden. By the Chinese remainder theorem the classes modulo $M$ correspond to tuples of classes modulo $a_1b_1,\dots,a_sb_s$, so at most \[\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{28}{31}\,a_jb_j=\Bigl(\frac{28}{31}\Bigr)^{s}M\] numbers below $M$ are grouse. Choosing $s$ with $(28/31)^s\le2025^{-2025}$ gives at most $M/2025^{2025}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

Problem 4

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 2, Problem 2; 2026-azerbaijan-tsts: IMO TST Day 1, Problem 2; 2026-japan-tst: Problem 11; 2026-taiwan-tst: Round 1, Mock IMO Day 2, Problem 5

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. The perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$ intersects $\Gamma$ again at $D\neq B$. Let $Q$ be the point on $\Gamma$, different from $B$, such that $AB=AQ$. Lines $BQ$ and $AC$ intersect at $R$. Line $DR$ intersects $\Gamma$ again at $S\neq D$. Segment $BC$ intersects the circumcircle of triangle $QRS$ at $T$. Lines $TQ$ and $RS$ intersect at $X$. Prove that $CX$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 ($T$ is the reflection of $Q$, then a harmonic division)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of $ABC$; $AB<AC$ gives $\gamma<\beta$. Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (so $B,E,D$ are collinear) and $H$ the orthocenter. We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: where $Q$ and $R$ are. The chords $AQ$ and $AB$ are equal, so the arcs $AQ$ and $AB$ (not containing $C$) both measure $2\gamma$. Since $2\gamma<2\beta$, the point $Q$ lies on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, strictly between $A$ and $C$, and the arc $QC$ measures $2\beta-2\gamma$. Hence chord $BQ$ crosses chord $AC$, so $R$ lies inside both segments, and by inscribed angles \[\angle ABQ=\angle AQB=\angle QCA=\angle ACB=\gamma .\] In particular $\angle BRA=180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma=\beta$.

Step 2: $T$ is the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$. Since $\angle QCA=\angle ACB$, line $CA$ bisects angle $QCB$, so the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CQ^*=CQ$. The chord $CQ$ subtends the arc $2\beta-2\gamma$ and the chord $CB$ the arc $2\alpha$; as $\beta-\gamma<\alpha$ (because $\beta<90^\circ<\alpha+\gamma$), $CQ^*=CQ<CB$ and $Q^*$ lies on segment $BC$.

Now $RQ=RQ^*$ and $QQ^*\perp AC$, so $QQ^*\parallel BD$. Hence \[\measuredangle RQ^*Q=\measuredangle Q^*QR=\measuredangle(BD,BQ)=\measuredangle DBQ=\measuredangle DSQ=\measuredangle RSQ,\] using the isosceles triangle $RQQ^*$, the parallel lines, the circle $\Gamma$ through $B,D,S,Q$, and the collinearity of $D,R,S$. So $Q^*$ lies on the circle $(QRS)$. It remains to see that $Q^*$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on this circle. Since $R$ lies between $B$ and $Q$, the power of $B$ with respect to the circle is $BR\cdot BQ>0$, so $B$ is outside it. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $QQ^*$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $AC$. Because $\angle QCA=\gamma<90^\circ$ and $\angle QRC=\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the point $M$ lies on ray $CA$ and on ray $RC$, hence strictly between $R$ and $C$: the points $R$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the chord $QQ^*$. As $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $QQ^*$, the chord is seen from $R$ under the angle $\angle QRQ^*=2\angle QRM=2\beta$ and from $C$ under $\angle QCQ^*=2\gamma$. A point on the other side of the chord from $R$ lies inside the circle exactly when it sees the chord under an angle greater than $180^\circ-2\beta$; here $2\gamma>180^\circ-2\beta$ because $\alpha<90^\circ$. So $C$ is inside the circle, and segment $BC$, which joins an outside point to an inside point, meets the circle exactly once. Hence $T=Q^*$. Consequently line $TQ$ is the line $QQ^*$, perpendicular to $AC$.

Step 3: a harmonic division on $AC$. Recall that $M$, the midpoint of $QQ^*$, lies on $AC$. The line $CR=CA$ is the internal bisector of angle $QCB$; let the external bisector meet line $BQ$ at $R'$. Then $(B,Q;R,R')=-1$. Project line $BQ$ onto line $AC$ in the direction perpendicular to $AC$: $B\mapsto E$, $Q\mapsto M$, $R\mapsto R$, and $R'\mapsto C$ (since $CR'\perp CA$). Parallel projection preserves cross-ratios, so $(E,M;R,C)=-1$, i.e. \[\frac{\overline{RM}}{\overline{RE}}=-\frac{\overline{CM}}{\overline{CE}}. \tag{1}\]

Step 4: $X$ lies on the altitude from $C$. Use coordinates with $AC$ as $x$-axis; points of $AC$ have abscissae $r,c,m,e$ (for $R,C,M,E$). As the triangle is acute, $D$ is the reflection of $H$ in $AC$: $D=(e,d)$, $H=(e,-d)$. The point $X$ lies on line $QQ^*$, i.e. $X=(m,x)$, and on line $RD$: \[x=d\,\frac{m-r}{e-r}.\] The altitude from $C$ is line $CH$; it meets line $QQ^*$ at $X'=(m,x')$ with $x'=-d\,\dfrac{m-c}{e-c}$. By (1) these are equal, so $X=X'$ lies on line $CH$, and $CX\perp AB$.

Solution 2 (Three pairs of similar right triangles)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of $ABC$; $AB<AC$ gives $\gamma<\beta$. Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (so $B,E,D$ are collinear). We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Where $Q$ and $R$ are. The chords $AQ$ and $AB$ are equal, so the arcs $AQ$ and $AB$ (not containing $C$) both measure $2\gamma$. Since $2\gamma<2\beta$, the point $Q$ lies on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, strictly between $A$ and $C$, and the arc $QC$ measures $2\beta-2\gamma$. Hence chord $BQ$ crosses chord $AC$, so $R$ lies inside both segments, and by inscribed angles \[\angle ABQ=\angle AQB=\angle QCA=\angle ACB=\gamma .\] In particular $\angle BRA=180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma=\beta$.

$T$ is the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$. Since $\angle QCA=\angle ACB$, line $CA$ bisects angle $QCB$, so the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CQ^*=CQ$. The chord $CQ$ subtends the arc $2\beta-2\gamma$ and the chord $CB$ the arc $2\alpha$; as $\beta-\gamma<\alpha$ (because $\beta<90^\circ<\alpha+\gamma$), $CQ^*=CQ<CB$ and $Q^*$ lies on segment $BC$.

Now $RQ=RQ^*$ and $QQ^*\perp AC$, so $QQ^*\parallel BD$. Hence \[\measuredangle RQ^*Q=\measuredangle Q^*QR=\measuredangle(BD,BQ)=\measuredangle DBQ=\measuredangle DSQ=\measuredangle RSQ,\] using the isosceles triangle $RQQ^*$, the parallel lines, the circle $\Gamma$ through $B,D,S,Q$, and the collinearity of $D,R,S$. So $Q^*$ lies on the circle $(QRS)$. It remains to see that $Q^*$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on this circle. Since $R$ lies between $B$ and $Q$, the power of $B$ with respect to the circle is $BR\cdot BQ>0$, so $B$ is outside it. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $QQ^*$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $AC$. Because $\angle QCA=\gamma<90^\circ$ and $\angle QRC=\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the point $M$ lies on ray $CA$ and on ray $RC$, hence strictly between $R$ and $C$: the points $R$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the chord $QQ^*$. As $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $QQ^*$, the chord is seen from $R$ under the angle $\angle QRQ^*=2\angle QRM=2\beta$ and from $C$ under $\angle QCQ^*=2\gamma$. A point on the other side of the chord from $R$ lies inside the circle exactly when it sees the chord under an angle greater than $180^\circ-2\beta$; here $2\gamma>180^\circ-2\beta$ because $\alpha<90^\circ$. So $C$ is inside the circle, and segment $BC$, which joins an outside point to an inside point, meets the circle exactly once. Hence $T=Q^*$, and line $TQ$ is perpendicular to $AC$ at $M$ and parallel to $BD$; moreover $M$ lies strictly between $R$ and $C$.

Positions on $AC$. Since $\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the foot $E$ lies on ray $RA$; hence $R$ lies strictly between $E$ and $M$. Since the triangle is acute, $E$ lies inside segment $AC$, and so do $M$ and $R$.

Three similarities. All of the following triangles are right-angled at $M$ or $E$.

Solution 1 (Halving the triple and counting the halvings)

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$, $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. Parity lemma. Call a triple reduced if not all its entries are even. For a reduced triple $r$: if $r$ has exactly one even entry, $\Phi(r)$ has exactly two; if $r$ has exactly two even entries, $\Phi(r)$ is all odd; if $r$ is all odd, $\Phi(r)$ is all even. (A gcd is even only if both arguments are even; e.g. for $a$ even and $b,c$ odd all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ gives odd, even, even.) In particular $\Phi(r)$ is all even if and only if $r$ is all odd.

Reduced sequence. Let $2^{e_t}$ be the largest power of $2$ dividing all of $a_t,b_t,c_t$, $r_t=T_t/2^{e_t}$ (reduced) and $x_t=a_t/2^{e_t}\ge1$, so $a_t=2^{e_t}x_t$. Since $T_{t+1}=2^{e_t}\Phi(r_t)$, the parity lemma gives: if $r_t$ is not all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t$ and $x_{t+1}=x_t+g\ge x_t+1$ ($g$ a gcd); if $r_t$ is all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t+\delta$ with $\delta\ge1$ and $x_{t+1}=(x_t+g)/2^\delta$.

Let $t_1<\dots<t_K$ be the times $t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}$ with $r_t$ all odd, $\delta_i$ the corresponding increments, $d_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, $s=N-1-t_K$ and $y_i=x_{t_i+1}$. Put \[D=(2-t_1)+\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)=3K-2025+s\ \ (\ge0).\] Also $x_N\ge y_K+s$, $y_1\ge\frac{x_{t_1}+1}{2^{\delta_1}}\ge\frac{t_1+2}{2^{\delta_1}}$ (as $x_{t_1}\ge x_0+t_1$), and \[y_{i+1}\ge\frac{x_{t_{i+1}}+1}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}\ge\frac{y_i+d_i}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}. \tag{1}\] If $\delta_{j+1}=\dots=\delta_i=1$, the shortfalls $z_i=3-y_i$ satisfy $z_{i+1}\le\frac{z_i+(3-d_i)}{2}$, hence \[z_i\le\frac{z_j}{2^{\,i-j}}+\frac12\sum_{j\le l<i}(3-d_l). \tag{2}\] Since $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N$, it suffices to exclude $e_N=675$ with $x_N\le2$ and $e_N=676$ with $x_N=1$. $K=675$, all $\delta_i=1$. Then $D=s$. By (2) with $j=1$ and $z_1\le2-\frac{t_1}2\le2$: $z_K\le2^{-673}+\frac12\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)\le2^{-673}+\frac s2$. So $x_N\ge y_K+s=3-z_K+s\ge3-2^{-673}+\frac s2>2$, i.e. $x_N\ge3$.

$e_0=0$, $K=676$, all $\delta_i=1$. Now $D=3+s$ and (2) gives $z_K\le2^{-674}+\frac{3+s}2$, so $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge\frac32-2^{-674}+\frac s2>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

$e_0=0$, $K=675$, one $\delta_j=2$. Here $D=s$. If $j<K$: $y_j\ge1$, so $z_j\le2$ and (2) from $j$ gives $z_K\le1+\frac s2$, hence $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge2+\frac s2\ge2$. If $j=K$ and $s\ge1$: $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge1+1=2$. If $j=K$ and $s=0$: then $D=0$, so $t_1=2$ and all $d_i=3$; by (2) (all earlier $\delta=1$) $z_{K-1}<1$, so $y_{K-1}\ge3$, $x_{t_K}\ge y_{K-1}+2\ge5$ and $x_N=y_K\ge\frac{5+1}{4}>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

In every case $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N\ge3\cdot2^{675}$. Hence the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

Solution 2 (Doubling every three steps)

Write $T_t=(a_t,b_t,c_t)$ and $\Phi(a,b,c)=\bigl(a+\gcd(b,c),\,b+\gcd(c,a),\,c+\gcd(a,b)\bigr)$, so that $T_{t+1}=\Phi(T_t)$. For every positive integer $g$ we have $\Phi(ga,gb,gc)=g\,\Phi(a,b,c)$.

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$ and $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. For every $g$, \[g(3,5,6)\ \mapsto\ g(4,8,7)\ \mapsto\ g(5,9,11)\ \mapsto\ g(6,10,12)=2g\,(3,5,6),\] so $T_{3j}=2^j(3,5,6)$ for all $j\ge2$, and $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}=3\cdot2^{675}$.

Parity. A gcd of two integers is even only if both integers are even. Count the even entries of a triple. If exactly one entry is even, all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes that entry odd and the two odd entries even: two even entries. If exactly two entries are even, the gcd of the two even ones is even and the other two gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes all three entries odd. If all entries are odd, all gcds are odd and $\Phi$ makes all entries even. If all entries are even, so are all gcds and all new entries. So the number of even entries changes by $1\to2\to0\to3$ and $3\to3$; in particular, whatever triple $R$ of positive integers we start with, all entries of $\Phi(\Phi(\Phi(R)))$ are even.

Powers of two. We show by induction on $j$ that $2^j$ divides every entry of $T_t$ for $3j\le t\le 3j+3$. Suppose $T_{3j}=2^jR$ for a triple $R$ of positive integers (true for $j=0$). Then $T_{3j+i}=2^j\Phi^i(R)$ for $i=0,1,2,3$, and all entries of $\Phi^3(R)$ are even, so $T_{3j+3}=2^{j+1}R'$ for a triple $R'$ of positive integers. This proves the claim.

Lower bound. For $3j\le t\le3j+2$ the numbers $b_t,c_t$ are multiples of $2^j$, so $\gcd(b_t,c_t)\ge2^j$ and $a_{t+1}\ge a_t+2^j$. Hence \[a_{3j+3}\ge a_{3j}+3\cdot2^j\qquad(j\ge0).\] With $j=0$ and $j=1$: $a_3\ge a_0+3\ge4$ and $a_6\ge a_3+6\ge10$. But $a_6$ is a multiple of $2^2=4$, so $a_6\ge12=3\cdot2^2$. If $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for some $j\ge2$, then $a_{3j+3}\ge3\cdot2^j+3\cdot2^j=3\cdot2^{j+1}$. By induction $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for all $j\ge2$; in particular $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}\ge3\cdot2^{675}$.

Together with the construction, the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

  • First, Tam chooses a polynomial $P_k(x)=a_kx^k+a_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0$ whose coefficients $a_k,a_{k-1},\ldots,a_1,a_0$ are all of the numbers currently written on the blackboard in some order.   • Then, if the equation $P_k(x)=0$ has no real solutions, the game is stopped. Otherwise, Tim chooses a real number $r$ such that $P_k(r)=0$ and writes it on the blackboard, so there is now one more number on the blackboard.

Determine whether Tim can ensure that the game is never stopped, no matter what Tam does.

Show the answer

not determined

Show the solution

Solution (Partial progress: what each player can force)

This is partial progress only: the question whether Tim can keep the game going forever is not settled here.

Throughout, $m$ is the number of numbers on the board, so Tam's polynomial has degree $k=m-1$.

(1) Only rounds with $m$ odd matter. If all numbers on the board are nonzero, every arrangement has a nonzero constant term, so $0$ is never a root and the board never contains $0$. If $m$ is even, $k$ is odd and every arrangement has a real root. Hence the game can stop only in a round in which $m$ is odd.

(2) A winning criterion for Tam. Let $m=2s+1$, and suppose that the $s+1$ numbers of largest absolute value (ties broken arbitrarily) all have the same sign. Then Tam stops the game at once. Since a polynomial and its negative have the same roots, we may assume that these numbers $p_0,\dots,p_s$ are positive; call the remaining numbers $q_1,\dots,q_s$, so that $|q_j|\le\min_ip_i$ for every $j$. Tam chooses \[P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_jx^{2j-1}.\] For real $x$ and $1\le j\le s$, the AM-GM inequality gives \[\tfrac12p_{j-1}x^{2j-2}+\tfrac12p_jx^{2j}\ \ge\ \sqrt{p_{j-1}p_j}\,|x|^{2j-1}\ \ge\ |q_j|\,|x|^{2j-1}.\] Adding these $s$ inequalities, and noting that the left sides add up to $\sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}-\tfrac12p_0-\tfrac12p_sx^{2s}$, we get \[P(x)\ \ge\ \sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}-\sum_{j=1}^{s}|q_j|\,|x|^{2j-1}\ \ge\ \tfrac12p_0+\tfrac12p_sx^{2s}\ >\ 0,\] so $P$ has no real root. In particular Tam wins at once whenever $m$ is odd and all numbers have the same sign; once both signs are present, they stay present.

(3) Two situations in which every arrangement has a real root. Let $\Sigma$ be the sum of the numbers; $P(1)=\Sigma$ for every arrangement.

  • If $\Sigma=0$, then $1$ is a root of every arrangement.

  • If $\Sigma<0$ and exactly one number $N$ is negative, then every arrangement, of any degree, has a positive root: if $N$ is not the constant term, then $P(0)>0>P(1)$; if it is, the leading coefficient is positive and $P(1)<0<P(x)$ for large $x$. The same holds with all signs reversed.

These are not the only safe boards: for $1,4,-\tfrac92$ (positive sum, two positive numbers) every arrangement has a root, because $-\tfrac92$ must stand in the middle and $(\tfrac92)^2>4\cdot1\cdot4$.

(4) The second situation cannot be kept forever. Suppose the board has exactly one negative number $N$ and $\Sigma<0$, and Tam makes $N$ the constant term. For $0<|x|\le1$ every other term satisfies $a_ix^i\le a_i|x|^i\le a_i$, so $P(x)\le N+(\Sigma-N)=\Sigma<0$. Hence every real root $r$ has $|r|>1$. If Tim writes a positive root (to keep a single negative number), $\Sigma$ increases by more than $1$; if he writes a negative root, the board gets a second negative number of absolute value greater than $1$. So if Tam always does this, Tim can stay in this situation only for finitely many rounds.

(5) Experiments. An exhaustive search of the game tree for small boards (Tam tries every arrangement, Tim every real root, roots computed numerically) shows: from the two-number starts $1,-c$ ($c=4,5,8,20$) Tam wins by the round with $5$ numbers; from random three-number starts the only boards found from which Tim survives up to $7$ numbers were of the type in (3), for example $-13.94,\,3.06,\,3.88$, which by (4) lose that property after a number of rounds that grows with the size of the negative number. This is consistent with the guess that Tam can always stop the game, but it proves nothing.

What is missing. A strategy for Tam that works against every initial board (presumably the answer is that Tim cannot ensure that the game never stops), or an invariant for Tim more robust than those in (3).

Solution (T-pentominoes sharing three cells have the same type)

Ben can always find a good T-pentomino. Suppose, for contradiction, that no T-pentomino is good. Then every T-pentomino has $0$, $1$, $4$ or $5$ pink cells; call it pink-type if it has at least $4$ pink cells and green-type if it has at most $1$.

Lemma 1. Two T-pentominoes sharing at least $3$ cells have the same type. Indeed, if $T_1$ is pink-type, at most one of its cells is green, so any $3$ of its cells contain at least $2$ pink cells; if $T_2$ were green-type, those $3$ shared cells would contain at most $1$ pink cell.

Notation. Use unit vectors $d$ and $d^{\perp}$ along the grid directions. For a cell $c$ and a direction $d$ let $T(c,d)$ be the T-pentomino with bar $c-d^{\perp},c,c+d^{\perp}$ and stem $c+d,c+2d$. Call $c$ interior if it is at distance at least $2$ from every side of the grid; then all four $T(c,d)$ fit in the grid. The interior cells form a rectangle of size $(m-4)\times(n-4)$, nonempty and connected since $m,n\ge8$.

Lemma 2. All T-pentominoes with interior centre have the same type. First, $T(c,d)$ and $T(c,d^{\perp})$ share the cells $c$, $c+d$, $c+d^{\perp}$, so all four rotations about an interior $c$ have the same type; call it the type of $c$. Second, for neighbouring interior cells $c$ and $c+d$, the pentominoes $T(c,d)$ and $T(c+d,d^{\perp})$ share $c$, $c+d$, $c+2d$ (the stem of the first is part of the bar of the second). So neighbouring interior cells have the same type, and by connectedness all interior cells do.

The two blocks. Let $Q$ be the pink $3\times3$ block. Move its centre one step towards the middle of the grid in each coordinate in which the centre is at distance $1$ from a side; the resulting cell $c_1$ still lies in $Q$ and is interior (each coordinate is now at distance at least $2$ from the sides, as $m,n\ge5$). If $c_1$ is the centre of $Q$, any $T(c_1,d)$ has $4$ cells in $Q$; if $c_1$ is the midpoint of a side of $Q$, choosing $d$ pointing into $Q$ puts all $5$ cells in $Q$; if $c_1$ is a corner of $Q$, choosing $d$ along a side of $Q$ into $Q$ puts $4$ cells in $Q$. In each case $T(c_1,d)$ has at least $4$ pink cells and is pink-type. The same argument for the green block gives an interior-centred T-pentomino with at most $1$ pink cell. This contradicts Lemma 2.

Hence for every coloring as in the statement Ben can find a good T-pentomino (only $m,n\ge5$ was needed).

Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, $A_1$ the antipode of $A$ on $\Omega$, and $A'$ the reflection of $H$ in $BC$; as is well known, $A'$ is the second intersection of line $AH$ with $\Omega$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in $BC$; it reverses directed angles. Directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ are taken modulo $180^\circ$. We show that the circle $\omega=(A'MP)$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $A'$ and passes through $Q$.

Step 1: $XA'$ is tangent to $\Omega$. By $\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OM}$, the reflection $O''$ of $O$ in $BC$ satisfies $\overrightarrow{OO''}=\overrightarrow{AH}$, so $AHO''O$ is a parallelogram and $HO''\parallel AO$. The tangent $t$ to $\Omega$ at $A'$ is perpendicular to $OA'$, so $\sigma(t)$ passes through $\sigma(A')=H$ and is perpendicular to $\sigma(OA')=O''H$, i.e. perpendicular to $AO$. Hence $\sigma(t)$ is the line $HN$. A line and its mirror image in $BC$ meet $BC$ at the same point, so $t$ passes through $X=HN\cap BC$ (they are not parallel to $BC$, the triangle being scalene). Thus $XA'$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $A'$.

Step 2: $MH=MN$. $M$ is the midpoint of $HA_1$ (a standard fact: $BHCA_1$ is a parallelogram), and $\angle HNA_1=90^\circ$ because $N$ lies on line $AO=AA_1$ with $HN\perp AO$. So $M$ is the circumcentre of the right triangle $HNA_1$ and $MH=MN$. Let $\lambda$ be the line through $M$ perpendicular to $HN$ (parallel to $AO$); it is the axis of the isosceles triangle $MHN$, so the reflection in $\lambda$ swaps the lines $MH$, $MN$ and \[\angle(MH,MN)=2\angle(MH,\lambda),\qquad \angle(NH,NM)=\angle(HM,HN).\]

Step 3: $\omega$ is tangent to $XA'$ at $A'$. By the tangent-chord angle, we must show $\angle(A'X,A'P)=\angle(MA',MP)$. Since $A'X=\sigma(HN)$ and $A'P=AH=\sigma(AH)$, \[\angle(A'X,A'P)=-\angle(HN,AH)=\angle(HP,HN)=\angle(MP,MN),\] the last step because $H,M,N,P$ are concyclic. Put $\theta=\angle(BC,MP)$. As $MA'=\sigma(MH)$, \[\angle(MA',MP)=\angle(\sigma(MH),BC)+\theta=\angle(BC,MH)+\theta,\qquad \angle(MP,MN)=-\theta+\angle(BC,MN).\] So the required equality is $2\theta=\angle(MH,MN)$. Now, using $AH\perp BC$, the circle $(HMNP)$ and Step 2, \[\theta=\angle(BC,PH)+\angle(PH,PM)=90^\circ+\angle(NH,NM)=90^\circ+\angle(HM,HN)=\angle(HM,HN)+\angle(HN,\lambda)=\angle(MH,\lambda),\] hence $2\theta=2\angle(MH,\lambda)=\angle(MH,MN)$. So $\omega$ is tangent to the line $XA'$ at $A'$, and since $XA'$ is also tangent to $\Omega$ at $A'$ (Step 1), the circles $\omega$ and $\Omega$ are tangent at $A'$.

Step 4: $Q\in\omega$. The point $X$ lies on line $BC$, so its power with respect to $\Omega$ and to the circle $(BPC)$ is $XB\cdot XC$; on the other hand its power with respect to $\Omega$ is $XA'^2$ (tangent), and with respect to $(BPC)$ it is $XP\cdot XQ$. Its power with respect to $\omega$ is also $XA'^2$ (tangent). If $Q'$ is the second intersection of line $XP$ with $\omega$, then $XP\cdot XQ'=XA'^2=XP\cdot XQ$, so $Q'=Q$.

Thus $\omega=(PQM)$, and it is tangent to $\Omega$ at $A'$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates with $BC$ as the axis: the radical axis is tangent)

Choose coordinates with $M=(0,0)$, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(h,a)$ with $a>0$, and let $O=(0,o)$. As the triangle is acute, $O$ lies inside it, so $o>0$; as $AB\ne AC$, $h\ne0$. The circumcircle $\Omega$ has equation $x^2+y^2-2oy-1=0$, and $A\in\Omega$ gives $h^2+a^2-2ao=1$.

The orthocentre. With vectors, $H=A+B+C-2O$: indeed $B+C-2O$ is perpendicular to $BC$ (it is twice the vector from $O$ to the midpoint $M$), so $H-A$ is perpendicular to $BC$, and cyclically. Since $B+C=0$, $H=(h,k)$ with $k=a-2o$. Then $A\in\Omega$ reads \[h^2+ak=1.\tag{1}\] Put $d=a-o=k+o$, so that $A-O=(h,d)$.

The points $X$ and $N$. The line $HN$ is perpendicular to $AO$, so it is $\ell(x,y)=0$ with $\ell(x,y)=h(x-h)+d(y-k)$. Put $J=h^2+dk$; by (1), $J=h^2+k^2+ok=1-ok$. The line meets the $x$-axis at $X=(\xi,0)$ with $\xi=J/h$. Here $J\ne0$: otherwise $X=M$ would lie on $HN$, and the circle $(HMN)$ would not exist. Also $H\ne N$, because $H\in AO$ only when $AB=AC$.

The point $P$. The circle $\Gamma$ with diameter $HO$ has equation $x(x-h)+(y-k)(y-o)=0$, and it passes through $N$ because $\angle HNO=90^\circ$. The difference of the normalized equations of two circles through $H$ and $N$ is a linear function vanishing at $H$ and $N$, hence a multiple of $\ell$. So $(HMN)$ has equation \[x^2+y^2-hx-(k+o)y+ko+\alpha\,\ell(x,y)=0\] for some $\alpha$. It passes through $M$, so $ko-\alpha J=0$, i.e. $\alpha=\frac{ko}J$. On the line $AH$, which is $x=h$, the equation becomes a quadratic in $y$ whose roots add up to $k+o-\alpha d$; one root is $k$ (the point $H$), so the other is \[p=o-\alpha d=\frac{o(J-dk)}J=\frac{oh^2}J,\qquad P=(h,p),\quad p\ne0 .\]

The circle $(PQM)$. It passes through $M$, so its equation is $x^2+y^2-rx-vy=0$, and it meets the $x$-axis at $M$ and at $(r,0)$. The points $X,P,Q$ are collinear and $P,Q$ lie on both circles $(PQM)$ and $(BPC)$, so the power of $X$ with respect to the two circles is the same (the signed product $\overline{XP}\cdot\overline{XQ}$): $\xi(\xi-r)=(\xi+1)(\xi-1)$. Hence $r=\frac1\xi=\frac hJ$. Since $P$ lies on the circle, \[v=\frac{h^2+p^2-rh}{p}=p+\frac{h^2(1-1/J)}{oh^2/J}=p+\frac{J-1}{o}=p-k .\]

The radical axis. Subtracting the equation of $\Omega$ from that of $(PQM)$ gives the radical axis $-rx+(2o-v)y+1=0$. Here, by (1), \[2o-v=2o+k-p=a-\frac{oh^2}J=\frac{a(1-ok)-oh^2}{J}=\frac{a-o(ak+h^2)}J=\frac{a-o}J=\frac dJ,\] so, multiplying by $J$, the radical axis is $\rho:\ -hx+dy+J=0$. Its distance from $O=(0,o)$ is \[\frac{|do+J|}{\sqrt{h^2+d^2}}=\frac{h^2+d(k+o)}{\sqrt{h^2+d^2}}=\frac{h^2+d^2}{\sqrt{h^2+d^2}}=\sqrt{h^2+d^2}=OA,\] so $\rho$ is tangent to $\Omega$; the point of contact is $T=(h,o-d)=(h,-k)$ (it lies on $\rho$ because $-h^2-dk+J=0$), the reflection of $H$ in $BC$.

Conclusion. $T$ lies on $\Omega$ and on the radical axis, so it has power $0$ with respect to $(PQM)$ as well: $T$ is a common point. Every common point of the two circles lies on $\rho$, which meets $\Omega$ only at $T$. So the two (distinct) circles have exactly one common point, and they are tangent at $T$.

Problem 3

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Rearrangement and Ordering

Let $c\ge 2$ be a real number. For each integer $n\ge 4$, let $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ be a permutation of $1,2,\dots,n$, and write $a_{n+1}=a_1$. Define \[F_c(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_i-ca_{i+1}|.\] Determine the largest constant $f(c)$ with the following property: for every integer $n\ge 4$, there exists a permutation $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ of $1,2,\dots,n$ such that $F_c(a_1,\dots,a_n)\ge f(c)n^2$.

Show the answer

$f(c)=\dfrac{c^2+1}{2(c+1)}$

Show the solution

Solution (Splitting the absolute values by sign)

Fix $c\ge2$ and $n\ge4$ and let $M(n)$ be the maximum of $F_c$ over all permutations. Write $t_i=a_i-ca_{i+1}$; then $\sum_it_i=(1-c)\frac{n(n+1)}2$ and \[F_c=\sum_i|t_i|=\sum_i(-t_i)+2\!\!\sum_{i:\,t_i>0}\!\!t_i=(c-1)\frac{n(n+1)}2+2\!\!\sum_{i:\,t_i>0}\!\!t_i. \tag{1}\] More generally, $|t|\ge t$ and $|t|\ge-t$ give $F_c\ge(c-1)\frac{n(n+1)}2+2\sum_{i\in D}t_i$ for every set $D$ of indices.

For integers $0\le k\le n$ let \[G(k)=\underbrace{\bigl(n+(n-1)+\cdots+(n-k+1)\bigr)}_{k\text{ largest}}-c\underbrace{(1+2+\cdots+k)}_{k\text{ smallest}}=kn-\frac{k(k-1)}2-\frac{ck(k+1)}2.\]

Upper bound. In (1) let $D=\{i:t_i>0\}$ and $k=|D|$. The $a_i$ with $i\in D$ are $k$ distinct numbers, as are the $a_{i+1}$, so $\sum_{D}t_i\le G(k)$ (the $k$ largest numbers minus $c$ times the $k$ smallest ones; the two groups may overlap when $k>n/2$, which does not affect the bound). Hence $M(n)\le(c-1)\frac{n(n+1)}2+2\max_{k\ge0}G(k)$. As a function of a real $k$, $G$ is a concave quadratic with maximum at $k^*=\frac{2n+1-c}{2(c+1)}$, where \[G(k^*)=\frac{(2n+1-c)^2}{8(c+1)},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{M(n)}{n^2}\le\frac{c-1}2\cdot\frac{n+1}n+\frac{(2n+1-c)^2}{4(c+1)n^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\frac{c-1}2+\frac1{c+1}=\frac{c^2+1}{2(c+1)}.\] So no constant larger than $L=\frac{c^2+1}{2(c+1)}$ has the property.

Construction. Let $k$ be the integer nearest to $k^*$ (note $k^*\ge-\frac12$, so $k\ge0$, and $k\le k^*+\frac12\le\frac{n+1}3\le\frac n2$). Use the cyclic order \[n,\ 1,\ n-1,\ 2,\ \dots,\ n-k+1,\ k,\ \text{then the other numbers in any order}.\] With $D$ the indices of the $k$ steps $n\to1$, $n-1\to2$, …, $n-k+1\to k$, the general bound gives $F_c\ge(c-1)\frac{n(n+1)}2+2G(k)$. Since $G(k)=G(k^*)-\frac{c+1}2(k-k^*)^2\ge G(k^*)-\frac{c+1}8$, \[4(c+1)\bigl(F_c-Ln^2\bigr)\ge2(c^2-1)n(n+1)+(2n+1-c)^2-(c+1)^2-2(c^2+1)n^2=2(c-1)^2n-4c\] (the $n^2$ terms cancel). For $n\ge4$ and $c\ge2$ this is at least $8(c-1)^2-4c=4(2c-1)(c-2)\ge0$. Hence $F_c\ge Ln^2$ for this permutation, for every $n\ge4$.

Therefore the largest constant is $\boxed{f(c)=\dfrac{c^2+1}{2(c+1)}}$.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Modular Equations and Solutions

Let $n>2027$ be a positive integer such that $4n+1$ is a prime number. Mika writes the numbers \[2n+1,\quad 2n+3,\quad 2n+5,\quad\dots,\quad 4n+1\] on a blackboard. In each move, she picks from them two relatively prime numbers $a$ and $b$ in order. She then writes on the blackboard the smallest positive integer $c$ such that $b$ divides $ac-1$. Then she makes another move by repeating the process and proceeds indefinitely.

Find the largest number of distinct numbers that could ever appear on the board after finitely many moves by Mika.

Show the answer

$4n$

Show the solution

Solution (Halving with $2$, then modulus $x+\delta(x)$)

Write $p=4n+1$ (prime) and call a move with chosen $a,b$ the move $(a,b)$; it writes $c\in[1,b-1]$ with $ac\equiv1\pmod b$ (for $b\ge2$).

Upper bound. Every number written is smaller than the $b$ used, so all numbers stay in $[1,4n+1]$. The number $4n$ is never written: $c=4n$ needs $b=4n+1$ and $4na\equiv1$, i.e. $a\equiv-1\pmod{4n+1}$, so $a=4n$ must already be on the board; initially it is not (it is even). Hence at most $4n$ distinct numbers appear.

Construction: all numbers except $4n$. Initially the board has all odd numbers in $[2n+1,4n+1]$.

Step 1: everything up to $2n+1$. The move $(2n+1,p)$ writes $2$, since $2(2n+1)=p+1$. For odd $b\ge3$ the move $(2,b)$ writes $\frac{b+1}2$. Applied to the odd $b\in[2n+1,4n+1]$ this gives all integers in $[n+1,2n+1]$. If all integers in $[m,2n+1]$ are on the board ($m\ge3$), the odd ones among them give, by halving, all integers in $[\lceil\frac{m+1}2\rceil,m]$; repeating, we get every integer in $[2,2n+1]$, and the move $(3,2)$ writes $1$.

Step 2: the even numbers $x\in[2n+2,4n-2]$. For such $x$ let $\delta(x)$ be the smallest odd prime not dividing $x$. We produce these $x$ in order of increasing $\delta(x)$. Fix $x$ and put $d=\delta(x)$.

(i) $b:=x+d\le4n+1$. Otherwise $e=4n+1-x$ is odd with $3\le e<d$; a prime factor $q$ of $e$ is an odd prime $<d$, so $q\mid x$ by the choice of $d$, and then $q\mid x+e=p$, impossible since $p>x\ge q$. So $b$ is an odd number in $[2n+3,4n+1]$, on the board from the start, and $\gcd(x,b)=\gcd(x,d)=1$.

(ii) Let $r\in[1,b-1]$ with $rx\equiv1\pmod b$. If $r$ is on the board, the move $(r,b)$ writes $x$ (as $x<b$). From $x\equiv-d$ we get $dr\equiv-1\pmod b$, so $dr=kb-1$ with $1\le k\le d-1$; and $r$ is even iff $k$ is odd. Now $r$ is on the board unless $r$ is even and $r\ge2n+2$. Also $r\ne4n$, since $kb\le(d-1)(4n+1)<4nd+1$. So the only problem is $r\in[2n+2,4n-2]$ even. If $\delta(r)<d$, then $r$ was produced earlier. Suppose $\delta(r)\ge d$: every odd prime $q<d$ divides both $x$ and $r$, and as $b\equiv d\pmod q$, $q\mid dr=kb-1$ gives $kd\equiv1\pmod q$. Hence $P_d\mid kd-1$, where $P_d$ is the product of the odd primes below $d$, while $1\le kd-1<d^2$. This is impossible:

  • $d=3$: $k\in\{1,2\}$ and $k$ odd give $k=1$, $r=\frac{b-1}3\le\frac{4n}3<2n+2$.

  • $d=5$: $3\mid5k-1$ forces $k\equiv2\pmod3$, so $k=2$, which is even.

  • $d=7$: $15\mid7k-1$ has no solution with $1\le k\le6$.

  • $d=11$: $105\mid11k-1$ has no solution with $1\le k\le10$.

  • $d\ge13$: $P_d>d^2$. For $d=13$, $P_{13}=1155>169$; if $P_d>d^2$ and $d'$ is the next prime, then $d'<2d$ (Bertrand) and $P_{d'}=dP_d>d^3\ge d'^2\cdot\frac d4\ge d'^2$.

So $r$ is on the board, and $x$ can be written.

After Steps 1 and 2 every number of $[1,4n+1]$ except $4n$ is on the board (the odd ones from the start). So the largest possible number of distinct numbers is $\boxed{4n}$. (The hypothesis $n>2027$ was not used.)

Problem 5

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

Let $K$ be a positive integer, and let $L\in[0,2]$ be a real number. Three sequences of numbers $\{x_n\}$, $\{y_n\}$, and $\{z_n\}$ are defined recursively for $n\ge 0$ by the cyclic relations \[Kx_{n+1}=x_n^2+Ly_nz_n,\qquad Ky_{n+1}=y_n^2+Lz_nx_n,\qquad Kz_{n+1}=z_n^2+Lx_ny_n.\] Show that $K=4052$ is the smallest integer for which there exists an initial triple of integers $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ with $|x_0|\ge 2026$, $|y_0|\ge 2026$, and $|z_0|\ge 2026$ such that for all $L\in[0,2]$, the sequences $\{x_n\}$, $\{y_n\}$, and $\{z_n\}$ are all bounded.

Show the solution

Solution (The case $L=2$ decides the bound; an invariant region for the triple $(2026,2026,-2026)$)

The case $L=2$. Let $s=x+y+z$ and $D=(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2$, and let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, $u=x+\omega y+\omega^2z$, $v=x+\omega^2y+\omega z=\bar u$. For $L=2$ a direct expansion gives \[Ks_{n+1}=s_n^2,\qquad Ku_{n+1}=v_n^2,\qquad Kv_{n+1}=u_n^2.\] (For instance $K(x'+\omega y'+\omega^2z')=x^2+\omega y^2+\omega^2z^2+2yz+2\omega zx+2\omega^2xy=(x+\omega^2y+\omega z)^2$.) Since $|u|^2=x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx=\frac D2$, we get $\frac{D_{n+1}}{2K^2}=\bigl(\frac{D_n}{2K^2}\bigr)^2$ and $\frac{s_{n+1}}K=\bigl(\frac{s_n}K\bigr)^2$. If the sequences are bounded, $D_n$ and $s_n$ are bounded, which forces \[|s_0|\le K\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_0\le2K^2. \tag{1}\]

$K\ge4052$ is necessary. Let $|x_0|,|y_0|,|z_0|\ge2026$ give bounded sequences for $L=2$. If $x_0,y_0,z_0$ have the same sign, then $|s_0|\ge6078$, so $K\ge6078$ by (1). Otherwise two of them, say $x_0,y_0$, have the same sign and $z_0$ the other one, so $|x_0-z_0|,|y_0-z_0|\ge4052$ and $2K^2\ge D_0\ge2\cdot4052^2$. In both cases $K\ge4052$.

$K=4052$ works with $(x_0,y_0,z_0)=(2026,2026,-2026)$. The formulas for $x_{n+1}$ and $y_{n+1}$ are symmetric, so $x_n=y_n$ for all $n$, and with $L\in[0,2]$ fixed, \[Kx_{n+1}=x_n^2+Lx_nz_n,\qquad Kz_{n+1}=z_n^2+Lx_n^2.\] Put $p_n=2x_n+z_n$ and $q_n=x_n-z_n$; then $x_n=\frac{p_n+q_n}3$ and $z_n=\frac{p_n-2q_n}3$. Initially $p_0=2026$ and $q_0=4052=K$. We show by induction that $|p_n|\le K$ and $|q_n|\le K$. Drop the index $n$. Both $Kp'$ and $Kq'$ are linear in $L$, so it suffices to check the endpoints $L=0$ and $L=2$: \[Kp'=(2x^2+z^2)+L(x^2+2xz),\qquad Kq'=(x^2-z^2)+L(xz-x^2).\] At $L=0$: $Kp'=2x^2+z^2=\tfrac{p^2+2q^2}3\in[0,K^2]$ and $Kq'=(x-z)(x+z)=\tfrac{q(2p-q)}3$, whose absolute value is at most $\tfrac{K\cdot3K}3=K^2$. At $L=2$: $Kp'=(2x+z)^2=p^2\in[0,K^2]$ and $Kq'=-(x-z)^2=-q^2\in[-K^2,0]$. Hence $0\le p'\le K$ and $|q'|\le K$. Consequently $|x_n|=\frac{|p_n+q_n|}3\le\frac{2K}3$ and $|z_n|\le K$ for all $n$: the sequences are bounded for every $L\in[0,2]$.

So $K=4052$ is the smallest such integer.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Recursive Game Structure

Let $k$ and $n\ge 2$ be positive integers. At the start, a rabbit is placed on one of the squares of an $n\times n$ board. A hunter then pursues the rabbit over various rounds. In each round, the hunter identifies $k$ distinct squares. The rabbit, who knows the squares identified by the hunter, either moves one square to the right or one square up as long as this next square has not been chosen by the hunter in that round. If no move is possible for the rabbit, it is captured and the hunt ends. The hunter never sees the rabbit.

The board wraps around. This means that if the rabbit is on the rightmost column, moving one square to the right brings it to the square in the same row on the leftmost column. Similarly, if the rabbit is on the topmost row, moving one square up brings it to the square in the same column on the bottom row.

  • (a) Determine the least value of $k$ for which the hunter has a strategy guaranteeing capture after finitely many rounds.

  • (b) For this value of $k$, determine the least integer $M$ such that the hunter can guarantee capture within at most $M$ rounds.

Show the answer

(a) $k=2$; (b) $M=n(n-1)$

Show the solution

Solution (Diagonals evolve independently; counting removals)

The hunter does not see the rabbit (if he did, the two squares to the right of and above the rabbit would capture it in the first round). So the hunter chooses the squares of all rounds in advance, and must capture the rabbit whatever its starting square and its moves.

(a) With $k=1$ the rabbit always has a free move, so it is never captured. We show that $k=2$ suffices; thus the answer is $\boxed{k=2}$.

The set of possible positions. Use coordinates $(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_n^2$. Let $S_t$ be the set of squares where the rabbit can be after $t$ rounds (starting from $S_0=$ the whole board) and $B_t$ the squares chosen in round $t+1$. Then \[S_{t+1}=\bigl(S_t+(1,0)\bigr)\cup\bigl(S_t+(0,1)\bigr)\setminus B_t,\] and capture is guaranteed after $T$ rounds exactly when $S_T=\emptyset$.

Threads. The diagonal $\Delta_j=\{x+y\equiv j\}$ moves to $\Delta_{j+1}$. Describe a subset of a diagonal by the set $A\subseteq\mathbb Z_n$ of $x$-coordinates of its squares; the step $+(1,0)$ adds $1$ to $x$ and $+(0,1)$ keeps $x$, so a set $A$ on one diagonal becomes $A\cup(A+1)$ on the next. Thus each diagonal evolves independently (a “thread”), and $|A\cup(A+1)|=|A|+\rho(A)$, where $\rho(A)$ is the number of maximal cyclic runs of $A$ if $\emptyset\ne A\ne\mathbb Z_n$, and $\rho=0$ if $A$ is empty or full.

(b) Lower bound: at least $n(n-1)$ rounds. Fix a thread and let $A_t$ be its set after $t$ rounds; $A_0$ is full and $A_T=\emptyset$ for the round $T$ at which it becomes empty ($A_{T-1}\ne\emptyset$). Let $t_1<T$ be the last time with $A_{t_1}$ full, and let $b_t$ be the number of chosen squares of round $t+1$ that lie in $A_t\cup(A_t+1)$. Then \[0=|A_T|=n+\sum_{t_1<t<T}\rho(A_t)-\sum_{t_1\le t<T}b_t .\] Here $b_{t_1}\ge1$ (otherwise $A_{t_1+1}$ would be full), and $\rho(A_t)\ge1$ for $t_1<t<T$. If $h$ is the number of $t\in[t_1,T)$ with $b_t\ge1$, then all but one of them are in $(t_1,T)$, so $\sum b_t\ge n+(h-1)$; and $\sum b_t\le2h$. Hence $h\ge n-1$ and the thread absorbs at least $2n-2$ chosen squares. Each chosen square lies in one thread, so over the $n$ threads at least $2n(n-1)$ squares, i.e. at least $n(n-1)$ rounds, are needed.

(b) Upper bound: $n(n-1)$ rounds suffice. Clear the threads one after another; the others stay full meanwhile. To clear a full thread: in the first round choose two squares that are consecutive on its diagonal (after the move); the thread becomes one cyclic run of $n-2$ squares (for $n=2$ it is already empty). As long as the thread is a single run $A$ of length $\ell\ge1$, the set $A\cup(A+1)$ is a run of length $\ell+1$; choosing its two end squares leaves a run of length $\ell-1$. So the thread is empty after $1+(n-2)=n-1$ rounds, and all $n$ threads after $n(n-1)$ rounds.

Hence the least $M$ is $\boxed{n(n-1)}$.
