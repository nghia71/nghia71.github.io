Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

In triangle $ABC$, $\angle BAC=20^\circ$ and $\angle ACB=30^\circ$. The point $M$ is located in the interior of triangle $ABC$ such that $\angle MAC=\angle MCA=10^\circ$. Determine $\angle BMC$.

Show the answer

$\angle BMC=60^\circ$

Show solution 1 – Trigonometric Ceva

The angles of the triangle are $\angle A=20^\circ$, $\angle C=30^\circ$, $\angle B=130^\circ$, and $\angle BAM=10^\circ$, $\angle BCM=20^\circ$. Let $x=\angle CBM$, so $\angle ABM=130^\circ-x$. Trigonometric Ceva for the point $M$ gives \[\frac{\sin\angle BAM}{\sin\angle MAC}\cdot\frac{\sin\angle ACM}{\sin\angle MCB}\cdot\frac{\sin\angle CBM}{\sin\angle MBA}=1,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad \frac{\sin x}{\sin(130^\circ-x)}=\frac{\sin20^\circ}{\sin10^\circ}=2\cos10^\circ .\] The left side increases with $x$ on $(0^\circ,130^\circ)$ (indeed $\frac{\sin x}{\sin(130^\circ-x)}=\frac{\sin x}{\sin130^\circ\cos x-\cos130^\circ\sin x}=\frac{1}{\sin130^\circ\cot x-\cos130^\circ}$, and the positive denominator decreases because $\cot x$ does), so there is at most one solution, and $x=100^\circ$ is one: $\frac{\sin100^\circ}{\sin30^\circ}=2\cos10^\circ$. Hence $\angle CBM=100^\circ$ and \[\angle BMC=180^\circ-100^\circ-20^\circ=\boxed{60^\circ}.\]

Show solution 2 – The circumcentre and an equilateral triangle

Let $O$ be the circumcentre of $ABC$. Since $\angle ACB=30^\circ$, the central angle $AOB$ is $60^\circ$, so $ABO$ is equilateral. As $\angle B=130^\circ$ is obtuse, $O$ and $B$ lie on opposite sides of $AC$; the inscribed angle $\angle ABC=130^\circ$ subtends the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, which is therefore $260^\circ$, so the arc $AC$ containing $B$ is $100^\circ$ and $\angle AOC=100^\circ$ and $\angle OAC=\angle OCA=40^\circ$.

Both $M$ ($MA=MC$) and $O$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, which bisects $\angle AOC$. In triangle $AOM$: $\angle MAO=\angle MAC+\angle CAO=10^\circ+40^\circ=50^\circ$ and $\angle AOM=\frac12\angle AOC=50^\circ$, so $MA=MO$.

Thus $M$ and $B$ (with $BA=BO$) both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AO$, i.e. line $BM$ is the axis of symmetry of the equilateral triangle $ABO$ through $B$. This line makes the angle $30^\circ$ with line $BA$, so $\angle ABM$ is $30^\circ$ or $150^\circ$. Since $M$ is inside the triangle, the ray $BM$ lies inside $\angle ABC$, so $\angle ABM<130^\circ$ and hence $\angle ABM=30^\circ$. Hence $\angle MBC=130^\circ-30^\circ=100^\circ$ and $\angle BMC=180^\circ-100^\circ-20^\circ=\boxed{60^\circ}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

Suppose that the nonzero real numbers $x,y,z$ satisfy \[\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z=\frac1{xyz}.\] Determine the minimum value of \[\frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2}+\frac{y^4}{y^2+z^2}+\frac{z^4}{z^2+x^2}.\]

Show the answer

$\frac12$

Show solution 1 – Titu's lemma

Multiplying the condition by $xyz$ gives $xy+yz+zx=1$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in Engel form, \[\frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2}+\frac{y^4}{y^2+z^2}+\frac{z^4}{z^2+x^2}\ge\frac{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^2}{2(x^2+y^2+z^2)}=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}2\ge\frac{xy+yz+zx}2=\frac12 .\] For $x=y=z=\frac1{\sqrt3}$ the condition holds and each term equals $\frac{1}{6}$, so the minimum is $\boxed{\frac12}$.

Show solution 2 – Subtract a small part from each term

The condition is $xy+yz+zx=1$. Since $\frac{x^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}\le\frac{x^2+y^2}4$ (equivalent to $(x^2-y^2)^2\ge0$), \[\frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2}=x^2-\frac{x^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}\ge x^2-\frac{x^2+y^2}4 .\] Adding the three inequalities, \[\sum\frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2}\ge(x^2+y^2+z^2)-\frac{2(x^2+y^2+z^2)}4=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}2\ge\frac{xy+yz+zx}2=\frac12,\] with equality for $x=y=z=\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}$. The minimum is $\boxed{\frac12}$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Change of Variables and Strategic Reparameterization

Determine how many real numbers $x$ with $1\le x<3$ satisfy \[x^3-\lfloor x^3\rfloor=(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)^3.\]

Show the answer

$24$

Show the solution – Integer and fractional parts

Write $x=n+f$ with $n=\lfloor x\rfloor\in\{1,2\}$ and $f\in[0,1)$. The equation says $\{x^3\}=f^3$. Since $0\le f^3<1$, this holds exactly when $x^3-f^3$ is an integer (then $\lfloor x^3\rfloor=x^3-f^3$). Now \[x^3-f^3=n^3+3n^2f+3nf^2,\] so we need $g_n(f)=3nf(n+f)\in\mathbb Z$. The function $g_n$ is continuous and strictly increasing on $[0,1)$, with $g_n(0)=0$ and $g_n(f)\to3n(n+1)$ as $f\to1$. Hence it takes each of the integer values $0,1,\dots,3n(n+1)-1$ exactly once, giving $3n(n+1)$ solutions in $[n,n+1)$: $6$ for $n=1$ and $18$ for $n=2$.

In total there are $\boxed{24}$ such numbers.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

A $12\times 12$ square array is composed of unit squares. Three squares are removed from one of its major diagonals. Is it possible to cover completely the remaining part of the array by $47$ rectangular tiles of size $1\times 3$ without overlapping any of them?

Show the answer

No

Show solution 1 – Two colourings

Number rows and columns $0,\dots,11$. Every $1\times3$ tile covers three consecutive cells of a row or a column.

Colouring $c_1(i,j)=(i+j)\bmod3$ and colouring $c_2(i,j)=(i-j)\bmod3$: in both, any three consecutive cells in a row or column get the three different colours, so every tile covers one cell of each colour. The full board has $48$ cells of each colour in both colourings.

If the remaining $141$ cells were covered by $47$ tiles, each colour would occur exactly $47$ times among them, so the three removed cells would have three different colours in both colourings. But on the main diagonal $c_2(i,i)=0$ for all cells, and on the other diagonal $c_1(i,11-i)=11\bmod 3=2$ for all cells. Whichever diagonal the three cells come from, they have equal colours in one of the colourings — a contradiction. So the covering is impossible.

Show solution 2 – Roots of unity

Let $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and give the cell $(i,j)$ ($0\le i,j\le11$) the weights $u(i,j)=\varepsilon^{i+j}$ and $w(i,j)=\varepsilon^{i-j}$. A $1\times3$ tile has total weight $\varepsilon^{t}(1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)=0$ for both weights, and so does the whole board ($12$ is a multiple of $3$ in each row). Hence, if a tiling of the board minus three cells existed, the three removed cells would have total weight $0$ for $u$ and for $w$.

For cells $(i,i)$ of the main diagonal $w(i,i)=1$, so three of them have $w$-weight $3\ne0$. For cells $(i,11-i)$ of the other diagonal $u=\varepsilon^{11}$, so three of them have $u$-weight $3\varepsilon^{11}\ne0$. So no such covering exists.
