Preliminary Round

Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Prove that for all integers $n\geqslant 2$ the inequality \[\sqrt[2]{2+\sqrt[3]{3+\sqrt[4]{\cdots+\sqrt[n]{n}}}}<2\] holds.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Bounding from the inside out)

Fix $n\ge2$ and define numbers $x_n,x_{n-1},\dots,x_2$ from the innermost radical outwards: \[ x_n=\sqrt[n]{n},\qquad x_k=\sqrt[k]{k+x_{k+1}}\quad(k=n-1,n-2,\dots,2). \] The left-hand side of the inequality is $x_2$. We show $x_k<2$ for $k=n,n-1,\dots,2$ by downward induction.

First, $k+2\le 2^k$ for every integer $k\ge2$: there is equality for $k=2$, and if $k+2\le 2^k$ then $2^{k+1}\ge 2k+4>k+3$. In particular $n<n+2\le 2^n$, so $x_n=\sqrt[n]{n}<2$.

Now let $2\le k\le n-1$ and assume $x_{k+1}<2$. Then \[ k+x_{k+1}<k+2\le 2^k ,\qquad\text{so}\qquad x_k=\sqrt[k]{k+x_{k+1}}<2 . \] By induction $x_2<2$, which is the claim. (For $n=2$ the expression is just $\sqrt2<2$.)

Remark. The values increase with $n$ and approach about $1.9116$; the bound $2$ is chosen because $\sqrt[k]{k+2}\le 2$, with equality only at $k=2$, so the strict inequality comes from the innermost radical.

Solution 2 (Comparing with a tower of square roots)

Define $x_n,\dots,x_2$ as in Solution 1: $x_n=\sqrt[n]{n}$ and $x_k=\sqrt[k]{k+x_{k+1}}$ for $k=n-1,\dots,2$; the left-hand side is $x_2$. The idea is to replace every radical of index $k\ge4$ by a square root and to compare with the classical tower $\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\cdots}}$, which stays below $2$.

Lemma. For every integer $k\ge4$ and every real $x>0$, $(k+x)^2<(2+x)^k$, i.e. $\sqrt[k]{k+x}<\sqrt{2+x}$.

Proof. For $k=4$, \[ (2+x)^4-(4+x)^2=\bigl((2+x)^2-(4+x)\bigr)\bigl((2+x)^2+(4+x)\bigr)=(x^2+3x)\bigl((2+x)^2+4+x\bigr)>0 . \] If the claim holds for some $k\ge4$, then \[ (2+x)^{k+1}>(2+x)(k+x)^2>2(k+x)^2>(k+1+x)^2 , \] where the last step means $\sqrt2\,(k+x)>k+1+x$, i.e. $(\sqrt2-1)(k+x)>1$; this is true because $(\sqrt2-1)(k+x)>(\sqrt2-1)\cdot4>1.6$. This proves the Lemma.

Small $n$. For $n=2$ the expression is $\sqrt2<2$; for $n=3$ it is $\sqrt{2+\sqrt[3]3}<\sqrt{2+2}=2$, since $3<8$.

The tower. Let $n\ge4$. Define $y_n=x_n$ and $y_k=\sqrt{2+y_{k+1}}$ for $k=n-1,\dots,4$. We first show $x_k\le y_k$ for $4\le k\le n$, going down from $k=n$, where there is equality. If $x_{k+1}\le y_{k+1}$ for some $4\le k<n$, then by the Lemma (with $x=x_{k+1}>0$) \[ x_k=\sqrt[k]{k+x_{k+1}}<\sqrt{2+x_{k+1}}\le\sqrt{2+y_{k+1}}=y_k . \] Next, $y_k<2$ for $4\le k\le n$: indeed $y_n=\sqrt[n]{n}<2$ because $n<2^n$, and if $y_{k+1}<2$ then $y_k=\sqrt{2+y_{k+1}}<\sqrt4=2$.

The last two layers. So $x_4\le y_4<2$. Then $x_3=\sqrt[3]{3+x_4}<\sqrt[3]{5}<2$ (as $5<8$), and $x_2=\sqrt{2+x_3}<\sqrt{4}=2$. This proves the inequality for every $n\ge2$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle. Let $D$ be a point in its interior such that \[\angle DBA+2\cdot\angle BAC+\angle ACD=180^\circ.\] Let $I,J,K$ denote the midpoints of segments $DA$, $DB$ and $DC$ respectively. Show that there exists a point $X$, independent of the choice of $D$, which lies on the circumcircle of triangle $IJK$.

Show the answer

$X$ is the midpoint of $BC$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reflect $D$ in the midpoint of $BC$)

Write $\alpha=\angle BAC$ and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. We show that $X=M$ works.

Step 1: $\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha$. Since $D$ is inside the triangle, $\angle DBC=\angle ABC-\angle DBA$ and $\angle DCB=\angle ACB-\angle ACD$. Adding and using the hypothesis $\angle DBA+\angle ACD=180^\circ-2\alpha$, \[ \angle DBC+\angle DCB=(\angle ABC+\angle ACB)-(180^\circ-2\alpha)=(180^\circ-\alpha)-(180^\circ-2\alpha)=\alpha , \] so $\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha$.

Step 2: the reflection of $D$ lies on the circumcircle. Let $D'$ be the reflection of $D$ in $M$. The diagonals of $BDCD'$ bisect each other, so it is a parallelogram and $\angle BD'C=\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha$. The points $D$ and $A$ lie on the same side of $BC$, so $D'$ lies on the other side. Thus $A$ and $D'$ are on opposite sides of $BC$ and $\angle BAC+\angle BD'C=180^\circ$; hence $D'$ lies on the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $ABC$.

Step 3: a homothety. The homothety with centre $D$ and ratio $\tfrac12$ maps $A,B,C$ to the midpoints $I,J,K$, so it maps $\Gamma$ onto the circumcircle of $IJK$. It maps $D'\in\Gamma$ to the midpoint of $DD'$, which is $M$. Therefore $M$ lies on the circumcircle of $IJK$.

The point $X=M$, the midpoint of $BC$, does not depend on $D$.

Solution 2 (A midpoint parallelogram and supplementary angles)

Write $\alpha=\angle BAC$ and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. We show that $M$ lies on the circle $IJK$.

The angle at $D$. As $D$ is inside the triangle, $\angle DBC=\angle ABC-\angle DBA$ and $\angle DCB=\angle ACB-\angle ACD$, so $\angle DBC+\angle DCB=(180^\circ-\alpha)-(\angle DBA+\angle ACD)=(180^\circ-\alpha)-(180^\circ-2\alpha)=\alpha$ and $\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha$.

The angle at $M$. In triangle $DBC$ the points $J,K,M$ are the midpoints of $DB,DC,BC$. By the midline theorem $JM\parallel DC$ and $KM\parallel DB$, so $DJMK$ is a parallelogram and \[ \angle JMK=\angle JDK=\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha . \]

The angle at $I$. By the midline theorem in triangles $DAB$ and $DAC$, $IJ\parallel AB$ and $IK\parallel AC$, with the same directions from $I$ as from $A$; so $\angle JIK=\angle BAC=\alpha$.

Opposite sides. The line $JK$ is the image of the line $BC$ under the homothety with centre $D$ and ratio $\frac12$; it lies strictly between $D$ and $BC$. Hence $M\in BC$ lies on the side of $JK$ away from $D$. On the other hand $A$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $D$, so its image $I$ lies on the same side of $JK$ as $D$. Thus $I$ and $M$ are on opposite sides of $JK$.

Since $\angle JIK+\angle JMK=180^\circ$ and $I,M$ lie on opposite sides of $JK$, the quadrilateral $IJMK$ is cyclic. So the circle $IJK$ passes through the fixed point $X=M$, the midpoint of $BC$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A list of numbers is called productive if it includes two distinct numbers whose product is included in the list. What is the smallest number $k$ such that we can obtain a list that is not productive by removing $k$ numbers from the list \[1,2,\ldots,2025,2026\,?\]

Show the answer

$44$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Disjoint triples)

44 removals suffice. Remove $1,2,\dots,44$. The product of two distinct remaining numbers is at least $45\cdot46=2070>2026$, so it is not in the list. The list $45,\dots,2026$ is not productive.

At least 44 removals are needed. Suppose that after removing $k$ numbers the list $L$ is not productive.

If $1\in L$, then for every other number $a\in L$ the distinct numbers $1$ and $a$ have product $a\in L$; so $L=\{1\}$ and $k=2025$.

So assume $1$ was removed. For $i=2,3,\dots,44$ consider the triple \[ T_i=\{\,i,\;89-i,\;i(89-i)\,\}. \] Its numbers are distinct ($i<45\le 89-i<i(89-i)$) and lie in the list ($i(89-i)\le 44\cdot45=1980$). The triples are pairwise disjoint: the first entries are $2,\dots,44$, the second entries $87,\dots,45$, and the products $x(89-x)$ increase for $x<44.5$, running from $2\cdot87=174$ to $1980$, all larger than $87$. If all three numbers of some $T_i$ remained, the list would be productive. So at least one number is removed from each of the $43$ disjoint triples, and $1$ is removed as well: $k\ge 44$.

Hence the smallest $k$ is $\boxed{44}$.

Solution 2 (Look at the smallest number that stays)

Removing $1,\dots,44$ works, since $45\cdot 46=2070>2026$. Now let $L$ be a non-productive list obtained by removing $k$ numbers; we show $k\ge44$. Write $N=2026$.

If $1\in L$, every other $a\in L$ gives the product $1\cdot a=a\in L$, so $L=\{1\}$ and $k=2025$. Otherwise let $t\ge 2$ be the smallest number in $L$. The numbers $1,\dots,t-1$ are all removed, which is already $t-1$ removals; if $t\ge 45$ we are done. Let $2\le t\le 44$.

For every integer $x$ with $x>t$ and $N/t^2<x\le N/t$, the numbers $t$ and $x$ are distinct and $tx\le N$. As $t\in L$ and $L$ is not productive, $x$ and $tx$ are not both in $L$: at least one of them is removed. These pairs $\{x,tx\}$ are disjoint from each other, because every $tx>N/t\ge$ every $x$; and they avoid $1,\dots,t-1$, because all their numbers exceed $t$. Hence \[ k\ \ge\ (t-1)+\#\{x:\ \max(t,N/t^2)<x\le N/t\}. \]

  • If $13\le t\le 44$: $N/t^2\le 2026/169<12<t$, so the count is $\lfloor N/t\rfloor-t$ and $k\ge\lfloor N/t\rfloor-1\ge\lfloor 2026/44\rfloor-1=45$.

  • If $2\le t\le 12$: $N/t^2\ge 2026/144>t$, so the count is $\lfloor N/t\rfloor-\lfloor N/t^2\rfloor>\frac{N}{t}-\frac{N}{t^2}-1 =N\,\frac{t-1}{t^2}-1$. Since $\frac{t-1}{t^2}$ decreases for $t\ge2$, this is at least $2026\cdot\frac{11}{144}-1>150$.

In all cases $k\ge44$, so the answer is $\boxed{44}$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A sequence $(a_n)_{n\geqslant 0}$ of positive integers is called $k$-beautiful if, for all $n\geqslant 0$, the property $a_n=a_{n+k}$ and for all $n\geqslant 1$ the property \[a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}\mid a_n^2+k\] is satisfied. For which positive integers $k$ is there a $k$-beautiful sequence?

Show the answer

all positive integers $k$ except $k=2$

Show the solution

Solution (Blocks of ones and $k$'s; a parity obstruction for $k=2$)

A $k$-beautiful sequence exists for every $k\ne2$.

A sequence with $a_n=a_{n+k}$ is determined by one period $a_0,\dots,a_{k-1}$, and since it is periodic the divisibility condition holds for all $n\ge1$ as soon as it holds for every term, where the neighbours of a term are read cyclically in the period.

$k$ odd. Take $a_n=1$ for all $n$. Then $a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}=2$ divides $1+k$, which is even.

$k$ even, $k\ge4$. Write $k=3x+4y$ with integers $x,y\ge0$: for $k\equiv0\pmod 3$ take $y=0$; for $k\equiv1\pmod3$ take $y=1$ (here $k\ge4$); for $k\equiv2\pmod3$ take $y=2$ (here $k\ge8$, because $k$ is even and $k\ne2$). As one period, write $x$ blocks $1,1,k$ and $y$ blocks $1,1,k,k$ one after another; this has length $k$. Every block starts with $1,1$ and ends with $k$, so (cyclically)

  • each term $1$ has one neighbour $1$ and one neighbour $k$: the sum $k+1$ divides $1^2+k$;

  • a term $k$ in a block $1,1,k$ has the neighbours $1$ and $1$ (the next block starts with $1$): $2$ divides $k^2+k=k(k+1)$;

  • a term $k$ in a block $1,1,k,k$ has the neighbours $1,k$ or $k,1$: $k+1$ divides $k^2+k$.

So this sequence is $k$-beautiful. (For example $k=4$: $1,1,4,4$; $k=10$: $1,1,10,1,1,10,1,1,10,10$.)

$k=2$ is impossible. Then $a_0=a_2=a_4=\cdots$ and $a_1=a_3=\cdots$. The conditions for odd $n$ and for even $n\ge2$ read \[ 2a_0\mid a_1^2+2,\qquad 2a_1\mid a_0^2+2 . \] The first shows that $a_1^2$ is even, so $a_1$ is even and $a_1^2+2\equiv 2\pmod 4$. In the same way $a_0$ is even. But then $4\mid 2a_0$, and $2a_0$ cannot divide a number that is $\equiv2\pmod4$. Contradiction.

Hence $k$-beautiful sequences exist exactly for $\boxed{k\ne 2}$.

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with orthocenter $H$ and circumcircle $k$. Let $S$ be the intersection point of the tangents to $k$ at points $A$ and $B$, let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$, and let $H'$ be the reflection of $H$ with respect to $AB$. Let $P$ be the intersection point of the line $MH$ with the circumcircle $k$ such that $H$ lies between $M$ and $P$.

Prove that the points $S$, $H'$ and $P$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Equal ratios $PA:PB=H'A:H'B$)

Let $C'$ be the point of $k$ diametrically opposite $C$. If $CA=CB$, the points $S$, $H$, $M$, $C$ all lie on the symmetry axis of the triangle, $P=C$ and $H'$ is on that axis too, so there is nothing to prove. Assume $CA\ne CB$.

Step 1: $P$ lies on the circle $\omega$ with diameter $CH$. Since $CC'$ is a diameter, $C'B\perp BC$ and $C'A\perp AC$. Also $AH\perp BC$ and $BH\perp AC$. So $AH\parallel C'B$ and $BH\parallel C'A$: $AHBC'$ is a parallelogram and its diagonals bisect each other. Thus $M$ is the midpoint of $HC'$, the line $MH$ passes through $C'$, and $C'$ lies on the other side of $M$ than $H$. Hence $P\ne C'$ is the second intersection of the line $C'H$ with $k$, and $\angle CPC'=90^\circ$ gives $CP\perp PH$. So $P$ lies on $\omega$ (and $P\ne C$ because $CA\neq CB$).

Step 2: $PA:PB=AH:BH$. Let $A_1$ and $B_1$ be the feet of the altitudes from $A$ and $B$; they lie on $\omega$ (right angles over $CH$). With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, using the circle $k$ and then the circle $\omega$, \[ \angle(BP,BA_1)=\angle(BP,BC)=\angle(AP,AC)=\angle(AP,AB_1), \] \[ \angle(A_1P,A_1B)=\angle(A_1P,A_1C)=\angle(B_1P,B_1C)=\angle(B_1P,B_1A). \] So triangles $PBA_1$ and $PAB_1$ are similar, and $PB:PA=BA_1:AB_1$. The right triangles $BA_1H$ and $AB_1H$ have equal vertical angles at $H$, so they are similar and $BA_1:AB_1=BH:AH$. Therefore $PA:PB=AH:BH$.

Step 3: a secant through $S$. Let a line through $S$ meet $k$ at $X$ and $Y$. By the power of the point $S$, $SA^2=SX\cdot SY$, so triangles $SAX$ and $SYA$ (common angle at $S$, $SA:SX=SY:SA$) are similar and $AX:YA=SA:SY$. Likewise $BX:YB=SB:SY$. Since $SA=SB$, we get $XA:XB=YA:YB$.

Step 4: conclusion. It is well known that $H'$ lies on $k$ (indeed $\angle AH'B=\angle AHB=180^\circ-\angle ACB$ and $H'$ is on the other side of $AB$), and $H'A=HA$, $H'B=HB$. The line $SH'$ is not tangent to $k$ (the tangents from $S$ touch at $A$ and $B$), so it meets $k$ again at a point $Q\ne H'$. By Step 3 and Step 2, \[ QA:QB=H'A:H'B=AH:BH=PA:PB=:\lambda . \] The points $Z$ with $ZA:ZB=\lambda$ form a circle (the Apollonius circle; a line if $\lambda=1$). It is not $k$, since $A\in k$ does not satisfy it, so it meets $k$ in at most two points. The points $H'$, $Q$, $P$ lie on both. Here $H'\ne Q$, and $H'\ne P$ because $P$ lies on the same side of $AB$ as $C$ (it is on the ray from $M$ through $H$) while $H'$ lies on the other side. Hence $Q=P$, i.e. $S$, $H'$, $P$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers)

Let $k$ be the unit circle and let $a,b,c$ be the complex coordinates of $A,B,C$, so $\bar a=1/a$, etc. We use the standard facts:

  • the orthocentre is $h=a+b+c$;

  • the tangents at $A$ and $B$ meet at $s=\dfrac{2ab}{a+b}$ ($a+b\ne0$ since the triangle is acute);

  • the reflection of a point $z$ in the line $AB$ is $a+b-ab\bar z$;

  • a point $z$ lies on the line through two points $u,v$ of the unit circle iff $z+uv\bar z=u+v$.

Hence $h'=a+b-ab\left(\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c\right)=-\dfrac{ab}{c}$.

The point $P$. The midpoint of $h$ and $-c$ is $\frac{a+b}{2}$, which is $M$. So the line $MH$ passes through $C'=-c$, and $M$ lies between $H$ and $C'$; therefore $P$ is the second intersection $p\ne -c$ of the line through $-c$ and $h$ with $k$. Applying the chord equation to $u=-c$, $v=p$, $z=h$: \[ h-cp\,\bar h=p-c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\,(1+c\bar h)=h+c . \] Here $1+c\bar h=2+\frac ca+\frac cb=\frac{2ab+bc+ca}{ab}$, which is not $0$ (otherwise $h=-c$ would lie on $k$, impossible since $H$ is inside the acute triangle). So \[ p=\frac{ab\,(a+b+2c)}{2ab+bc+ca}. \]

Collinearity. Compute \[ p-h'=\frac{ab\,[\,c(a+b+2c)+2ab+bc+ca\,]}{c\,(2ab+bc+ca)}=\frac{2ab\,(a+c)(b+c)}{c\,(2ab+bc+ca)},\qquad s-h'=\frac{ab\,(a+b+2c)}{c\,(a+b)} . \] Note $s\ne h'$, as $|a+b+2c|\ge 2-|a+b|>0$. The ratio is \[ \rho=\frac{p-h'}{s-h'}=\frac{2\,(a+b)(a+c)(b+c)}{(a+b+2c)(2ab+bc+ca)} . \] Taking conjugates ($a\mapsto 1/a$ etc.): $(a+b)(a+c)(b+c)\mapsto\frac{(a+b)(a+c)(b+c)}{a^2b^2c^2}$, $\;a+b+2c\mapsto\frac{2ab+bc+ca}{abc}$ and $2ab+bc+ca\mapsto\frac{a+b+2c}{abc}$. Hence $\bar\rho=\rho$, so $\rho$ is real, and $S$, $H'$, $P$ are collinear.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Alice and Bob play a game. In the beginning, there is a pile of $n$ stones on the table, where $n$ is a positive integer. In each move, either one stone is removed from an arbitrary pile, or one pile is split into two non-empty piles. The two players alternate, and Alice begins. When a player has no valid moves, the other player wins.

Determine for each $n$ which of the two players has a winning strategy.

Show the answer

Alice wins for $n=1$ and for all even $n$; Bob wins for all odd $n\ge3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Balanced positions)

Answer. Alice wins for $n=1$ and for every even $n$; Bob wins for every odd $n\ge3$.

The game ends: each move lowers $2\cdot(\text{number of stones})-(\text{number of non-empty piles})$ by at least $1$ (removing a stone lowers it by $2$ or by $1$, a split lowers it by $1$), and this quantity is never negative. A player who cannot move faces a table without stones.

Call a position balanced if the number of piles with exactly one stone is even and the number of piles with an even number of stones is even. (Empty piles are ignored.)

  • 1. The final position (no stones) is balanced.

  • 2. Every move from a balanced position gives an unbalanced one. Removing the stone of a $1$-pile changes the number of $1$-piles by one. Removing a stone from a pile of $2$ turns an even pile into a $1$-pile; from an even pile $\ge4$ it makes the pile odd; from an odd pile $\ge3$ it makes the pile even. Splitting an even pile gives two odd piles or two even piles; splitting an odd pile gives one odd and one even pile. In every case the number of even piles changes by exactly one, except when a $1$-pile is emptied, where the number of $1$-piles changes by one.

  • 3. From an unbalanced position there is a move to a balanced one. If only the number of $1$-piles is odd, empty a $1$-pile. If only the number of even piles is odd, take an even pile: split $2$ into $1+1$, or remove a stone from a pile $\ge4$. If both are odd, take an even pile: remove a stone from a $2$ (it becomes a $1$-pile), or split a pile $e\ge4$ into $1+(e-1)$.

So the player who always moves to a balanced position never faces a balanced position, while the opponent always does; as the game ends, the opponent is the one who eventually cannot move.

The start is one pile of $n$ stones. For $n=1$ there is one $1$-pile and for even $n$ one even pile: unbalanced, so Alice wins. For odd $n\ge3$ the position is balanced, so Bob wins.

Solution 2 (Mirror strategy and Grundy values)

For $n=1$ Alice removes the stone and Bob cannot move. For even $n$ Alice splits the pile into two piles of $n/2$ and then copies every move of Bob on the twin pile; after each of her moves the piles come in equal pairs, so she always has a move, and Bob is the one who gets stuck.

For odd $n\ge3$ we use Sprague-Grundy theory. The game is a sum of independent one-pile games (each move acts on one pile), so a position is lost for the player to move exactly when the XOR of the Grundy values $G(m)$ of its piles is $0$. Here $G(0)=0$ and \[ G(m)=\operatorname{mex}\bigl(\{G(m-1)\}\cup\{G(i)\oplus G(m-i):1\le i\le m-1\}\bigr). \] Claim: $G(1)=1$, $G(m)=2$ for even $m$, and $G(m)=0$ for odd $m\ge3$. Indeed $G(1)=\operatorname{mex}\{0\}=1$ and $G(2)=\operatorname{mex}\{G(1),\,G(1)\oplus G(1)\}=\operatorname{mex}\{1,0\}=2$. Assume the claim below $m\ge3$.

  • $m$ odd: $G(m-1)=2$, and a split gives an odd and an even part, with value $G(\text{odd})\oplus2\in\{0\oplus2,1\oplus2\}=\{2,3\}$. The value $0$ is missing, so $G(m)=0$.

  • $m$ even ($m\ge4$): the options are $G(m-1)=0$, splits into two even parts ($2\oplus2=0$) and into two odd parts (values in $\{0,1\}$, and $1\oplus0=1$ occurs for $1+(m-1)$). Since $2$ is missing and $0,1$ occur, $G(m)=2$.

So for odd $n\ge3$ the starting position has Grundy value $0$: whatever Alice does, Bob can return to a position of value $0$, and Bob wins. For $n=1$ and even $n$ the value is $\ne0$ and Alice wins.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Determine all values $a$ and $b$ such that there exists a sequence $(u_n)_{n\geq 1}$ of positive integers with $u_1=a$, $u_2=b$, and \[u_{2n}=\mathrm{GM}(u_{2n+1},u_{2n-1})\quad\text{and}\quad u_{2n+1}=\mathrm{AM}(u_{2n},u_{2n+2})\quad\text{for } n\geq 1.\] Here, $\mathrm{AM}(x,y)$ denotes the arithmetic and $\mathrm{GM}(x,y)$ the geometric mean of the two numbers $x$ and $y$.

Show the answer

exactly the pairs of positive integers with $a\le b$ and $a\mid b^2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A closed form)

Answer. All pairs of positive integers $a\le b$ with $a\mid b^2$.

The conditions say $u_{2n}^2=u_{2n-1}u_{2n+1}$ and $u_{2n+2}=2u_{2n+1}-u_{2n}$. So from $u_1=a$, $u_2=b$ the sequence is forced: \[ u_{2n+1}=\frac{u_{2n}^2}{u_{2n-1}},\qquad u_{2n+2}=2u_{2n+1}-u_{2n}\qquad(n\ge1), \] as long as the terms stay positive. Put $d=b-a$ and $q_n=a+(n-1)d$ (an arithmetic progression with $q_1=a$, $q_2=b$).

Claim. If $u_1,\dots,u_{2N}$ are positive, then $u_{2n-1}=\dfrac{q_n^2}{a}$ and $u_{2n}=\dfrac{q_nq_{n+1}}{a}$ for $n\le N$. This holds for $n=1$. If it holds for $n<N$, then \[ u_{2n+1}=\frac{q_n^2q_{n+1}^2/a^2}{q_n^2/a}=\frac{q_{n+1}^2}{a},\qquad u_{2n+2}=\frac{2q_{n+1}^2-q_nq_{n+1}}{a}=\frac{q_{n+1}(2q_{n+1}-q_n)}{a}=\frac{q_{n+1}q_{n+2}}{a}. \]

Necessity. $u_3=b^2/a$ must be an integer, so $a\mid b^2$. If $b<a$, then $d<0$ and $q_n\to-\infty$; let $N\ge2$ be the first index with $q_N\le0$. All terms of a valid sequence are positive, so the claim applies to every $n$ and gives $u_{2N-2}=q_{N-1}q_N/a\le0$, a contradiction. So $a\le b$.

Sufficiency. Let $a\le b$ and $a\mid b^2$, and define $u_{2n-1}=q_n^2/a$, $u_{2n}=q_nq_{n+1}/a$. Since $d\ge0$, all $q_n\ge a>0$, so all terms are positive. Modulo $a$ we have $q_n\equiv(n-1)d$ and $d^2=(b-a)^2\equiv b^2\equiv0$, so $q_n^2\equiv(n-1)^2d^2\equiv0$ and $q_nq_{n+1}\equiv(n-1)n\,d^2\equiv0$: all terms are integers. Finally $u_{2n}^2=q_n^2q_{n+1}^2/a^2=u_{2n-1}u_{2n+1}$ with $u_{2n}>0$, so $u_{2n}$ is the geometric mean, and $\frac{u_{2n}+u_{2n+2}}2=\frac{q_{n+1}(q_n+q_{n+2})}{2a}=\frac{q_{n+1}^2}{a}=u_{2n+1}$ because $q_n+q_{n+2}=2q_{n+1}$. So the answer is $\boxed{a\le b \text{ and } a\mid b^2}$.

Solution 2 (Ratios and an inductive invariant)

As in the statement, the sequence is forced: $u_{2n+1}=u_{2n}^2/u_{2n-1}$ and $u_{2n+2}=2u_{2n+1}-u_{2n}$.

Necessity of $a\le b$. Let $\rho_n=u_{2n}/u_{2n-1}>0$. Then $u_{2n+1}=\rho_nu_{2n}$ and $u_{2n+2}=u_{2n}(2\rho_n-1)$, so \[ \rho_{n+1}=\frac{u_{2n+2}}{u_{2n+1}}=\frac{2\rho_n-1}{\rho_n},\qquad 1-\rho_{n+1}=\frac{1-\rho_n}{\rho_n}. \] Suppose $\rho_1=b/a<1$. Then $0<\rho_n<1$ for all $n$: $\rho_n>0$ because all terms are positive, and if $\rho_n<1$ the formula gives $1-\rho_{n+1}=(1-\rho_n)/\rho_n>0$, i.e. $\rho_{n+1}<1$. Consequently $1-\rho_{n+1}>1-\rho_n$, so $\rho_n\le\rho_1$ and $1-\rho_{n+1}\ge(1-\rho_n)/\rho_1$. Hence $1-\rho_n\ge(1-\rho_1)\rho_1^{-(n-1)}$, which exceeds $1$ for large $n$, contradicting $\rho_n>0$. So $b\ge a$. Also $u_3=b^2/a$ is an integer, so $a\mid b^2$.

Sufficiency. Let $a\le b$, $a\mid b^2$. We show by induction: $x=u_{2n-1}$ and $y=u_{2n}$ are positive integers with $x\le y$ and $x\mid y^2$. For $n=1$ this is the hypothesis. Given it for $n$:

  • $u_{2n+1}=y^2/x$ is a positive integer, and $u_{2n+1}\ge y$ because $y\ge x$;

  • $u_{2n+2}=2u_{2n+1}-y\ge u_{2n+1}>0$ is an integer;

  • $\dfrac{u_{2n+2}^2}{u_{2n+1}}=\dfrac{y^2(2y-x)^2/x^2}{y^2/x}=\dfrac{(2y-x)^2}{x}=\dfrac{4y^2}{x}-4y+x$ is an integer, since $x\mid y^2$.

So all terms are positive integers. They satisfy the conditions: $u_{2n}=\sqrt{u_{2n-1}u_{2n+1}}$ because $u_{2n+1}=u_{2n}^2/u_{2n-1}$ and $u_{2n}>0$, and $u_{2n+1}=\frac{u_{2n}+u_{2n+2}}{2}$ by the definition of $u_{2n+2}$.

Hence the admissible pairs are exactly those with $\boxed{a\le b \text{ and } a\mid b^2}$.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Determine all real numbers $\alpha$ for which a function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ exists such that \[f(f(x)+y)=\alpha x+f(x+f(f(y)))\] for all real numbers $x$ and $y$.

Show the answer

$\alpha=0$ and $\alpha=2$

Show the solution

Solution (Surjective, injective, then linear)

Answer: $\alpha\in\{0,2\}$.

Examples. For $\alpha=0$, $f(x)=x$ works: both sides equal $x+y$. For $\alpha=2$, $f(x)=-x$ works: the left side is $-(-x+y)=x-y$ and the right side is $2x-(x+y)=x-y$.

No other $\alpha$. Let $\alpha\ne0$ and let $f$ be a solution; write $Q(x,y)$ for the equation and $c=f(0)$.

  • 1. $Q(0,y)$: $f(c+y)=f\bigl(f(f(y))\bigr)$ for all $y$.

  • 2. $Q(x,-f(x))$: $c=\alpha x+f\bigl(x+f(f(-f(x)))\bigr)$, so $f$ takes the value $c-\alpha x$ for every real $x$. As $\alpha\ne0$, $f$ is surjective.

  • 3. $f$ is injective. Let $f(u)=f(v)$. Comparing $Q(x,u)$ and $Q(x,v)$ (their right-hand sides agree since $f(f(u))=f(f(v))$) gives $f(f(x)+u)=f(f(x)+v)$ for all $x$. As $f(x)$ runs through all reals, $f(t+u)=f(t+v)$ for all $t$, i.e. $f$ has period $p=u-v$. Now compare $Q(x+p,y)$ with $Q(x,y)$: the left sides are equal because $f(x+p)=f(x)$, and the right sides differ by $\alpha p$. So $\alpha p=0$ and $u=v$.

  • 4. From 1 and injectivity: $f(f(y))=y+c$ for all $y$; in particular $f(f(0))=c$.

  • 5. $Q(x,0)$: $f(f(x))=\alpha x+f(x+f(f(0)))=\alpha x+f(x+c)$. With 4 this gives $x+c=\alpha x+f(x+c)$, so $f(t)=(1-\alpha)(t-c)+c$ for all $t$.

  • 6. Then $f(f(y))=(1-\alpha)^2(y-c)+c$. Comparing with $f(f(y))=y+c$ (the coefficients of $y$) gives $(1-\alpha)^2=1$, so $\alpha=2$.

Hence a solution exists exactly for $\boxed{\alpha\in\{0,2\}}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement

Let $ABCDEF$ be a convex hexagon with the property that each of the three segments joining the midpoints of the opposite sides divides the total area of the hexagon in half.

Show that the three segments intersect in a common point.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Twelve small triangles)

Let $M_1,\dots,M_6$ be the midpoints of $AB,BC,CD,DE,EF,FA$, let $[\,\cdot\,]$ denote area and $S=[ABCDEF]$.

The point $O$. Going around the boundary, the points $M_1,M_3,M_4,M_6$ appear in this order, so the chords $M_1M_4$ and $M_3M_6$ of the convex hexagon cross; let $O$ be their intersection, an interior point of the hexagon.

Bookkeeping. Join $O$ to the six vertices and the six midpoints. Since $M_1$ is the midpoint of $AB$, the triangles $OAM_1$ and $OM_1B$ have equal bases and the same height, hence equal areas; call it $p$. Define $q,r,s,t,u$ in the same way for the sides $BC,CD,DE,EF,FA$. Then $S=2(p+q+r+s+t+u)$.

Because $O$ lies on $M_1M_4$, the part of the hexagon cut off by $M_1M_4$ on the side of $B,C,D$ is the union of the triangles $OM_1B,\,OBM_2,\,OM_2C,\,OCM_3,\,OM_3D,\,ODM_4$, with area $p+2q+2r+s$. It equals $\frac S2=p+q+r+s+t+u$, so \[ q+r=t+u. \tag{1} \] In the same way $O\in M_3M_6$ gives $r+2s+2t+u=p+q+r+s+t+u$, i.e. \[ s+t=p+q. \tag{2} \] Adding (1) and (2) and cancelling $q+t$ gives $r+s=u+p$.

The third segment. Consider the polygon $\Pi=M_2\,C\,D\,E\,M_5\,O$. Its area, computed as the sum of the triangles $OM_2C,OCD,ODE,OEM_5$, is \[ [\Pi]=q+2r+2s+t=(q+r+s+t)+(r+s)=(q+r+s+t)+(u+p)=\tfrac S2 . \] The segment $M_2M_5$ also bisects the hexagon, so $[M_2CDEM_5]=\frac S2$. By the shoelace formula (signed areas, vertices counterclockwise), $[\Pi]-[M_2CDEM_5]$ equals the signed area of triangle $M_5OM_2$. Hence this triangle has area $0$, so $O$ lies on the line $M_2M_5$, and since $O$ is inside the convex hexagon, on the segment $M_2M_5$.

Thus the three segments pass through the common point $O$.

Solution 2 (Cross products)

Label the hexagon counterclockwise, let $M_1,\dots,M_6$ be the midpoints of $AB,BC,CD,DE,EF,FA$ and let $S$ be its area. We identify points with vectors from a fixed origin and write $U\times V=u_1v_2-u_2v_1$ for $U=(u_1,u_2)$, $V=(v_1,v_2)$; this is antisymmetric ($V\times U=-U\times V$, $U\times U=0$) and linear in each factor. The shoelace formula says that for a polygon $P_1P_2\dots P_m$ with vertices in counterclockwise order, $2[P_1P_2\dots P_m]=P_1\times P_2+P_2\times P_3+\dots+P_m\times P_1$, whatever the origin. In particular \[ 2S=A\times B+B\times C+C\times D+D\times E+E\times F+F\times A . \]

Each bisection condition as one equation. The segment $M_1M_4$ cuts off the convex polygon $M_1BCDM_4$, whose vertices are in the same cyclic order as those of the hexagon, hence counterclockwise. Since $M_1\times B=\frac12(A+B)\times B=\frac12A\times B$ and $D\times M_4=\frac12D\times(D+E)=\frac12D\times E$, \[ 2[M_1BCDM_4]=\tfrac12A\times B+B\times C+C\times D+\tfrac12D\times E+M_4\times M_1 . \] The condition $2[M_1BCDM_4]=S$ therefore reads, after using $M_4\times M_1=-M_1\times M_4$ and the formula for $2S$, \[ 2\,M_1\times M_4=B\times C+C\times D-E\times F-F\times A. \tag{1} \] Shifting all letters by one and by two steps around the hexagon, the conditions for $M_2M_5$ and $M_3M_6$ read \[ 2\,M_2\times M_5=C\times D+D\times E-F\times A-A\times B,\tag{2} \] \[ 2\,M_3\times M_6=D\times E+E\times F-A\times B-B\times C.\tag{3} \]

One linear relation. Computing $(1)-(2)+(3)$, every product on the right cancels: \[ 2\,\bigl(M_1\times M_4-M_2\times M_5+M_3\times M_6\bigr)=0 . \] This holds for every choice of the origin.

Conclusion. Going around the boundary, the points $M_1,M_3,M_4,M_6$ appear in this order, so the chords $M_1M_4$ and $M_3M_6$ of the convex hexagon cross at a point $O$ inside it. Take $O$ as the origin. The vectors $M_1$ and $M_4$ point in opposite directions from $O$, so $M_1\times M_4=0$; likewise $M_3\times M_6=0$. Hence $M_2\times M_5=0$. The points $M_2$, $M_5$ lie on the boundary and $O$ inside, so these vectors are non-zero, and a vanishing cross product means that they are parallel: $M_2$, $O$, $M_5$ are collinear. Finally, a line through an interior point meets a convex polygon in a segment whose points other than its two endpoints are interior; the boundary points $M_2$, $M_5$ are therefore the endpoints of this segment, so $O$ lies on the segment $M_2M_5$. The three segments are concurrent.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence

On two strips, each consisting of $100$ cells numbered from $1$ to $100$, there are $50$ tokens each.

On the first strip, the tokens are located on cells $1,3,5,\ldots,99$. Each token is then moved one or more cells to the right, i.e., to a cell with a higher number. The tokens may not jump over each other, and no cell may contain two or more tokens. Let $A$ be the number of possible configurations of the tokens that can be reached this way.

On the second strip, the tokens are located on cells $1,2,3,\ldots,50$. Again, each token is moved one or more cells to the right. The tokens may not jump over each other, and no cell may contain two or more tokens. Moreover, a token cannot be moved further than to the position double its starting position. Let $B$ be the number of possible configurations for the tokens that can be reached this way.

Prove that $A=B$ holds.

Show the answer

proof ($A=B=C_{50}=\frac1{51}\binom{100}{50}$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reverse and complement the shifts)

Reachable configurations. Tokens cannot jump, so they keep their order; let token $i$ ($i=1,\dots,50$, from the left) end at cell $y_i$, so $y_1<y_2<\dots<y_{50}$.

On the first strip token $i$ starts at $2i-1$, so a reachable configuration satisfies $y_i\ge 2i$ and $y_{50}\le100$. Conversely, every increasing $y$ with these properties is reachable: move the tokens in the order $50,49,\dots,1$, each straight to its target; when token $i$ moves from $2i-1$ to $y_i$, the cells in between are free (tokens $j>i$ already sit at $y_j>y_i$, tokens $j<i$ are still at $2j-1<2i-1$).

On the second strip token $i$ starts at $i$, and by the same argument the reachable configurations are exactly the increasing $x_1<\dots<x_{50}$ with $i<x_i\le 2i$ (then $x_{50}\le100$ automatically).

Normalising. Put $z_i=y_i-i$. Then $y_{i+1}>y_i$ means $z_{i+1}\ge z_i$, and the conditions become $z_i\ge i$ and $z_{50}\le 50$; so $A$ counts the non-decreasing sequences with \[ i\le z_i\le 50\qquad(1\le i\le 50). \] Similarly $w_i=x_i-i$ turns the second strip into non-decreasing sequences with $1\le w_i\le i$; $B$ counts these.

Bijection. Map $z$ to $w$ with $w_i=51-z_{51-i}$. Reversing the order and changing the sign keeps the sequence non-decreasing, $w_i\le 51-(51-i)=i$, and $w_i\ge 51-50=1$. The same formula maps the $w$'s back ($z_i=51-w_{51-i}$ satisfies $z_i\ge 51-(51-i)=i$ and $z_i\le 50$), and the two maps are inverse to each other. Hence $A=B$.

(Both numbers equal the Catalan number $C_{50}$.)

Solution 2 (Both are Dyck words)

Tokens cannot jump, so they keep their order; number them $1,\dots,50$ from the left. On the first strip token $i$ starts at $2i-1$ and moves right, so it ends at a cell $y_i\ge2i$. On the second strip token $i$ starts at $i$, moves right and may not pass cell $2i$, so it ends at a cell $x_i$ with $i<x_i\le2i$. Conversely, every set of $50$ cells meeting these conditions is reachable: move the tokens one at a time from the rightmost to the leftmost, each straight to its target. This never passes an occupied cell, because the tokens to the right already sit beyond the target and those to the left are still at their starting cells, to the left of the current token. Hence the reachable configurations of the first strip are exactly those with $i$-th token at $y_i\ge2i$, and those of the second strip exactly those with $i<x_i\le2i$. A Dyck word is a word of $50$ letters $U$ and $50$ letters $D$ in which every prefix has at least as many $U$'s as $D$'s. We map both families bijectively to Dyck words of length $100$.

First strip. Read cells $1,\dots,100$ and write $U$ for an empty cell, $D$ for an occupied one. If some $y_i<2i$, the prefix up to cell $y_i$ has $i$ letters $D$ and $y_i-i<i$ letters $U$. Conversely, if all $y_i\ge 2i$, a prefix of length $m$ containing $j$ tokens has $y_j\le m$, so $2j\le m$ and $\#D\le\#U$. Hence the configurations correspond exactly to the Dyck words.

Second strip. Here $x_1\ge2$, so cell $1$ is always empty, and conversely any $50$ tokens in cells $2,\dots,100$ satisfy $x_i\ge i+1$. Read cells $2,\dots,100$, write $U$ for an occupied and $D$ for an empty cell, and append one more $D$: this gives $50$ letters $U$ and $50$ letters $D$. Let $h_m$ be the number of $U$'s minus the number of $D$'s among the first $m$ letters. The condition $x_i\le 2i$ means that cells $2,\dots,2i$ (the first $2i-1$ letters) contain at least $i$ tokens, i.e. $h_{2i-1}\ge1$.

  • If $h_{2i-1}\ge1$ for $i=1,\dots,50$, then also $h_{2i}\ge h_{2i-1}-1\ge0$ and $h_0=0$, so the word is a Dyck word.

  • If the word is a Dyck word, then $h_{2i-1}\ge0$, and $h_{2i-1}$ is odd, so $h_{2i-1}\ge1$.

Every Dyck word ends with $D$, so deleting the last letter inverts the construction. Hence the second-strip configurations also correspond exactly to the Dyck words of length $100$.

Therefore $A=B$ (both are the number of Dyck words of length $100$, the Catalan number $C_{50}$).
