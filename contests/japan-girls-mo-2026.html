Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

A $100\times 100$ grid of unit squares is given. Among them, $2500$ squares are colored black in such a way that no two distinct black squares share a side or a vertex. Determine the minimum possible number of black squares that lie in at least one of the following positions:

  • the first row from the top,

  • the $100$th row from the top,

  • the first column from the left,

  • the $100$th column from the left.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Divide the board into disjoint $2\times2$ blocks. Use the exclusion rule and the total number of black squares to determine the occupancy of each block.
Hint 2: key idea
Record the horizontal and vertical offsets of each black square within its block. Along a row or column of blocks, check which adjacent change of offset would cause contact, then use the endpoint offsets to count contributions to the border. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Count the extra border contributions from block lines with both offsets and account for corners counted twice. Relate black corners on a common side to such lines, and for opposite black corners test where changes between constant offsets would meet. For a construction, choose the offsets so that every estimate in your count is met with equality.
Common trap
For opposite black corners, check diagonal contact between blocks as well as contact across a shared side.
Show the answer

$99$

Show solution 1 – Blocks, monotone offsets and the corners

Cut the board into $2500$ blocks of size $2\times2$, forming $50$ block-rows and $50$ block-columns. Any two cells of one block touch, so each block holds at most one black cell; as there are $2500$ black cells, every block holds exactly one. In block $(i,j)$ (block-row $i$ from the top, block-column $j$ from the left, $1\le i,j\le 50$) describe the black cell by its offsets $x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ (left or right column of the block) and $y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ (top or bottom row of the block).

Two rules. If $x_{ij}=1$ and $x_{i,j+1}=0$, the black cells of these two horizontally adjacent blocks lie in adjacent columns and in rows at most one apart, so they touch. Hence along every block-row the offsets $x$ never go from $1$ to $0$: each block-row reads $0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1$. In the same way, along every block-column the offsets $y$ read $0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1$ from top to bottom.

Counting. Call a block-column mixed if it contains both values of $y$, and a block-row mixed if it contains both values of $x$. The top block of column $j$ has its black cell in the first row of the board iff $y_{1j}=0$, and the bottom block has it in the last row iff $y_{50,j}=1$. By the first rule at least one of these happens, and both happen when the column is mixed. So each block-column yields at least one black cell on the top or bottom edge, and a mixed one yields two; likewise for block-rows and the left/right edges. Adding these $100$ contributions we count at least $100+m$ black edge cells, where $m$ is the number of mixed lines. A black cell is counted twice only if it lies on two edges, i.e. in one of the $4$ corner cells. If $c$ corner cells are black, the number of black edge cells is therefore at least $100+m-c$.

Claim: $c\le m+1$. This is clear for $c\le1$. If two black corners lie on a common side, that side's line is mixed: e.g. black top-left and top-right corners mean $x_{1,1}=0$ and $x_{1,50}=1$, so block-row $1$ is mixed. Any three corners contain two such pairs with different sides, so $c=3$ gives $m\ge2$, and $c=4$ gives $m\ge4$. It remains to treat two opposite black corners with no mixed line. Then every block-row is constant, $x_{ij}=X_i$, and every block-column is constant, $y_{ij}=Y_j$.

  • Top-left and bottom-right black: $X_1=Y_1=0$ and $X_{50}=Y_{50}=1$. Choose $i$ with $X_i=0$, $X_{i+1}=1$ and $j$ with $Y_{j-1}=0$, $Y_j=1$. Block $(i,j)$ has offsets $(0,1)$ and block $(i+1,j-1)$ has offsets $(1,0)$; their black cells are the cells $(2i,2j-1)$ and $(2i+1,2j-2)$ of the board, which share a vertex. Contradiction.

  • Top-right and bottom-left black: $X_1=1$, $Y_{50}=0$, $X_{50}=0$, $Y_1=1$. Choose $i$ with $X_i=1$, $X_{i+1}=0$ and $j$ with $Y_j=1$, $Y_{j+1}=0$. Blocks $(i,j)$ and $(i+1,j+1)$ have offsets $(1,1)$ and $(0,0)$; their black cells $(2i,2j)$ and $(2i+1,2j+1)$ share a vertex. Contradiction.

So $c\le m+1$ always, and the number of black edge cells is at least $100+m-c\ge 99$.

Construction. Put the black cell in the bottom-right cell of every block, i.e. blacken the cells $(2i,2j)$, $1\le i,j\le50$. Two of them are at least two rows or two columns apart, so none touch. The edge cells among them are those in row $100$ or column $100$: $50+50-1=99$.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{99}$.

Show solution 2 – Only the inner $98\times98$ square

Call the four rows and columns listed in the problem the border; the other squares form a $98\times98$ square $Q$ (rows and columns $2$ to $99$).

At least $99$. Cut $Q$ into $49\cdot49=2401$ blocks of size $2\times2$. Any two squares of such a block share a side or a vertex, so each block contains at most one black square. Hence at most $2401$ black squares lie in $Q$, and at least $2500-2401=99$ black squares lie on the border.

Exactly $99$ is possible. Colour black the $50\cdot50=2500$ squares whose row number and column number are both odd. Two such squares differ by at least $2$ in their row or in their column, so they never share a side or a vertex. None of them lies in row $100$ or column $100$; row $1$ contains $50$ of them and column $1$ contains $50$, with the corner square counted twice. So exactly $50+50-1=99$ black squares lie on the border.

The minimum is $\boxed{99}$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Sequences with Arithmetic Constraints

Let $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ be a sequence of positive integers. For each positive integer $n$, define $b_n=\dfrac{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n}{n}$. Suppose that all $b_1,b_2,\ldots$ are positive integers, and that every positive integer appears exactly once among the values $b_1,b_2,\ldots$. Determine all possible sequences $a_n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the sum of the first $n$ terms in terms of $b_n$. Compare it with the sum ending one term earlier to see what positivity of $a_n$ requires.
Hint 2: key idea
Use positivity of $a_n$ to compare $nb_n$ with $(n-1)b_{n-1}$. Then use the permutation condition: every positive integer must appear somewhere, so ask what happens to a small value that has not yet appeared by a given index. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Suppose some value $v$ is missing from the first few averages; at the index where it finally appears, use distinctness to lower-bound the immediately preceding average. Apply the strict inequality from positivity to test whether this later position is possible. Once the averages are pinned down, recover the original terms by taking differences of prefix sums.
Common trap
The averages can be a permutation without being ordered, so any bound on the preceding average needs justification.
Show the answer

$a_n=2n-1$ for all $n$

Show solution 1 – The first misplaced value never appears

Since $a_n=nb_n-(n-1)b_{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$, the condition $a_n\ge1$ says \[nb_n>(n-1)b_{n-1}\qquad(n\ge2).\tag{1}\] We show $b_n=n$ for all $n$. Suppose not, and let $m$ be the least index with $b_m\ne m$. Then $b_i=i$ for $i<m$, so the values $1,\dots,m-1$ are used up and $b_m>m$. The value $m$ must appear somewhere, at an index $t$; as $b_i=i\ne m$ for $i<m$ and $b_m\ne m$, we have $t>m$. The value $b_{t-1}$ is not $m$ (the values are distinct and $b_t=m$), and it is not one of $1,\dots,m-1$, because these values sit at the indices $1,\dots,m-1$ while $t-1\ge m$. So $b_{t-1}\ge m+1$. Now (1) with $n=t$ gives \[tm=tb_t>(t-1)b_{t-1}\ge(t-1)(m+1)=tm+t-m-1,\] so $t<m+1$, i.e. $t\le m$, a contradiction. Hence $b_n=n$ for every $n$, and \[a_1=b_1=1,\qquad a_n=nb_n-(n-1)b_{n-1}=n^2-(n-1)^2=2n-1 .\] Conversely, for $a_n=2n-1$ we get $a_1+\dots+a_n=n^2$, so $b_n=n$: every $b_n$ is a positive integer and every positive integer occurs exactly once. The only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=2n-1}$.

Show solution 2 – Every average is at least its index

Since $a_n=nb_n-(n-1)b_{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$, the condition $a_n\ge1$ says $nb_n>(n-1)b_{n-1}$.

Claim: $b_n\ge n$ for every $n$. Suppose $b_n\le n-1$ for some $n\ge2$. If $b_{n-1}>b_n$, then $b_{n-1}\ge b_n+1$ and \[a_n=nb_n-(n-1)b_{n-1}\le nb_n-(n-1)(b_n+1)=b_n-(n-1)\le0,\] which is impossible. Also $b_{n-1}\ne b_n$, as the values are distinct. Hence $b_{n-1}<b_n\le n-1$, so $b_{n-1}\le n-2$. The same reasoning now applies to $n-1$ (if $n-1\ge2$), and so on down to $b_1\le0$, which contradicts $b_1\ge1$. This proves the claim (for $n=1$ it is just $b_1\ge1$).

All averages are known. The value $1$ occurs as some $b_n$, and $b_n\ge n$ forces $n=1$: so $b_1=1$. Suppose $b_i=i$ for all $i<m$. The value $m$ occurs as some $b_n$ with $n\le m$ by the claim, and $n<m$ is impossible because those indices carry the values $1,\dots,m-1$. So $b_m=m$. By induction $b_n=n$ for every $n$, and \[a_1=1,\qquad a_n=n^2-(n-1)^2=2n-1\quad(n\ge2).\] Conversely, for $a_n=2n-1$ the sum of the first $n$ terms is $n^2$, so $b_n=n$, and every positive integer occurs exactly once. The only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=2n-1}$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

Find all the positive integer pairs $(m,n)$ such that $2^m=8n^6+n^2-1$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try reducing the two sides modulo a small prime. Compare the cases in which that prime divides $n$ or does not divide $n$.
Hint 2: key idea
Work modulo $3$ to restrict the parity of $m$. Use that parity to write the left side as twice a square and group it with the term involving $n^6$. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Factor the grouped terms as twice a difference of squares. Compare the sizes of the integer factors with the remaining term to bound $n$, and check directly whatever values the bound leaves.
Common trap
A size comparison between integer factors applies only when the factors are positive.
Show the answer

$(m,n)=(3,1)$

Show solution 1 – Modulo 3, then a difference of squares

$m$ is odd. If $3\mid n$, then $8n^6+n^2-1\equiv-1\equiv2\pmod 3$. If $3\nmid n$, then $n^2\equiv1$ and $n^6\equiv1$, so $8n^6+n^2-1\equiv8\equiv2\pmod3$. Thus $2^m\equiv2\pmod3$, which forces $m$ to be odd (for even $m$, $2^m=4^{m/2}\equiv1$).

Factorizing. Write $m=2k+1$ and $y=2^k$, so $2^m=2y^2$. The equation becomes \[n^2-1=2y^2-8n^6=2\,(y-2n^3)(y+2n^3).\] If $n=1$, then $2^m=8$ and $m=3$. If $n\ge2$, the left side is positive, so $y>2n^3$, i.e. $y-2n^3\ge1$. Then \[n^2-1=2(y-2n^3)(y+2n^3)\ge2(4n^3+1)>n^2-1,\] a contradiction. So the only solution is $\boxed{(m,n)=(3,1)}$; indeed $8+1-1=8=2^3$.

Show solution 2 – Modulo 3, then squeezing between consecutive squares

As in any solution, reduce modulo $3$: for $3\mid n$ the right side is $\equiv-1\equiv2$, and for $3\nmid n$ it is $\equiv8+1-1\equiv2\pmod 3$, because $n^2\equiv n^6\equiv1$. Since $2^m\equiv(-1)^m\pmod3$, $m$ must be odd.

For odd $m$ the number $2^{m+1}$ is a perfect square, and \[2^{m+1}=16n^6+2n^2-2 .\] For $n\ge2$ we have $0<2n^2-2<8n^3+1$, hence \[(4n^3)^2<16n^6+2n^2-2<(4n^3)^2+8n^3+1=(4n^3+1)^2 .\] A perfect square cannot lie strictly between two consecutive squares, so $n\ge2$ is impossible. For $n=1$ the equation reads $2^m=8$, so $m=3$. The unique solution is $\boxed{(m,n)=(3,1)}$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Graph Coloring and Labeling

For each pair of integers $(i,j)$ with $1\le i\le j\le 100$, exactly one of the statements $x_i=x_j$ or $x_i\ne x_j$ is written on a blackboard. Determine the maximum nonnegative integer $m$ such that the following holds:

It is possible to assign to each of $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{100}$ a value equal to $0$ or $1$ in such a way that at least $m$ of the statements written on the blackboard are satisfied.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Separate statements comparing a variable with itself from statements comparing two different variables. Think about when the truth of a statement becomes fixed if you choose the values in index order.
Hint 2: key idea
At each step, compare the two choices for the new variable against every variable already assigned. The two choices satisfy complementary subsets of those statements, so the better choice gives a guaranteed contribution. (LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure)
Hint 3: approach
For an arbitrary board, set up the sum of the rounded-up half-counts guaranteed by the stepwise choices. For an upper bound, look for a board that every assignment satisfies poorly, and express its score in terms of the sizes of the groups assigned zero and one. Compare the resulting bounds.
Common trap
A self-comparison may be written as an inequality and then contributes nothing regardless of the assignment.
Show the answer

$m=2500$

Show solution 1 – Greedy assignment, and the all-different board

Reading. The board is arbitrary, so we look for the largest $m$ such that, whatever statements are written, some assignment of $0$s and $1$s satisfies at least $m$ of them. (If the board could be chosen favourably, writing $x_i=x_j$ everywhere and taking every $x_i=0$ would satisfy all $5050$ statements, a trivial question.) Note that for $i=j$ the board may carry the false statement $x_i\ne x_i$.

Upper bound. Suppose the board says $x_i\ne x_j$ for every pair $i\le j$. The $100$ statements $x_i\ne x_i$ are never true. If $a$ of the variables equal $0$ and $100-a$ equal $1$, the true statements are the pairs $i<j$ with different values: there are $a(100-a)\le50\cdot50=2500$ of them. So for this board no assignment satisfies more than $2500$ statements, and $m\le2500$.

Lower bound: a greedy choice. For any board, choose $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{100}$ in this order. When $x_j$ is chosen, look at the $j-1$ statements about the pairs $(i,j)$ with $i<j$; their truth depends only on $x_j$ (the earlier values being fixed), and the two possible values of $x_j$ make complementary sets of them true. So we can choose $x_j$ to make at least $\lceil (j-1)/2\rceil$ of them true. In total at least \[\sum_{j=1}^{100}\Big\lceil\frac{j-1}{2}\Big\rceil=(0+1)+(1+2)+(2+3)+\cdots+(49+50)=\sum_{k=1}^{50}(2k-1)=2500\] statements with $i<j$ are satisfied (the statements with $i=j$ can only help). Hence $m=\boxed{2500}$.

Show solution 2 – Removing two variables at a time

We look for the largest $m$ such that, for every board, some assignment satisfies at least $m$ statements.

The bound $2500$ cannot be improved. Write $x_i\ne x_j$ for all $i\le j$. The statements with $i=j$ are false, and an assignment with $a$ zeros satisfies exactly $a(100-a)\le2500$ of the others.

Every board allows $2500$. Let $f(n)$ be the least number of statements with $i<j$ that can be satisfied for any board on $n$ variables; we show $f(n)\ge f(n-2)+(n-1)$ for $n\ge3$ and $f(2)=1$. Given a board on $x_1,\dots,x_n$, first assign $x_1,\dots,x_{n-2}$ so that at least $f(n-2)$ statements among them hold. Then choose the pair $(x_{n-1},x_n)$ so that the statement about $x_{n-1},x_n$ holds; there are two such pairs, and they differ by swapping both values. For each $i\le n-2$, the two statements $(i,n-1)$ and $(i,n)$ have, say, $s_i$ true statements for one of the two pairs and $2-s_i$ for the other, because swapping both values flips both statements. So one of the two pairs makes at least half of the $2(n-2)$ statements $(i,n-1),(i,n)$ true. Altogether at least $f(n-2)+1+(n-2)$ statements hold.

Hence $f(100)\ge 1+3+5+\cdots+99=50^2=2500$, and the answer is $m=\boxed{2500}$.

Show solution 3 – An average over random assignments

We look for the largest $m$ such that, for every board, some assignment satisfies at least $m$ statements.

$m\le2500$. Write $x_i\ne x_j$ for all $i\le j$. The $100$ statements $x_i\ne x_i$ are false, and an assignment with $a$ zeros satisfies exactly $a(100-a)\le2500$ of the others.

$m\ge2500$ for every board. Split the indices into the $50$ pairs $\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{99,100\}$. For each pair $\{i,j\}$ there are exactly two assignments of $(x_i,x_j)$ that make the statement about $i,j$ true, and they are obtained from each other by switching both values; in each of them $x_i$ is $0$ once and $1$ once. Choose one of the two at random, with probability $\frac12$ each, independently for the $50$ pairs. Then the $50$ statements inside pairs are always true. Now take a statement about $i<j$ with $i,j$ in different pairs. The values $x_i$ and $x_j$ are independent and each is $0$ or $1$ with probability $\frac12$, so $x_i=x_j$ holds with probability $\frac12$, and the statement is true with probability $\frac12$. There are $\binom{100}2-50=4900$ such statements, so the expected number of true statements (ignoring those with $i=j$) is \[50+\tfrac12\cdot4900=2500 .\] A random quantity is at least its expected value for some outcome, so some assignment satisfies at least $2500$ statements. Hence $m=\boxed{2500}$.

Problem 5

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Centroid, Incenter, Circumcenter, and Orthocenter

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle with $AB<AC$. Let $O$ be its circumcenter, and let $J$ be the excircle center lying inside $\angle A$ (that is, the center of the circle tangent to $BC$, the ray $AB$, and the ray $AC$, which is not the incircle). Let the line through $J$ perpendicular to $AO$ meet the circumcircle of triangle $BCJ$ again at a point $P\ne J$. On line $BP$, choose a point $Q\ne B$, and on line $CP$, choose a point $R\ne C$, such that $AB=AQ$ and $AC=AR$. Prove that the incenter of triangle $ABC$ coincides with the orthocenter of triangle $PQR$. (Here $XY$ denotes the length of segment $XY$.)

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the incenter $I$, the $A$-excenter $J$, and the internal and external angle bisectors at $B$ and $C$. Draw the tangent to the circumcircle at $A$.
Hint 2: key idea
The internal and external bisectors at a vertex are perpendicular, so look for a circle with diameter $IJ$ that also contains $B$ and $C$. Use the angle this diameter subtends at $P$ and the condition on $PJ$ to determine the direction of $PI$. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the arc midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$, together with tangent–chord angles, to find mirrors that take $B$ and $C$ to $Q$ and $R$. Track where the images lie on the tangent and on the circles centered at those arc midpoints. Use the resulting cyclic angle relations to set up two altitude tests.
Common trap
Treat the angle relations as angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$ when points lie on extensions.
Show solution 1 – Arc midpoints and the tangent at A

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, $t$ its tangent at $A$, $I$ the incenter, and let $N_b$, $N_c$ be the midpoints of the arcs $CA$ and $AB$ of $\Gamma$ not containing $B$ and $C$; they lie on the lines $BI$ and $CI$. Recall two standard facts.

  • Incenter–excenter lemma: $N_cA=N_cB=N_cI$ and $N_bA=N_bC=N_bI$.

  • $I$ and $J$ lie on one circle $\omega$ through $B$ and $C$ with diameter $IJ$, because $\angle IBJ=\angle ICJ=90^\circ$ (internal and external bisectors are perpendicular).

Step 1: $PI\parallel AO$. $P$ lies on $\omega$, so $\angle IPJ=90^\circ$ ($P\ne I$: the line $IJ$ is the line $AI$, which is not perpendicular to $AO$, as $\angle(AI,AO)=\frac{|\angle B-\angle C|}{2}<90^\circ$). Also $PJ\perp AO$ by definition. Hence $PI\parallel AO$.

Step 2: $BP\perp AN_c$ and $CP\perp AN_b$. As $B,C,I,P$ lie on $\omega$ and $IP\parallel AO\perp t$, \[\measuredangle(BP,BC)=\measuredangle(IP,IC)=\measuredangle(AO,CN_c)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(t,CN_c).\] By the tangent–chord angle, $\measuredangle(t,AN_c)=\measuredangle(CA,CN_c)$, and $CN_c$ bisects angle $C$, so $\measuredangle(CA,CN_c)=\measuredangle(CN_c,CB)$. Therefore \[\measuredangle(t,CN_c)=\measuredangle(CA,CN_c)+\measuredangle(AN_c,CN_c)=\measuredangle(CN_c,CB)+\measuredangle(AN_c,CN_c)=\measuredangle(AN_c,BC),\] and $\measuredangle(BP,AN_c)=\measuredangle(BP,BC)+\measuredangle(BC,AN_c)=90^\circ$. In the same way $CP\perp AN_b$.

Step 3: $Q$ and $R$ lie on $t$, and on the circles centered at $N_c$ and $N_b$. By the tangent–chord angle and the bisector $CN_c$, \[\measuredangle(t,AN_c)=\measuredangle(CA,CN_c)=\measuredangle(CN_c,CB)=\measuredangle(AN_c,AB),\] so the line $AN_c$ bisects the angle between the lines $AB$ and $t$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in $AN_c$. It maps line $AB$ onto $t$, and it maps line $BP$ onto itself because $BP\perp AN_c$. So $\sigma(B)$ is a point of line $BP$ with $A\sigma(B)=AB$, different from $B$ (as $B\notin AN_c$); hence $\sigma(B)=Q$, and $Q\in t$. Since $N_c$ is on the mirror, $N_cQ=N_cB$, so $Q$ lies on the circle through $A,B,I$ centered at $N_c$. Likewise $R\in t$ and $R$ lies on the circle through $A,C,I$ centered at $N_b$.

Step 4: two altitudes. From $A,C,I,R$ concyclic and $R,A\in t$, \[\measuredangle(RI,t)=\measuredangle(RI,RA)=\measuredangle(CI,CA)=\measuredangle(CN_c,CA)=\measuredangle(AN_c,t),\] using once more $\measuredangle(t,AN_c)=\measuredangle(CA,CN_c)$. So $RI\parallel AN_c$, and by Step 2, $RI\perp BP=PQ$. Symmetrically, from $A,B,I,Q$ concyclic, $QI\parallel AN_b\perp CP=PR$. Thus $I$ lies on the altitudes of triangle $PQR$ from $R$ and from $Q$, so $I$ is the orthocenter of $PQR$. (Step 1 also shows directly that $PI\perp t=QR$, the third altitude.)

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers on the circle $BICJ$

Let $I$ be the incenter. The internal and external bisectors at $B$ are perpendicular, and so are those at $C$; hence $\angle IBJ=\angle ICJ=90^\circ$ and $B,C$ lie on the circle $\omega$ with diameter $IJ$, which also contains $P$. Use complex numbers with $\omega$ the unit circle, $I=1$ and $J=-1$; lowercase letters denote the numbers of the points, so $|b|=|c|=|p|=1$ and $\bar b=1/b$, $\bar c=1/c$, $\bar p=1/p$. For a nonzero $z$ we call $z/\bar z$ the direction ratio of the direction $z$: two lines are parallel exactly when their direction ratios are equal, and perpendicular exactly when they are negatives of each other.

The point $A$. $A$ lies on the line $IJ$, the bisector of angle $A$, which is the real axis; so $a$ is real. The lines $AB$ and $AC$ are mirror images in this axis, so the arguments of $b-a$ and $c-a$ are opposite modulo $180^\circ$, and $(b-a)(c-a)$ is real. Equating it with its conjugate $(1/b-a)(1/c-a)$ and multiplying by $bc$ gives \[bc(b-a)(c-a)-(1-ab)(1-ac)=(bc-1)\bigl(1+bc-a(b+c)\bigr)=0 .\] If $bc=1$, then $c=\bar b$, so $C$ is the mirror image of $B$ in line $AI$ and $AB=AC$, which is excluded. Also $b+c\ne0$, since $BC$ is not a diameter of $\omega$ ($\angle BIC=90^\circ+\frac12\angle A\ne90^\circ$). Hence \[a=\frac{1+bc}{b+c}.\]

The circumcenter. Let $o=\frac{bc}{b+c}$; then $\bar o=\frac{1/(bc)}{(b+c)/(bc)}=\frac1{b+c}$. We have \[o-b=-\frac{b^2}{b+c},\quad \bar o-\bar b=-\frac{c}{b(b+c)},\qquad o-a=-\frac1{b+c},\quad \bar o-a=-\frac{bc}{b+c},\] so $|o-b|^2=|o-a|^2=\frac{bc}{(b+c)^2}$, and by symmetry also $|o-c|^2$ has this value. Thus $o$ is equidistant from $A,B,C$: it is the circumcenter $O$. Moreover $o-a=-\bar o$, so the direction ratio of $AO$ is $\bar o/o=\frac1{bc}$.

The point $P$. The direction ratio of the chord $JP$ is $\frac{p+1}{1/p+1}=p$. Since $JP\perp AO$, we get $p=-\frac1{bc}$ (and $p\ne-1$ because $bc\ne1$, so indeed $P\ne J$).

The points $Q$ and $R$. The circle with center $A$ through $B$ meets the line $BP$ again at the reflection of $B$ in the perpendicular from $A$ to $BP$. The direction ratio of $BP$ is $\frac{p-b}{1/p-1/b}=-bp$, so the perpendicular through $A$ has direction ratio $bp$, and the reflection of a point $z$ in it is $a+bp\,(\bar z-a)$ (using $\bar a=a$). Hence \[q=a+bp\Bigl(\frac1b-a\Bigr)=a+p-abp,\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad r=a+p-acp .\]

$IQ\perp PC$. The direction ratio of $PC$ is $-pc$, so we must show $q-1=pc\,(\bar q-1)$. Here $\bar q=a+\bar p-a\bar b\bar p=a-bc+ac$. Substituting $p=-\frac1{bc}$ and multiplying by $bc$, a direct expansion gives \[bc\bigl[(q-1)-pc(\bar q-1)\bigr]=(c+1)\bigl(a(b+c)-(1+bc)\bigr)=0 .\] Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$ gives $IR\perp PB$ in the same way.

Conclusion. $Q$ lies on line $PB$ and $R$ on line $PC$, so $IQ\perp PR$ and $IR\perp PQ$: the point $I$ lies on two altitudes of triangle $PQR$. Hence $I$ is the orthocenter of triangle $PQR$.
