Grade 9

Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Symmetric and Cyclic Systems

We consider the real numbers $a$, $b$, $c$ and the equations \[x^2+4ax+(b+c)^2=0,\qquad x^2+4bx+(c+a)^2=0,\qquad x^2+4cx+(a+b)^2=0,\] respectively.

  • a) Show that at least one of the equations has real solutions.

  • b) Prove that if the equations admit a common real solution, then $a=b=c$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sum of the discriminants, then pairwise differences)

a) The discriminants of the three equations are \[\Delta_1=16a^2-4(b+c)^2,\qquad \Delta_2=16b^2-4(c+a)^2,\qquad \Delta_3=16c^2-4(a+b)^2 .\] Since $(b+c)^2+(c+a)^2+(a+b)^2=2(a^2+b^2+c^2)+2(ab+bc+ca)$, \[\Delta_1+\Delta_2+\Delta_3=8\bigl(a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca\bigr)=4\bigl((a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\bigr)\ge0 .\] Three numbers with a nonnegative sum cannot all be negative, so some $\Delta_k\ge0$ and that equation has real solutions.

b) Let $x$ be a common real solution. Subtracting the second equation from the first, \[4(a-b)x+(b+c)^2-(c+a)^2=0\iff (a-b)\bigl(4x-a-b-2c\bigr)=0,\] and in the same way $(b-c)(4x-b-c-2a)=0$ and $(c-a)(4x-c-a-2b)=0$.

Suppose $a,b,c$ are not all equal.

  • If $a,b,c$ are pairwise distinct, then $4x=a+b+2c$ and $4x=b+c+2a$; subtracting gives $c=a$, a contradiction.

  • Otherwise exactly two of them are equal; by the symmetry of the system we may take $a=b\neq c$. From the second relation, $4x=b+c+2a=3a+c$. Substituting $x=\frac{3a+c}4$ into the first equation and multiplying by $16$: \[(3a+c)^2+16a(3a+c)+16(a+c)^2=73a^2+54ac+17c^2=0 .\] As a quadratic form this has discriminant $54^2-4\cdot73\cdot17=2916-4964<0$, so it vanishes only for $a=c=0$, contradicting $a\neq c$.

Hence $a=b=c$.

Solution 2 (One sum of squares)

a) The discriminant of $x^2+4ax+(b+c)^2=0$ is $4\bigl((2a)^2-(b+c)^2\bigr)$, and similarly for the others. If all three equations had no real solutions, then \[4a^2<(b+c)^2,\qquad 4b^2<(c+a)^2,\qquad 4c^2<(a+b)^2 .\] Adding, $4(a^2+b^2+c^2)<2(a^2+b^2+c^2)+2(ab+bc+ca)$, that is, $a^2+b^2+c^2<ab+bc+ca$. This is impossible, because \[a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca=\tfrac12\bigl((a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\bigr)\ge0 .\]

b) Let $x$ be a common real solution and put $p=x+2a$, $q=x+2b$, $r=x+2c$. Then $x^2+4ax=p^2-4a^2$, so the first equation reads $p^2=4a^2-(b+c)^2$, and similarly $q^2=4b^2-(c+a)^2$, $r^2=4c^2-(a+b)^2$. Adding, \[p^2+q^2+r^2=2(a^2+b^2+c^2)-2(ab+bc+ca)=(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2 .\] Since $a-b=\frac{p-q}2$, $b-c=\frac{q-r}2$, $c-a=\frac{r-p}2$, the right-hand side equals \[\tfrac14\bigl((p-q)^2+(q-r)^2+(r-p)^2\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(p^2+q^2+r^2-pq-qr-rp\bigr).\] Therefore $p^2+q^2+r^2+pq+qr+rp=0$, which can be written as \[\tfrac12\bigl((p+q+r)^2+p^2+q^2+r^2\bigr)=0 .\] A sum of squares of real numbers vanishes only if every term is $0$, so $p=q=r=0$, that is, $a=b=c=-\frac x2$.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Addition and Balance Relations

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral, $O$ the intersection of its diagonals, and $G_1$, $G_2$, $G_3$ the centroids of the triangles $ABD$, $ABC$ and $ODC$, respectively. Prove that $O$ is the centroid of the triangle $G_1G_2G_3$ if and only if $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Position vectors from O)

Use position vectors with origin $O$, and write $\vec A,\vec B,\vec C,\vec D$ for the position vectors of the vertices, so $\vec O=\vec 0$. Then \[\vec G_1=\tfrac13(\vec A+\vec B+\vec D),\quad \vec G_2=\tfrac13(\vec A+\vec B+\vec C),\quad \vec G_3=\tfrac13(\vec C+\vec D).\] The centroid of $G_1G_2G_3$ has position vector $\frac13(\vec G_1+\vec G_2+\vec G_3)=\frac29(\vec A+\vec B+\vec C+\vec D)$, so \[O \text{ is the centroid of } G_1G_2G_3\iff \vec A+\vec B+\vec C+\vec D=\vec 0 .\] Because $ABCD$ is convex, $O$ lies strictly inside both diagonals, so $\vec C=-\gamma\vec A$ and $\vec D=-\delta\vec B$ with $\gamma,\delta>0$, and $\vec A,\vec B$ are not parallel ($A$, $O$, $B$ are not collinear). The condition becomes \[(1-\gamma)\vec A+(1-\delta)\vec B=\vec 0,\] which, since $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ are linearly independent, holds if and only if $\gamma=\delta=1$, i.e. $O$ is the midpoint of both diagonals. A quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other is a parallelogram, and conversely. This proves the equivalence.

(In the parallelogram case $\vec G_1=\frac13\vec A$, $\vec G_2=\frac13\vec B$, $\vec G_3=-\frac13(\vec A+\vec B)$, which are not collinear, so $G_1G_2G_3$ is a genuine triangle.)

Solution 2 (Midpoints of the diagonals)

Take any origin. The centroid of $G_1G_2G_3$ is \[\tfrac19\bigl[(A+B+D)+(A+B+C)+(O+C+D)\bigr]=\tfrac19\bigl[2(A+B+C+D)+O\bigr],\] so it equals $O$ if and only if $2(A+B+C+D)=8O$, that is, \[\frac{M+N}2=O,\qquad\text{where } M=\frac{A+C}2,\ N=\frac{B+D}2\] are the midpoints of the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$. So the condition says exactly: $O$ is the midpoint of $MN$.

If $ABCD$ is a parallelogram, then $M=N=O$ and the condition holds.

Conversely, assume $O$ is the midpoint of $MN$. The point $M$ lies on line $AC$ and $N$ lies on line $BD$, and these lines meet only at $O$. If $M\ne O$, then line $MO$ is line $AC$, and $N$, lying on line $MO$, belongs to both diagonals' lines, so $N=O$; but then $O$ is the midpoint of $MO$, forcing $M=O$, a contradiction. Hence $M=O$, and then $N=2O-M=O$. Both diagonals have midpoint $O$, so they bisect each other and $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Find all the positive integers $n$ for which the number \[4^{n-1}+n^2+11\] is a perfect square.

Show the answer

$n=3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squeezing between consecutive squares)

Put $X=2^{n-1}$, so the number is $N=X^2+n^2+11>X^2$. The next square after $X^2$ is $(X+1)^2=X^2+2^n+1$. If \[n^2+11<2^n+1,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad n^2+10<2^n,\] then $X^2<N<(X+1)^2$ and $N$ is not a square.

The inequality $n^2+10<2^n$ holds for $n=6$ ($46<64$), and if it holds for some $n\ge6$ then \[2^{n+1}>2n^2+20>(n+1)^2+10,\] because $2n^2+20-(n+1)^2-10=n^2-2n+9>0$. So it holds for all $n\ge6$.

For $n\le5$ we compute $N$: $13,\ 19,\ 36,\ 91,\ 292$ for $n=1,2,3,4,5$. Only $36=6^2$ is a square ($292$ lies between $17^2=289$ and $18^2$). The answer is $\boxed{n=3}$.

Solution 2 (Factoring a difference of squares)

Suppose $4^{n-1}+n^2+11=k^2$ with $k$ a positive integer, and put $X=2^{n-1}$. Then \[(k-X)(k+X)=n^2+11>0,\] so $k>X$, hence $k-X\ge1$ and $k+X\le n^2+11$. Since $k\ge X+1$, this gives $2X+1\le k+X\le n^2+11$, i.e. \[2^n\le n^2+10 .\] This fails for $n=6$ ($64>46$), and for larger $n$ the left side doubles at each step while the right side grows by $2n+1<n^2+10$, so it fails for every $n\ge6$. Thus $n\le5$.

Checking $n=1,\dots,5$: $n^2+11=12,15,20,27,36$ must equal $(k-X)(k+X)$ with $X=1,2,4,8,16$, where both factors have the same parity and $k+X>2X$.

  • $n=1$: $12=2\cdot6$ gives $k=4$, $X=2\ne1$; no.

  • $n=2$: $15=1\cdot15$ or $3\cdot5$ gives $X=7$ or $1$, not $2$; no.

  • $n=3$: $20=2\cdot10$ gives $k=6$, $X=4$; yes: $16+9+11=36$.

  • $n=4$: $27=1\cdot27$ or $3\cdot9$ gives $X=13$ or $3$, not $8$; no.

  • $n=5$: $36=2\cdot18$ gives $X=8$, not $16$ (and $6\cdot6$ gives $X=0$); no.

The only solution is $\boxed{n=3}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Divisibility Chains and Factor Relations

Find all the sequences of positive integers $(a_n)_{n\ge1}$ that satisfy the following conditions simultaneously: \[\begin{aligned}&(1)\ \ i+j \text{ divides } a_i+a_j;\\ &(2)\ \ a_i+a_j \text{ divides } (i+j)^2,\end{aligned}\] for any positive integers $i$ and $j$.

Show the answer

$a_n=n$ for all $n$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Large primes pin the sequence down)

The sequence $a_n=n$ works: $i+j$ divides $i+j$, and $i+j$ divides $(i+j)^2$.

Conversely, let $(a_n)$ satisfy the conditions. With $i=j$: $2i\mid 2a_i$ and $2a_i\mid 4i^2$, so \[i\mid a_i\quad\text{and}\quad a_i\mid 2i^2\qquad\text{for every } i. \tag{$*$}\]

Step 1: $a_1=1$. By $(*)$, $a_1\in\{1,2\}$. Suppose $a_1=2$. By $(*)$, $a_2\in\{2,4,8\}$, and with $i=1$, $j=2$: $3\mid 2+a_2$ and $2+a_2\mid 9$, so $2+a_2\in\{3,9\}$, i.e. $a_2\in\{1,7\}$, which contradicts $a_2\in\{2,4,8\}$. So $a_1=1$.

Step 2: $a_p=p$ for every odd prime $p$. By $(*)$, $a_p=pd$ with $d\mid 2p$, so $a_p\in\{p,2p,p^2,2p^2\}$. Condition (1) with $i=1$, $j=p$ says $p+1\mid 1+a_p$. Modulo $p+1$ we have $p\equiv-1$, so:

  • $a_p=2p$: $1+a_p\equiv-1$, and $p+1\nmid 1$;

  • $a_p=p^2$: $1+a_p\equiv 2$, and $p+1\ge4$ does not divide $2$;

  • $a_p=2p^2$: $1+a_p\equiv3$, and $p+1\ge4$ does not divide $3$.

Hence $a_p=p$.

Step 3: $a_n=n$ for every $n$. Fix $n$. For every odd prime $p$, condition (1) with $i=n$, $j=p$ gives $n+p\mid a_n+p$, hence $n+p\mid (a_n+p)-(n+p)=a_n-n$. Choose an odd prime $p>a_n$ (there are infinitely many primes). Since $a_n$ and $n$ are positive, $|a_n-n|<\max(a_n,n)<n+p$, and the only multiple of $n+p$ with absolute value less than $n+p$ is $0$. So $a_n=n$.

Therefore the only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=n}$.

Solution 2 (Working only with the index 1)

The sequence $a_n=n$ clearly satisfies both conditions. Now let $(a_n)$ be any solution. Taking $i=j$ gives $2i\mid 2a_i\mid 4i^2$, so \[i\mid a_i\quad\text{and}\quad a_i\mid 2i^2 . \tag{$*$}\]

$a_1=1$. From $(*)$, $a_1\in\{1,2\}$. If $a_1=2$, then $(*)$ gives $a_2\in\{2,4,8\}$, while $i=1$, $j=2$ gives $3\mid a_2+2\mid 9$, so $a_2+2\in\{3,9\}$; none of $4,6,10$ is $3$ or $9$. Hence $a_1=1$.

The two candidates for $a_n$. With $i=1$, $j=n$: $n+1\mid 1+a_n$ and $1+a_n\mid (n+1)^2$. Write $1+a_n=(n+1)t$ with $t$ a positive integer; then $t\mid n+1$. Also $n\mid a_n=(n+1)t-1=nt+(t-1)$, so $n\mid t-1$. Thus $t=1$, or $t-1\ge n$, i.e. $t\ge n+1$; together with $t\mid n+1$ the latter forces $t=n+1$. So \[a_n=n\qquad\text{or}\qquad a_n=(n+1)^2-1=n(n+2).\]

The second candidate almost never works. If $a_n=n(n+2)$, then $(*)$ gives $n(n+2)\mid 2n^2$, so $n+2\mid 2n=2(n+2)-4$, hence $n+2\mid4$, i.e. $n=2$. So $a_n=n$ for all $n\ne2$, and $a_2\in\{2,8\}$.

$a_2=2$. Condition (2) with $i=2$, $j=3$ (where $a_3=3$) requires $a_2+3\mid 25$. For $a_2=8$ this reads $11\mid25$, false. So $a_2=2$.

Hence the only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=n}$.

Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Transcendental and Growth-Based Modeling

Let $a,b,x,y>0$ be real numbers with $ab\neq1$, and $c$ a positive integer such that \[\log_a\sqrt{x}=\log_b\sqrt{cx+y}=\log_{ab}y.\]

  • a) Prove that if $c=1$, then $\dfrac{y}{x}$ is irrational.

  • b) Prove that $\dfrac{y}{x}$ is rational if and only if $c$ is the product of two consecutive positive integers.

Show the answer

proof ($\frac yx=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4c}}2$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The common value of the logarithms)

Let $t$ be the common value of the three logarithms. (The bases $a$, $b$, $ab$ are positive and different from $1$.) Then \[\sqrt x=a^t,\qquad \sqrt{cx+y}=b^t,\qquad y=(ab)^t=a^tb^t=\sqrt x\cdot\sqrt{cx+y}.\] Squaring the last relation, $y^2=x(cx+y)=cx^2+xy$. Dividing by $x^2>0$ and writing $r=\frac yx>0$: \[r^2-r-c=0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad r=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4c}}2\] (the other root is negative).

Hence $r$ is rational if and only if $\sqrt{1+4c}$ is rational, i.e. if and only if the positive integer $1+4c$ is a perfect square. A perfect square that is odd is the square of an odd number, so this means $1+4c=(2k+1)^2$ for some integer $k\ge1$ (as $1+4c\ge5$), i.e. $4c=4k^2+4k$, i.e. $c=k(k+1)$. This proves b).

a) For $c=1$ we get $r=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$, which is irrational because $5$ is not a perfect square. (Equivalently, $1$ is not of the form $k(k+1)$ with $k\ge1$, since $1\cdot2=2>1$.)

Solution 2 (Rational roots of a monic integer quadratic)

Denote by $t$ the common value of the logarithms; then $x=a^{2t}$, $cx+y=b^{2t}$ and $y=a^tb^t$. Multiplying the first two, $x(cx+y)=(a^tb^t)^2=y^2$. So $r=\frac yx$ is a positive root of \[P(X)=X^2-X-c .\] b) If $r$ is rational, write $r=\frac uv$ in lowest terms with $u,v$ positive integers. From $u^2=uv+cv^2$ we get $v\mid u^2$, and as $\gcd(u,v)=1$, $v=1$. So $r$ is a positive integer $k'$ with $k'^2-k'=c$, i.e. $c=k'(k'-1)$, and $k'\ge2$ because $c\ge1$. Thus $c=k(k+1)$ with $k=k'-1\ge1$.

Conversely, if $c=k(k+1)$, then $P(X)=X^2-X-k(k+1)=(X-(k+1))(X+k)$, whose only positive root is $k+1$; so $r=k+1$ is rational.

a) If $c=1$ and $r$ were rational, the argument above would give an integer $k'\ge2$ with $k'(k'-1)=1$, impossible since $k'(k'-1)\ge2$. Hence $\frac yx$ is irrational.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Polar Form and De Moivre Structure

Find all the functions $f:\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ that satisfy the following conditions simultaneously:

  • $f(z_1z_2)=f(z_1)f(z_2)$, for any $z_1,z_2\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$;

  • $\overline{f(z)}=f\left(\dfrac1z\right)$, for any $z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$;

  • $\overline{z}\,f(z)\in(0,\infty)$, for any $z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$.

Show the answer

$f(z)=\dfrac{z}{|z|}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor out z)

Check. For $f(z)=\frac z{|z|}$: $f(z_1z_2)=\frac{z_1z_2}{|z_1||z_2|}=f(z_1)f(z_2)$; $\overline{f(z)}=\frac{\overline z}{|z|}$ and $f\bigl(\frac1z\bigr)=\frac{1/z}{1/|z|}=\frac{|z|}{z}=\frac{|z|\,\overline z}{|z|^2}=\frac{\overline z}{|z|}$; and $\overline z f(z)=|z|>0$.

Uniqueness. Let $f$ satisfy the conditions. By the third condition, $\overline z f(z)=s(z)$ with $s(z)>0$, so \[f(z)=\frac{s(z)}{\overline z}=\frac{s(z)}{|z|^2}\,z=h(z)\,z,\qquad h(z)=\frac{s(z)}{|z|^2}>0 .\] From $f(1)=f(1)^2$ and $f(1)\neq0$ we get $f(1)=1$, and then $f(z)f\bigl(\frac1z\bigr)=f(1)=1$. Using the second condition, \[1=f(z)f\Bigl(\frac1z\Bigr)=f(z)\,\overline{f(z)}=|f(z)|^2=h(z)^2|z|^2 .\] As $h(z)>0$, $h(z)=\frac1{|z|}$, and $f(z)=\dfrac{z}{|z|}$. So the only function is $\boxed{f(z)=\dfrac z{|z|}}$.

Solution 2 (Positive reals and the unit circle separately)

First, $f(1)^2=f(1)\neq0$ gives $f(1)=1$, so $f\bigl(\frac1z\bigr)=\frac1{f(z)}$ for every $z$.

Positive reals. For $r>0$, the third condition says $r f(r)>0$, so $f(r)$ is a positive real number; then the second condition gives $f(r)=\overline{f(r)}=f\bigl(\frac1r\bigr)=\frac1{f(r)}$, so $f(r)^2=1$ and $f(r)=1$.

Unit circle. For $|u|=1$ we have $\overline{f(u)}=f\bigl(\frac1u\bigr)=\frac1{f(u)}$, so $|f(u)|^2=1$. The third condition says $\overline u f(u)=\rho>0$, so $f(u)=\rho u$ (as $\overline u=\frac1u$), and $|f(u)|=\rho=1$. Thus $f(u)=u$.

All $z$. Writing $z=|z|\cdot u$ with $u=\frac z{|z|}$ on the unit circle, multiplicativity gives \[f(z)=f(|z|)\,f(u)=1\cdot u=\frac z{|z|}.\] This function satisfies all three conditions (for instance $\overline z\cdot\frac z{|z|}=|z|>0$), so the answer is $\boxed{f(z)=\dfrac z{|z|}}$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · Transcendental and Growth-Based Modeling

Solve in $\mathbb{R}$ the equation \[2+5\cdot6^x=3\cdot2^x+4\cdot3^x.\]

Show the answer

$x\in\{-1,\,0\}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A hidden product equal to 1)

Divide by $6^x$ and put $p=2^{-x}$, $q=3^{-x}$ (so $6^{-x}=pq$): \[2pq+5=3q+4p\iff 2pq-4p-3q+6=1\iff (2p-3)(q-2)=1 .\] Let $t=-x$, so $p=2^t$ and $q=3^t$ are increasing functions of $t$. Since the product is positive, the two factors have the same sign.

  • Both positive: then $2\cdot2^t-3$ and $3^t-2$ are positive and strictly increasing in $t$, so their product is strictly increasing, and the equation has at most one solution in this region. $t=1$ works: $(4-3)(3-2)=1$.

  • Both negative: then $3-2\cdot2^t$ and $2-3^t$ are positive and strictly decreasing, so their product is strictly decreasing and there is at most one solution. $t=0$ works: $(-1)(-1)=1$.

Hence $t\in\{0,1\}$, i.e. the solutions are $\boxed{x=0\text{ and }x=-1}$. Check: $x=0$: $2+5=3+4$; $x=-1$: $2+\frac56=\frac32+\frac43=\frac{17}6$.

Solution 2 (Solve for one exponential and compare monotonicity)

Put $u=2^x>0$. The equation is linear in $3^x$: \[3^x(5u-4)=3u-2 .\] If $5u=4$, the left side is $0$ and the right side is $\frac{12}5-2\ne0$, so $u\neq\frac45$ and \[3^x=\frac{3u-2}{5u-4}=\frac35+\frac{2}{5(5u-4)}=:R(u).\] On each of the intervals $u<\frac45$ and $u>\frac45$, $R$ is strictly decreasing in $u$, hence strictly decreasing in $x$ (as $u=2^x$ is increasing), while $3^x$ is strictly increasing in $x$. So each of the two ranges of $x$ contains at most one solution.

  • $u>\frac45$, i.e. $x>\log_2\frac45$: $x=0$ gives $u=1$, $R(1)=1=3^0$. Solution.

  • $u<\frac45$: $x=-1$ gives $u=\frac12$, $R\bigl(\frac12\bigr)=\frac{-1/2}{-3/2}=\frac13=3^{-1}$. Solution.

Therefore the solutions are $\boxed{x\in\{-1,0\}}$.

Problem 4

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Roots of Unity

For any finite set $A=\{z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_n\}$ of nonzero complex numbers with $n\ge4$ elements, we define the set \[B(A)=\{z_iz_j \mid 1\le i<j\le n\}.\] Find the sets $A$ for which $A=B(A)$.

Show the answer

$A=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid z^n=1\}$, the set of the $n$-th roots of unity

Show the solution

Solution (Moduli, then the polynomial with roots A)

The $n$-th roots of unity work. Let $U_n=\{z\mid z^n=1\}$. A product of two of them is again in $U_n$, so $B(U_n)\subseteq U_n$. Conversely, for $w\in U_n$ the $n$ numbers $z\in U_n$ give $n$ factorizations $w=z\cdot\frac wz$ with both factors in $U_n$; the factors are equal only if $z^2=w$, which has at most $2$ solutions. Since $n\ge4>2$, some factorization uses two distinct elements, so $w\in B(U_n)$.

Now let $A$ be any set with $A=B(A)$; in particular every product of two distinct elements of $A$ lies in $A$.

Step 1: all elements have modulus $1$. Order the moduli $r_1\le r_2\le\cdots\le r_n$ (with $r_k=|z_k|$ after relabelling). The product $z_{n-1}z_n$ lies in $A$, so $r_{n-1}r_n\le r_n$, i.e. $r_{n-1}\le1$; similarly $z_1z_2\in A$ gives $r_1r_2\ge r_1$, i.e. $r_2\ge1$. As $n\ge4$, $r_2\le r_{n-1}$, hence $r_2=\cdots=r_{n-1}=1$. If $r_n>1$, then $z_n$ is the only element of modulus $r_n$, yet the $n-2\ge2$ distinct products $z_nz_2,\dots,z_nz_{n-1}$ all have modulus $r_n$ and lie in $A$, a contradiction. So $r_n=1$, and in the same way (products $z_1z_j$, $2\le j\le n-1$) $r_1=1$.

Step 2: a polynomial identity. Let $S=\sum_{a\in A}a$ and $P=\prod_{a\in A}a\neq0$. Fix $z\in A$. The $n-1$ numbers $zw$, $w\in A\setminus\{z\}$, are distinct and lie in $A$, so they are all elements of $A$ except one, say $m$. Comparing sums and products, \[z(S-z)=S-m,\qquad z^{n-1}\cdot\frac Pz=\frac Pm .\] The second gives $m=z^{2-n}$; substituting in the first, $zS-z^2=S-z^{2-n}$, and multiplying by $z^{n-2}$: \[z^n-Sz^{n-1}+Sz^{n-2}-1=0 .\] So the $n$ distinct elements of $A$ are the $n$ roots of this monic polynomial of degree $n$: \[\prod_{a\in A}(X-a)=X^n-SX^{n-1}+SX^{n-2}-1 .\]

Step 3: $S=0$. Comparing constant terms, $(-1)^nP=-1$. Because $n\ge4$, the coefficient of $X$ is $0$; this coefficient is $(-1)^{n-1}e_{n-1}$, where $e_{n-1}=\sum_{a\in A}\frac Pa$. So $\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a=0$. But $|a|=1$ means $\frac1a=\overline a$, hence $\overline S=0$ and $S=0$.

Therefore $\prod_{a\in A}(X-a)=X^n-1$, i.e. $A$ is exactly the set of $n$-th roots of unity: \[\boxed{A=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid z^n=1\}}.\]
