Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool

Define the subsets of the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$: \[A=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid z=re^{i\theta},\ r\geq 0,\ \theta\in[0,\pi/41]\},\] \[B=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid z=x+iy,\ x,y\in\mathbb{R},\ |x-y|<2025\},\] where $i$ is the imaginary unit, and $re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$.

Find all complex-coefficient polynomials $P(z)$ such that for every $z\in A$, we have $P(z)\in B$.

Show the answer

the constant polynomials $P(z)=c$ with $|\operatorname{Re}c-\operatorname{Im}c|<2025$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A bounded polynomial on every ray)

Answer: exactly the constant polynomials $P(z)=c$ with $c\in B$, i.e. $|\operatorname{Re}c-\operatorname{Im}c|<2025$.

A constant $c\in B$ clearly works. Conversely, let $P$ satisfy the condition and put \[Q(z)=(1+i)P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}q_kz^k .\] For $w=x+iy$ we have $(1+i)w=(x-y)+i(x+y)$, so $\operatorname{Re}\bigl((1+i)w\bigr)=x-y$. Hence the condition says \[\bigl|\operatorname{Re}Q(z)\bigr|<2025\qquad\text{for all }z\in A .\tag{1}\] Fix $\theta\in[0,\pi/41]$ and let $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r\ge0$. Then \[\operatorname{Re}Q(re^{i\theta})=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\operatorname{Re}\bigl(q_ke^{ik\theta}\bigr)\,r^k\] is a real polynomial in $r$ which, by (1), is bounded on $[0,\infty)$. A real polynomial that is bounded on $[0,\infty)$ is constant. Therefore \[\operatorname{Re}\bigl(q_ke^{ik\theta}\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for all }k\ge1\text{ and all }\theta\in[0,\pi/41].\tag{2}\] Fix $k\ge1$. For $\theta=0$, (2) gives $\operatorname{Re}q_k=0$, so $q_k=it$ with $t$ real. For $\theta_k=\frac{\pi}{41k}\in(0,\pi/41]$, (2) gives \[0=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(it\,e^{ik\theta_k}\bigr)=-t\sin\frac{\pi}{41},\] so $t=0$. Thus $q_k=0$ for all $k\ge1$, $Q$ is constant, and $P=c$ is constant; the condition then says $c\in B$.

So the required polynomials are the constants: \[\boxed{P(z)=c\ \text{ with }c=x_0+iy_0,\ |x_0-y_0|<2025}.\]

Solution 2 (The leading coefficient)

Answer: exactly the constant polynomials $P(z)=c$ with $c\in B$, i.e. $|\operatorname{Re}c-\operatorname{Im}c|<2025$.

A constant $c\in B$ clearly works. For $w=x+iy$ we have $(1+i)w=(x-y)+i(x+y)$, so $w\in B$ if and only if $\bigl|\operatorname{Re}\bigl((1+i)w\bigr)\bigr|<2025$.

Suppose that a polynomial $P$ of degree $d\ge1$ satisfies the condition, and let $Q(z)=(1+i)P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^dq_kz^k$, with $q_d\ne0$. Then $|\operatorname{Re}Q(z)|<2025$ for all $z\in A$. Fix $\theta\in[0,\pi/41]$. For $r>0$, \[\frac{\operatorname{Re}Q(re^{i\theta})}{r^d}=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(q_de^{id\theta}\bigr)+\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\operatorname{Re}\bigl(q_ke^{ik\theta}\bigr)\,r^{k-d}.\] As $r\to\infty$, the left side tends to $0$ (its absolute value is less than $2025/r^d$) and the right side tends to $\operatorname{Re}(q_de^{id\theta})$. Hence \[\operatorname{Re}\bigl(q_de^{id\theta}\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for every }\theta\in[0,\pi/41].\] For $\theta=0$ this says that $q_d$ is purely imaginary, $q_d=it$ with a real $t\ne0$. For $\theta=\frac{\pi}{41d}$ it says that $q_de^{i\pi/41}$ is purely imaginary too; but $\operatorname{Re}\bigl(it\,e^{i\pi/41}\bigr)=-t\sin\frac{\pi}{41}\ne0$. This contradiction shows that $P$ is constant, $P=c$, and then the condition says $c\in B$: \[\boxed{P(z)=c\ \text{ with }c=x_0+iy_0,\ |x_0-y_0|<2025}.\]

Solution 3 (Averaging over a circle inside the sector)

Answer: exactly the constant polynomials $P(z)=c$ with $c\in B$, i.e. $|\operatorname{Re}c-\operatorname{Im}c|<2025$.

A constant $c\in B$ clearly works. For $w=u+iv$ we have $(1+i)w=(u-v)+i(u+v)$, so $w\in B$ if and only if $\bigl|\operatorname{Re}\bigl((1+i)w\bigr)\bigr|<2025$. Suppose that a polynomial $P$ of degree $d\ge1$ satisfies the condition and put $F(z)=(1+i)P(z)$, a polynomial of degree $d$ with leading coefficient $f_d\ne0$. Then $|\operatorname{Re}F(z)|<2025$ for all $z\in A$.

Discs inside the sector. Let $\alpha=\frac{\pi}{82}$ (half the opening angle of $A$), let $\rho>0$ and $z_0=\frac{\rho}{\sin\alpha}e^{i\alpha}$. The distance from $z_0$ to each of the two boundary rays of $A$ is $|z_0|\sin\alpha=\rho$, so the circle $|z-z_0|=\rho$ lies in $A$.

Extracting the leading coefficient. Expand $F$ around $z_0$: $F(z_0+\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^d\beta_j\zeta^j$; the top coefficient is $\beta_d=f_d$. Choose an integer $m>2d$, let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/m}$ and $\zeta_k=\rho\,\omega^k$ $(k=0,\dots,m-1)$. Recall that for an integer $l$, \[\frac1m\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\omega^{kl}=\begin{cases}1,&m\mid l,\\0,&m\nmid l.\end{cases}\] Since $|\omega|=1$, $\overline{\zeta_k^{\,j}}=\rho^j\omega^{-kj}$, and $\operatorname{Re}F=\frac12(F+\overline F)$ gives \[\frac1m\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Re}F(z_0+\zeta_k)\,\omega^{-kd}=\frac12\sum_{j=0}^d\beta_j\rho^j\cdot\frac1m\sum_k\omega^{k(j-d)}+\frac12\sum_{j=0}^d\overline{\beta_j}\rho^j\cdot\frac1m\sum_k\omega^{-k(j+d)} .\] In the first sum $-d\le j-d\le0$, and $m\mid j-d$ only for $j=d$; in the second sum $d\le j+d\le2d<m$, so $m\nmid j+d$. Hence the left side equals $\frac12f_d\rho^d$.

Conclusion. All points $z_0+\zeta_k$ lie in $A$, so each $|\operatorname{Re}F(z_0+\zeta_k)|<2025$, and $|\omega^{-kd}|=1$. The average therefore has absolute value less than $2025$: \[\frac12|f_d|\,\rho^d<2025\qquad\text{for every }\rho>0 .\] Letting $\rho\to\infty$ gives $f_d=0$, a contradiction. So $P$ is constant, $P=c$, and the condition says $c\in B$: \[\boxed{P(z)=c\ \text{ with }c=x_0+iy_0,\ |x_0-y_0|<2025}.\]

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ be inscribed in a circle with center $O$ and radius $1$ and satisfy $\angle ABC<\angle BCD<90^\circ$. Let $P$ be the intersection of lines $BA$ and $CD$, and $Q$ be the intersection of diagonals $AC$ and $BD$. Suppose there exists a point $K$ on segment $BC$ such that $P,A,K,D$ are concyclic, and $KB\cdot KC=\frac12 OP^2$. The line perpendicular to $BC$ and passing through $Q$ intersects line $OP$ at point $T$.

  • Find all possible values of $OP$;

  • Find all possible values of $TB+TC$.

Show the answer

$OP=\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}$; $TB+TC$ takes exactly the values in the open interval $(2\sqrt2,\,3)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangent length, a triangle inequality, coordinates)

Let $\omega$ be the unit circle and $d=OP$. Since $\angle ABC+\angle BCD<180^\circ$, the rays $BA$ and $CD$ meet at $P$; so $A$ lies on the segment $PB$, $D$ on the segment $PC$, and $\angle PBC=\angle ABC$, $\angle PCB=\angle BCD$.

Step 1: $PK^2=OP^2-1$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, using the circles $PAKD$ and $ABCD$, \[\measuredangle(KP,KA)=\measuredangle(DP,DA)=\measuredangle(DC,DA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(BK,BA).\] By the converse of the tangent–chord theorem, the line $PK$ is tangent at $K$ to the circle $ABK$. Hence $PK^2=PA\cdot PB$, the power of $P$ with respect to $\omega$: \[PK^2=d^2-1 .\]

Step 2: $OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}3$ and $K$ is the midpoint of $OP$. The point $K$ lies on the chord $BC$, so $KB\cdot KC=1-OK^2$, and the hypothesis gives $OK^2=1-\frac{d^2}2$. The triangle inequality $OK+KP\ge OP$ reads \[\sqrt{1-\tfrac{d^2}2}+\sqrt{d^2-1}\ \ge\ d .\] Squaring: $\frac{d^2}2+2\sqrt{(1-\frac{d^2}2)(d^2-1)}\ge d^2$, i.e. $4(1-\frac{d^2}2)(d^2-1)\ge\frac{d^4}4$, i.e. \[0\ \ge\ \tfrac94d^4-6d^2+4=\bigl(\tfrac32d^2-2\bigr)^2 .\] Therefore $d^2=\frac43$, and equality holds in the triangle inequality: $K$ lies on the segment $OP$, with $OK=PK=\frac1{\sqrt3}$. So \[OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}3\qquad\text{and}\qquad K\text{ is the midpoint of }OP .\] (Step 5 shows that such configurations exist, so this value does occur.)

Step 3: the point $T$. Take coordinates with $O=(0,0)$, $P=(\frac2{\sqrt3},0)$, $K=(\frac1{\sqrt3},0)$. The lines $AD$ and $BC$ are not parallel (otherwise $ABCD$ is an isosceles trapezoid and $\angle ABC=\angle BCD$); let $R=AD\cap BC$. By Brocard's theorem for the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, the line $QR$ is the polar of $P$ with respect to $\omega$ and $O$ is the orthocentre of triangle $PQR$. The polar of $P$ is the line $x=\frac1{OP}=\frac{\sqrt3}2$. So $R=(\frac{\sqrt3}2,r)$ and $Q=(\frac{\sqrt3}2,y_Q)$; here $r\ne0$, since $R\in BC$ and the line $BC$ is not the line $OP$. From $OQ\perp PR$: \[\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\,y_Q\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2-\tfrac2{\sqrt3},\,r\Bigr)=-\tfrac14+r\,y_Q=0,\qquad y_Q=\frac1{4r}.\] The line $BC$ has direction $\overrightarrow{KR}=(\frac{\sqrt3}2-\frac1{\sqrt3},r)=(\frac1{2\sqrt3},r)$. For $T=(\tau,0)$ the condition $QT\perp BC$ is \[\Bigl(\tau-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\Bigr)\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt3}-\frac1{4r}\cdot r=0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\tau=\sqrt3 .\] Thus $T=(\sqrt3,0)$ is a fixed point, the inverse of $K$ in $\omega$ $(OK\cdot OT=1)$. Note also $PT=\sqrt3-\frac2{\sqrt3}=\frac1{\sqrt3}=PK$: the circle with centre $P$ through $K$ passes through $T$ (dashed in the figure).

Step 4: $TB+TC=\sqrt3\,BC$. For a point $X=(\cos t,\sin t)$ of $\omega$, \[XT^2=4-2\sqrt3\cos t,\qquad XK^2=\tfrac43-\tfrac2{\sqrt3}\cos t=\tfrac13XT^2 .\] So $XT=\sqrt3\,XK$ for every $X\in\omega$, and $TB+TC=\sqrt3(KB+KC)=\sqrt3\,BC$.

Step 5: the possible lengths of $BC$. Let $\varphi\in(0^\circ,90^\circ]$ be the angle between the lines $BC$ and $OP$, let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and $H$ the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $BC$. Since $K$ is the midpoint of $OP$ and $OM\perp BC$, the points $M,H$ are symmetric about $K$ and \[KM=KH=\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sqrt3},\qquad OM=PH=\frac{\sin\varphi}{\sqrt3},\qquad MB=MC=\sqrt{1-\tfrac13\sin^2\varphi}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}.\] Necessary conditions. $\angle PBC<\angle PCB$ means $PC<PB$, so $H\neq M$ (that is, $\varphi\ne90^\circ$) and $C$ is the endpoint on the same side of $M$ as $H$. Since $D$ lies between $P$ and $C$ and $PD\cdot PC=\frac13$, we need $PC>\frac1{\sqrt3}=PK$. Now $H$ lies between $K$ and $C$ (as $\angle PCB<90^\circ$), and $PC^2-PK^2=HC^2-HK^2$, so the condition is $HC>HK$, i.e. $MC-2KM>KM$: \[\sqrt{\tfrac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}>\sqrt3\cos\varphi\iff\cos^2\varphi<\tfrac14 .\] These conditions are sufficient. Let $0<\cos\varphi<\frac12$, draw the chord $BC$ through $K$ at angle $\varphi$ to $OP$, with $C$ on the side of $H$, and let $A,D$ be the second intersections of $PB,PC$ with $\omega$. Then $HC>HK>0$, so $H$ is an interior point of the segment $BC$ closer to $C$: $\angle PBC<\angle PCB<90^\circ$. Also $PB>PC>PK=\frac1{\sqrt3}$, so $A,D$ are the intersections nearer to $P$ and $ABCD$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral with $\angle ABC<\angle BCD<90^\circ$. $K$ is on the segment $BC$ and $KB\cdot KC=1-OK^2=\frac23=\frac12OP^2$. Finally $PK^2=\frac13=PA\cdot PB=PD\cdot PC$, so $PK$ is tangent to the circles $ABK$ and $DCK$, whence $\angle PKA=\angle ABC$ and $\angle PKD=\angle BCD$; as $A$ and $D$ lie on opposite sides of the line $PK$, $\angle AKD=\angle ABC+\angle BCD=180^\circ-\angle APD$, and $P,A,K,D$ are concyclic.

So $\cos^2\varphi$ takes exactly the values in $(0,\frac14)$, and $BC=2\sqrt{\frac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}$ takes exactly the values in $\bigl(2\sqrt{\tfrac23},\sqrt3\bigr)$. By Step 4, $TB+TC=\sqrt3\,BC$.

Answer. \[\boxed{OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boxed{2\sqrt2<TB+TC<3}\ \ (\text{every value of this open interval occurs}).\]

Solution 2 (The point T by poles and polars)

Let $\omega$ be the unit circle and $d=OP$. Since $\angle ABC+\angle BCD<180^\circ$, the rays $BA$ and $CD$ meet at $P$; so $A$ lies on the segment $PB$, $D$ on the segment $PC$, and $\angle PBC=\angle ABC$, $\angle PCB=\angle BCD$.

Step 1: $PK^2=OP^2-1$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, using the circles $PAKD$ and $ABCD$, \[\measuredangle(KP,KA)=\measuredangle(DP,DA)=\measuredangle(DC,DA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(BK,BA).\] By the converse of the tangent–chord theorem, the line $PK$ is tangent at $K$ to the circle $ABK$. Hence $PK^2=PA\cdot PB$, the power of $P$ with respect to $\omega$: \[PK^2=d^2-1 .\]

Step 2: $OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}3$ and $K$ is the midpoint of $OP$. The point $K$ lies on the chord $BC$, so $KB\cdot KC=1-OK^2$, and the hypothesis gives $OK^2=1-\frac{d^2}2$. The triangle inequality $OK+KP\ge OP$ reads \[\sqrt{1-\tfrac{d^2}2}+\sqrt{d^2-1}\ \ge\ d .\] Squaring: $\frac{d^2}2+2\sqrt{(1-\frac{d^2}2)(d^2-1)}\ge d^2$, i.e. $4(1-\frac{d^2}2)(d^2-1)\ge\frac{d^4}4$, i.e. \[0\ \ge\ \tfrac94d^4-6d^2+4=\bigl(\tfrac32d^2-2\bigr)^2 .\] Therefore $d^2=\frac43$, and equality holds in the triangle inequality: $K$ lies on the segment $OP$, with $OK=PK=\frac1{\sqrt3}$. So \[OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}3\qquad\text{and}\qquad K\text{ is the midpoint of }OP .\] (Step 5 shows that such configurations exist, so this value does occur.)

Step 3: the point $T$, synthetically. Poles and polars are taken with respect to $\omega$. Let $T'$ be the inverse of $K$ in $\omega$: the point of the ray $OK$ with $OK\cdot OT'=1$, so $OT'=\sqrt3$. We show $T=T'$.

Let $R=AD\cap BC$ (the lines are not parallel because $\angle ABC\ne\angle BCD$). By Brocard's theorem the polar of $P$ is the line $QR$ and the polar of $Q$ is the line $PR$.

(a) $RK=RP$. The polar of $P$ is perpendicular to $OP$ at distance $\frac1{OP}=\frac{\sqrt3}2$ from $O$. The perpendicular bisector of $PK$ is perpendicular to $OP$ at distance $\frac{OK+OP}2=\frac{\sqrt3}2$ from $O$. So these two lines coincide, and $R$, which lies on the polar of $P$, is equidistant from $P$ and $K$.

(b) The pole of $\ell$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $Q$ perpendicular to $BC$. It does not pass through $O$: otherwise $\ell=OQ$ is perpendicular to both $PR$ (Brocard) and $BC$, so $PR\parallel BC$, and since $R\in BC$ the point $P$ would lie on $BC$. Let $W$ be the pole of $\ell$. As $Q\in\ell$, the point $W$ lies on the polar of $Q$, the line $PR$. As $OW\perp\ell$ and $\ell\perp BC$, we have $OW\parallel BC$.

(c) $WK\perp OP$. In triangle $POW$ the line $BC$ passes through the midpoint $K$ of $PO$, is parallel to $OW$ and meets $PW$ at $R$. So $R$ is the midpoint of $PW$, and by (a) $RW=RP=RK$. Thus $K$ lies on the circle with diameter $PW$, $\angle PKW=90^\circ$.

(d) Conclusion. The polar of $T'$ is the line through $K$ perpendicular to $OK$, which is the line $KW$ by (c). So $W$ lies on the polar of $T'$, and by La Hire's theorem $T'$ lies on the polar of $W$, which is $\ell$. Since $T'$ also lies on $OP$ and $\ell\ne OP$ (otherwise $OP\perp BC$ and the figure would be symmetric, $\angle ABC=\angle BCD$), $T'=\ell\cap OP=T$.

Step 4: $TB+TC=\sqrt3\,BC$. Let $X$ be a point of $\omega$ not on the line $OP$. Since $OK\cdot OT=1=OX^2$, we have $\frac{OK}{OX}=\frac{OX}{OT}$, and the triangles $OKX$ and $OXT$ share the angle at $O$; so they are similar and $\frac{XK}{XT}=\frac{OK}{OX}=\frac1{\sqrt3}$, i.e. $XT=\sqrt3\,XK$. The points $B,C$ are not on the line $OP$ (the line $BC$ meets $OP$ only at $K$, which is inside $\omega$). Hence $TB+TC=\sqrt3(KB+KC)=\sqrt3\,BC$.

Step 5: the possible lengths of $BC$. Let $\varphi\in(0^\circ,90^\circ]$ be the angle between the lines $BC$ and $OP$, let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and $H$ the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $BC$. Since $K$ is the midpoint of $OP$ and $OM\perp BC$, the points $M,H$ are symmetric about $K$ and \[KM=KH=\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sqrt3},\qquad OM=PH=\frac{\sin\varphi}{\sqrt3},\qquad MB=MC=\sqrt{1-\tfrac13\sin^2\varphi}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}.\] Necessary conditions. $\angle PBC<\angle PCB$ means $PC<PB$, so $H\neq M$ (that is, $\varphi\ne90^\circ$) and $C$ is the endpoint on the same side of $M$ as $H$. Since $D$ lies between $P$ and $C$ and $PD\cdot PC=\frac13$, we need $PC>\frac1{\sqrt3}=PK$. Now $H$ lies between $K$ and $C$ (as $\angle PCB<90^\circ$), and $PC^2-PK^2=HC^2-HK^2$, so the condition is $HC>HK$, i.e. $MC-2KM>KM$: \[\sqrt{\tfrac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}>\sqrt3\cos\varphi\iff\cos^2\varphi<\tfrac14 .\] These conditions are sufficient. Let $0<\cos\varphi<\frac12$, draw the chord $BC$ through $K$ at angle $\varphi$ to $OP$, with $C$ on the side of $H$, and let $A,D$ be the second intersections of $PB,PC$ with $\omega$. Then $HC>HK>0$, so $H$ is an interior point of the segment $BC$ closer to $C$: $\angle PBC<\angle PCB<90^\circ$. Also $PB>PC>PK=\frac1{\sqrt3}$, so $A,D$ are the intersections nearer to $P$ and $ABCD$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral with $\angle ABC<\angle BCD<90^\circ$. $K$ is on the segment $BC$ and $KB\cdot KC=1-OK^2=\frac23=\frac12OP^2$. Finally $PK^2=\frac13=PA\cdot PB=PD\cdot PC$, so $PK$ is tangent to the circles $ABK$ and $DCK$, whence $\angle PKA=\angle ABC$ and $\angle PKD=\angle BCD$; as $A$ and $D$ lie on opposite sides of the line $PK$, $\angle AKD=\angle ABC+\angle BCD=180^\circ-\angle APD$, and $P,A,K,D$ are concyclic.

So $\cos^2\varphi$ takes exactly the values in $(0,\frac14)$, and $BC=2\sqrt{\frac{2+\cos^2\varphi}3}$ takes exactly the values in $\bigl(2\sqrt{\tfrac23},\sqrt3\bigr)$. By Step 4, $TB+TC=\sqrt3\,BC$.

Answer. \[\boxed{OP=\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boxed{2\sqrt2<TB+TC<3}\ \ (\text{every value of this open interval occurs}).\]

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Initially there are $n$ red cards and $n$ blue cards. One writes one $0$ on every red card and one $1$ on every blue card. An operation is to choose a red card and a blue card, such that the number on the red card is smaller than the number on the blue card, and then replace the two numbers by their arithmetic mean.

Determine the smallest $n$ such that it is possible to execute finitely many operations, so that the sum of the numbers on the red cards is larger than $100$.

Show the answer

$n=106$

Show the solution

Solution (Thresholds, a fair coin and a potential)

We prove that the largest possible sum on the red cards is exactly \[W_n=n\Bigl(1-\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}\Bigr),\] and that $W_n>100$ first happens for $n=106$.

A coin-tossing function. For integers $i,j\ge0$, not both $0$, let $q(i,j)$ be the probability that, tossing a fair coin repeatedly, the $i$-th head comes before the $j$-th tail. Thus $q(0,j)=1$ for $j\ge1$, $q(i,0)=0$ for $i\ge1$, and looking at the first toss, \[q(i,j)=\tfrac12q(i-1,j)+\tfrac12q(i,j-1)\qquad(i,j\ge1).\tag{1}\] Clearly $q(i,j)\ge q(i+1,j)$ and $q(i,j)\le q(i,j+1)$, and $q(i-1,j)>q(i,j-1)$ for $i,j\ge1$ (the second event is contained in the first, and with positive probability exactly one of them occurs).

Step 1: a strategy reaching $\sum_{i=1}^nq(i,n)$. Number the red cards and the blue cards $1,\dots,n$. For $j=1,2,\dots,n$ in turn, let blue card $j$ meet the red cards $1,2,\dots,n$ in this order. We claim that after the operation of red $i$ with blue $j$ both cards show $q(i,j)$. Indeed, before it red $i$ shows $q(i,j-1)$ (its value after meeting blue $j-1$, or $0=q(i,0)$) and blue $j$ shows $q(i-1,j)$ (or $1=q(0,j)$); the operation is allowed because $q(i,j-1)<q(i-1,j)$, and the mean is $q(i,j)$ by (1). At the end red card $i$ shows $q(i,n)$, so the red sum is $\sum_{i=1}^nq(i,n)$.

Step 2: no strategy does better. At any moment, for $0\le t<1$ let \[a(t)=\#\{\text{red cards with number}>t\},\qquad d(t)=\#\{\text{blue cards with number}\le t\}.\] Both are step functions of $t$; $a$ is non-increasing and $d$ is non-decreasing in $t$; initially $a=d=0$. All numbers stay in $[0,1]$ (a mean of two numbers of $[0,1]$ is in $[0,1]$), and a red number $x\in[0,1]$ equals $\int_0^1[x>t]\,dt$; so the red sum equals $\int_0^1a(t)\,dt$. Consider an operation on a red card $r$ and a blue card $b$ $(r<b)$ with mean $m$. For $t\in[r,m)$ the red card passes above $t$: $a(t)$ grows by $1$. For $t\in[m,b)$ the blue card drops to at most $t$: $d(t)$ grows by $1$. For other $t$ nothing changes. Note that for $t\in[r,b)$ we had $a(t)\le n-1$ and $d(t)\le n-1$ before the operation.

For $0\le a,d\le n$ define \[V(a,d)=a+\sum_{i=1}^{n-a}q(i,n-d).\] Then $V(a,d)\ge a$, $V(0,0)=\sum_{i=1}^nq(i,n)$, and for $a,d\le n-1$, with $\pi(a,d)=1-q(n-a,n-d)\ge0$: \[V(a+1,d)-V(a,d)=\pi(a,d),\qquad V(a,d+1)-V(a,d)=-\pi(a,d).\] The first is immediate. For the second, (1) gives $q(i,j-1)-q(i,j)=q(i,j)-q(i-1,j)$, so with $j=n-d$ the difference telescopes: $\sum_{i=1}^{n-a}\bigl(q(i,j-1)-q(i,j)\bigr)=q(n-a,j)-q(0,j)=-\pi(a,d)$. Moreover, by the monotonicity of $q$, the number $\pi(a,d)$ does not increase when $a$ increases and does not decrease when $d$ increases.

Let $\Phi=\int_0^1V(a(t),d(t))\,dt$. Initially $\Phi=V(0,0)$. In the operation above, $\Phi$ changes by \[\int_r^m\pi(a(t),d(t))\,dt-\int_m^b\pi(a(t),d(t))\,dt=\int_0^{\frac{b-r}2}\Bigl(\pi\bigl(a(r+\tau),d(r+\tau)\bigr)-\pi\bigl(a(m+\tau),d(m+\tau)\bigr)\Bigr)d\tau\ \le0,\] (values before the operation), because $a(m+\tau)\le a(r+\tau)$ and $d(m+\tau)\ge d(r+\tau)$. So $\Phi$ never increases, and at every moment \[\text{red sum}=\int_0^1a(t)\,dt\le\int_0^1V(a(t),d(t))\,dt=\Phi\le V(0,0)=\sum_{i=1}^nq(i,n).\]

Step 3: the closed form. Toss a fair coin until there are $n$ heads or $n$ tails. The number of heads at that moment is $\sum_{i=1}^n[\text{the }i\text{-th head comes before the }n\text{-th tail}]$, so its expectation is $W_n:=\sum_{i=1}^nq(i,n)$. By symmetry the expected number of tails is the same; if $\mu$ is the smaller of the two numbers, the larger is $n$, so $W_n=\frac12(n+E\mu)$. Now continue tossing until $2n$ tosses are made, and let $Z$ be the final value of $|\#\text{heads}-\#\text{tails}|$. When we stopped, the difference was $n-\mu$ and exactly $2n-(n+\mu)=n-\mu$ tosses remained, so the leader cannot be overtaken and the expected final difference is still $n-\mu$. Hence $EZ=n-E\mu$ and \[W_n=n-\tfrac12EZ.\] Finally, with $k$ the number of heads in $2n$ tosses, using $k\binom{2n}k=2n\binom{2n-1}{k-1}$, $\sum_{j\ge n}\binom{2n-1}j=2^{2n-2}$ and $\sum_{k>n}\binom{2n}k=\frac12\bigl(4^n-\binom{2n}n\bigr)$: \[EZ=\frac2{4^n}\sum_{k>n}(2k-2n)\binom{2n}k=\frac4{4^n}\Bigl(2n\cdot4^{n-1}-\frac n2\Bigl(4^n-\binom{2n}n\Bigr)\Bigr)=\frac{2n\binom{2n}n}{4^n}.\] So the maximum red sum is $W_n=n\bigl(1-c_n\bigr)$ with $c_n=\binom{2n}n/4^n=\prod_{k=1}^n\frac{2k-1}{2k}$.

Step 4: the smallest $n$. We use $\dfrac1{4n}\le c_n^2\le\dfrac1{3n+1}$. Both follow by multiplying, for $k=1,\dots,n$, the inequalities \[\frac{k-1}{k}\le\Bigl(\frac{2k-1}{2k}\Bigr)^2\ (k\ge2),\qquad\Bigl(\frac{2k-1}{2k}\Bigr)^2\le\frac{3k-2}{3k+1}\ (k\ge1),\] which hold because the middle term minus the left side is $\frac1{4k^2}>0$, and because after multiplying by $4k^2(3k+1)$ the right side minus the middle term is $k-1\ge0$; the products telescope to $\frac14\cdot\frac1n$ and $\frac1{3n+1}$.

  • For $n\le105$: $W_n\le n-\frac{\sqrt n}2\le105-\frac{\sqrt{105}}2<105-5=100$ (the function $n-\frac{\sqrt n}2$ is increasing). So the red sum never exceeds $100$.

  • For $n=106$: $W_{106}\ge106\bigl(1-\frac1{\sqrt{319}}\bigr)>106-\frac{106}{17.8}>106-5.96>100$, and Step 1 gives a sequence of operations with red sum $W_{106}\approx100.198$.

The smallest $n$ is $\boxed{106}$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

There are $30$ colors of cards, with $70$ cards of each color. Initially, any $70$ cards are chosen and arranged from top to bottom into a stack. One operation is defined as follows: from the bottom $20$ cards, choose a card $X$ whose color does not appear among the top $50$ cards; then, from the top $50$ cards, choose a card $Y$ whose color appears at least twice among the top $50$ cards; extract $X$ and insert it back immediately above $Y$. Repeat the above operations until no such $X$ can be selected.

  • Prove that the number of operations that can be performed is finite;

  • For all possible initial situations and operation methods, find the maximum possible number of operations.

Show the answer

finite; the maximum number of operations is $390$

Show the solution

Solution (The tail of single cards)

Number the positions $1,\dots,70$ from top to bottom; the top part is positions $1$–$50$, the bottom part positions $51$–$70$. Since there are $70$ cards of each colour, every sequence of $70$ colours can occur as the initial stack.

Notation. Let $C\le30$ be the number of colours in the whole stack (it never changes) and $c$ the number of colours in the top part. A card of the top part is single if no other card of the top part has its colour, and repeated otherwise. As $50>30$, there is always a repeated card. Let $q$ be the position of the lowest repeated card of the top part and $s=50-q$; the $s$ cards below it in the top part (the tail) are single. Since at least one colour is repeated, \[s\le c-1 .\tag{1}\]

One operation. $X$ is inserted immediately above a repeated card $Y$, whose position $y$ satisfies $y\le q$. The cards in positions $y,\dots,50$ move down by one place; the card $Z$ of position $50$ becomes the top card of the bottom part; $X$ is a single card of the new top part.

  • If $s\ge1$, then $Z$ is single: its colour leaves the top part while the colour of $X$ enters, so $c$ is unchanged. As a single colour leaves and a new colour enters once, every other colour keeps its multiplicity in the top part, so the repeated cards are the same as before (with positions at or below $y$ shifted by one). The lowest repeated card is now at position $q+1$, followed by the old tail without $Z$: $s$ decreases by exactly $1$.

  • If $s=0$, then $Z$ is repeated, its colour stays in the top part and $c$ increases by $1$.

When the process stops. If $c<C$, some colour of the stack is missing in the top part; a card $X$ of this colour is in the bottom part, and a repeated $Y$ exists, so an operation is possible. If $c=C$, no $X$ exists. So the process stops exactly when $c=C$.

(a) Finiteness. $c$ never decreases and is at most $30$; while $c$ is constant, $s$ decreases by $1$ in each operation, and $s\le49$. So there are at most $30\cdot50$ operations.

(b) An upper bound. Let $c_0$ and $s_0$ be the initial values, and for $c_0<c'\le C$ let $s(c')$ be the tail length just after $c$ has become $c'$. If $c_0=C$, no operation is possible at all. Let $c_0<C$. While $c=c'<C$, exactly $s(c')$ operations of the first kind are made (with $s(c_0)=s_0$), followed by one operation of the second kind. Hence the total number of operations is \[s_0+(C-c_0)+\sum_{c'=c_0+1}^{C-1}s(c')\ \le\ (c_0-1)+(C-c_0)+\sum_{c'=c_0+1}^{C-1}(c'-1)=(C-1)+\sum_{j=c_0}^{C-2}j ,\] by (1). Besides $C\le30$ we have $C\le c_0+20$, because each colour missing in the top part occupies at least one of the $20$ bottom cards.

  • If $c_0\le10$, then $C\le c_0+20$ and the bound is at most $(c_0+19)+\sum_{j=c_0}^{c_0+18}j=20c_0+190\le390$.

  • If $c_0\ge10$, then $C\le30$ and the bound is at most $29+\sum_{j=10}^{28}j=29+361=390$.

So at most $390$ operations are possible.

(c) $390$ operations are possible. Use colours $0,1,\dots,29$. Initially the top part is $41$ cards of colour $0$ followed by one card of each of the colours $1,\dots,9$, and the bottom part has one card of each of the colours $10,\dots,29$. Thus $c_0=10$, $s_0=9$, $C=30$. In every operation choose any admissible $X$ and let $Y$ be the lowest card of colour $0$ in the top part.

We claim that just after $c$ becomes $c'$ $(11\le c'\le30)$ the top part is $51-c'$ cards of colour $0$ followed by $c'-1$ single cards, so $s(c')=c'-1$. Indeed, starting from “$u$ cards of colour $0$, then $s$ single cards” $(u+s=50)$, each of the next $s$ operations inserts a new single card just above the lowest $0$ and removes the last card of the tail; after them the top part is “$u-1$ zeros, $s$ single cards, one $0$”. The next operation inserts $X$ above this last $0$, which leaves the top part: now it is “$u-1$ zeros, then $s+1$ single cards”, and $c$ has grown by $1$. Starting with $(u,s)=(41,9)$ this proves the claim; colour $0$ always keeps at least $21$ cards in the top part, so $Y$ is always a repeated card.

The number of operations is therefore $s_0+(C-c_0)+\sum_{c'=11}^{29}(c'-1)=9+20+\sum_{j=10}^{28}j=9+20+361=390$.

Answer. The number of operations is always finite, and its maximum is $\boxed{390}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

Determine all real numbers $\lambda$ such that there exists $N>0$ satisfying the following property:

For any integer $n\geq N$ and $n$ non-negative reals $x_1,\cdots,x_n$ with sum $1$, the following inequality holds: \[\Bigl(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_ix_j\Bigr)^2\leq\lambda\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}x_ix_jx_k+\frac1n\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_i-x_j)^2.\]

Show the answer

all $\lambda>\dfrac32$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Power sums and one variable)

Answer: exactly the numbers $\lambda>\frac32$.

Rewriting with power sums. Let $S_k=\sum x_i^k$, so $S_1=1$; write $s=S_2$. Then \[\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j=\frac{1-s}2,\qquad e_3:=\sum_{i<j<k}x_ix_jx_k=\frac{1-3s+2S_3}6,\qquad\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2=nS_2-S_1^2=ns-1 ,\] and the inequality becomes \[\frac{(1-s)^2}4\le\lambda\,e_3+s-\frac1n .\tag{1}\] Two facts: $\frac1n\le s\le1$ (because $ns-1=\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2\ge0$ and $x_i^2\le x_i$), and \[S_3\ge s^2,\qquad\text{since}\qquad\sum x_i(x_i-s)^2=S_3-2s\cdot s+s^2\cdot1=S_3-s^2\ge0 .\] Consequently \[e_3\ \ge\ \frac{1-3s+2s^2}6=\frac{(1-s)(1-2s)}6 ,\tag{2}\] with equality exactly when all positive $x_i$ are equal (each positive $x_i$ must equal $s$).

$\lambda\le\frac32$ fails for every $n\ge3$. Take $x_1=\dots=x_n=\frac1n$. Then $s=\frac1n$, $e_3=\binom n3\frac1{n^3}=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}$, and (1) reads $\frac{(n-1)^2}{4n^2}\le\lambda\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}$, i.e. \[\lambda\ \ge\ \frac32\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-2}\ >\ \frac32 .\] So for $\lambda\le\frac32$ the inequality is false for all $n\ge3$, and no $N$ exists.

$\lambda>\frac32$ works. Since $e_3\ge0$, the right side of (1) increases with $\lambda$; so it suffices to treat $\lambda=\frac32+\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon\le1$. By (2), \[\lambda e_3+s-\frac1n-\frac{(1-s)^2}4\ \ge\ \frac{(1-s)(1-2s)-(1-s)^2}4+s-\frac1n+\varepsilon\,\frac{(1-s)(1-2s)}6=h(s),\] where \[h(s)=\frac{s(3+s)}4-\frac1n+\varepsilon\,\frac{1-3s+2s^2}6 .\] For $s\ge0$, $h'(s)=\frac34+\frac s2+\frac{\varepsilon(4s-3)}6\ge\frac34-\frac\varepsilon2>0$, so $h$ is increasing on $[\frac1n,1]$ and \[h(s)\ge h\Bigl(\frac1n\Bigr)=\frac{3n+1}{4n^2}-\frac1n+\varepsilon\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}=\frac{(n-1)\bigl(2\varepsilon(n-2)-3\bigr)}{12n^2}\ \ge0\qquad\text{if }n\ge2+\frac3{2\varepsilon}.\] Hence (1) holds for all $n\ge N:=2+\frac3{2\varepsilon}$.

Remark (the best constant for a fixed $n\ge3$). The same computation with $\varepsilon=\frac3{2(n-2)}\le\frac32$ (then still $h'\ge0$ and $h(\frac1n)=0$) shows that for a fixed $n$ the inequality holds exactly for $\lambda\ge\frac{3(n-1)}{2(n-2)}$; these numbers decrease to $\frac32$ but never reach it.

Conclusion. The required numbers are $\boxed{\lambda>\dfrac32}$.

Solution 2 (Cauchy-Schwarz and a factorisation)

Answer: exactly the numbers $\lambda>\frac32$.

Write $e_2=\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j$, $e_3=\sum_{i<j<k}x_ix_jx_k$ and $S_k=\sum_ix_i^k$; so $S_1=1$, and put $s=S_2$. Expanding $S_1^2$ and $S_1^3$, \[e_2=\frac{1-s}2,\qquad e_3=\frac{1-3s+2S_3}6,\qquad\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2=(n-1)S_2-2e_2=ns-1 .\] So the inequality is \[\frac{(1-s)^2}4\le\lambda\,e_3+s-\frac1n .\tag{1}\] Here $s\ge\frac1n$, because $ns-1$ is a sum of squares.

The lemma $S_3\ge s^2$, by Cauchy-Schwarz. Writing $x_i^2=x_i^{1/2}\cdot x_i^{3/2}$, \[s^2=\Bigl(\sum_ix_i^{1/2}x_i^{3/2}\Bigr)^2\le\Bigl(\sum_ix_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_ix_i^3\Bigr)=S_3 .\] Hence $e_3\ge\frac{1-3s+2s^2}6=\frac{(1-s)(1-2s)}6$, with equality exactly when all positive $x_i$ are equal (proportional vectors $(x_i^{1/2})$ and $(x_i^{3/2})$).

$\lambda\le\frac32$ fails. For $n\ge3$ and $x_1=\dots=x_n=\frac1n$ we have $s=\frac1n$ and $e_3=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}$, and (1) becomes $\frac{(n-1)^2}{4n^2}\le\lambda\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}$, i.e. $\lambda\ge\frac32\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-2}>\frac32$. So for $\lambda\le\frac32$, (1) fails for every $n\ge3$ and no $N$ exists.

$\lambda>\frac32$ works. As $e_3\ge0$, it is enough to treat $\lambda=\frac32+\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon\le1$. By the lemma, the right side of (1) minus the left side is at least \[h(s)=\frac32\cdot\frac{(1-s)(1-2s)}6+s-\frac1n-\frac{(1-s)^2}4+\varepsilon\,\frac{1-3s+2s^2}6=\frac{3s+s^2}4-\frac1n+\varepsilon\,\frac{1-3s+2s^2}6 .\] Let $t=\frac1n\le s$. Factoring the differences of the polynomial parts, \[h(s)-h(t)=(s-t)\Bigl(\frac{3+s+t}4+\varepsilon\,\frac{2(s+t)-3}6\Bigr)\ge(s-t)\Bigl(\frac34-\frac\varepsilon2\Bigr)\ge0,\] because $s+t\ge0$. Finally \[h(t)=\frac{3n+1}{4n^2}-\frac1n+\varepsilon\,\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}=\frac{(n-1)\bigl(2\varepsilon(n-2)-3\bigr)}{12n^2}\ge0\qquad\text{for }n\ge2+\frac3{2\varepsilon}.\] So (1) holds for all $n\ge N:=2+\frac3{2\varepsilon}$, and the required numbers are $\boxed{\lambda>\dfrac32}$.

Solution 3 (Pairwise averaging for a fixed n)

Answer: exactly the numbers $\lambda>\frac32$.

Write $e_2=\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j$, $e_3=\sum_{i<j<k}x_ix_jx_k$ and $S_2=\sum_ix_i^2$. Since $\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2=(n-1)S_2-2e_2$ and $S_2+2e_2=1$, the last term of the inequality is $\frac1n\bigl(nS_2-1\bigr)=S_2-\frac1n$.

The uniform vector. For $n\ge3$ and $x_1=\dots=x_n=\frac1n$: $e_2=\binom n2\frac1{n^2}=\frac{n-1}{2n}$, $e_3=\binom n3\frac1{n^3}=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6n^2}>0$, and $S_2-\frac1n=0$. So the inequality holds exactly when \[\lambda\ \ge\ \frac{e_2^2}{e_3}=\frac{(n-1)^2}{4n^2}\cdot\frac{6n^2}{(n-1)(n-2)}=L_n,\qquad L_n=\frac{3(n-1)}{2(n-2)}=\frac32+\frac3{2(n-2)} .\] Necessity. $L_n>\frac32$ for every $n\ge3$. So if $\lambda\le\frac32$, the inequality fails at the uniform vector for every $n\ge3$, and no $N$ exists.

Claim: for $n\ge5$ the inequality holds with $\lambda=L_n$. Note $L_n\le2$ for $n\ge5$ (this is $3n-3\le4n-8$). Consider \[\Phi(x_1,\dots,x_n)=e_2^2-L_ne_3-S_2+\frac1n\] on the set $\Delta$ of vectors with non-negative entries and sum $1$; we must show $\Phi\le0$ on $\Delta$. We already know that $\Phi=0$ at the uniform vector.

Averaging two entries. Fix all entries except $x_a,x_b$, and fix their sum $x_a+x_b=\mu$. Let $S'$ be the sum of the other $n-2$ entries (so $S'\le1$), and $e_2',e_3'$ their elementary symmetric sums. With $t=x_ax_b$, \[e_2=C+t,\qquad e_3=D+S't,\qquad S_2=E-2t,\qquad\text{where}\quad C=\mu S'+e_2',\ D=\mu e_2'+e_3',\ E=\mu^2+\sum_{i\ne a,b}x_i^2\] do not depend on $t$. Hence, if $t$ changes from $t_1$ to $t_2$, \[\Phi(t_2)-\Phi(t_1)=(t_2-t_1)\bigl(2C+t_1+t_2\bigr)-L_nS'(t_2-t_1)+2(t_2-t_1)=(t_2-t_1)\bigl(2C+t_1+t_2+2-L_nS'\bigr).\] Here $C\ge0$ and $2-L_nS'\ge2-2\cdot1=0$. Replacing $x_a,x_b$ by their mean $\frac\mu2$ changes $t$ from $t_1=x_ax_b$ to $t_2=\frac{\mu^2}4$, and $t_2-t_1=\frac{(x_a-x_b)^2}4$. If $x_a\ne x_b$, then $t_2>t_1\ge0$, so the bracket is at least $t_1+t_2>0$ and $\Phi$ strictly increases.

The maximum. $\Phi$ is a continuous function on the closed bounded set $\Delta$, so it attains a maximum at some vector $x^*$. If $x^*$ had two different entries, averaging them would give a vector of $\Delta$ with a larger value of $\Phi$. So all entries of $x^*$ are equal, $x^*$ is the uniform vector, and $\max_\Delta\Phi=0$. This proves the claim.

Sufficiency. Let $\lambda>\frac32$. Since $L_n=\frac32+\frac3{2(n-2)}$ decreases to $\frac32$, there is $N\ge5$ with $L_n\le\lambda$ for all $n\ge N$. For such $n$, as $e_3\ge0$, \[e_2^2\le L_ne_3+S_2-\frac1n\le\lambda e_3+S_2-\frac1n ,\] which is the required inequality. Hence the answer is $\boxed{\lambda>\dfrac32}$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Let $n\geq 3$ be an odd integer such that $\gcd(p-1,n)=1$ for every prime divisor $p$ of $n$. Find the number of $(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}_n^3$ in terms of $n$ such that $\gcd(a,b,c,n)=1$ and there is a permutation $(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$ of $\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ satisfying that $n\mid ax_{k+2}+bx_{k+1}+cx_k$ for $1\leq k\leq n$, where indices are taken modulo $n$.

Show the answer

$\varphi(n)\Bigl(\dfrac{n}{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^{2}$ if $9\nmid n$, and $\dfrac23\,\varphi(n)\Bigl(\dfrac{n}{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^{2}$ if $9\mid n$ (here $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ is the product of the distinct primes dividing $n$)

Show the solution

Solution (The recurrence modulo prime powers)

Let $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ be the product of the distinct primes dividing $n$. We prove that the number of triples is \[\varphi(n)\Bigl(\frac n{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^2\ \ \text{if }9\nmid n,\qquad\frac23\,\varphi(n)\Bigl(\frac n{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^2\ \ \text{if }9\mid n .\] We extend $(x_k)$ to all integers $k$ with period $n$; then $ax_{k+2}+bx_{k+1}+cx_k\equiv0\pmod n$ for all $k$, and in every block of $n$ consecutive indices each residue modulo $n$ occurs once. A triple with this property is called good.

Step 1: necessary conditions modulo a prime $p\mid n$. Let $(a,b,c)$ be good. Reduce modulo $p$: $y_k=x_k\bmod p$ has period $n$, takes every residue modulo $p$ (each exactly $n/p$ times per period), and $ay_{k+2}+by_{k+1}+cy_k=0$ in $\mathbb F_p$, where $a,b,c$ are not all $0$ in $\mathbb F_p$.

$a\ne0$ and $c\ne0$ in $\mathbb F_p$. If $a=0$ and $b\ne0$, then $y_{k+1}=-\frac cby_k$; some $y_k$ is $0$, hence all later terms are $0$, and by periodicity all terms are $0$: impossible. If $a=b=0$, then $c\ne0$ and $y_k=0$ for all $k$: impossible. The case $c=0$ is symmetric (read the sequence backwards).

The polynomial $f(t)=at^2+bt+c$ is $a(t-1)^2$ in $\mathbb F_p[t]$. Its roots are non-zero (as $c\ne0$) and lie in $\mathbb F_p$, or, if $f$ is irreducible, in the field $\mathbb F_p[t]/(f)\cong\mathbb F_{p^2}$, which contains one root $\rho$ and hence the other root $-\frac ba-\rho$.

If the roots $\rho\ne\sigma$ are distinct, choose $A,B\in\mathbb F_{p^2}$ with $A+B=y_0$, $A\rho+B\sigma=y_1$ (the determinant is $\sigma-\rho\ne0$). The sequence $A\rho^k+B\sigma^k$ satisfies the recurrence and agrees with $y_k$ at $k=0,1$; going forwards, and backwards since $c\ne0$, $y_k=A\rho^k+B\sigma^k$ for all $k$. The sequence $y_{k+n}-y_k=A(\rho^n-1)\rho^k+B(\sigma^n-1)\sigma^k$ is zero, so by the same determinant at $k=0,1$ we get $A(\rho^n-1)=B(\sigma^n-1)=0$. Write $n=p^em$ with $p\nmid m$. In characteristic $p$, $\rho^n=1$ means $(\rho^m-1)^{p^e}=0$, i.e. $\rho^m=1$. So in all cases $y_{k+m}=y_k$. Then every residue occurs in a period of length $n$ a number of times divisible by $n/m=p^e$; but it occurs $n/p$ times, and $p^e\nmid n/p$. Contradiction.

So $f$ has a double root $\rho=-\frac b{2a}\in\mathbb F_p^{*}$ ($p$ is odd) and $y_k=(A+Bk)\rho^k$ with $A,B\in\mathbb F_p$, not both zero: both $\rho^k$ and $k\rho^k$ satisfy the recurrence (as $f(\rho)=f'(\rho)=0$), and $A,B$ can be matched to $y_0,y_1$ because $\rho\ne0$. Since $p\mid n$, $y_{k+n}=(A+Bk)\rho^{k+n}=\rho^ny_k$, so $\rho^n=1$. Also $\rho^{p-1}=1$, and $\gcd(n,p-1)=1$ by hypothesis; hence $\rho=1$.

Therefore: for every prime $p\mid n$, $a$ is invertible modulo $p$ and $b\equiv-2a$, $c\equiv a\pmod p$. In particular $a$ is invertible modulo $n$. Multiplying a triple by a unit modulo $n$ does not change whether it is good, so the number of good triples is $\varphi(n)$ times the number of good triples with $a=1$. From now on $a=1$, and $b,c$ are integers with \[b+2=p\beta_p,\qquad1+b+c=p\gamma_p\qquad\text{for every prime }p\mid n\tag{1}\] $(\beta_p,\gamma_p$ integers$)$.

Step 2: an operator identity. Let $E$ be the shift of sequences, $(Ex)_k=x_{k+1}$, and $D=E-1$. Then $E^2+bE+c=D^2+(b+2)D+(1+b+c)$. Fix a prime $p\mid n$ and work in the ring \[\mathcal R=\mathbb Z[D]/(D^2+p\beta D+p\gamma),\qquad\beta=\beta_p,\ \gamma=\gamma_p,\] whose elements are uniquely $u+vD$ with integers $u,v$. In $\mathcal R$ we have $D^2=-p(\beta D+\gamma)\in p\mathcal R$.

Lemma. For $j\ge0$ write $(1+D)^{p^j}-1=p^j(\alpha_j+\delta_jD)$ in $\mathcal R$ (we show that this is possible, with integers $\alpha_j,\delta_j$). Then $p\mid\alpha_j$ for all $j$, and

  • if $p\ge5$: $\delta_j\equiv1\pmod p$ for all $j$;

  • if $p=3$: $\delta_0=1$ and $\delta_j\equiv1-\gamma\pmod3$ for all $j\ge1$.

Proof. For $j=0$: $\alpha_0=0$, $\delta_0=1$. Let $F_j=(1+D)^{p^j}-1=p^j(\alpha_j+\delta_jD)$ with $p\mid\alpha_j$. Then \[F_{j+1}=(1+F_j)^p-1=pF_j+\sum_{i=2}^{p-1}\binom piF_j^i+F_j^p .\] Since $p\mid\alpha_j$ and $D^2\in p\mathcal R$, we have $(\alpha_j+\delta_jD)^2\in p\mathcal R$, so $F_j^2\in p^{2j+1}\mathcal R$. The middle terms have $p\mid\binom pi$ and lie in $p^{2j+2}\mathcal R\subseteq p^{j+2}\mathcal R$. The last term: $(\alpha_j+\delta_jD)^p\in p^{(p-1)/2}\mathcal R$, so $F_j^p\in p^{pj+(p-1)/2}\mathcal R$, and $pj+\frac{p-1}2\ge j+2$ if $p\ge5$, or if $p=3$ and $j\ge1$. In these cases $F_{j+1}\equiv pF_j\pmod{p^{j+2}\mathcal R}$, i.e. $F_{j+1}=p^{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}+\delta_{j+1}D)$ with $\alpha_{j+1}\equiv\alpha_j$, $\delta_{j+1}\equiv\delta_j\pmod p$. It remains to compute $F_1$ for $p=3$: $D^2=-3(\beta D+\gamma)$ and $D^3=-3(\beta D^2+\gamma D)\equiv-3\gamma D\pmod{9\mathcal R}$, so \[F_1=3D+3D^2+D^3\equiv3(1-\gamma)D\pmod{9\mathcal R},\] i.e. $\alpha_1\equiv0$, $\delta_1\equiv1-\gamma\pmod3$. $\square$

If an integer sequence satisfies $x_{k+2}+bx_{k+1}+cx_k=0$ exactly, then $(D^2+p\beta D+p\gamma)x=0$, so every identity of $\mathcal R$ may be applied to $x$: for all $k$ and $j$, \[x_{k+p^j}-x_k=p^j\bigl(\alpha_jx_k+\delta_j(x_{k+1}-x_k)\bigr).\tag{2}\] If the recurrence holds only modulo $9$, the congruence $F_1\equiv3(1-\gamma)D$ still gives $x_{k+3}-x_k\equiv3(1-\gamma)(x_{k+1}-x_k)\pmod9$.

Step 3: which pairs $(b,c)$ are good. Claim: $(1,b,c)$ is good if and only if (1) holds and, in case $9\mid n$, $\gamma_3\not\equiv1\pmod3$, that is, $1+b+c\not\equiv3\pmod9$.

Necessity. (1) was proved in Step 1. Let $9\mid n$ (so $1+b+c$ is well defined modulo $9$, as $b,c$ are residues modulo $n$) and $\gamma_3\equiv1\pmod 3$. Then every solution satisfies $x_{k+3}\equiv x_k\pmod9$, so it takes at most three values modulo $9$ and is not a permutation modulo $n$.

Sufficiency. Define integers $x_1=0$, $x_2=1$, $x_{k+2}=-bx_{k+1}-cx_k$ $(k\ge1)$. Let $p^e$ be the exact power of a prime $p$ dividing $n$. Modulo $p$ the recurrence is $x_{k+2}-2x_{k+1}+x_k\equiv0$, so $x_{k+1}-x_k\equiv x_2-x_1=1\pmod p$ for all $k$. By the lemma (and the assumption $\gamma_3\not\equiv1$ when $p=3$, $e\ge2$), for $0\le j\le e-1$ the number $\delta_j$ is not divisible by $p$, and (2) gives \[x_{k+p^j}-x_k\equiv p^j\delta_j\not\equiv0\pmod{p^{j+1}}\quad(0\le j\le e-1),\qquad x_{k+p^e}\equiv x_k\pmod{p^e}\tag{3}\] for all $k\ge1$. We claim that $x_k\equiv x_l\pmod{p^e}$ implies $k\equiv l\pmod{p^e}$. Otherwise write $l-k=p^jw$ with $p\nmid w$, $w>0$ and $j<e$; adding (3) along $k,k+p^j,\dots,k+wp^j$ gives $x_l-x_k\equiv wp^j\delta_j\not\equiv0\pmod{p^{j+1}}$, a contradiction.

Doing this for all primes $p\mid n$: $x_{k+n}\equiv x_k\pmod n$, and $x_k\equiv x_l\pmod n$ implies $k\equiv l\pmod n$. So $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are pairwise distinct modulo $n$ (replace the residue $0$ by $n$ to get a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$), and by periodicity the recurrence holds modulo $n$ for all indices taken modulo $n$. So $(1,b,c)$ is good.

Step 4: counting. By the Chinese remainder theorem we count the pairs $(b,c)$ modulo each prime power $p^e\,\|\,n$. Condition (1) fixes $(b,c)$ modulo $p$: there are $p^{2(e-1)}$ pairs modulo $p^e$. If $p=3$ and $e\ge2$, write $b=1+3\beta'$, $c=1+3\gamma'$; then $1+b+c=3+3(\beta'+\gamma')\equiv3\pmod 9$ exactly when $3\mid\beta'+\gamma'$, which excludes one third of the pairs. Since $\prod_{p^e\|n}p^{2(e-1)}=\bigl(n/\operatorname{rad}(n)\bigr)^2$, the number of good triples is \[\boxed{\varphi(n)\Bigl(\frac n{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^2\ \ (9\nmid n),\qquad\frac23\,\varphi(n)\Bigl(\frac n{\operatorname{rad}(n)}\Bigr)^2\ \ (9\mid n)}\] For example the count is $p-1$ for a prime $n=p$, $\varphi(n)$ for squarefree $n$, $36$ for $n=9$ and $500$ for $n=25$.
