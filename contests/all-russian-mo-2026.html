Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Initially, there are $75$ candies on a plate. Petya and Vasya take turns removing any positive number of candies from the plate until none remain. Petya goes first. For what smallest $k$ can Petya play in such a way that he guarantees that at the end of the game the numbers of candies taken by the boys differ by at most $k$?

(The boys do not eat the candies during the game.)

Show the answer

$k=25$

Show the solution

Solution (A copying strategy for Petya, two threats for Vasya)

Let $P$ and $V$ be the numbers of candies taken by Petya and Vasya at the end, so $P+V=75$ and the quantity in question is $|P-V|$.

Petya can guarantee $|P-V|\le 25$. Petya first takes $25$ candies; $50$ remain. From now on he keeps the following property: before each move of Vasya the number $r$ of candies on the plate is even, and so far Petya has taken exactly $25$ candies more than Vasya. Suppose this holds and Vasya takes $y$ candies, $1\le y\le r$.

  • If $2y\le r$, Petya also takes $y$ candies. The difference is $25$ again and $r-2y$ candies (an even number) remain. If $r-2y=0$ the game is over with $P-V=25$.

  • If $2y>r$, Petya takes all the remaining $r-y$ candies (if $y=r$ the game is already over). The game ends with \[P-V=25-y+(r-y)=25+r-2y.\] Here $r-2y<0$ gives $P-V<25$, and $y\le r\le 50$ gives $P-V\ge 25-r\ge-25$.

The number of candies decreases, so the game ends, and in every case $|P-V|\le25$.

Vasya can force $|P-V|\ge 25$. Let Petya take $x$ candies on his first move.

  • If $x\le 25$, Vasya takes all the other $75-x$ candies. Then $V-P=75-2x\ge 25$.

  • If $x\ge 26$, Vasya takes exactly one candy on each of his moves until the game ends. Suppose Vasya makes $v$ moves in total, so $V=v$. Between two consecutive moves of Vasya, Petya makes a move and takes at least one candy, so after his first move Petya takes at least $v-1$ more candies. Hence \[75=P+V\ge x+(v-1)+v\ge 25+2v,\] that is, $v\le 25$, and $P-V=75-2v\ge 25$.

So Petya cannot guarantee a difference smaller than $25$, and he can guarantee $25$. The answer is $k=\boxed{25}$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Call a natural number $n$ strange if there exist pairwise distinct natural numbers $a,b,c,d,e>1$ such that \[n=a^{a^a}=b^{b^c}=d^{e^e}.\] Are there finitely or infinitely many strange numbers?

(Recall that $x^{y^z}$ means $x$ raised to the power $y^z$.)

Show the answer

infinitely many

Show the solution

Solution (Towers of powers of two)

There are infinitely many strange numbers. We look for $a,b,d,e$ among numbers of the form $2^{2^s}$, for which the tower is easy to compute: for integers $s,w\ge0$ and $z\ge1$, \[\bigl(2^{2^s}\bigr)^{(2^w)^z}=2^{\,2^s\cdot 2^{wz}}=2^{\,2^{\,s+wz}}.\tag{1}\] Fix an integer $u\ge 2$ and put \[a=2^{2^u},\qquad b=2,\qquad c=u+2^{\,u+2^u},\qquad e=4,\qquad d=2^{2^{\,c-8}}.\] The three towers are equal. First, $a^a=\bigl(2^{2^u}\bigr)^{2^{2^u}}=2^{\,2^u\cdot 2^{2^u}}=2^{\,2^{\,u+2^u}}$, hence \[a^{a^a}=\bigl(2^{2^u}\bigr)^{2^{2^{u+2^u}}}=2^{\,2^u\cdot 2^{2^{u+2^u}}}=2^{\,2^{\,u+2^{u+2^u}}}=2^{2^c}.\] Next, $b^{b^c}=2^{2^c}$. Finally $e^e=4^4=2^8$, and by (1) with $s=c-8$, $w=8$, $z=1$, \[d^{e^e}=\bigl(2^{2^{c-8}}\bigr)^{2^8}=2^{\,2^{c-8}\cdot 2^8}=2^{2^c}.\] So $n_u=2^{2^c}=a^{a^a}=b^{b^c}=d^{e^e}$.

The five numbers are distinct and greater than $1$. Since $u\ge2$ we have $a=2^{2^u}\ge16$, so $b=2<e=4<a$. Next, $c>2^{\,u+2^u}>2^{2^u}=a$. Finally $c\ge 2+2^{6}=66$, so $2^{c-8}>c$ (because $2^{58}>66$ and the left side doubles when $c$ grows by $1$), and therefore $d=2^{2^{c-8}}>2^{c}>c$. Thus \[b<e<a<c<d .\] Infinitely many values. The exponent $c=u+2^{u+2^u}$ increases with $u$, so the numbers $n_u=2^{2^c}$, $u=2,3,4,\dots$, are pairwise distinct strange numbers. (The smallest one, for $u=2$, is $n_2=16^{16^{16}}=2^{2^{66}}=\bigl(2^{2^{58}}\bigr)^{4^4}$.)

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

$\triangle ABC$ is a scalene triangle with $\angle A=60^\circ$. Points $X,Y$ lie on segment $BC$ such that $2BX=AB$ and $2CY=AC$. Prove that there exists a circle through $X,Y$ that is tangent to the incircle and some excircle of $\triangle ABC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle on a diameter cut from the bisector)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, let $\omega$ be the incircle (centre $I$, radius $r$) and $\omega_a$ the excircle opposite $A$ (centre $I_a$, radius $r_a$). The points $A,I,I_a$ lie on the bisector of angle $A$.

Two facts from $\angle A=60^\circ$. (i) The distance from $I$ to $AB$ is $r$ and $\angle IAB=30^\circ$, so $AI=2r$; in the same way $AI_a=2r_a$. (ii) By the law of cosines $a^2=b^2+c^2-bc$, hence \[4a^2-(b+c)^2=3(b-c)^2>0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad b+c<2a,\] because the triangle is scalene.

The circle. On the ray $AI$ take the points $P$ and $Q$ with $AP=3r$ and $AQ=r_a$. Then $IP=r$, so $P\in\omega$, and $Q$ is the midpoint of $AI_a$, so $I_aQ=r_a$ and $Q\in\omega_a$. With $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ we have $r=(s-a)\tan30^\circ$ and $r_a=s\tan 30^\circ$, so \[r_a-3r=\frac{3a-2s}{\sqrt3}=\frac{2a-b-c}{\sqrt3}>0,\] and $P\ne Q$. Let $\gamma$ be the circle with diameter $PQ$. Its centre lies on the line $II_a$, and $P$ is a common point of $\gamma$ and $\omega$ lying on their line of centres; so the two circles have the same tangent at $P$ (the perpendicular to $AI$), i.e. $\gamma$ touches $\omega$ at $P$. In the same way $\gamma$ touches $\omega_a$ at $Q$. It remains to prove that $X$ and $Y$ lie on $\gamma$, i.e. that $\angle PYQ=90^\circ=\angle PXQ$.

$PY\parallel CI$. Let the bisector $AI$ meet $BC$ at $L$. By the bisector theorem $LC=\frac{ab}{b+c}$, and in triangle $ALC$ the bisector $CI$ gives $\frac{AI}{IL}=\frac{AC}{LC}=\frac{b+c}{a}$, so $IL=\frac{2ra}{b+c}$. Since $b+c<2a$, we get $IP=r<IL$ and $CY=\frac b2<LC$: the point $P$ lies on the segment $IL$ and $Y$ on the segment $CL$, and \[\frac{LP}{LI}=1-\frac{r}{IL}=1-\frac{b+c}{2a},\qquad \frac{LY}{LC}=1-\frac{b/2}{ab/(b+c)}=1-\frac{b+c}{2a}.\] The ratios are equal, so $PY\parallel IC$ (converse of Thales' theorem in triangle $LIC$).

$QY\perp CI$. Let $B_1$ be the midpoint of $AC$. Then $CB_1=\frac b2=CY$, so triangle $CB_1Y$ is isosceles and its bisector $CI$ is perpendicular to $B_1Y$. On the other hand $B_1Q$ is a midline of triangle $ACI_a$, so $B_1Q\parallel CI_a$, and $CI_a\perp CI$ (internal and external bisectors at $C$). Thus $B_1Y$ and $B_1Q$ are both lines through $B_1$ perpendicular to $CI$, so they coincide: $Q$ lies on the line $B_1Y$, and $QY\perp CI$.

Hence $QY\perp PY$, i.e. $\angle PYQ=90^\circ$ and $Y\in\gamma$ (if $Y$ coincides with $P$ or $Q$ it lies on $\gamma$ trivially). Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$ (use $LB=\frac{ac}{b+c}$, $BX=\frac c2$, the midpoint of $AB$ and the bisectors at $B$) gives $\angle PXQ=90^\circ$ and $X\in\gamma$.

So $\gamma$ passes through $X$ and $Y$ and is tangent to the incircle and to the excircle opposite $A$.

Solution 2 (Powers of B and C)

Use the notation $a,b,c,s,r,r_a,I,I_a$ of the triangle, $\omega$ for the incircle and $\omega_a$ for the excircle opposite $A$. Since $\angle A=60^\circ$ we have $a^2=b^2+c^2-bc$, $r=\dfrac{s-a}{\sqrt3}$, $r_a=\dfrac{s}{\sqrt3}$, $AI=2r$ and $AI_a=2r_a$.

Coordinates. Put $A$ at the origin and the bisector of angle $A$ along the positive $x$-axis: \[B=\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}c,\ \tfrac12c\Bigr),\qquad C=\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}b,\ -\tfrac12b\Bigr),\qquad I=(2r,0),\qquad I_a=(2r_a,0).\] Let $P=(3r,0)$ and $Q=(r_a,0)$; then $P\in\omega$ and $Q\in\omega_a$. As $4a^2-(b+c)^2=3(b-c)^2>0$, we have $r_a-3r=\frac{2a-b-c}{\sqrt3}>0$, so $P\ne Q$. Let $\gamma$ be the circle with diameter $PQ$: \[\gamma:\quad x^2+y^2-(3r+r_a)x+3rr_a=0 .\] Its centre is on the $x$-axis, which is the line of centres of $\omega$ and $\omega_a$; since $P\in\gamma\cap\omega$ and $Q\in\gamma\cap\omega_a$ lie on this line, $\gamma$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $P$ and to $\omega_a$ at $Q$.

The coefficients. Using $2s=a+b+c$ and $(b+c)^2-a^2=3bc$, \[3r+r_a=\frac{4s-3a}{\sqrt3}=\frac{2b+2c-a}{\sqrt3},\qquad 3rr_a=s(s-a)=\frac{(b+c)^2-a^2}{4}=\frac{3bc}{4}.\] Powers of $B$ and $C$ with respect to $\gamma$. Substituting the coordinates of $B$ ($|AB|^2=c^2$, $x_B=\frac{\sqrt3}2c$): \[\operatorname{pow}_\gamma(B)=c^2-\frac{(2b+2c-a)c}{2}+\frac{3bc}4=\frac{ac}{2}-\frac{bc}{4}=\frac c2\Bigl(a-\frac b2\Bigr)=BX\cdot BY,\] and symmetrically $\operatorname{pow}_\gamma(C)=\dfrac b2\Bigl(a-\dfrac c2\Bigr)=CY\cdot CX$. (Here $BY=a-\frac b2$ and $CX=a-\frac c2$.)

Conclusion. For a point $Z$ of the line $BC$ with $BZ=t$ (measured towards $C$) the power $\operatorname{pow}_\gamma(Z)$ is a quadratic polynomial $f(t)$ with leading coefficient $1$. The polynomial $g(t)=(t-BX)(t-BY)$ is also monic, and \[f(0)=BX\cdot BY=g(0),\qquad f(a)=CX\cdot CY=(a-BX)(a-BY)=g(a).\] So $f-g$ is a polynomial of degree at most $1$ with two different roots $0$ and $a$; hence $f=g$. Therefore the power of $X$ and of $Y$ with respect to $\gamma$ is $0$, i.e. $X,Y\in\gamma$. The circle $\gamma$ passes through $X$ and $Y$ and touches the incircle and the excircle opposite $A$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · also Grade 11, Problem 3

Several participants from $n>1$ regions arrived at a competition. Some of them are friends (friendship is always mutual). It turned out that for any seating of several participants (at least three) around a round table such that every two neighbors are friends, the number of participants from each region at the table does not exceed half of the total number of participants seated at the table. Prove that we can partition the participants into $n$ rooms such that any two friends end up in different rooms.

Show the solution

Solution (Repairing two classes at a time)

Here $n\ge2$ by hypothesis. Let $G$ be the graph whose vertices are the participants and whose edges are the friendships. A seating as in the statement is a cycle of $G$ (with at least three vertices). Call a partition of the vertices into $n$ classes $V_1,\dots,V_n$ (empty classes are allowed) good if every cycle of $G$ has at most half of its vertices in each class. The partition into regions is good, and we must find a partition into $n$ independent classes (classes without edges inside): these are the rooms.

Observation. An independent set $W$ contains at most half of the vertices of any cycle, because it cannot contain two consecutive vertices of the cycle. So a class that is independent never violates the condition, whatever the other classes are.

Lemma. Let $V_1,\dots,V_n$ be a good partition and $i\ne j$. Then $V_i\cup V_j$ can be split again into two sets $W_i$, $W_j$, both independent in $G$; replacing $V_i,V_j$ by $W_i,W_j$ gives a good partition.

Proof. Let $H$ be the subgraph of $G$ induced by $V_i\cup V_j$. Take any cycle $Z$ of $H$, with $\ell$ vertices. All of them lie in $V_i\cup V_j$, and at most $\ell/2$ lie in $V_i$ and at most $\ell/2$ in $V_j$; hence exactly $\ell/2$ lie in each, and $\ell$ is even. So $H$ has no odd cycle, i.e. $H$ is bipartite: its vertex set splits into $W_i$ and $W_j$ so that every edge of $H$ joins $W_i$ with $W_j$. An edge of $G$ with both ends in $V_i\cup V_j$ is an edge of $H$, so $W_i$ and $W_j$ are independent in $G$. In the new partition the classes $W_i$, $W_j$ satisfy the condition by the Observation, and the other classes have not changed, so they still contain at most half of the vertices of every cycle. $\blacksquare$

The procedure. Apply the Lemma to the pairs of classes $(1,2),(1,3),\dots,(1,n)$ in this order (this is where $n\ge 2$ is used). After the first step classes $1$ and $2$ are independent. The step with the pair $(1,j)$ does not touch the classes $2,\dots,j-1$, which are already independent, and makes classes $1$ and $j$ independent. After the last step all $n$ classes are independent. Put the participants of class $m$ into room $m$: any two friends are in different rooms.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Katya has $2n$ spools of ribbon, where $n$ is a positive integer. Initially, the lengths of ribbon wound on the spools are \[1^2,\ 2^2,\ \dots,\ (2n)^2\] decimeters, respectively. Every hour Katya chooses a positive integer $i$ and cuts off $i$ decimeters of ribbon from every spool that still has at least $i$ decimeters left. After some time, all spools whose initial ribbon lengths were odd become empty. Prove that at that moment the amount of ribbon remaining on each of the other spools is less than $4n$ decimeters.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An invariant for two neighbouring spools)

Fix $k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ and follow two spools: the one with initial length $(2k-1)^2$ and the one with initial length $(2k)^2$. Let $x$ and $y$ be their current lengths and $D=(2k)^2-(2k-1)^2=4k-1$.

Claim. At every moment \[\text{(I1)}\ \ y\le x+D,\qquad\qquad\text{(I2)}\ \ \text{if } y<x \text{ then } y<D .\] Initially $y=x+D$, so both hold. Suppose they hold and Katya cuts $i$ decimeters. Four cases.

  • $x<i$ and $y<i$: nothing changes.

  • $x\ge i$ and $y\ge i$: both lengths decrease by $i$. Then $y-x$ does not change, so (I1) holds; and if $y-i<x-i$, then $y<x$, so $y<D$ and $y-i<D$.

  • $x<i\le y$: only $y$ is cut, and the new value is $y'=y-i\le x+D-i<D$ because $x<i$. So (I2) holds, and (I1) holds since $y'<D\le x+D$.

  • $y<i\le x$: only $x$ is cut. Before the cut $y<x$, so $y<D$ by (I2). After the cut $x'=x-i\ge0$, hence $y<D\le x'+D$: (I1) holds, and (I2) holds because $y<D$.

So (I1) and (I2) hold all the time.

Conclusion. At the moment in question all spools with odd initial length are empty, in particular $x=0$, and (I1) gives $y\le D=4k-1\le 4n-1<4n$. Every spool with even initial length is the spool $(2k)^2$ for some $k\le n$, so each of them has less than $4n$ decimeters left.

Solution 2 (Peeling off the first cut)

A sequence of cuts $i_1,i_2,\dots,i_m$ acts on every spool in the same way: a spool with $t$ decimeters is left with $\Phi(t)$ decimeters, where $\Phi=\varphi_{i_m}\circ\dots\circ\varphi_{i_1}$ and \[\varphi_i(t)=\begin{cases}t-i,& t\ge i,\\ t,& t<i.\end{cases}\] Clearly $0\le\Phi(t)\le t$ for every integer $t\ge 0$.

Lemma. If $0\le s\le t$ are integers and $\Phi(s)=0$, then $\Phi(t)\le t-s$.

Proof by induction on the number $m$ of cuts. For $m=0$ we have $\Phi(s)=s=0$ and $\Phi(t)=t=t-s$. Let $m\ge1$, let $i$ be the first cut and $\Psi$ the result of the remaining $m-1$ cuts, so $\Phi(u)=\Psi(\varphi_i(u))$.

  • If $i\le s$: then $\varphi_i(s)=s-i$, $\varphi_i(t)=t-i$, and $0\le s-i\le t-i$ with $\Psi(s-i)=0$. By the induction hypothesis $\Phi(t)=\Psi(t-i)\le(t-i)-(s-i)=t-s$.

  • If $s<i\le t$: then $\varphi_i(s)=s$, so $\Psi(s)=0$, and $\varphi_i(t)=t-i$. If $t-i\ge s$, the induction hypothesis gives $\Phi(t)=\Psi(t-i)\le t-i-s<t-s$. If $t-i<s$, then simply $\Phi(t)=\Psi(t-i)\le t-i<t-s$, because $i>s$.

  • If $i>t$: neither length changes, $\Psi(s)=0$, and $\Phi(t)=\Psi(t)\le t-s$ by the induction hypothesis. $\blacksquare$

Conclusion. Let $\Phi$ describe all the cuts made up to the moment when the spools with odd initial lengths are empty. For $k=1,\dots,n$ take $s=(2k-1)^2$ and $t=(2k)^2$: $\Phi(s)=0$, so by the Lemma \[\Phi\bigl((2k)^2\bigr)\le(2k)^2-(2k-1)^2=4k-1<4n .\] Thus each of the other spools has less than $4n$ decimeters left.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $O$ be the center of the circumcircle of an acute scalene triangle $ABC$. From point $A$, drop perpendiculars $AP$ and $AQ$ to the extensions of segments $BO$ and $CO$ beyond $O$, respectively. A circle centered at $T$ passes through points $P$ and $Q$ and is tangent to segment $BC$. Prove that \[TO\parallel BC.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The projection of A on the line through O)

Let $R$ be the circumradius, $\alpha=\angle BAC$, and let $\ell$ be the line through $O$ parallel to $BC$. Let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $\ell$. We show that $D$ is the centre of a circle through $P,Q$ touching the segment $BC$, and that there is only one such circle; then $T=D$ and $TO=\ell\parallel BC$.

The circle with diameter $AO$. Since $\angle APO=\angle AQO=\angle ADO=90^\circ$, the points $P,Q,D$ lie on the circle $\Omega$ with diameter $AO=R$. Here $D\ne O$, because $AB\ne AC$ means that $A$ is not on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ (the perpendicular to $\ell$ at $O$), and $P,Q\ne O$ since they lie beyond $O$; hence also $D\ne P$ and $D\ne Q$ (the lines $\ell$ and $BO$ meet only at $O$).

The chords $DP$ and $DQ$. In $\Omega$ the chord $DP$ is seen from the point $O$ of $\Omega$ under the angle between the lines $OD=\ell$ and $OP=OB$, or under its supplement. Since $\ell\parallel BC$, this angle equals $\angle OBC=90^\circ-\alpha$ (triangle $OBC$ is isosceles with $\angle BOC=2\alpha$). By the extended law of sines in $\Omega$ (diameter $R$) \[DP=R\sin(90^\circ-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha,\qquad\text{and in the same way}\qquad DQ=R\cos\alpha .\] Tangency. The distance from $D$ to the line $BC$ is the distance between the parallel lines $\ell$ and $BC$, i.e. the distance from $O$ to $BC$, which is $R\cos\alpha$. Hence the circle $\omega$ with centre $D$ and radius $R\cos\alpha$ passes through $P$ and $Q$ and touches the line $BC$ at the foot $K$ of the perpendicular from $D$. As $AD\perp\ell\parallel BC$, the point $K$ is the foot of the altitude from $A$, which lies inside the segment $BC$ because the triangle is acute.

Uniqueness. The point $O$ is inside the triangle, and $P$, $Q$ lie on the rays $BO$, $CO$ beyond $O$; so the segments $BP$ and $CQ$ cross at $O$, and $BCPQ$ is a convex quadrilateral with diagonals $BP$, $CQ$ and opposite sides $BC$, $PQ$. Therefore the line $PQ$ does not meet the segment $BC$.

If $PQ\parallel BC$, a circle through $P,Q$ tangent to the line $BC$ must touch it on the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, so there is only one such circle. Otherwise let $S$ be the intersection of the lines $PQ$ and $BC$; $S$ is outside the segment $BC$. If a circle through $P,Q$ touches the line $BC$ at $K'$, then $SK'^2=SP\cdot SQ$ (power of $S$), so $K'$ is one of two points symmetric with respect to $S$. One of them is $K$, inside the segment; the other one, $K^*$, is outside the segment $BC$, since otherwise the midpoint $S$ of $KK^*$ would lie on the segment. Finally, a circle through $P,Q$ touching the line $BC$ at a given point $K'$ is unique: its centre is the intersection of the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$ with the perpendicular to $BC$ at $K'$, and these lines are not parallel.

So the circle of the statement is $\omega$, its centre is $T=D$, and $TO$ is the line $\ell$, parallel to $BC$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the unit circumcircle)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles at $A,B,C$. Take $O$ as the origin, the circumradius equal to $1$, and \[B=(-\sin\alpha,\,-\cos\alpha),\qquad C=(\sin\alpha,\,-\cos\alpha),\qquad A=(\sin(\gamma-\beta),\ \cos(\gamma-\beta)).\] Indeed these are unit vectors, the line $BC$ is $y=-\cos\alpha$, and $A\cdot C=-\cos(\gamma-\beta+\alpha)=\cos2\beta$, $A\cdot B=-\cos(\gamma-\beta-\alpha)=\cos2\gamma$ (use $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180^\circ$), which are the cosines of the central angles $\angle AOC=2\beta$ and $\angle AOB=2\gamma$.

The feet of the perpendiculars from $A$ to the lines $OB$, $OC$ are $P=(A\cdot B)B=\cos2\gamma\cdot B$ and $Q=\cos 2\beta\cdot C$. They lie beyond $O$, so $v=-\cos2\gamma>0$ and $u=-\cos2\beta>0$, and \[P=(v\sin\alpha,\ v\cos\alpha),\qquad Q=(-u\sin\alpha,\ u\cos\alpha).\] A circle that works. Let $T_0=(\sin(\gamma-\beta),0)$. Using $2\sin(\gamma-\beta)\sin\alpha=2\sin(\gamma-\beta)\sin(\gamma+\beta)=\cos2\beta-\cos2\gamma$, \[|T_0P|^2=\sin^2(\gamma-\beta)+2\cos2\gamma\,\sin(\gamma-\beta)\sin\alpha+\cos^22\gamma=\sin^2(\gamma-\beta)+\cos2\beta\cos2\gamma ,\] and the same computation gives $|T_0Q|^2=\sin^2(\gamma-\beta)-2\cos2\beta\sin(\gamma-\beta)\sin\alpha+\cos^22\beta=\sin^2(\gamma-\beta)+\cos2\beta\cos2\gamma$. Since \[\cos2\beta\cos2\gamma=\tfrac12\bigl(\cos(2\beta+2\gamma)+\cos(2\beta-2\gamma)\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(\cos2\alpha+1-2\sin^2(\beta-\gamma)\bigr)=\cos^2\alpha-\sin^2(\beta-\gamma),\] we get $|T_0P|=|T_0Q|=\cos\alpha$, which is also the distance from $T_0$ to the line $BC$. So the circle with centre $T_0$ and radius $\cos\alpha$ passes through $P$, $Q$ and touches the line $BC$ at $K=(\sin(\gamma-\beta),-\cos\alpha)$. This point is inside the segment $BC$: $|\sin(\gamma-\beta)|<\sin(\gamma+\beta)=\sin\alpha$, because $\sin(\gamma+\beta)\mp\sin(\gamma-\beta)$ equals $2\cos\gamma\sin\beta>0$ or $2\sin\gamma\cos\beta>0$.

It is the only one. As $\beta\ne\gamma$ and $2\beta,2\gamma\in(90^\circ,180^\circ)$, we have $u\ne v$, so $PQ$ is not parallel to $BC$. The line $PQ$ meets the line $y=-\cos\alpha$ at the point $S$ with parameter $\lambda=-\dfrac{1+v}{u-v}$ on $P+\lambda(Q-P)$, whose abscissa is \[x_S=\sin\alpha\Bigl(v+\frac{(1+v)(u+v)}{u-v}\Bigr)=\sin\alpha\cdot\frac{u+v+2uv}{u-v},\qquad\text{so}\qquad |x_S|>\sin\alpha .\] If a circle through $P,Q$ touches the line $BC$ at $K'$, then $SK'^2=SP\cdot SQ$; hence there are at most two tangency points, symmetric about $S$, and for each of them one circle (its centre is the intersection of the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$ and the perpendicular to $BC$ at $K'$). Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $n$ be an odd positive integer. Consider an $n\times n$ grid. Define the distance between two cells to be the minimum number of steps needed to move from one cell to the other, moving each time to a side-adjacent cell. Thus, the distance between two side-adjacent cells is $1$. Suppose $R$ cells are colored red and $B$ cells are colored blue. It is known that any line joining the centers of a red cell and a blue cell is not parallel to either diagonal of the grid. In addition, the distance between any red cell and any blue cell is never equal to $n$. Prove that \[\sqrt{R}+\sqrt{B}\le n.\]

Show the solution

Solution (A change of coordinates modulo n)

Number the columns and rows $0,1,\dots,n-1$, so a cell is a pair $(x,y)$ with $0\le x,y\le n-1$. To the cell $(x,y)$ assign the pair of residues \[F(x,y)=\bigl(x+y \bmod n,\ \ x-y \bmod n\bigr).\] $F$ is injective. If $F(x,y)=F(x',y')$, then adding and subtracting the two congruences gives $2x\equiv2x'$ and $2y\equiv 2y'\pmod n$. As $n$ is odd, $x\equiv x'$, $y\equiv y'\pmod n$, hence $x=x'$, $y=y'$. So $F$ maps the $n^2$ cells one-to-one onto the $n^2$ cells of a new $n\times n$ board, whose rows are labelled by the first residue $x+y$ and whose columns by the second residue $x-y$.

Key claim. If $p$ is a red cell and $q$ a blue cell, then $F(p)$ and $F(q)$ lie in different rows and in different columns of the new board.

Proof. Let $q-p=(d,e)$; then $|d|,|e|\le n-1$ and $(d,e)\ne(0,0)$. Suppose $F(p)$, $F(q)$ are in the same new row, i.e. $n\mid d+e$. Since $|d+e|\le 2n-2$, we have $d+e\in\{0,\,n,\,-n\}$.

  • If $d+e=0$, the line through the two centres has direction $(1,-1)$: it is parallel to a diagonal of the grid, which is forbidden.

  • If $d+e=n$, then $d\ge1$ and $e\ge1$ (if, say, $d\le0$, then $e\ge n$), so the distance between the cells is $|d|+|e|=n$, which is forbidden. The case $d+e=-n$ is the same with both numbers negative.

The same argument with $e$ replaced by $-e$ shows that $n\nmid d-e$, i.e. the two images are in different new columns. $\blacksquare$

Counting. Let the images of the red cells occupy $r_1$ rows and $c_1$ columns of the new board, and the images of the blue cells $r_2$ rows and $c_2$ columns. By the claim no row and no column is used by both colours, so \[r_1+r_2\le n,\qquad c_1+c_2\le n .\] All red images lie in the intersection of their $r_1$ rows and $c_1$ columns, and $F$ is injective, so $R\le r_1c_1$; similarly $B\le r_2c_2$. By the AM–GM inequality \[\sqrt R+\sqrt B\le\sqrt{r_1c_1}+\sqrt{r_2c_2}\le\frac{r_1+c_1}{2}+\frac{r_2+c_2}{2}=\frac{(r_1+r_2)+(c_1+c_2)}2\le n .\] Remark. One can also finish with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: $\sqrt{r_1c_1}+\sqrt{r_2c_2}\le\sqrt{(r_1+r_2)(c_1+c_2)}\le n$.

Problem 8

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · also Grade 10, Problem 8

Let a triangle have pairwise distinct positive integer side lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$. Prove that at least one of the numbers \[ab+1,\quad bc+1,\quad ca+1\] is not a perfect square.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two integers that are too close)

Suppose, on the contrary, that \[ab+1=r^2,\qquad ac+1=s^2,\qquad bc+1=t^2\] with positive integers $r,s,t$. Let $c$ be the largest side; by the triangle inequality $a+b-c>0$.

Compare the integers $st$ and $cr$: \[(st)^2-(cr)^2=(ac+1)(bc+1)-c^2(ab+1)=c(a+b)+1-c^2=c(a+b-c)+1>0 .\] Hence $st>cr$, and since both are integers, $st\ge cr+1$. Therefore \[c(a+b-c)+1=(st)^2-(cr)^2\ge(cr+1)^2-(cr)^2=2cr+1,\] so $a+b-c\ge2r$. But $r=\sqrt{ab+1}>\sqrt{ab}$, hence \[c\le a+b-2r<a+b-2\sqrt{ab}=\bigl(\sqrt a-\sqrt b\bigr)^2<\max(a,b),\] which contradicts the choice of $c$ as the largest side. So at least one of $ab+1$, $bc+1$, $ca+1$ is not a perfect square.

Remark. The argument shows more: if $a<b<c$ are positive integers and $ab+1$, $ac+1$, $bc+1$ are perfect squares, then $c\ge a+b+2\sqrt{ab+1}$. Indeed, the computation above excludes $c\le a+b$, so $c>a+b$ and $(st)^2=(cr)^2-c(c-a-b)+1<(cr)^2$; if we had $c-a-b\le2r-1$, then $(st)^2\ge(cr)^2-2cr+c+1>(cr-1)^2$, and the integer $st$ would lie strictly between $cr-1$ and $cr$. Examples with equality: $(1,3,8)$ and $(2,4,12)$.

Solution 2 (A fourth number)

Suppose that $ab+1=r^2$, $ac+1=s^2$, $bc+1=t^2$ with positive integers $r,s,t$, and let $a<b<c$. If $a=1$, the triangle inequality $c<a+b=b+1$ gives $c\le b$, which is false; so $2\le a<b<c$.

Consider the integers \[d_1=a+b+c+2abc+2rst,\qquad d_2=a+b+c+2abc-2rst .\] Their product is \[d_1d_2=(a+b+c+2abc)^2-4(ab+1)(ac+1)(bc+1).\] Expanding, $(a+b+c+2abc)^2=(a+b+c)^2+4abc(a+b+c)+4a^2b^2c^2$ and $4(ab+1)(ac+1)(bc+1)=4a^2b^2c^2+4abc(a+b+c)+4(ab+bc+ca)+4$, so \[d_1d_2=a^2+b^2+c^2-2ab-2bc-2ca-4=-(N+4),\qquad N=a(b+c-a)+b(c+a-b)+c(a+b-c).\] By the triangle inequalities $N>0$. Thus $d_1d_2<0$; as $d_1>0$, the integer $d_2$ is negative, $d_2\le-1$, and therefore \[d_1\le |d_1d_2|=N+4=2(ab+bc+ca)-(a^2+b^2+c^2)+4<2(ab+bc+ca)+4 .\] On the other hand $rst=\sqrt{(ab+1)(ac+1)(bc+1)}>abc$, so $d_1>4abc$. Since $a\ge2$, $ab<bc$, $ca<bc$ and $bc\ge 12$, \[4abc\ge 8bc=6bc+2bc>2(ab+bc+ca)+4 .\] So $d_1>4abc>2(ab+bc+ca)+4>d_1$, a contradiction. Hence the three numbers cannot all be perfect squares.

Remark. Only the fact that the product $(ab+1)(bc+1)(ca+1)$ is a perfect square was used.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · also Grade 11, Problem 1

Is it possible to divide the $2026$ numbers \[1+\sqrt2,\ 1+2\sqrt2,\ \dots,\ 1+2026\sqrt2\] into two nonempty groups such that the products of the numbers in the groups have the same fractional part?

Show the answer

No

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Parity of the coefficient of the square root)

No. Every product of some of the given numbers can be written uniquely as $p+q\sqrt2$ with integers $p,q$ (uniquely because $\sqrt2$ is irrational).

Claim. If $p+q\sqrt2$ is the product of the numbers $1+k\sqrt2$ over $k$ in a set $S$, then $p$ is odd and $q\equiv\sum_{k\in S}k\pmod 2$.

Proof by induction on $|S|$. For a single number $1+k\sqrt2$ this is clear. If it holds for $p+q\sqrt2$ and we multiply by one more number $1+k\sqrt2$, then \[(p+q\sqrt2)(1+k\sqrt2)=(p+2kq)+(q+kp)\sqrt2 ,\] where $p+2kq$ is odd and $q+kp\equiv q+k\pmod2$ because $p$ is odd. $\blacksquare$

Now suppose the numbers are divided into two non-empty groups with products $p_1+q_1\sqrt2$ and $p_2+q_2\sqrt2$. By the claim \[q_1+q_2\equiv 1+2+\dots+2026=\frac{2026\cdot2027}{2}=1013\cdot2027\equiv1\pmod 2,\] so $q_1\ne q_2$. Two real numbers have the same fractional part exactly when their difference is an integer. But the difference of the two products is $(p_1-p_2)+(q_1-q_2)\sqrt2$ with $q_1-q_2\ne0$, which is irrational. Hence the two products never have the same fractional part.

Solution 2 (The norm modulo 8)

No. Every number of the form $x+y\sqrt2$ with integers $x,y$ determines $x$ and $y$ uniquely, because $\sqrt2$ is irrational. For such a number put \[N(x+y\sqrt2)=x^2-2y^2 .\] Two properties of $N$. (i) $N$ is multiplicative: since $(x+y\sqrt2)(x'+y'\sqrt2)=(xx'+2yy')+(xy'+x'y)\sqrt2$, expanding gives \[(xx'+2yy')^2-2(xy'+x'y)^2=x^2x'^2+4y^2y'^2-2x^2y'^2-2x'^2y^2=(x^2-2y^2)(x'^2-2y'^2).\] (ii) If $x$ is odd, then $N(x+y\sqrt2)\equiv1\pmod 8$ when $y$ is even and $N(x+y\sqrt2)\equiv-1\pmod 8$ when $y$ is odd. Indeed $x^2\equiv1\pmod8$ for odd $x$, while $2y^2\equiv0\pmod 8$ for even $y$ and $2y^2\equiv2\pmod8$ for odd $y$ (as then $y^2\equiv1\pmod4$).

Rational parts are odd. If $x$ and $x'$ are odd, the rational part $xx'+2yy'$ of the product is odd. Each given number $1+k\sqrt2$ has rational part $1$, so every product of some of the given numbers has an odd rational part, and (ii) applies to it.

The contradiction. Suppose the numbers are divided into two non-empty groups with products $\alpha_1=p_1+q_1\sqrt2$ and $\alpha_2=p_2+q_2\sqrt2$ having the same fractional part. Then $\alpha_1-\alpha_2=(p_1-p_2)+(q_1-q_2)\sqrt2$ is an integer, so $q_1=q_2$. By (ii) the norms $N(\alpha_1)$ and $N(\alpha_2)$ are congruent to the same number $\pm1$ modulo $8$, hence by (i) \[N(\alpha_1\alpha_2)=N(\alpha_1)N(\alpha_2)\equiv(\pm1)^2=1\pmod 8 .\] On the other hand $\alpha_1\alpha_2$ is the product of all $2026$ numbers, so by (i) \[N(\alpha_1\alpha_2)=\prod_{k=1}^{2026}N(1+k\sqrt2)=\prod_{k=1}^{2026}\bigl(1-2k^2\bigr).\] By (ii) the factor $1-2k^2$ is $\equiv-1\pmod8$ for odd $k$ and $\equiv1\pmod 8$ for even $k$. There are $1013$ odd numbers among $1,\dots,2026$, so $N(\alpha_1\alpha_2)\equiv(-1)^{1013}=-1\pmod8$. This contradicts $N(\alpha_1\alpha_2)\equiv1\pmod8$. Hence no such division exists.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Monotonicity and strong induction)

Write $S_m=a_1+\dots+a_m$, so the condition is $S_{2n-1}=a_n^2$ for all $n\ge1$.

Step 1: first consequences. For $n=1$: $a_1=a_1^2$, so $a_1=1$. Subtracting the conditions for $n+1$ and $n$, \[a_{2n}+a_{2n+1}=a_{n+1}^2-a_n^2\qquad(n\ge1).\tag{1}\] The left side is positive, hence $a_{n+1}>a_n$: the sequence is strictly increasing.

Step 2: where is the $3$? Since $a_1=1$ and the sequence increases, $3$ can only be $a_2$ or $a_3$, and $a_3=3$ would force $a_2=2$. But for $n=1$ relation (1) gives $a_3=a_2^2-a_2-1$, which is $1$ for $a_2=2$. So $a_2=3$ and $a_3=9-3-1=5$.

Step 3: $a_k=2k-1$ for all $k$. We prove by induction on $n\ge2$ that $a_k=2k-1$ for all $k\le 2n-1$; for $n=2$ this is Step 2. Assume it for some $n\ge2$. Two possibilities remain: \[(a_{2n},a_{2n+1})=(4n-1,\,4n+1)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(a_{2n},a_{2n+1})=(4n-2,\,4n+2).\] Suppose the second one holds. This is a contradiction. Hence $a_{2n}=4n-1$ and $a_{2n+1}=4n+1$, which is the statement for $n+1$.

Step 4: check. The sequence $a_n=2n-1$ contains $3$ and satisfies the condition, because the sum of the first $m$ odd numbers is $m^2$: \[a_1+\dots+a_{2n-1}=(2n-1)^2=a_n^2 .\] So the only such sequence is $\boxed{a_n=2n-1}$.

Solution 2 (A lower bound through the infimum)

Write $S_m=a_1+\dots+a_m$, so the condition is $S_{2n-1}=a_n^2$ for all $n\ge1$.

Monotonicity. For $n=1$ we get $a_1=a_1^2$, so $a_1=1$. Since all terms are positive, $a_{n+1}^2-a_n^2=S_{2n+1}-S_{2n-1}=a_{2n}+a_{2n+1}>0$, so the sequence is strictly increasing. As its terms are natural numbers and $a_1=1$, it follows that $a_n\ge n$ for all $n$.

Claim: $a_n\ge 2n-1$ for all $n$. Put $x_n=\dfrac{a_n}{2n-1}$ and let $q$ be the greatest lower bound (infimum) of the numbers $x_1,x_2,\dots$. From $a_n\ge n$ we get $x_n\ge\frac{n}{2n-1}>\frac12$, so $q\ge\frac12>0$. By the definition of $q$ we have $a_i\ge q(2i-1)$ for every $i$, and the sum of the first $2n-1$ odd numbers is $(2n-1)^2$, so \[a_n^2=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}a_i\ \ge\ q\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}(2i-1)=q\,(2n-1)^2,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad x_n\ge\sqrt q\quad\text{for every }n .\] Thus $\sqrt q$ is a lower bound of the $x_n$, and since $q$ is the greatest one, $q\ge\sqrt q$. As $q>0$, this gives $q\ge1$, which is the claim.

Where the $3$ is. If $a_j=3$, then $2j-1\le a_j=3$ by the claim, so $j\le2$; since $a_1=1$, we get $a_2=3$.

Then \[\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}a_i=a_n^2=(2n-1)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}(2i-1),\] and each $a_i$ on the left is at least the corresponding $2i-1$ on the right; so equality holds term by term: $a_i=2i-1$ for all $i\le 2n-1$. In particular $a_{2n-1}=2(2n-1)-1$, so the same holds with $2n-1$ in place of $n$. Starting from $n_0=2$ and putting $n_{j+1}=2n_j-1$ we get $n_j=2^j+1$, which grows without bound, and $a_i=2i-1$ for all $i\le 2n_j-1$. Hence $a_i=2i-1$ for every $i$.

Check. The sequence of odd numbers contains $3$, and $a_1+\dots+a_{2n-1}=(2n-1)^2=a_n^2$. So the only such sequence is $\boxed{a_n=2n-1}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

An acute scalene triangle $ABC$ is inscribed in a circle with center $O$, and its altitudes intersect at $H$. The tangents to the circumcircle of $BHC$ at $B$, to the circumcircle of $AHB$ at $A$, and to the circumcircle of $CHA$ at $C$ form a triangle $T_1$. Similarly, the tangents to the circumcircle of $BHC$ at $C$, to the circumcircle of $AHB$ at $B$, and to the circumcircle of $CHA$ at $A$ form a triangle $T_2$. Let $I_1$ and $I_2$ be the incenters of $T_1$ and $T_2$.

Prove that $HI_1OI_2$ is a parallelogram, or that the points $H,I_1,O,I_2$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Take $O$ as the origin and denote the position vector of a point by the same letter; let $R$ be the circumradius, so $|A|=|B|=|C|=R$ and $H=A+B+C$. We prove $I_1+I_2=H$. Then the segments $I_1I_2$ and $HO$ have the same midpoint, so $HI_1OI_2$ is a parallelogram, or (if it degenerates) the four points are collinear.

The six tangents. The point $B+C$ is at distance $R$ from $B$, from $C$ and from $H$ (since $H-(B+C)=A$), so it is the centre of the circle $(BHC)$. The tangent to $(BHC)$ at $B$ is perpendicular to the radius $B-(B+C)=-C$, so it is the line $\{Z:\ Z\cdot C=B\cdot C\}$; the tangent at $C$ is $\{Z:\ Z\cdot B=B\cdot C\}$. In the same way the centres of $(AHB)$ and $(CHA)$ are $A+B$ and $C+A$, and we get \[T_1:\quad Z\cdot C=B\cdot C,\qquad Z\cdot B=A\cdot B,\qquad Z\cdot A=C\cdot A;\] \[T_2:\quad Z\cdot B=B\cdot C,\qquad Z\cdot A=A\cdot B,\qquad Z\cdot C=C\cdot A.\] Lemma. Let a triangle $T$ be bounded by the lines $Z\cdot A=p$, $Z\cdot B=q$, $Z\cdot C=s$. Then its incentre $J$ satisfies \[J\cdot A-p=J\cdot B-q=J\cdot C-s .\] Proof. The system $V\cdot A+kR^2=p$, $V\cdot B+kR^2=q$, $V\cdot C+kR^2=s$ in the unknowns $V$ (a vector) and $k$ (a number) has a unique solution: subtracting, $V\cdot(A-B)=p-q$ and $V\cdot(A-C)=p-s$ determine $V$ because $A-B$ and $A-C$ are not parallel, and then $k$ is determined. The map $h(Z)=kZ+V$ sends the line $Z\cdot A=R^2$ to the line $W\cdot A=kR^2+V\cdot A=p$, and similarly for $B$ and $C$. The three lines $Z\cdot A=R^2$, $Z\cdot B=R^2$, $Z\cdot C=R^2$ are the tangents to the circumcircle at $A,B,C$; they bound the tangential triangle $T_0$, and, the triangle $ABC$ being acute, the circumcircle is the incircle of $T_0$, so the incentre of $T_0$ is $O$. Since $T$ is a genuine triangle, $k\ne0$; thus $h$ is a homothety (or a translation) taking $T_0$ to $T$, and it takes the incentre $O$ of $T_0$ to the incentre of $T$. Hence $J=h(O)=V$, and $J\cdot A-p=J\cdot B-q=J\cdot C-s=-kR^2$. $\blacksquare$

Conclusion. By the Lemma, for some numbers $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$, \[I_1\cdot A-C\cdot A=I_1\cdot B-A\cdot B=I_1\cdot C-B\cdot C=\lambda_1,\qquad I_2\cdot A-A\cdot B=I_2\cdot B-B\cdot C=I_2\cdot C-C\cdot A=\lambda_2 .\] Adding the corresponding equalities and using $A\cdot B+A\cdot C=A\cdot H-R^2$ (and the same for $B$, $C$), we get for $W=I_1+I_2-H$: \[W\cdot A=W\cdot B=W\cdot C=\lambda_1+\lambda_2-R^2 .\] So $W\cdot(A-B)=W\cdot(A-C)=0$; as $A-B$ and $A-C$ are not parallel, $W=0$. Thus $I_1+I_2=H=H+O$, which is what we wanted.

Solution 2 (Reflection in the nine-point centre)

Let $R$ be the circumradius and $N$ the midpoint of $OH$. Let $O_A,O_B,O_C$ be the centres of the circles $(BHC)$, $(CHA)$, $(AHB)$. We use two classical facts: these circles have radius $R$ and $O_A,O_B,O_C$ are the reflections of $O$ in $BC$, $CA$, $AB$; and $\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OM_a}=\overrightarrow{OO_A}$, where $M_a$ is the midpoint of $BC$.

Step 1: the half-turn $\sigma$ about $N$. From $\overrightarrow{AH}=\overrightarrow{OO_A}$ the quadrilateral $AHO_AO$ is a parallelogram, so the midpoint of $AO_A$ is the midpoint $N$ of $OH$. Hence the reflection $\sigma$ in the point $N$ exchanges $A\leftrightarrow O_A$, and likewise $B\leftrightarrow O_B$, $C\leftrightarrow O_C$, $O\leftrightarrow H$. Also $AO_B=CO_B=OA=OC=R$, so $AOCO_B$ is a rhombus and $\overrightarrow{AO_B}=\overrightarrow{OC}$; similarly $\overrightarrow{CO_A}=\overrightarrow{OB}$ and $\overrightarrow{BO_C}=\overrightarrow{OA}$.

Step 2: the image of $T_1$. The side of $T_1$ tangent to $(BHC)$ at $B$ is the line through $B$ perpendicular to $O_AB$. Its image under $\sigma$ is the line through $\sigma(B)=O_B$ perpendicular to $\sigma(O_A)\sigma(B)=AO_B$. The side of $T_2$ tangent to $(CHA)$ at $A$ is the line through $A$ perpendicular to $O_BA$. So these two lines are perpendicular to the segment $AO_B$ at its two ends: the first is obtained from the second by the translation by $\overrightarrow{AO_B}=\overrightarrow{OC}$. In the same way:

  • $\sigma$(tangent to $(AHB)$ at $A$) is the tangent to $(BHC)$ at $C$ (a side of $T_2$) translated by $\overrightarrow{CO_A}=\overrightarrow{OB}$;

  • $\sigma$(tangent to $(CHA)$ at $C$) is the tangent to $(AHB)$ at $B$ (a side of $T_2$) translated by $\overrightarrow{BO_C}=\overrightarrow{OA}$.

Thus the sides of $T_2$ are perpendicular to $OA$, $OB$, $OC$, and the triangle $\sigma(T_1)$ is obtained by moving the side perpendicular to $OX$ by the vector $\overrightarrow{OX}$ (of length $R$), for $X=A,B,C$.

Step 3: a lemma on such shifts. Let $\Delta$ be a triangle with sides perpendicular to $OA$, $OB$, $OC$, with incentre $J$ and inradius $\rho$. Move the side perpendicular to $OX$ by the vector $\overrightarrow{OX}$, $X=A,B,C$. If the three new lines are not concurrent, the triangle they bound has incentre $J$.

Proof. Let $u_X=\overrightarrow{OX}/R$. The incircle of $\Delta$ touches the side perpendicular to $OX$ at $J+\varepsilon_X\rho\,u_X$ with $\varepsilon_X=\pm1$. The centre of the incircle lies inside the triangle of its three points of tangency, so $\sum t_X\varepsilon_Xu_X=0$ with some $t_X>0$. Also $O$ lies inside the acute triangle $ABC$, so $\sum s_Xu_X=0$ with some $s_X>0$. Three pairwise non-parallel vectors in the plane have, up to a factor, only one linear relation; hence the numbers $t_X\varepsilon_X$ are proportional to $s_X$, and $\varepsilon_A=\varepsilon_B=\varepsilon_C=\varepsilon$. After the shift, the three lines pass through the points $J+(\varepsilon\rho+R)u_X$ and are perpendicular to $u_X$; they are not concurrent, so $m=\varepsilon\rho+R\ne0$. They are the tangents at $J+mu_X$ to the circle with centre $J$ and radius $|m|$, and the triangle with vertices $J+mu_X$ is homothetic to $ABC$ (ratio $m/R$, centre corresponding to $O$), hence acute and containing $J$. Therefore this circle is the incircle of the new triangle, and its incentre is $J$. $\blacksquare$

Step 4. Apply the lemma to $\Delta=T_2$ (incentre $I_2$): the shifted triangle is $\sigma(T_1)$, a genuine triangle, so its incentre is $I_2$. But the incentre of $\sigma(T_1)$ is $\sigma(I_1)$. Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Let $m$ and $k$ be natural numbers with $m>100$ and $1<k<2m$.

Initially, $2m$ clay balls of mass $1$ are arranged in a row. Petya and Vasya alternate moves, starting with Petya, for a total of $2m-1$ moves. Each move consists of merging two adjacent balls into one.

Petya wins if at some moment there is a ball of mass $k$. Otherwise, Vasya wins. Who has a winning strategy?

Show the answer

Petya wins if $2\le k\le m+1$; Vasya wins if $m+2\le k\le 2m-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Dividers and pairing strategies)

Answer. Petya wins for $2\le k\le m+1$; Vasya wins for $m+2\le k\le 2m-1$.

Reformulation. Put the row of balls on the segment $[0,2m]$, the $j$-th ball occupying $[j-1,j]$, and mark the points $0,1,\dots,2m$. Merging two adjacent balls erases the mark between them; the marks $0$ and $2m$ are never erased. So the players alternately erase one of the marks $1,\dots,2m-1$ (Petya erases $m$ of them, Vasya $m-1$), and the balls are the segments between consecutive remaining marks. A ball of mass $k$ exists exactly when two remaining marks $i$ and $i+k$ have no remaining mark between them.

Petya wins for $2\le k\le m$. Petya splits all marks except one mark $z$ into pairs and first erases $z$:

  • if $k\le m-1$: the pairs $\{i,i+k\}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$ (they cover the marks $1,\dots,2k$), $z=2m-1$, and the remaining $2m-2k-2$ marks $2k+1,\dots,2m-2$ paired in any way;

  • if $k=m$: the pairs $\{i,i+m\}$ for $i=1,\dots,m-1$, and $z=m$.

Afterwards, whenever Vasya erases a mark, Petya erases the other mark of its pair. After each move of Petya every pair is either complete or completely erased. Call the pairs $\{i,i+k\}$ long. After Petya's first move all long pairs are present, at the end none is, and the number of long pairs present after Petya's moves decreases by at most one per round; so after some move of Petya exactly one long pair $\{i,i+k\}$ is present. All marks strictly between $i$ and $i+k$ belong to other long pairs (or equal $z=m$ when $k=m$), so they are erased. Thus there is a ball of mass $k$, and Petya wins.

Petya wins for $k=m+1$. Petya first erases the mark $m$ and uses the pairs \[\{j,\ j+m+1\}\ \ (1\le j\le m-2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{m-1,\ m+1\},\] which cover all the other marks; he again answers inside the pair. After Petya's moves the present marks form complete pairs, and the number of pairs decreases by one per round, from $m-1$ to $0$; consider the moment after a move of Petya when exactly one pair is left. If it is $\{j,j+m+1\}$, all marks between $j$ and $j+m+1$ are erased and there is a ball of mass $m+1$. If it is $\{m-1,m+1\}$, the remaining marks are $0,\ m-1,\ m+1,\ 2m$ and Vasya must erase $m-1$ or $m+1$: this creates the ball $[0,m+1]$ or the ball $[m-1,2m]$, of mass $m+1$. In both cases Petya wins.

Vasya wins for $m+2\le k\le 2m-1$. Put $d=2m-k$, so $1\le d\le m-2$ and $d<k$. A ball of mass $k$ is a segment $[i,i+k]$ with $0\le i\le d$. Call the marks $d+1,\dots,k-1$ central; there are $k-d-1=2k-2m-1\ge3$ of them, and each of them lies strictly inside every segment $[i,i+k]$. Vasya's strategy: on each of his moves

  • if the mark $k$ is present, erase it;

  • otherwise, if some of the marks $1,\dots,d$ are present, erase the smallest of them;

  • otherwise erase any mark.

Suppose that nevertheless a ball $[i,i+k]$ appears, and consider the first moment when this happens. At this moment the marks $i$ and $i+k$ are present and the $k-1$ marks between them are erased; these are the marks $i+1,\dots,d$, all the central marks, and the marks $k,\dots,i+k-1$.

Which moves of Vasya were made before? If $i\ge1$, the mark $i\in\{1,\dots,d\}$ is present, so Vasya has never been in the third case; if $i=0$, the mark $i+k=k$ is present, so Vasya has not moved at all, since his first move would have erased $k$ (unless Petya did). Hence every move of Vasya so far erased either the mark $k$ (at most once) or the smallest present mark among $1,\dots,d$, which was smaller than $i$ because $i$ itself is still present. In particular Vasya erased no central mark, and at most $1+(i-1)=i$ marks in total (none if $i=0$).

The ball cannot appear after a move of Vasya: his move would erase the last mark inside $[i,i+k]$, but he erases only $k$ (possible only on his first move, when at most two marks have been erased, while at least three central marks must be gone) or marks smaller than $i$.

The ball cannot appear after a move of Petya. Suppose it appears after Petya's $p$-th move; Vasya has made $p-1$ moves. Among the $k-1$ marks inside $[i,i+k]$ Vasya erased at most one (the mark $k$), so Petya erased at least $k-2$ of them: $p\ge k-2$. On the other hand $p-1\le i\le d$. Hence $k-2\le d+1=2m-k+1$, i.e. $2k\le 2m+3$, so $k\le m+1$, a contradiction.

So no ball of mass $k$ ever appears, and Vasya wins.

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (A regular 202-gon without one vertex)

Yes. Let $A_0,A_1,\dots,A_{201}$ be the vertices of a regular $202$-gon inscribed in a circle with centre $Z$, numbered in order, and let $\mathcal P$ be the convex $201$-gon $A_1A_2\dots A_{201}$ (its vertices lie on a circle, so it is convex). Its sides are $A_iA_{i+1}$ for $1\le i\le200$ and $A_{201}A_1$; every other segment $A_iA_j$ is a diagonal.

When are two chords perpendicular? Let $\theta=\frac{360^\circ}{202}$, so $A_i$ has polar angle $i\theta$ (indices may be taken modulo $202$). The perpendicular bisector of the chord $A_iA_j$ passes through $Z$ and has direction angle $\frac{(i+j)\theta}{2}$. Let $A_iA_j$ be a diagonal of $\mathcal P$ and let $t$ be the residue of $i+j+101$ modulo $202$. We count the unordered pairs $\{k,l\}$ of distinct residues modulo $202$ with $k+l\equiv t$. For each of the $202$ values of $k$ there is one $l$, and $l=k$ happens for at most two values of $k$ (the solutions of $2k\equiv t$); so there are at least $\frac{202-2}{2}=100$ such pairs. From them we discard:

  • the pair containing the residue $0$ (at most one pair, since $A_0$ is not a vertex of $\mathcal P$);

  • the pairs of the form $\{k,k+1\}$ (sides of the $202$-gon): then $2k+1\equiv t$, which has at most two solutions $k$, so at most two pairs;

  • the pair $\{1,201\}$ (the side $A_{201}A_1$ of $\mathcal P$): at most one pair.

At least $100-4=96$ pairs remain. Each of them gives a segment $A_kA_l$ with $1\le k,l\le201$ which is not a side of $\mathcal P$, i.e. a diagonal of $\mathcal P$, and by (1) it is perpendicular to $A_iA_j$. It is a different diagonal, since a segment is not perpendicular to itself.

So in the $201$-gon $\mathcal P$ every diagonal is perpendicular to some other diagonal.

$250\,000$

Show the solution

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ with real coefficients and leading coefficient $1$.

Suppose there exist $100$ pairwise distinct real roots $x_1,\dots,x_{100}$ of $P(x)$ and $100$ pairwise distinct real roots $y_1,\dots,y_{100}$ of $P(x)-1$ such that \[|x_i-y_i|=1\quad\text{for all } i=1,\dots,100.\] What is the smallest possible value of $n$?

Show the answer

$n=101$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power sums for the bound, a shifted product for the example)

Answer: $101$. Since $P$ has $100$ distinct roots, $n\ge100$.

$n=100$ is impossible. Suppose $n=100$. Then \[P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{100}(x-x_i),\qquad P(x)-1=\prod_{i=1}^{100}(x-y_i).\] These polynomials differ only in the constant term, so the elementary symmetric polynomials $e_1,\dots,e_{99}$ of $(x_i)$ and of $(y_i)$ coincide. By Newton's identities the power sum $p_j=\sum_it_i^{\,j}$ is a polynomial in $e_1,\dots,e_j$; hence \[\sum_{i=1}^{100}y_i^{\,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{100}x_i^{\,j}\quad(0\le j\le 99),\qquad\text{so}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{100}\bigl(g(y_i)-g(x_i)\bigr)=0\tag{1}\] for every polynomial $g$ of degree at most $99$. Let $I_+$ be the set of indices with $y_i=x_i+1$ and $I_-$ the set with $y_i=x_i-1$. Then (1) reads \[\sum_{i\in I_+}\bigl(g(x_i+1)-g(x_i)\bigr)=\sum_{i\in I_-}\bigl(g(y_i+1)-g(y_i)\bigr).\] Every polynomial $f$ of degree at most $98$ can be written as $f(x)=g(x+1)-g(x)$ with $\deg g\le99$ (the differences of $1,x,\dots,x^{99}$ have degrees $-\infty,0,1,\dots,98$ and span all polynomials of degree at most $98$). Therefore \[\sum_{i\in I_+}f(x_i)=\sum_{i\in I_-}f(y_i)\qquad\text{for all }f\text{ with }\deg f\le98 .\tag{2}\] With $f=1$ we get $|I_+|=|I_-|=50$. With $f=x^j$, $1\le j\le 50$, we see that the $50$ numbers $x_i$ $(i\in I_+)$ and the $50$ numbers $y_i$ $(i\in I_-)$ have the same power sums $p_1,\dots,p_{50}$, hence (Newton's identities again) the same elementary symmetric polynomials, i.e. they are the roots of the same polynomial of degree $50$. So some $x_i$ equals some $y_j$. But $P(x_i)=0\ne1=P(y_j)$, a contradiction. Thus $n\ge101$.

An example with $n=101$. Let \[g(x)=(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+100),\qquad Q(x)=g(x)-\tfrac1{100},\qquad P(x)=x\,Q(x)=x(x+1)\cdots(x+100)-\tfrac{x}{100}.\] $P$ is monic of degree $101$. Since $(x+1)g(x+1)=(x+101)g(x)$, \[P(x+1)-P(x)=(x+101)g(x)-\tfrac{x+1}{100}-x\,g(x)+\tfrac{x}{100}=101\,g(x)-\tfrac1{100}=101\,Q(x)+1 .\tag{3}\] So if $Q(x_0)=0$, then $P(x_0)=0$ and $P(x_0+1)=1$. It remains to show that $Q$ has $100$ distinct real roots. For $k=1,\dots,99$, \[g\bigl(-k-\tfrac12\bigr)=\prod_{j=1}^{100}\bigl(j-k-\tfrac12\bigr)\] has exactly $k$ negative factors, and its absolute value is $\prod_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(i-\frac12\bigr)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{100-k}\bigl(i-\frac12\bigr)$; one of these two products has at least $50$ factors and is at least $\frac12\cdot\frac32\cdot\frac52\cdot\frac72>6$, the other is at least $\frac12$. So $\bigl|g(-k-\frac12)\bigr|>3>\frac1{100}$, and $Q(-k-\frac12)$ has the sign $(-1)^k$. Also $Q(0)=Q(-101)=100!-\frac1{100}>0$. Hence at the $101$ points \[-101<-99\tfrac12<-98\tfrac12<\dots<-1\tfrac12<0\] the signs of $Q$ are $+,-,+,-,\dots,-,+$: they alternate, so $Q$ has a root in each of the $100$ intervals between consecutive points. These are $100$ distinct real numbers $x_1,\dots,x_{100}$, roots of $P$; the numbers $y_i=x_i+1$ are distinct roots of $P(x)-1$ by (3), and $|x_i-y_i|=1$.

Therefore the smallest possible degree is $\boxed{101}$.

Solution 2 (A discrete Wronskian)

Answer: $101$. Clearly $n\ge100$.

$n=100$ is impossible. Suppose $n=100$, so $P(x)=\prod(x-x_i)$ and $P(x)-1=\prod(x-y_i)$. Let $I_+=\{i:\ y_i=x_i+1\}$, $I_-=\{i:\ y_i=x_i-1\}$ and \[F(x)=\prod_{i\in I_+}(x-x_i),\qquad G(x)=\prod_{i\in I_-}(x-y_i),\] monic polynomials of degrees $p$ and $q$, $p+q=100$. For $i\in I_-$ we have $x-x_i=(x-1)-y_i$, and for $i\in I_+$ we have $x-y_i=(x-1)-x_i$. Hence \[P(x)=F(x)\,G(x-1),\qquad P(x)-1=F(x-1)\,G(x),\] \[F(x)G(x-1)-F(x-1)G(x)=1\quad\text{identically}.\tag{1}\] For a polynomial $U$ put $\nabla U(x)=U(x)-U(x-1)$; if $U$ has degree $u\ge1$ and leading coefficient $c$, then $\nabla U$ has degree $u-1$ and leading coefficient $uc$, and $\nabla U=0$ if $U$ is constant. For any polynomials $U,V$ \[U(x)V(x-1)-U(x-1)V(x)=U(V-\nabla V)-(U-\nabla U)V=\nabla U\cdot V-U\cdot\nabla V .\tag{2}\] If $U,V$ have degrees $u,v$ and leading coefficients $c,c'$, the coefficient of $x^{u+v-1}$ on the right of (2) is $(u-v)cc'$.

Case $p\ne q$. By (2) with $U=F$, $V=G$ the left side of (1) has degree exactly $p+q-1=99$; it cannot be the constant $1$.

Case $p=q=50$. Let $E=F-G$, a polynomial of degree $e<50$. Since the expression (2) vanishes for $U=V$ and is linear in $U$, (1) becomes $E(x)G(x-1)-E(x-1)G(x)=1$. So $E\ne0$, and by (2) the left side has leading coefficient $(e-50)c\ne0$ at $x^{e+49}$, where $e+49\ge49$; again it is not constant. Contradiction.

Hence $n\ge 101$.

An example with $n=101$. Take $g(x)=(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+100)$ and $P(x)=x\,g(x)-\dfrac x{100}$, a monic polynomial of degree $101$. From $(x+1)g(x+1)=(x+101)g(x)$, \[P(x+1)-P(x)=(x+101)g(x)-\tfrac{x+1}{100}-x\,g(x)+\tfrac{x}{100}=101\,g(x)-\tfrac1{100}.\] So with $Q(x)=g(x)-\frac1{100}$ we have $P(x)=x\,Q(x)$ and \[P(x+1)=P(x)+1+101\,Q(x).\] Thus every root $x_0$ of $Q$ satisfies $P(x_0)=0$, $P(x_0+1)=1$. The polynomial $Q$ has $100$ distinct real roots: at the points $-101,\ -99\frac12,\ -98\frac12,\dots,-1\frac12,\ 0$ its signs alternate. Indeed $Q(-101)=Q(0)=100!-\frac1{100}>0$, and for $1\le k\le99$ the number $g(-k-\frac12)=\prod_{j=1}^{100}(j-k-\frac12)$ has $k$ negative factors and absolute value $\prod_{i\le k}(i-\frac12)\prod_{i\le100-k}(i-\frac12)>\frac12\cdot\bigl(\frac12\cdot\frac32\cdot\frac52\cdot\frac72\bigr)>\frac1{100}$, so $Q(-k-\frac12)$ has the sign $(-1)^k$. The $100$ roots $x_i$ of $Q$ and the numbers $y_i=x_i+1$ satisfy all the requirements.

So the answer is $\boxed{101}$.

Solution (Height above a parabola)

No. Consider the parabola $y=\frac12x^2$ and, for a point $(a,b)$, its signed height above the parabola \[h(a,b)=b-\tfrac12a^2 .\] The tangent to the parabola at the point with abscissa $a$ has slope $a$. So a move from $(a_i,b_i)$ goes along the line $\ell$ through this point with slope $a_i$, that is, along the line parallel to the tangent to the parabola at the abscissa $a_i$. The parabola lies above each of its tangents and touches it only at one point; hence, moving along $\ell$ away from the abscissa $a_i$, the chip gets lower with respect to the parabola. In formulas: the new point is $(a_{i+1},\ b_i+a_i(a_{i+1}-a_i))$ with $a_{i+1}\ne a_i$ (the line is not vertical), and \[h(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})-h(a_i,b_i)=a_i(a_{i+1}-a_i)-\tfrac12\bigl(a_{i+1}^2-a_i^2\bigr)=-\tfrac12(a_{i+1}-a_i)^2<0 .\] Thus $h$ strictly decreases with every move, so the chip can never be at the same point twice. In particular it cannot return to the starting point.

Remark. Equivalently, if the chip returned after $N$ moves, adding the equalities $b_{i+1}-b_i=a_i(a_{i+1}-a_i)$ for $i=1,\dots,N$ would give $0=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{N}(a_{i+1}-a_i)^2<0$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A board contains $2026$ pairwise distinct integers greater than $1$. It turns out that for every number $a$ written on the board, there exist at least $k$ pairs of written numbers $b<c$ such that \[bc-1\] is divisible by \[a-1.\] Find the largest possible value of $k$.

Show the answer

$k=1012$

Show the solution

Solution (The largest number gives the bound, powers of two the example)

Answer: $1012$.

Upper bound. Let $M$ be the largest number on the board; $M\ge 2027$. Consider the pairs $b<c$ of written numbers with $M-1\mid bc-1$.

  • $c\ne M$. Indeed, $M\equiv1\pmod{M-1}$, so $c=M$ would give $bc-1\equiv b-1$, and $M-1\mid b-1$ is impossible for $2\le b<M$.

  • So $2\le b<c\le M-1$. The numbers $2,3,\dots,M-1$ have pairwise different remainders modulo $M-1$. If $\{b,c\}$ and $\{b,c'\}$ are two such pairs with a common element $b$, then $bc\equiv bc'\equiv1$, hence $c\equiv c\,(bc')=(bc)\,c'\equiv c'\pmod{M-1}$, and so $c=c'$.

Thus the pairs for $a=M$ are pairwise disjoint and consist of written numbers different from $M$. There are $2025$ such numbers, so there are at most $\lfloor 2025/2\rfloor=1012$ pairs. Hence $k\le1012$.

Example. Write the numbers $2^1,2^2,\dots,2^{2026}$. We use that $2^i-1$ divides $2^s-1$ whenever $i\mid s$. So for $a=2^i$ every pair of exponents $j<l$ with $i\mid j+l$ gives a pair $b=2^j<c=2^l$ with $a-1\mid bc-1$. We show that for each $i\in\{1,\dots,2026\}$ there are at least $1012$ such pairs $(j,l)$, $1\le j<l\le2026$.

Case $i\le1013$. For every $j\in\{1,\dots,2026\}$ the residue class of $-j$ modulo $i$ contains at least $\lfloor2026/i\rfloor\ge2$ numbers of $\{1,\dots,2026\}$, so at least one number $l\ne j$. This gives at least $2026$ ordered pairs $(j,l)$, $j\ne l$, with $i\mid j+l$, i.e. at least $1013$ unordered pairs.

Case $1014\le i\le2026$. The pairs with $j+l=i$ are $j=1,\dots,\bigl\lfloor\frac{i-1}{2}\bigr\rfloor$. The pairs with $j+l=2i$, $j<l\le2026$, are $j=2i-2026,\dots,i-1$ (here $2i-2026\ge2$): there are $2026-i$ of them. Altogether, with $i=2026-t$, $0\le t\le1012$, we get at least \[\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2025-t}{2}\Bigr\rfloor+t\ \ge\ \frac{2024-t}{2}+t=1012+\frac t2\ \ge\ 1012\] pairs.

So in this example every written number $a$ has at least $1012$ pairs, and the largest possible value is $k=\boxed{1012}$.

Problem 7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

A sphere centered at $I$ is inscribed in tetrahedron $ABCD$ and touches its faces $BCD$, $CDA$, $DAB$, and $ABC$ at points $A_1$, $B_1$, $C_1$, and $D_1$, respectively. Segment $A_1B_1$ intersects the plane $C_1D_1I$ at point $E$. Prove that the midpoint of edge $AB$ lies in the plane $CDE$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Two ways to cut the segment in the ratio of the altitudes)

Let $r$ be the radius of the sphere, $M$ the midpoint of $AB$, and $h_A$, $h_B$ the distances from $A$ to the plane $BCD$ and from $B$ to the plane $ACD$. Tangent segments from a point to a sphere are equal, so we may put \[t_A=AB_1=AC_1=AD_1,\quad t_B=BA_1=BC_1=BD_1,\quad t_C=CA_1=CB_1=CD_1,\quad t_D=DA_1=DB_1=DC_1 .\] Step 1: the plane $C_1D_1I$. $IC_1$ is perpendicular to the plane $DAB$ and $ID_1$ to the plane $ABC$; both planes contain $AB$. So $AB\perp IC_1$, $AB\perp ID_1$, and the plane $\pi=C_1D_1I$ is perpendicular to $AB$. For a point $X$ put \[\varphi(X)=XA^2-XB^2-(t_A^2-t_B^2).\] In coordinates, $\varphi(X)=2\,\overrightarrow{OX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}+\text{const}$, so $\varphi$ is a linear (affine) function, constant on the planes perpendicular to $AB$. Since $\varphi(C_1)=t_A^2-t_B^2-(t_A^2-t_B^2)=0$, the plane $\pi$ is exactly the set $\varphi=0$.

Step 2: $A_1A^2=t_A^2+2rh_A$. Let $A'$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to the plane $BCD$. The points $A$ and $I$ lie on the same side of this plane at heights $h_A$ and $r$, above the points $A'$ and $A_1$. Hence \[AI^2=(h_A-r)^2+A'A_1^2,\qquad AA_1^2=h_A^2+A'A_1^2 .\] Also $AI^2=AB_1^2+B_1I^2=t_A^2+r^2$ (right angle at $B_1$). Subtracting, $AA_1^2=t_A^2+r^2-(h_A-r)^2+h_A^2=t_A^2+2rh_A$. In the same way $BB_1^2=t_B^2+2rh_B$.

Step 3: where $\pi$ cuts $A_1B_1$. Using $A_1B=t_B$ and $B_1A=t_A$, \[\varphi(A_1)=A_1A^2-t_A^2=2rh_A>0,\qquad\varphi(B_1)=t_B^2-B_1B^2=-2rh_B<0 .\] A linear function with these values at the ends of the segment $A_1B_1$ vanishes at exactly one of its points, namely at the point $E$ with \[A_1E:EB_1=h_A:h_B .\tag{1}\] Step 4: where the plane $CDM$ cuts $A_1B_1$. Let $\sigma$ be the plane $CDM$. The points $A$ and $B$ are on opposite sides of $\sigma$. The point $A_1$ is inside the face $BCD$, so it is on the same side of $\sigma$ as $B$; similarly $B_1$ is on the side of $A$. Hence the segment $A_1B_1$ meets $\sigma$ at one point $E'$, and \[\frac{A_1E'}{E'B_1}=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(A_1,\sigma)}{\operatorname{dist}(B_1,\sigma)}=\frac{V(A_1CDM)}{V(B_1CDM)},\] the two tetrahedra having the common face $CDM$. Compute the volumes from the other side: the distance from $M$ to the plane $BCD$ is $\frac12h_A$ and to the plane $ACD$ is $\frac12h_B$, so \[V(A_1CDM)=\tfrac13[A_1CD]\cdot\tfrac{h_A}{2},\qquad V(B_1CDM)=\tfrac13[B_1CD]\cdot\tfrac{h_B}2 .\] The triangles $A_1CD$ and $B_1CD$ are congruent ($CA_1=CB_1=t_C$, $DA_1=DB_1=t_D$, common side $CD$), so $[A_1CD]=[B_1CD]$ and \[A_1E':E'B_1=h_A:h_B .\tag{2}\] Step 5. By (1) and (2) the points $E$ and $E'$ divide the segment $A_1B_1$ in the same ratio, so $E=E'$ lies in the plane $CDM$. The point $E$ is inside the tetrahedron, hence not on the line $CD$; therefore the plane $CDE$ is the plane $CDM$, and it contains the midpoint $M$ of $AB$.

Solution 2 (Area-weighted normals and the angles at the edge CD)

Let $S_A,S_B,S_C,S_D$ be the areas of the faces opposite $A,B,C,D$, and $n_A,n_B,n_C,n_D$ the outer unit normals of these faces. Let $r$ be the inradius and take $I$ as the origin, so that $A_1=r\,n_A$, $B_1=r\,n_B$, $C_1=r\,n_C$, $D_1=r\,n_D$.

Step 1: $S_An_A+S_Bn_B+S_Cn_C+S_Dn_D=0$. For this step only, write $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ for the vectors $\overrightarrow{DA},\overrightarrow{DB},\overrightarrow{DC}$, named so that $(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c>0$. Then $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ is perpendicular to the face $DAB$ and points to the side of $C$, so $2S_Cn_C=\mathbf b\times\mathbf a$; similarly $2S_An_A=\mathbf c\times\mathbf b$ and $2S_Bn_B=\mathbf a\times\mathbf c$. For the face $ABC$ the vector $(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c+\mathbf a\times\mathbf b+\mathbf c\times\mathbf a$ has positive scalar product with $\mathbf a$, so it points away from $D$ and equals $2S_Dn_D$. The sum of the four vectors is $0$.

Step 2: the point $E$. The plane $C_1D_1I$ passes through the origin and is spanned by $n_C$ and $n_D$. The point \[E_0=\frac{S_A\,A_1+S_B\,B_1}{S_A+S_B}=\frac{r\,(S_An_A+S_Bn_B)}{S_A+S_B}=-\frac{r\,(S_Cn_C+S_Dn_D)}{S_A+S_B}\] lies on the segment $A_1B_1$ and in this plane. The segment is not contained in the plane (otherwise all four normals would be coplanar, which is impossible for a tetrahedron), so $E=E_0$ and \[A_1E:EB_1=S_B:S_A .\tag{1}\] Step 3: distances to a plane through $CD$. Let $\Pi$ be the plane through $C$, $D$ and $E$ ($E$ is inside the tetrahedron, so it is not on the line $CD$). All points $X$ of the open half-plane of the face $BCD$ bounded by the line $CD$ satisfy $\operatorname{dist}(X,\Pi)=\operatorname{dist}(X,CD)\cdot\sin\theta_1$ with the same angle $\theta_1$ (the angle between this half-plane and $\Pi$), and they lie on one side of $\Pi$; similarly for the half-plane of the face $ACD$ with an angle $\theta_2$. The triangles $A_1CD$ and $B_1CD$ are congruent (their sides are $CD$ and the tangent segments from $C$ and from $D$), so $A_1$ and $B_1$ are at the same distance $\rho$ from the line $CD$. Since $E\in\Pi$ lies between $A_1$ and $B_1$, \[\frac{A_1E}{EB_1}=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(A_1,\Pi)}{\operatorname{dist}(B_1,\Pi)}=\frac{\rho\sin\theta_1}{\rho\sin\theta_2},\qquad\text{so by (1)}\qquad\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2}=\frac{S_B}{S_A},\] and $A_1$, $B_1$ are on opposite sides of $\Pi$.

Step 4: $A$ and $B$. The point $B$ lies in the half-plane of the face $BCD$, at distance $\dfrac{2S_A}{CD}$ from the line $CD$; the point $A$ lies in the half-plane of the face $ACD$, at distance $\dfrac{2S_B}{CD}$ from $CD$. Therefore \[\operatorname{dist}(B,\Pi)=\frac{2S_A\sin\theta_1}{CD}=\frac{2S_B\sin\theta_2}{CD}=\operatorname{dist}(A,\Pi),\] and $A$, $B$ are on opposite sides of $\Pi$ (as $B_1$ and $A_1$ are). A plane that separates $A$ and $B$ and is equidistant from them passes through the midpoint of $AB$. So the midpoint of $AB$ lies in the plane $\Pi=CDE$.

Solution 3 (A pencil of planes through CD)

Take $I$ as the origin and the radius of the sphere as the unit of length. Write $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ for the position vectors of $A_1$ and $B_1$; they are distinct unit vectors, and $\mathbf a\ne-\mathbf b$, because the planes $BCD$ and $CDA$ are not parallel (they share the line $CD$). The face $BCD$ is the plane tangent to the sphere at $A_1$, that is $\{X:\ \mathbf a\cdot X=1\}$; similarly the face $CDA$ is $\{X:\ \mathbf b\cdot X=1\}$. Consider the affine functions \[F(X)=1-\mathbf a\cdot X,\qquad G(X)=1-\mathbf b\cdot X .\] Values at the vertices. The points $C$ and $D$ lie on both faces, so $F$ and $G$ vanish at $C$, at $D$ and hence on the whole line $CD$. Also $F(B)=0$ and $G(A)=0$. Put $u=F(A)$ and $v=G(B)$. The vertex $A$ is not on the face $BCD$ and lies on the same side of it as $I$, where $F(I)=1>0$; so $u>0$, and in the same way $v>0$.

The plane $C_1D_1I$. The radius $IC_1$ is perpendicular to the face $DAB$ and $ID_1$ to the face $ABC$; both faces contain $AB$. So the position vectors of $C_1$ and $D_1$ are perpendicular to $B-A$, and they are not parallel (the two faces are different planes through $AB$). Hence the plane through $I$, $C_1$, $D_1$ is $\{X:\ X\cdot(B-A)=0\}$.

The point $E$. A point of the segment $A_1B_1$ is $t\mathbf a+(1-t)\mathbf b$ with $0\le t\le1$. Using $\mathbf a\cdot B=1$, $\mathbf a\cdot A=1-u$, $\mathbf b\cdot A=1$, $\mathbf b\cdot B=1-v$, \[\bigl(t\mathbf a+(1-t)\mathbf b\bigr)\cdot(B-A)=t\,u-(1-t)\,v .\] So $E=t\mathbf a+(1-t)\mathbf b$ with $tu=(1-t)v$, i.e. $t=\dfrac{v}{u+v}$, and $0<t<1$. Then \[F(E)=1-t-(1-t)\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=(1-t)(1-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\qquad G(E)=t(1-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b).\] The midpoint. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$. An affine function takes at a midpoint the average of its values at the ends, so $F(M)=\frac u2$ and $G(M)=\frac v2$.

One plane through all of them. Let \[L(X)=v\,F(X)-u\,G(X)=(v-u)-(v\,\mathbf a-u\,\mathbf b)\cdot X .\] Since $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ are not parallel and $u,v>0$, the vector $v\mathbf a-u\mathbf b$ is not zero, so $\Pi=\{X:\ L(X)=0\}$ is a plane. It contains the line $CD$ (where $F=G=0$), the point $M$ (as $v\cdot\frac u2-u\cdot\frac v2=0$) and the point $E$ (as $L(E)=(1-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\bigl(v(1-t)-ut\bigr)=0$). Finally, $E$ is not on the line $CD$, because $F(E)=(1-t)(1-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)>0$ (recall $t<1$ and $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b<1$ for distinct unit vectors). Therefore $\Pi$ is the plane $CDE$, and it contains the midpoint $M$ of $AB$.

Problem 8

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $a\le b$ be odd integers greater than $1$. On a square grid of cell side length $1$, square napkins of size $2\times2$ are placed along the grid lines so that no cell is covered by more than one napkin. It turns out that for every axis-parallel grid rectangle with horizontal side length $a$ and vertical side length $b$, its lower-left corner is the center of one of the napkins if and only if its upper-right corner is the center of one of the napkins. Find the smallest positive number $\alpha$ such that for every positive integer $N$, one can always find an $N\times N$ grid square containing at most \[\alpha N^2\] napkins entirely inside it.

Show the answer

$\alpha=\dfrac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4(ab+b-a)}$

Show the solution

Solution (A pinwheel example, and holes charged to napkins)

Answer: $\alpha=\dfrac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4(ab+b-a)}$. Put \[D=ab+b-a=(a+1)(b-1)+1,\qquad\alpha_0=\frac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4D}=\frac14\Bigl(1-\frac1D\Bigr).\] We prove (A) that no $\alpha<\alpha_0$ has the required property and (B)–(D) that $\alpha_0$ has it.

Throughout, “cell $(x,y)$” is the unit cell with lower-left corner $(x,y)$, and a cell not covered by a napkin is called a hole. The base of a napkin is its upper-right cell: the napkin with base $(x,y)$ consists of the cells $(x-1,y-1),(x,y-1),(x-1,y),(x,y)$, and its centre is the point $(x,y)$. By the condition of the problem, there is a napkin with base $\beta$ if and only if there is a napkin with base $\beta+v$, where $v=(a,b)$; that is, the translation by $v$ maps the set of napkins onto itself.

(A) An arrangement with few holes. For a cell $(x,y)$ let $\varphi(x,y)\in\{0,1,\dots,D-1\}$ be the residue of $x-(a+1)y$ modulo $D$. Split the residues $0,1,\dots,D-2$ into $\frac{b-1}{2}$ consecutive blocks of length $2a+2$, and let \[U=\Bigl\{\,j(2a+2)+2i\ :\ 0\le j\le\tfrac{b-3}{2},\ 0\le i\le\tfrac{a-1}{2}\Bigr\}.\] The sets $\{u,\ u+1,\ u+a+1,\ u+a+2\}$, $u\in U$, are pairwise disjoint and their union is $\{0,1,\dots,D-2\}$: inside the $j$-th block the pairs $\{u,u+1\}$ fill its first half and the pairs $\{u+a+1,u+a+2\}$ fill its second half.

Rule: put a napkin on the cells $(x,y),(x+1,y),(x,y+1),(x+1,y+1)$ exactly when $\varphi(x,y+1)\in U$. If $\varphi(x,y+1)=u$, the four cells of this napkin have the residues \[\varphi(x,y+1)=u,\quad\varphi(x+1,y+1)=u+1,\quad\varphi(x,y)=u+a+1,\quad\varphi(x+1,y)=u+a+2\] (as integers, since $u+a+2\le D-2$). Consequently:

  • A cell with residue $D-1$ is a hole.

  • A cell with residue $\rho\le D-2$ is covered by exactly one napkin. Indeed $\rho$ lies in exactly one of the four-element sets above, and its place in the set ($u$, $u+1$, $u+a+1$ or $u+a+2$) tells which corner of a napkin the cell must be (upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right); the napkin having the cell at this corner is prescribed by the rule (its upper-left cell has residue $u\in U$). No other napkin of the arrangement contains the cell: a napkin containing it at some corner would put $\rho$ at the corresponding place of its own four-element set.

  • $\varphi(x+a,y+b)=\varphi(x,y)$, because $a-(a+1)b=-D$. So the arrangement is mapped onto itself by the translations by $\pm v$, and the condition of the problem holds.

In every row $\varphi$ increases by $1$ (modulo $D$) from each cell to the next, so among any $D$ consecutive cells of a row exactly one is a hole. Take any $N\times N$ square and the $(N-2)\times(N-2)$ square in its middle. The inner square contains at most $(N-2)\bigl(\frac{N-2}{D}+1\bigr)$ holes, and every napkin covering one of its cells lies inside the big square. Hence the big square contains at least \[\frac14\Bigl((N-2)^2-\frac{(N-2)^2}{D}-(N-2)\Bigr)=\alpha_0(N-2)^2-\frac{N-2}{4}\] napkins. For every $\alpha<\alpha_0$ this is greater than $\alpha N^2$ when $N$ is large. So no $\alpha<\alpha_0$ has the required property: $\alpha\ge\alpha_0$. (The figure shows the arrangement for $a=3$, $b=5$, $D=17$: four napkins and one hole per $17$ cells.)

(B) Every napkin has a hole nearby. Let $\beta=(x,y)$ be the base of a napkin and let $P(\beta)$ be the $a\times b$ rectangle formed by the cells $(x+i,y+j)$, $0\le i\le a-1$, $0\le j\le b-1$. Colour the cell $(x+i,y+j)$ black if $i+j$ is even and white otherwise. Since $a$ and $b$ are odd, the four corner cells of $P(\beta)$ are black, and $P(\beta)$ has $\frac{ab+1}{2}$ black and $\frac{ab-1}{2}$ white cells.

The cells that a napkin shares with $P(\beta)$ form a rectangle with sides $0$, $1$ or $2$. If they form a single cell, the napkin sticks out of $P(\beta)$ both horizontally and vertically (recall $a,b\ge3$), so this cell is a corner of $P(\beta)$ and it is black; otherwise the common part is empty, a $1\times2$ or $2\times1$ domino, or the whole napkin, and contains equally many black and white cells. Hence every napkin covers at least as many black cells of $P(\beta)$ as white ones. Moreover, our napkin meets $P(\beta)$ only in the corner cell $(x,y)$, and the napkin with base $\beta+v$ meets $P(\beta)$ only in the opposite corner cell $(x+a-1,y+b-1)$; these are two different napkins (their bases differ), and each of them covers one black cell and no white cell of $P(\beta)$. As the napkins do not overlap, adding over all napkins shows that the covered black cells of $P(\beta)$ outnumber the covered white cells by at least $2$, while all black cells outnumber all white cells by exactly $1$. Therefore $P(\beta)$ has more uncovered white cells than uncovered black cells; in particular $P(\beta)$ contains a white hole. Choose one of them and call it $h(\beta)$.

(C) Each hole is chosen by at most $m=\frac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4}$ napkins. Fix a hole $h=(p,q)$. If $h=h(\beta)$, then $\beta=(p-i,q-j)$ with $0\le i\le a-1$, $0\le j\le b-1$ and $i+j$ odd; there are $\frac{ab-1}{2}$ such pairs $(i,j)$. Two different napkins cannot have bases $\beta$ and $\beta'$ with $\beta'-\beta\in\{(\pm1,0),(0,\pm1),(\pm1,\pm1)\}$, since then they would share a cell. Group the pairs $(i,j)$ by the odd sum $s=i+j$. In one group, the pairs $(i,j)$ and $(i+1,j-1)$ give bases differing by $(-1,1)$, so the group is a chain in which no two consecutive members are both bases; a chain of length $\ell$ contains at most $\lceil \ell/2\rceil$ bases. Since $a\le b$, the chain with sum $s$ has length $s+1$ for $s\le a-1$ and $a+b-1-s$ for $s\ge b-1$ (both even, since $s$ is odd and $a+b$ is even), and length $a$, which is odd, for $a-1<s<b-1$, that is, for the $\frac{b-a}{2}$ values $s=a,a+2,\dots,b-2$. The lengths of all chains add up to $\frac{ab-1}{2}$, so $h$ is chosen by at most \[\frac12\Bigl(\frac{ab-1}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{ab+b-a-1}{4}=\frac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4}=m\] napkins.

(D) Counting. Let an $M\times M$ grid square $K$ contain $k$ napkins entirely. Their bases are cells of $K$, so by (B) their chosen holes lie in the $(M+a-1)\times(M+b-1)$ rectangle $K'$ with the same lower-left corner as $K$, and by (C) there are at least $k/m$ different chosen holes. The $4k$ cells of these napkins and these holes are different cells of $K'$, so \[k\Bigl(4+\frac1m\Bigr)\le(M+a-1)(M+b-1)<(M+a)(M+b).\] Since $4+\frac1m=\frac{4\left((a+1)(b-1)+1\right)}{(a+1)(b-1)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_0}$, this means \[k\le\alpha_0(M+a)(M+b).\tag{$*$}\] Now fix $N$ and suppose that every $N\times N$ grid square contains more than $\alpha_0N^2$ napkins, that is, at least $c$ napkins for some integer $c>\alpha_0N^2$ (for instance $c=\lfloor\alpha_0N^2\rfloor+1$). For an integer $s\ge1$, a square of side $M=sN$ is cut into $s^2$ squares $N\times N$; a napkin lying entirely in one of them lies entirely in the big square, so the big square contains at least $s^2c$ napkins, and by $(*)$ \[s^2c\le\alpha_0(sN+a)(sN+b),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad c\le\alpha_0\Bigl(N+\frac as\Bigr)\Bigl(N+\frac bs\Bigr).\] Letting $s\to\infty$ gives $c\le\alpha_0N^2$, a contradiction. Hence for every $N$ some $N\times N$ grid square contains at most $\alpha_0N^2$ napkins, i.e. $\alpha_0$ has the required property.

Together with (A), the smallest such number is \[\alpha=\boxed{\dfrac{(a+1)(b-1)}{4(ab+b-a)}}.\]
