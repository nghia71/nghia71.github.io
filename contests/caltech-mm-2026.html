Individual Round

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Geometric Sequences

The arithmetic sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ with $a_1\neq a_2$ satisfies that $a_1,a_2,a_n$ and $a_1,a_4,a_p$ are both geometric sequences. In this case, $p$ can be represented as $cn+d$ for some integers $c$ and $d$. Compute $c\cdot d$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the first four terms of an arithmetic sequence using a starting value and a common difference. In each of the two listed triples, mark which term is in the middle.
Hint 2: key idea
For three terms in geometric progression, the middle term squared equals the product of the outer terms. Write that relation for each triple using $a_k=a+(k-1)\delta$. (LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns)
Hint 3: approach
Use the first relation to express the common difference in terms of $a$ and $n$. Substitute into the second relation, simplify to a linear expression in $n$ for $p$, and identify its coefficient and constant term.
Common trap
Check that $a$ and $\delta$ are nonzero before dividing by them.
Show the answer

$-180$

Show the solution – Translate both conditions

Write $a_k=a+(k-1)\delta$ with $a=a_1$ and $\delta=a_2-a_1\ne0$. Three numbers $u,v,w$ in geometric progression satisfy $v^2=uw$. Note $a\neq0$: if $a_1=0$, then $a_2^2=a_1a_n=0$ would give $a_2=0=a_1$. The condition $a_2^2=a_1a_n$ reads $(a+\delta)^2=a\bigl(a+(n-1)\delta\bigr)$, i.e. $2a\delta+\delta^2=(n-1)a\delta$; dividing by $\delta$, $\delta=(n-3)a$. The condition $a_4^2=a_1a_p$ reads $(a+3\delta)^2=a\bigl(a+(p-1)\delta\bigr)$, i.e. $6a+9\delta=(p-1)a$. Substituting $\delta=(n-3)a$ and dividing by $a$: \[p-1=6+9(n-3),\qquad p=9n-20 .\] So $c=9$, $d=-20$ and $c\cdot d=\boxed{-180}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 27: Base Systems and Positional Arithmetic · Digit Patterns Across Bases

For positive integers $n$, let $f(n)$ denote the number whose base-$3$ expansion coincides with the binary expansion of $n$. Compute the number of positive integers less than or equal to $2026$ that can be written in the form $f(a+b)-f(a)-f(b)$ for some positive integers $a$ and $b$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try adding two small binary numbers that produce a carry. Rewrite the two addends and the sum using the same digit strings in base three, and compare the new values.
Hint 2: key idea
Write the binary addition rule in a single column with an incoming and an outgoing carry. When you weight that equation by the corresponding power of three, the carry enters the next column with a different place value. (LPS VI, Chapter 27: Base Systems and Positional Arithmetic)
Hint 3: approach
Sum the weighted column equations and shift the index on the incoming carries to isolate the contribution of each outgoing carry. To see which carry patterns are possible, try making the two addends equal and placing their ones at the desired carry sources. Use the resulting description of the base-three digits to set up a count within the given bound.
Common trap
A no-carry addition gives zero, which is outside the requested range.
Show the answer

$127$

Show the solution – Each carry contributes a power of 3

Write $a=\sum a_k2^k$, $b=\sum b_k2^k$ in binary and add them column by column: with carries $c_0=0$, $c_1,c_2,\dots\in\{0,1\}$ and result digits $s_k$, \[a_k+b_k+c_k=s_k+2c_{k+1}.\] Then $f(a+b)=\sum s_k3^k$ and $f(a)+f(b)=\sum(a_k+b_k)3^k=\sum(s_k+2c_{k+1}-c_k)3^k$. Since $\sum_k c_k3^k=\sum_k c_{k+1}3^{k+1}$, \[f(a+b)-f(a)-f(b)=\sum_k c_{k+1}\left(3^{k+1}-2\cdot3^k\right)=\sum_{k:\,c_{k+1}=1}3^k .\] So every value is a sum of distinct powers of $3$: a number whose base-$3$ digits are all $0$ or $1$, positive iff at least one carry occurs.

Conversely every nonempty finite set $K$ of positions occurs as the set of carry sources: take $a=b=\sum_{k\in K}2^k$. In the column $k\in K$ the sum is $2$ or $3$ (with the incoming carry), producing a carry; in a column $k\notin K$ the sum is $0$ or $1$, producing none. So the values are exactly the positive integers with base-$3$ digits in $\{0,1\}$.

Those up to $2026$: all such numbers with at most $7$ base-$3$ digits are at most $1111111_3=1093\le2026$, and those with $8$ or more digits are at least $3^7=2187$. So the count is $2^7-1=\boxed{127}$.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Constraint-Based Enumeration

Find the number of ways to fill each cell of a $10\times 10$ grid with a $0$ or a $1$ such that every $2\times 2$ subgrid contains exactly two $0$s and exactly two $1$s.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch two neighbouring rows of a smaller grid and check the condition one square at a time. Notice that consecutive checks reuse the same middle column of those two rows.
Hint 2: key idea
For two consecutive rows $u$ and $v$, record $e_j=u_j+v_j-1$ in each column. The rule on a square forces $e_j$ and $e_{j+1}$ to be negatives of each other. (LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration)
Hint 3: approach
Decide what the two rows look like when all the $e_j$ are zero and when their signs alternate without zeros. Use this to show what an alternating row allows and what a nonalternating row forces above and below it. Count the choices in the all-alternating case and in the case with a nonalternating row separately.
Common trap
When counting from a nonalternating row, exclude the alternating starting rows so the two counts stay disjoint.
Show the answer

$2046$

Show solution 1 – Alternating rows or complementary rows

Call a row alternating if it is $0101\ldots$ or $1010\ldots$

Lemma. If two consecutive rows $u,v$ satisfy the condition and $u$ is not alternating, then $v$ is the complement of $u$. Proof: put $e_j=u_j+v_j-1\in\{-1,0,1\}$. The condition for columns $j,j+1$ says $e_j+e_{j+1}=0$. So either all $e_j=0$, i.e. $v_j=1-u_j$ for all $j$, or all $|e_j|=1$ with alternating signs, in which case $u_j=v_j=\frac{1+e_j}{2}$ alternates, contrary to the assumption.

Counting. If every row is alternating, each pair of consecutive alternating rows has every $2\times2$ block with two $0$s and two $1$s, so any choice of the $10$ rows works: $2^{10}$ grids. If some row is not alternating, then by the lemma (applied downwards, and upwards by symmetry) every row is the complement of its neighbours, so the grid is determined by its first row, which can be any non-alternating row: $2^{10}-2$ grids; and these grids do satisfy the condition (each block is $u_j,u_{j+1},1-u_j,1-u_{j+1}$). The total is $2^{10}+2^{10}-2=\boxed{2046}$.

Show solution 2 – A sign change turns the grid into a sum

Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,10$ and replace each entry $g_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ by the sign \[y_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\,(2g_{ij}-1)\in\{1,-1\}.\] This is a bijection between fillings and sign arrays.

The condition. A $2\times2$ block has two $0$s and two $1$s exactly when the four numbers $2g-1$ in it add up to $0$. In the block with top-left cell $(i,j)$ the four factors $(-1)^{i+j}$ are $\varepsilon,-\varepsilon,-\varepsilon,\varepsilon$ (with $\varepsilon=(-1)^{i+j}$) at the cells $(i,j),(i,j+1),(i+1,j),(i+1,j+1)$. So the condition becomes \[y_{ij}-y_{i,j+1}-y_{i+1,j}+y_{i+1,j+1}=0,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad y_{i+1,j+1}-y_{i,j+1}=y_{i+1,j}-y_{ij}\] for all $i,j\le9$: the difference between two consecutive rows is the same in every column. Applying this repeatedly, $y_{ij}-y_{1j}=y_{i1}-y_{11}$, so \[y_{ij}=a_i+b_j\qquad\text{with } a_i=y_{i1},\ b_j=y_{1j}-y_{11}.\] Conversely every sign array of the form $y_{ij}=a_i+b_j$ satisfies all the block conditions, because then $y_{ij}-y_{i,j+1}-y_{i+1,j}+y_{i+1,j+1}=0$ identically.

Which such arrays consist of signs? If two of the $a_i$ differ, they are $1$ and $-1$. Then for each $j$ both $1+b_j$ and $-1+b_j$ must be $\pm1$, which forces $b_j=0$: every column of $y$ equals the column $(a_1,\dots,a_{10})$, so $y_{ij}$ depends only on $i$. If all $a_i$ are equal, then $y_{ij}=a_1+b_j$ depends only on $j$. Conversely, every sign array whose entries depend only on the row (or only on the column) has the form $a_i+b_j$.

Counting. The sign arrays that depend only on the row are $2^{10}$, those that depend only on the column are $2^{10}$, and the arrays in both families are the $2$ constant arrays. So the number of fillings is $2^{10}+2^{10}-2=\boxed{2046}$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Intersecting Chords and Circle Metrics

Two strips of paper with widths $10$ and $14$ overlap on a region that is a parallelogram with area $168$. A point $P$ lies inside this parallelogram such that the feet of the altitudes from $P$ to the four sides of the parallelogram all lie on a circle with area $50\pi$. The four possible points $P$ which satisfy this form another parallelogram. What is its area?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the two strips and mark their midlines. For a trial point $P$, draw its perpendiculars to both edges of each strip and label the pairs of feet.
Hint 2: key idea
The feet belonging to each strip are endpoints of a chord whose length is the strip width. The midline of that strip perpendicularly bisects the chord, so the two midlines locate the circle's centre. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the circle's radius and the two half-chords to find the possible signed projections of $P$ from the centre along the two strip directions. Use the strip widths and overlap area to recover the sine of the angle between the directions, and check that the candidate points and feet lie in the required places. In the two projection coordinates, identify the rectangle and determine how the projection map scales area.
Common trap
The area measured in the two projection coordinates generally differs from the area in the original plane.
Show the answer

$24$

Show the solution – The centre is the centre of the parallelogram

Let strip $1$ lie between parallel lines $\ell_1,\ell_1'$ at distance $10$, with unit direction $\mathbf d_1$, and strip $2$ between $\ell_2,\ell_2'$ at distance $14$, with unit direction $\mathbf d_2$; let $\theta$ be the angle between the strips. The parallelogram has area $\frac{10\cdot14}{\sin\theta}=168$, so $\sin\theta=\frac56$. Let $O$ be its centre, the intersection of the two midlines $m_1,m_2$ of the strips.

The feet $F_1\in\ell_1$, $F_1'\in\ell_1'$ from $P$ lie on the line through $P$ perpendicular to strip $1$, so $F_1F_1'$ is a chord of length $10$ of the circle $\omega$ through the four feet, perpendicular to $\ell_1$; its perpendicular bisector is $m_1$. Likewise the other two feet form a chord of length $14$ whose perpendicular bisector is $m_2$. So the centre of $\omega$ is $O$, and with $t_1=\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf d_1$ (the signed distance from $O$ to the first chord) and $t_2=\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf d_2$, \[R^2=5^2+t_1^2=7^2+t_2^2=50 .\] So $|t_1|=5$ and $|t_2|=1$. Conversely, if $t_1=\pm5$, $t_2=\pm1$, the four feet are at distance $\sqrt{5^2+5^2}=\sqrt{7^2+1^2}=\sqrt{50}$ from $O$.

These four points are admissible. Take coordinates with origin $O$, $\mathbf d_1=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf d_2=(c,s)$, $s=\frac56$; after a reflection in $m_1$ we may assume $c=\frac{\sqrt{11}}6$. With the unit normals $\mathbf n_1=(0,1)$, $\mathbf n_2=(-s,c)$, the parallelogram is $|X\cdot\mathbf n_1|\le5$, $|X\cdot\mathbf n_2|\le7$. The point $P=(x,y)$ has $x=t_1$ and $cx+sy=t_2$, so \[|P\cdot\mathbf n_1|=|y|=\frac{|t_2-ct_1|}{s}\le\frac{1+5c}{s}=\frac{6+5\sqrt{11}}5<5,\qquad |P\cdot\mathbf n_2|=\frac{|ct_2-t_1|}{s}\le\frac{c+5}{s}=\frac{30+\sqrt{11}}5<7,\] so $P$ is inside. The feet on the sides of strip $1$ are $t_1\mathbf d_1\pm5\mathbf n_1$, with $|X\cdot\mathbf n_2|=|-st_1\pm5c|\le5(s+c)<7$; the feet on the sides of strip $2$ are $t_2\mathbf d_2\pm7\mathbf n_2$, with $|X\cdot\mathbf n_1|=|st_2\pm7c|\le s+7c<5$. So all feet lie on the sides themselves.

The map $\mathbf v\mapsto(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf d_1,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf d_2)$ is linear with determinant $\pm\sin\theta$, so it multiplies areas by $\sin\theta=\frac56$. It sends the four points $P$ to the vertices $(\pm5,\pm1)$ of a $10\times2$ rectangle, of area $20$. Hence the four points form a parallelogram of area $20\div\frac56=\boxed{24}$.

Problem 10

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry · Spatial Coordinate and Vector Methods

A cube is suspended in the air with its eight vertices $1,2,3,4,5,6,7$, and $8$ units off of the ground. What is its side length?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Start at the lowest vertex and draw the three edges leaving it. For each of the other vertices, record which of those edge directions you must follow to reach it.
Hint 2: key idea
The height gained along a path is the sum of the vertical components of its edges. Thus the differences from the lowest height are the subset sums of the three components. (LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Order the components and use the smallest missing height difference to find each new component in turn. Divide the edge vectors by their common length to get an orthonormal basis, then express the upward unit vector in that basis. Use its squared length to set up an equation for the side length.
Common trap
The three edge components are height gains measured from the lowest vertex, not heights measured from the ground.
Show the answer

$\sqrt{21}$

Show solution 1 – Vertical components of three edges

Let $V$ be the lowest vertex and $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$ the three edge vectors from $V$; they are mutually perpendicular with length $s$. Let $a,b,c$ be their vertical components. The eight vertices are $V+\varepsilon_1\mathbf e_1+\varepsilon_2\mathbf e_2+\varepsilon_3\mathbf e_3$ with $\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}$, so their heights are $1+\{0,a,b,c,a+b,b+c,c+a,a+b+c\}$, and these must be $1,2,\dots,8$. In particular $a,b,c\ge0$ (as $V$ is lowest), and the eight subset sums of $\{a,b,c\}$ are $0,1,\dots,7$.

Order $a\le b\le c$. The eight subset sums are distinct, so $0<a<b<c$, and the smallest positive sum is $a=1$. Then $b\ge2$, so every sum of two or three of the numbers is at least $3$; hence the sum $2$ is $b$ or $c$, and as $b\le c$, $b=2$. The sums not involving $c$ are $0,1,2,3$, and those involving $c$ are at least $c$; so $4=c$. Thus $\{a,b,c\}=\{1,2,4\}$.

Now write $\mathbf e_i=s\mathbf u_i$ with $\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\mathbf u_3$ orthonormal. The vertical unit vector $\mathbf k$ has coordinates $(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1,\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_2,\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_3)$ in this orthonormal basis, so \[1=|\mathbf k|^2=\sum_i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_i)^2=\frac{a^2+b^2+c^2}{s^2}=\frac{1+4+16}{s^2}.\] Hence $s=\boxed{\sqrt{21}}$. (Such a cube exists: complete $(1,2,4)/\sqrt{21}$ to an orthonormal frame.)

Show solution 2 – The variance of the heights

Let $s$ be the side length, $M$ the centre of the cube, and $\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\mathbf u_3$ unit vectors along three mutually perpendicular edges. The vertices are $M+\frac s2(\varepsilon_1\mathbf u_1+\varepsilon_2\mathbf u_2+\varepsilon_3\mathbf u_3)$ with $\varepsilon_i\in\{1,-1\}$. Let $\mathbf k$ be the upward unit vector and $c_i=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_i$. In the orthonormal basis $\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\mathbf u_3$ the vector $\mathbf k$ has coordinates $(c_1,c_2,c_3)$, so $c_1^2+c_2^2+c_3^2=|\mathbf k|^2=1$.

The height of a vertex minus the height $m$ of $M$ is $\frac s2(\varepsilon_1c_1+\varepsilon_2c_2+\varepsilon_3c_3)$. Average the square of this over the eight sign choices. Each $\varepsilon_i^2=1$, and each product $\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j$ with $i\ne j$ is $+1$ for four choices and $-1$ for the other four, so the mixed terms average to $0$. Hence \[\frac18\sum_{\text{vertices}}(\text{height}-m)^2=\frac{s^2}{4}\left(c_1^2+c_2^2+c_3^2\right)=\frac{s^2}4 .\] Also the eight heights average to $m$, since the vertices come in pairs symmetric about $M$.

The heights are $1,2,\dots,8$, with mean $m=\frac92$ and \[\frac18\sum_{k=1}^8\Bigl(k-\frac92\Bigr)^2=\frac18\cdot2\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}+\frac94+\frac{25}4+\frac{49}4\Bigr)=\frac{21}4 .\] Therefore $\frac{s^2}4=\frac{21}4$ and $s=\boxed{\sqrt{21}}$. Such a cube exists: tilt a cube of side $\sqrt{21}$ so that its edges from the lowest vertex rise by $1,2,4$ (take $(c_1,c_2,c_3)=(1,2,4)/\sqrt{21}$); its heights are $1$ plus the subset sums of $\{1,2,4\}$, namely $1,\dots,8$.

Problem 11

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

Suppose the polynomial $x^4+ax^3+69x^2+bx+100$ has real coefficients and roots $z_1,z_2,z_3$, and $z_4$, none of which are real. The possible values of $|z_1|+|z_2|+|z_3|+|z_4|$ form an open interval $(m,M)$. Compute $m$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Group the roots so that each group has the same distance from the origin. For a root $p+qi$, distinguish the real part $p$ from its modulus.
Hint 2: key idea
Real coefficients pair the roots with their conjugates, so write a real quadratic for each pair in terms of its modulus and real part. Compare the constant and quadratic coefficients of their product with the given polynomial. (LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
Use those coefficients to express the square of the sum of the two moduli in terms of the product of the real parts. Bound this product using the nonreal condition. To test sharpness, look for a family of admissible polynomials along which the sum of moduli approaches your bound.
Common trap
A strict lower bound alone does not identify the endpoint of an open interval without an approaching family.
Show the answer

$14$

Show the solution – Pair the conjugate roots

The coefficients are real and no root is real, so the roots are $z,\bar z,w,\bar w$. Let $|z|=r$, $|w|=s$, $\operatorname{Re}z=p$, $\operatorname{Re}w=q$, with $|p|<r$, $|q|<s$ (the roots are not real). Then \[x^4+ax^3+69x^2+bx+100=(x^2-2px+r^2)(x^2-2qx+s^2).\] Comparing constant terms, $r^2s^2=100$, so $rs=10$; comparing $x^2$ terms, $r^2+s^2+4pq=69$. The required sum is $2(r+s)$, and \[(r+s)^2=r^2+s^2+2rs=89-4pq>89-4rs=49,\] since $pq\le|p||q|<rs$. So $|z_1|+|z_2|+|z_3|+|z_4|>14$.

Values arbitrarily close to $14$ occur. For $0<\lambda<1$ choose $r,s>0$ with $rs=10$ and $r^2+s^2=69-40\lambda$ (possible since $69-40\lambda>29>20=2rs$), and put $p=\sqrt\lambda\,r$, $q=\sqrt\lambda\,s$. Then $|p|<r$, $|q|<s$ and $4pq=40\lambda$, so $(x^2-2px+r^2)(x^2-2qx+s^2)$ has the required form with no real roots, and the sum of the moduli is $2(r+s)=2\sqrt{89-40\lambda}$, which tends to $14$ as $\lambda\to1$. Hence $m=\boxed{14}$.

Team Round

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Forcing Structure via Extremes

Call a binary string encompassing if by erasing some digits from the string, one can obtain any binary string of length $2026$.

  • (a) Find, with proof, the minimum possible length $N$ of an encompassing string.

  • (b) Find, with proof, the number of length $N$ encompassing strings (where $N$ is the answer to part (a) of the question).

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try target strings of length two and test which ones you can obtain by erasing digits from a short candidate string. Remember that the surviving digits keep their original order.
Hint 2: key idea
Match any target greedily by always using the earliest available occurrence of its next digit. To force the match as far to the right as possible at one step, ask for the digit whose next occurrence is later. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Use these hard choices to bound how many positions a match must advance at each step, then look for a string that attains the bound. At minimum length every greedy step must make its smallest possible advance, so work out what this forces on the string before counting.
Common trap
Erasing digits preserves order, so having enough copies of each digit alone does not show that a string is encompassing.
Show the answer

(a) $N=4052$; (b) $2^{2026}$

Show solution 1 – An adversarial target string

Let $n=2026$. A string $t$ can be obtained from $w$ by erasing digits iff the greedy procedure succeeds: match $t_1$ with the first occurrence of $t_1$ in $w$, then $t_2$ with the first occurrence of $t_2$ after that, and so on (any embedding can be shifted left to the greedy one).

Lower bound. Let $w$ be encompassing. Build a target $t$ adversarially: having matched $t_1,\dots,t_{k-1}$ greedily up to position $q_{k-1}$ ($q_0=0$), let $t_k$ be the digit whose first occurrence after position $q_{k-1}$ is later (if one digit does not occur after $q_{k-1}$, choose it, and the matching fails). Then $t_k$ is matched at the first position $q_k$ such that both digits occur in positions $q_{k-1}+1,\dots,q_k$; in particular $q_k\ge q_{k-1}+2$. Since $w$ is encompassing, all $n$ digits of $t$ get matched, so the length of $w$ is at least $q_n\ge 2n=4052$. The string $(01)^{n}$ has length $4052$ and is encompassing (take the $k$-th digit of any target from the $k$-th block $01$). So $N=\boxed{4052}$.

Counting. Split a string of length $4052$ into $2026$ consecutive blocks of two digits. If every block is $01$ or $10$, the string is encompassing, as above. Conversely, for an encompassing $w$ of length $4052$, the adversarial matching above has $q_k\ge q_{k-1}+2$ and $q_{2026}\le4052$, so $q_k=2k$ for every $k$. By the choice of $q_k$, the two positions $2k-1,2k$ contain both digits: every block is $01$ or $10$. Hence the number of encompassing strings of length $N$ is $\boxed{2^{2026}}$.

Show solution 2 – Count the digits, then induct

Write $k$ for the length of the targets ($k=2026$) and call a string $k$-encompassing if every binary string of length $k$ can be obtained from it by erasing digits.

(a) A $k$-encompassing string must contain the target $00\ldots0$ ($k$ zeros), so it has at least $k$ zeros, and likewise at least $k$ ones: its length is at least $2k$. The string $(01)^k$ of length $2k$ works: the $i$-th digit of any target can be taken from the $i$-th block $01$. Hence $N=2\cdot2026=\boxed{4052}$.

(b) Claim. A string of length $2k$ is $k$-encompassing exactly when it is a concatenation of $k$ blocks, each $01$ or $10$. We prove this by induction on $k$. For $k=1$ the string must contain both digits, so it is $01$ or $10$.

Let $k\ge2$ and let $w=w_1w_2\ldots w_{2k}$ be $k$-encompassing. By (a) it has exactly $k$ zeros and $k$ ones.

  • $w_1\ne w_2$. Suppose $w_1=w_2$ and let $b$ be the other digit; its first occurrence is at some position $p\ge3$. Take any target that starts with $b$. Its first digit uses a position $\ge p$, so the remaining $k-1$ digits, which can be any string of length $k-1$, come from $w_{p+1}\ldots w_{2k}$. Then this piece is $(k-1)$-encompassing, so by (a) its length $2k-p$ is at least $2(k-1)$, i.e. $p\le2$, a contradiction.

  • The rest. Let $b=w_2$; it is the digit different from $w_1$, so it first occurs at position $2$. Any target starting with $b$ uses a position $\ge2$ for its first digit, so its other $k-1$ digits (an arbitrary string of length $k-1$) come from $w_3\ldots w_{2k}$. Hence $w_3\ldots w_{2k}$ is $(k-1)$-encompassing, of length $2(k-1)$, and by induction it is a concatenation of $k-1$ blocks $01$ or $10$.

Conversely, if $w_1w_2$ is $01$ or $10$ and $w_3\ldots w_{2k}$ is $(k-1)$-encompassing, then $w$ is $k$-encompassing: the first digit of a target is taken from $w_1w_2$ and the rest from $w_3\ldots w_{2k}$. This proves the claim (the induction also gives that every concatenation of such blocks works).

Each of the $2026$ blocks is chosen independently as $01$ or $10$, so the number of encompassing strings of length $N$ is $\boxed{2^{2026}}$.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

In acute scalene triangle $ABC$, the foot of the altitude from $A$ is $D$. A line $\ell$ passes through $A$, and the feet of the altitudes from $B$ and $C$ to $\ell$ are $B'$ and $C'$, respectively. Show that the line through $B'$ perpendicular to $AC$ and the line through $C'$ perpendicular to $AB$ intersect on $AD$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch $\ell$ slanting through $A$ and mark the two projection feet. For each perpendicular in the claim, locate where it would cross the altitude $AD$.
Hint 2: key idea
Put $D$ at the origin, with $BC$ horizontal and $AD$ vertical. Use a unit vector along $\ell$ to express the signed distances of $B'$ and $C'$ from $A$. (LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation)
Hint 3: approach
Write $B'$ and $C'$ using their signed projections along $\ell$. Put $X=(0,y)$ on $AD$ and express perpendicularity of $XB'$ and $AC$ with a dot product. Solve for $y$ in terms of both projection lengths, then see what changes when you exchange $B$ and $C$.
Common trap
A projection foot can lie on either side of $A$ on $\ell$, so keep the sign of its projection.
Show solution 1 – A symmetric formula for the intersection

Put $D$ at the origin with $A=(0,a)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$, where $a>0$ and $b<0<c$ (the triangle is acute, so $D$ lies between $B$ and $C$; we only need $a\ne0$). Let $\mathbf u=(\cos t,\sin t)$ be a unit vector along $\ell$. The foot of $B$ on $\ell$ is $B'=A+s_B\mathbf u$ and the foot of $C$ is $C'=A+s_C\mathbf u$, where \[s_B=(B-A)\cdot\mathbf u=b\cos t-a\sin t,\qquad s_C=(C-A)\cdot\mathbf u=c\cos t-a\sin t .\] The line through $B'$ perpendicular to $AC$ is $\{X:(X-B')\cdot(C-A)=0\}$ with $C-A=(c,-a)$. It is not parallel to $AD$ (the $y$-axis), since $AC$ is not horizontal. Its intersection with $AD$ is $X=(0,y)$ with $-c\,B'_x-a(y-B'_y)=0$, i.e. \[y=B'_y-\frac{c}{a}B'_x=a+s_B\sin t-\frac ca s_B\cos t=a-\frac{s_B}{a}\left(c\cos t-a\sin t\right)=a-\frac{s_Bs_C}{a}.\] This expression is symmetric in $B$ and $C$. Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$, the line through $C'$ perpendicular to $AB$ meets $AD$ at the same point $\left(0,a-\frac{s_Bs_C}a\right)$. So the two lines (which are not parallel, since $AB\nparallel AC$) intersect at this point of line $AD$.

Show solution 2 – Dot products with origin A

Take $A$ as the origin and write $\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ for the position vectors of $B,C$; they are not parallel since $A,B,C$ are not collinear. Let $\mathbf u$ be a unit vector along $\ell$. The foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $\ell$ is $B'=(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u$, and similarly $C'=(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u$.

The two lines. The line through $B'$ perpendicular to $AC$ is the set of points $X$ with $(X-B')\cdot\mathbf c=0$, that is, \[X\cdot\mathbf c=B'\cdot\mathbf c=(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf u)(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf u).\] The line through $C'$ perpendicular to $AB$ is the set of $X$ with \[X\cdot\mathbf b=C'\cdot\mathbf b=(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf u)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf u).\] These lines are perpendicular to the non-parallel vectors $\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf b$, so they are not parallel and meet at exactly one point $X$.

The common point is on $AD$. The right-hand sides are equal, so subtracting gives $X\cdot(\mathbf c-\mathbf b)=0$: the vector from $A$ to $X$ is perpendicular to $\overrightarrow{BC}=\mathbf c-\mathbf b$. The points with this property form the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$, which is the altitude $AD$. Hence the two lines meet on line $AD$. (The argument covers every position of $\ell$, including $B'=A$ or $C'=A$.)

Problem 1.30

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure · Variance and Concentration

A real number is randomly chosen between $0$ and $1$ (uniformly), and written in base $3$ as a string of $0$'s, $1$'s, and $2$'s: \[0.xyz\ldots_3=x\cdot\tfrac13+y\cdot\tfrac19+z\cdot\tfrac1{27}+\cdots .\] It is then interpreted as a decimal in base $9$: \[0.xyz\ldots_9=x\cdot\tfrac19+y\cdot\tfrac1{81}+z\cdot\tfrac1{729}+\cdots .\] What is the expected value of the square of this base-$9$ decimal?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the intervals corresponding to the possible first digits in base $3$. Split each one according to the second digit and compare the lengths of the smaller intervals.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the lengths of intervals for fixed digit strings to justify treating the digits as independent and uniformly distributed. Find the mean and variance of a single digit before accounting for the base-$9$ place weights. (LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Start with finitely many digits and sum their weighted means and variances, using the square of each place weight for its variance. Control the omitted digits by a geometric tail and evaluate the two resulting geometric series. Recall the identity relating the mean square to the mean and variance.
Common trap
The expected square includes the variance as well as the square of the mean.
Show the answer

$\frac{23}{960}$

Show solution 1 – Mean and variance of independent digits

For a uniformly random number in $[0,1]$, the base-$3$ digits $x_1,x_2,\dots$ are independent and each is uniform on $\{0,1,2\}$: each string of the first $n$ digits corresponds to an interval of length $3^{-n}$, so it has probability $3^{-n}$, the product of the $n$ one-digit probabilities (numbers with two expansions form a set of probability $0$). The base-$9$ value is $Y=\sum_{k\ge1}x_k9^{-k}$. We compute with the partial sums $Y_n=\sum_{k\le n}x_k9^{-k}$; since $0\le Y-Y_n\le2\sum_{k>n}9^{-k}=\frac{9^{-n}}4$ and $0\le Y\le\frac14$, the mean and the mean square of $Y_n$ tend to those of $Y$, so the infinite sums below are justified.

Each digit has mean $1$ and variance $\frac{0+1+4}{3}-1=\frac23$. By linearity, $E[Y]=\sum_k9^{-k}=\frac18$. Since the digits are independent, variances add, and the variance of $x_k9^{-k}$ is $\frac23\cdot81^{-k}$: \[\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\frac23\sum_{k\ge1}81^{-k}=\frac23\cdot\frac1{80}=\frac1{120}.\] Hence $E[Y^2]=\operatorname{Var}(Y)+E[Y]^2=\frac1{120}+\frac1{64}=\frac{8+15}{960}=\boxed{\dfrac{23}{960}}$.

Show solution 2 – Self-similarity

For a uniformly random number in $[0,1]$, the base-$3$ digits $x_1,x_2,\dots$ are independent and uniform on $\{0,1,2\}$: each string of the first $n$ digits corresponds to an interval of length $3^{-n}$, the product of the $n$ one-digit probabilities (numbers with two expansions form a set of probability $0$). The base-$9$ value $Y=\sum_k x_k9^{-k}$ satisfies \[Y=\frac{x_1+Y'}{9},\qquad Y'=\sum_{k\ge1}x_{k+1}9^{-k},\] where $Y'$ has the same distribution as $Y$ and is independent of $x_1$. All these expectations are finite, as $0\le Y\le\frac14$. Taking expectations, $m_1=E[Y]$ satisfies $9m_1=1+m_1$, so $m_1=\frac18$. Squaring, with $E[x_1]=1$ and $E[x_1^2]=\frac53$, \[81\,E[Y^2]=E[x_1^2]+2E[x_1]E[Y']+E[Y'^2]=\frac53+\frac14+E[Y^2],\] so $80\,E[Y^2]=\frac{23}{12}$ and $E[Y^2]=\boxed{\dfrac{23}{960}}$.
