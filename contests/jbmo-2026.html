Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $a,b,c$ be positive real numbers such that \[a^2+b^2+c^2\ge 3.\] Prove that \[\frac{a^4}{a^2+2b+2c}+\frac{b^4}{b^2+2c+2a}+\frac{c^4}{c^2+2a+2b}\ge\frac{3}{5}.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Titu's lemma and the power mean)

Write $s=a^2+b^2+c^2\ge 3$ and $p=a+b+c$. The three denominators add up to \[(a^2+2b+2c)+(b^2+2c+2a)+(c^2+2a+2b)=s+4p .\] By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in Engel form (Titu's lemma), \[\frac{a^4}{a^2+2b+2c}+\frac{b^4}{b^2+2c+2a}+\frac{c^4}{c^2+2a+2b}\ge\frac{(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2}{s+4p}=\frac{s^2}{s+4p}.\] By the QM–AM inequality, $p=a+b+c\le\sqrt{3(a^2+b^2+c^2)}=\sqrt{3s}$, so the denominator is at most $s+4\sqrt{3s}$ and \[\text{LHS}\ge\frac{s^2}{s+4\sqrt{3s}} .\] Put $t=\sqrt{s/3}$, so $t\ge 1$ and $s=3t^2$, $\sqrt{3s}=3t$. Then \[\frac{s^2}{s+4\sqrt{3s}}=\frac{9t^4}{3t^2+12t}=\frac{3t^3}{t+4},\qquad \frac{3t^3}{t+4}-\frac35=\frac{3(5t^3-t-4)}{5(t+4)}=\frac{3(t-1)(5t^2+5t+4)}{5(t+4)}\ge 0\] because $t\ge 1$. Hence the left-hand side is at least $\frac35$. Equality needs $t=1$ and $a=b=c$, that is $a=b=c=1$, and there the left-hand side is indeed $3\cdot\frac15=\frac35$.

Solution 2 (AM-GM on each term)

Let $s=a^2+b^2+c^2\ge 3$, $p=a+b+c$ and $D_a=a^2+2b+2c$, $D_b=b^2+2c+2a$, $D_c=c^2+2a+2b$ (all positive). The weight $\frac1{25}$ below is chosen so that the AM–GM step is an equality at $a=b=c=1$, where $D_a=5$. By AM–GM, \[\frac{a^4}{D_a}+\frac{D_a}{25}\ge 2\sqrt{\frac{a^4}{25}}=\frac{2a^2}{5},\] and similarly for $b$ and $c$. Adding the three inequalities and using $D_a+D_b+D_c=s+4p$, \[\text{LHS}\ge\frac{2s}{5}-\frac{s+4p}{25}=\frac{9s-4p}{25}.\] From $(a-1)^2\ge 0$ we get $2a\le a^2+1$, and similarly for $b,c$; adding, $2p\le s+3$. Therefore \[9s-4p\ge 9s-2(s+3)=7s-6\ge 7\cdot 3-6=15,\] and the left-hand side is at least $\frac{15}{25}=\frac35$. Equality holds for $a=b=c=1$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Determine all pairs $(a,b)$ of positive integers such that $a+1$ divides $b+2$ and $b$ divides $2a^2$.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(a,2a)$ for every positive integer $a$, together with $(2,1)$, $(3,2)$ and $(3,18)$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Working with both quotients)

Write \[b+2=k(a+1),\qquad 2a^2=mb\] with positive integers $k,m$.

The case $k=1$. Then $b=a-1$, so $a\ge 2$. Since $\gcd(a-1,a)=1$, the condition $a-1\mid 2a^2$ forces $a-1\mid 2$, so $a=2$ or $a=3$. This gives $(2,1)$ and $(3,2)$; both work ($3\mid 3$, $1\mid 8$ and $4\mid 4$, $2\mid 18$).

The case $k\ge 2$. Modulo $a+1$ we have $a\equiv-1$, so $2a^2\equiv 2$ and $b\equiv-2$. Hence $2\equiv 2a^2=mb\equiv-2m\pmod{a+1}$, that is, $a+1\mid 2m+2$. Let $t=\frac{2m+2}{a+1}$, a positive integer; then $m=\frac{t(a+1)}{2}-1$. Multiplying $b+2=k(a+1)$ by $m$ gives $mk(a+1)=2a^2+2m=2(a-1)(a+1)+(2m+2)$, and dividing by $a+1$, \[mk=2(a-1)+t.\tag{*}\] Since $k\ge 2$, $(*)$ gives $2(a-1)+t\ge 2m=t(a+1)-2$, i.e. $2a\ge ta$, so $t\le 2$.

  • $t=2$: then $m=a$, and $(*)$ reads $ak=2a$, so $k=2$ and $b=2(a+1)-2=2a$. Every pair $(a,2a)$ works: $a+1\mid 2a+2$ and $2a\mid 2a^2$.

  • $t=1$: then $m=\frac{a-1}{2}$, so $a$ is odd and $a\ge 3$ (as $m\ge 1$). Now $(*)$ reads $k(a-1)=2(2a-1)=4(a-1)+2$, so $a-1\mid 2$ and $a=3$, $m=1$, $k=5$, $b=5\cdot 4-2=18$. Indeed $4\mid 20$ and $18\mid 18$.

Hence the solutions are \[(a,b)=\boxed{(a,2a)\ (a\ge 1),\ (2,1),\ (3,2),\ (3,18)}.\]

Solution 2 (Squaring the congruence)

Write $b+2=k(a+1)$ with $k$ a positive integer.

If $k=1$, then $b=a-1$ (so $a\ge 2$); since $b=a-1\mid 2a^2$ and $\gcd(a-1,a)=1$, we get $a-1\mid 2$: the pairs $(2,1)$ and $(3,2)$, which both work. If $k=2$, then $b=2a$, which divides $2a^2$: every pair $(a,2a)$ is a solution.

Now let $k\ge 3$. From $ka=b+2-k$ we get $k^2a^2\equiv(k-2)^2\pmod b$. Since $b\mid 2a^2$, also $b\mid 2k^2a^2$, hence \[b\mid 2(k-2)^2 .\] As $k\ne 2$, the right-hand side is positive, so $b\le 2(k-2)^2<2k^2$. On the other hand $b=ka+(k-2)>ka$. Therefore $ka<2k^2$, i.e. $a<2k$. Let $m=\frac{2a^2}{b}$ (a positive integer). Then \[m<\frac{2a^2}{ka}=\frac{2a}{k}<4,\] so $m\in\{1,2,3\}$. The equation $mb=2a^2$ becomes $mk(a+1)=2a^2+2m$. Since $2a^2+2m\equiv 2+2m\pmod{a+1}$, we need $a+1\mid 2m+2$, and then $k=\frac{2a^2+2m}{m(a+1)}$:

  • $m=1$: $a+1\mid 4$, so $a\in\{1,3\}$, giving $k=2$ (not allowed here) or $k=\frac{20}{4}=5$; the latter gives $(a,b)=(3,18)$.

  • $m=2$: $a+1\mid 6$, so $a\in\{1,2,5\}$, giving $k=\frac32,\ 2,\ \frac92$; none is an integer $\ge 3$.

  • $m=3$: $a+1\mid 8$, so $a\in\{1,3,7\}$, giving $k=\frac43,\ 2,\ \frac{13}{3}$; none is an integer $\ge 3$.

So $k\ge 3$ gives only $(3,18)$ (check: $4\mid 20$, $18\mid 18$). All solutions: \[(a,b)=\boxed{(a,2a)\ (a\ge 1),\ (2,1),\ (3,2),\ (3,18)}.\]

Solution 3 (The two complementary divisors of $4a^2$)

Let $(a,b)$ be a solution and put $A=a+1$, so $A\ge 2$ and $2a=2A-2$. Since $b\mid 2a^2$, the number \[c=\frac{4a^2}{b}=2\cdot\frac{2a^2}{b}\] is an even positive integer, and $bc=(2a)^2$.

Both $b$ and $c$ are $\equiv-2\pmod A$. For $b$ this is the condition $A\mid b+2$. Modulo $A$ we have $a\equiv-1$, so with the integer $d=\frac{2a^2}{b}=\frac c2$ we get $bd=2a^2\equiv 2$, while $bd\equiv-2d$. Hence $-2d\equiv 2$, that is, $A\mid 2d+2=c+2$.

The case $b=c$. Then $b^2=(2a)^2$, so $b=2a$. Every such pair works: $a+1\mid 2a+2$ and $2a\mid 2a^2$.

The case $b\ne c$. As $bc=(2a)^2$, the smaller of $b,c$, call it $s$, satisfies $1\le s<2a=2A-2$, and the larger is $\ell=\frac{(2A-2)^2}{s}$. The positive integers that are $\equiv-2\pmod A$ are $A-2,\ 2A-2,\ 3A-2,\dots$ (for $A=2$ the first of these is $0$, which is not positive). The only one below $2A-2$ is $A-2$, so $A\ge 3$ and $s=A-2$. Then \[\ell=\frac{(2A-2)^2}{A-2}=\frac{4A(A-2)+4}{A-2}=4A+\frac{4}{A-2},\] so $A-2\mid 4$ and $A\in\{3,4,6\}$, with $\ell=16,\,18,\,25$ respectively. The condition $\ell\equiv-2\pmod A$ holds for $16\equiv1\equiv-2\pmod3$ and $18\equiv2\equiv-2\pmod4$, but fails for $25\equiv1\not\equiv4\pmod6$.

  • $A=3$, $a=2$: $\{b,c\}=\{1,16\}$. Since $c$ is even, $c=16$ and $b=1$.

  • $A=4$, $a=3$: $\{b,c\}=\{2,18\}$. Both are even, so $b=2$ or $b=18$.

These pairs work: $3\mid 3$ and $1\mid 8$; $4\mid 4$ and $2\mid 18$; $4\mid 20$ and $18\mid 18$. Hence the solutions are \[(a,b)=\boxed{(a,2a)\ (a\ge 1),\ (2,1),\ (3,2),\ (3,18)}.\]

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $n\ge 3$ be an integer. There are $n$ colored lamps arranged in a circle. Pressing the button of a lamp once changes its color as follows: \[\text{green}\to\text{red},\quad\text{red}\to\text{blue},\quad\text{blue}\to\text{green}.\] Initially, all lamps are colored red. Aladdin makes moves on these lamps. Each move consists of the following three steps:

  • he chooses a lamp $L$, without pressing its button;

  • he presses the button of the clockwise neighbor of $L$ once;

  • he presses the button of the counterclockwise neighbor of $L$ twice.

For each $n$, determine the maximum possible number of lamps that are colored green simultaneously, after finitely many moves.

Show the answer

$n$ if $3\mid n$; $n-1$ if $3\nmid n$ and $n$ is odd; $n-2$ if $3\nmid n$ and $n$ is even

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Invariants and telescoping moves)

Number the lamps $0,1,\dots,n-1$ clockwise (indices modulo $n$), so the clockwise neighbour of lamp $i$ is lamp $i+1$. Record the colours as residues modulo $3$: red $=0$, blue $=1$, green $=2$. Pressing a button adds $1$ (modulo $3$), so a move at lamp $i$ adds $1$ to the value $x_{i+1}$ and $2\equiv-1$ to the value $x_{i-1}$ (these are different lamps since $n\ge 3$). Initially all $x_j=0$.

Invariants. A move adds $1+2=3\equiv 0$ to the total, so $\sum_j x_j\equiv 0\pmod 3$ at all times. If $n$ is even, the lamps $i-1$ and $i+1$ have the same parity, so a move adds $+1$ and $-1$ inside one parity class; hence the sum over the even-numbered lamps and the sum over the odd-numbered lamps are each $\equiv 0\pmod 3$ at all times.

Upper bound. Let $n$ be odd. If all $n$ lamps were green, the total would be $2n$, which is $\equiv 0\pmod 3$ only when $3\mid n$. So for $3\nmid n$ at most $n-1$ lamps are green. Let $n=2h$ be even. Each parity class has $h$ lamps; if all of them were green, its sum would be $2h$, which is $\equiv 0\pmod 3$ only when $3\mid h$, i.e. $3\mid n$. So for $3\nmid n$ each class has at most $h-1$ green lamps, and at most $n-2$ lamps are green.

The transfer move. For any lamp $j$ and $k\ge 1$, make one move at each of the lamps $j+1,j+3,\dots,j+2k-1$. The move at $j+2r-1$ adds $+1$ at $j+2r$ and $-1$ at $j+2r-2$; adding up, all intermediate changes cancel and the net effect is $+1$ at lamp $j+2k$ and $-1$ at lamp $j$, nothing else. So we can move one unit from lamp $j$ to lamp $j+2k$. When $n$ is odd, the numbers $j+2k$ cover all residues modulo $n$, so a unit can be moved between any two lamps; when $n$ is even, between any two lamps of the same parity class.

Construction. Take any target values $y_j$ whose sum (for odd $n$), or whose sum in each parity class (for even $n$), is $\equiv 0\pmod 3$. Fix a base lamp $r$ (one in each class if $n$ is even). For every other lamp $j$ in the same class, move $y_j$ units from $r$ to $j$. Now lamp $j$ shows $y_j$, and lamp $r$ shows $-\sum_{j\ne r}y_j\equiv y_r$. So every such target is reachable. We use the targets:

  • $n$ odd, $3\mid n$: all lamps green (total $2n\equiv 0$): $n$ green lamps.

  • $n$ odd, $3\nmid n$: $n-1$ green lamps and one lamp with value $v\equiv-2(n-1)\equiv n+2\pmod 3$, which is $0$ (red) if $n\equiv 1$ and $1$ (blue) if $n\equiv 2\pmod 3$: $n-1$ green lamps.

  • $n=2h$ even: in each class, all $h$ lamps green if $3\mid h$; otherwise $h-1$ green lamps and one lamp with value $\equiv h+2\pmod 3$, which is red or blue. This gives $n$ green lamps if $3\mid n$ and $n-2$ otherwise.

Hence the maximum is \[\boxed{\ n\ \text{if } 3\mid n;\qquad n-1\ \text{if } 3\nmid n,\ n\text{ odd};\qquad n-2\ \text{if } 3\nmid n,\ n\text{ even}\ }.\]

Solution 2 (Counting moves per lamp)

Use the numbering and residues of Solution 1: a move at lamp $i$ adds $1$ to $x_{i+1}$ and $-1$ to $x_{i-1}$ (modulo $3$). The effects of moves simply add up, so the final state depends only on how many moves were made at each lamp, and only modulo $3$. If $c_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ moves are made at lamp $i$, lamp $j$ ends with \[x_j\equiv c_{j-1}-c_{j+1}\pmod 3,\] because lamp $j$ is the clockwise neighbour of lamp $j-1$ and the counterclockwise neighbour of lamp $j+1$. Conversely every choice of $(c_0,\dots,c_{n-1})$ can be realised. So the reachable states are exactly the vectors $(x_j)$ for which the system $c_{j+1}\equiv c_{j-1}-x_j$ $(j=0,\dots,n-1)$ has a solution.

Follow this system in steps of $2$: it determines $c_{j+1}$ from $c_{j-1}$. Starting from $c_1$ and walking $1\to3\to5\to\cdots$, we return to the start after $L$ steps, where $L=n$ if $n$ is odd and $L=n/2$ if $n$ is even. Going once around the walk, the value of $c$ changes by $-\sum x_j$, the sum taken over the lamps $j$ passed in between. Hence a solution exists iff these sums are $\equiv 0\pmod 3$:

  • $n$ odd: the walk visits every index, so the condition is $\sum_{j}x_j\equiv 0\pmod 3$ (and then any start value $c_1$ works);

  • $n$ even: the walk from $c_1$ uses the even lamps $j$, and the walk from $c_0$ uses the odd lamps; the conditions are $\sum_{j\text{ even}}x_j\equiv 0$ and $\sum_{j\text{ odd}}x_j\equiv 0\pmod 3$.

So the reachable states are precisely the colourings with these sums divisible by $3$. It remains to maximise the number of greens ($x_j=2$) under these conditions. For $n$ odd: all $n$ green needs $3\mid 2n$; otherwise $n-1$ greens plus one lamp of value $\equiv-2(n-1)\pmod 3$, which is $0$ or $1$ (never $2$, because $3\nmid n$), is allowed. For $n=2h$ even: each class of $h$ lamps can be all green iff $3\mid h$, and otherwise holds $h-1$ greens and one red or blue lamp. Therefore the maximum is \[\boxed{\ n\ \text{if } 3\mid n;\qquad n-1\ \text{if } 3\nmid n,\ n\text{ odd};\qquad n-2\ \text{if } 3\nmid n,\ n\text{ even}\ }.\]

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB\ne AC$ and let $I$ be its incenter. Let $P$ and $Q$ be points inside $\triangle ABC$ such that $BP=PC>BQ=QC$. Lines $BP$ and $CQ$ meet at $X$. Suppose that the line $AI$ is tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $IPQ$. Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $ABQ$, $ACP$ and $PQX$ have a common point.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion turns it into Miquel)

Throughout, $\Gamma$ is the circumcircle of $ABC$, $M$ is the second intersection of line $AI$ with $\Gamma$ (the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$), $D=AI\cap BC$, and $N$ is the midpoint of $BC$. Let $\angle A$ denote $\angle BAC$.

Step 1: the basic facts.

  • $MB=MC=MI$ (incenter–excenter lemma): $\angle MBI=\angle MBC+\angle CBI=\frac{\angle A}{2}+\frac{\angle B}{2}=\angle BAI+\angle ABI =\angle MIB$, so $MB=MI$; similarly $MC=MI$. Let $\omega$ be the circle with center $M$ and radius $MB$; it passes through $B,I,C$.

  • $PB=PC$ and $QB=QC$, so $P$ and $Q$ lie on the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $BC$; as $P\ne Q$, line $PQ$ is $m$. The line $m$ passes through $M$ and $N$. Since $AB\ne AC$, the line $AI$ is not $m$, so $M$ is the only common point of $AI$ and $PQ$.

  • The line $AI$ touches the circle $IPQ$ at $I$, and $M$ lies on this tangent and on the secant $PQ$. Computing the power of $M$ in two ways, $MP\cdot MQ=MI^2=MB^2$, and since this power is positive, $P$ and $Q$ lie on the same ray from $M$.

Step 2: $X$ lies on $\omega$. Triangles $MBP$ and $MQB$ have the common angle at $M$ and $MB:MP=MQ:MB$, so they are similar and $\angle MBP=\angle MQB$. Let $x=\angle PBC=\angle PCB$ and $y=\angle QBC=\angle QCB$. As $M$ and $P$ are on opposite sides of $BC$, $\angle MBP=\angle MBC+\angle CBP=\frac{\angle A}{2}+x$. Also $N$ lies between $M$ and $Q$, so in the right triangle $QNB$, $\angle MQB=\angle NQB=90^\circ-y$. Hence \[x+y=90^\circ-\tfrac{\angle A}{2}.\] In triangle $XBC$ we have $\angle XBC=x$ and $\angle XCB=y$, so $\angle BXC=90^\circ+\frac{\angle A}{2}=\angle BIC$. As $X$ and $I$ are on the same side of $BC$, the point $X$ lies on the circle $BIC$, which is $\omega$.

Step 3: inversion. Let $\Psi$ be the inversion with center $M$ and radius $MB$. It fixes $B$, $C$, $X$ (they lie on $\omega$) and swaps $P$ and $Q$ (because $MP\cdot MQ=MB^2$ with $P,Q$ on one ray from $M$). The circle $\Gamma$ passes through $M$, $B$, $C$, so $\Psi$ maps it to the line $BC$; hence $\Psi(A)$ is the point of $BC$ on line $MA$, namely $\Psi(A)=D$.

Step 4: Miquel. In triangle $XBC$ the points $D$, $P$, $Q$ lie on the lines $BC$, $XB$, $XC$ and differ from the vertices ($D$ is inside segment $BC$; $P\ne X$ because line $CQ$ meets $m$ only at $Q$; similarly $Q\ne X$). By Miquel's theorem the circles $(BDP)$, $(CDQ)$ and $(XPQ)$ have a common point $W$.

The circle $(XPQ)$ does not pass through $M$: otherwise $\Psi$ would map it to a line through $\Psi(X)=X$, $\Psi(P)=Q$, $\Psi(Q)=P$, but $X\notin m$ (line $BP$ meets $m$ only at $P\ne X$). So $W\ne M$; let $Z=\Psi(W)$. The circle $(BDP)$ does not pass through $M$ either, since otherwise its image would be a line through $B$, $A$, $Q$, and these are not collinear ($Q$ is inside the triangle). So $\Psi$ maps it to the circle through $\Psi(B)=B$, $\Psi(D)=A$, $\Psi(P)=Q$, i.e. to the circle $(ABQ)$. In the same way $\Psi$ maps $(CDQ)$ to $(ACP)$, and it maps $(XPQ)$ to the circle through $X$, $Q$, $P$, i.e. to itself.

Since $W$ lies on $(BDP)$, $(CDQ)$ and $(XPQ)$, its image $Z$ lies on the circumcircles of $ABQ$, $ACP$ and $PQX$. So these three circles have a common point.

Solution 2 (A trigonometric lemma and an angle chase)

Let $M$ be the midpoint of arc $BC$ of the circumcircle not containing $A$; it lies on $AI$, and $MB=MC=MI$ (incenter–excenter lemma). Since $PB=PC$ and $QB=QC$, the points $P,Q$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, which passes through $M$ and meets $AI$ only at $M$ (as $AB\ne AC$). The line $AI$ touches the circle $IPQ$ at $I$, so the power of $M$ gives $MP\cdot MQ=MI^2=MB^2>0$; in particular $P,Q$ lie on one ray from $M$.

Lemma: $\angle PBC+\angle QBC=\frac{\angle B+\angle C}{2}$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $BC$ and choose the unit so that $BN=1$. With $x=\angle PBC$, $y=\angle QBC$, $\alpha=\frac{\angle A}{2}=\angle MBN$ we have $NP=\tan x$, $NQ=\tan y$, $NM=\tan\alpha$, $MB=\frac1{\cos\alpha}$, and $N$ lies between $M$ and $P,Q$. So $MP\cdot MQ=MB^2$ reads \[(\tan x+\tan\alpha)(\tan y+\tan\alpha)=\frac1{\cos^2\alpha}.\] Using $\tan u+\tan v=\frac{\sin(u+v)}{\cos u\cos v}$ this becomes $\sin(x+\alpha)\sin(y+\alpha)=\cos x\cos y$, i.e. \[\cos(x-y)-\cos(x+y+2\alpha)=\cos(x-y)+\cos(x+y),\] so $\cos(x+y+2\alpha)+\cos(x+y)=2\cos(x+y+\alpha)\cos\alpha=0$. As $\cos\alpha\ne 0$ and $0<x+y+\alpha<270^\circ$, we get $x+y+\alpha=90^\circ$, i.e. $x+y=90^\circ-\frac{\angle A}{2}=\frac{\angle B+\angle C}{2}$.

The angle chase. We use directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Place $B$ to the left of $C$ with $A$ above $BC$, and measure the direction of each line counterclockwise from $BC$. By symmetry in the perpendicular bisector, $\angle PCB=x$ and $\angle QCB=y$. The directions are: $BA$: $\angle B$; $BQ$: $y$; $BP$: $x$; $CQ$: $-y$; $CP$: $-x$; $CA$: $-\angle C$.

Let $Z$ be the second common point of the circles $(ABQ)$ and $(PQX)$ (if they touch at $Q$, take $Z=Q$ and read $ZQ$ as the common tangent). Using these two circles, \[\measuredangle(ZA,ZP)=\measuredangle(ZA,ZQ)+\measuredangle(ZQ,ZP)=\measuredangle(BA,BQ)+\measuredangle(XQ,XP) =(y-\angle B)+(x+y),\] since $XQ$ is the line $CQ$ and $XP$ is the line $BP$. On the other hand $\measuredangle(CA,CP)=-x+\angle C$. The difference is \[(x+2y-\angle B)-(\angle C-x)=2(x+y)-(\angle B+\angle C)=0\] by the Lemma. Hence $\measuredangle(ZA,ZP)=\measuredangle(CA,CP)$, so $Z$ lies on the circle $(ACP)$. (If $Z=A$ or $Z=P$, there is nothing to prove: then $A$, respectively $P$, already lies on all three circles.) Thus $Z$ is a common point of the circumcircles of $ABQ$, $ACP$ and $PQX$.
