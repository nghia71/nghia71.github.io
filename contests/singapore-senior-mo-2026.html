Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

In square $ABCD$, $E,F$ are points on the sides $AB$ and $BC$ respectively. Let $N$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ onto $CE$. The circular arc from $C$ to $A$ centred at $B$ with radius $BC$ intersects the segment $DN$ at $K$. If $\angle FND=90^\circ$ and $KF$ is parallel to $NB$, find the value of $\dfrac{BE+BF}{AB}$.

Show the answer

$3-\sqrt5$

Show solution 1 – Coordinates centred at $N$

Take $AB=1$ (the ratio does not depend on the size). Let $\theta=\angle BCE$ and put $b=\sin\theta$, $c=\cos\theta$. The point $E$ is not $B$ (otherwise $N=B$ and $\angle FND=\angle FBD=45^\circ$), so $0<\theta\le45^\circ$ and $BE=\tan\theta=\frac bc$. In the right triangle $BNC$ (right angle at $N$, hypotenuse $BC=1$) we have $NB=b$ and $NC=c$.

Place $N$ at the origin, the $x$-axis along $NC$ and the $y$-axis along $NB$: \[N=(0,0),\quad C=(c,0),\quad B=(0,b).\] The foot $N$ of the altitude from the right angle $B$ of triangle $EBC$ lies between $E$ and $C$, so $E$ is on the negative $x$-axis. The ray $BA$ (which contains $E$) is perpendicular to $\overrightarrow{BC}=(c,-b)$, so its unit vector is $\pm(b,c)$; it must be $(-b,-c)$, since $B+\frac bc(-b,-c)=(-\frac{b^2}c,0)$ is then the point $E$ of the negative $x$-axis with $BE=\frac bc$. Hence \[A=B+(-b,-c)=(-b,\,b-c),\qquad D=C+(-b,-c)=(c-b,\,-c),\] and a point $F$ of side $BC$ with $BF=f$ is $F=B+f(c,-b)=(fc,\,b-fb)$.

The right angle at $N$. \[\overrightarrow{ND}\cdot\overrightarrow{NF}=(c-b)fc-c(b-fb)=c\,(fc-b),\] so $\angle FND=90^\circ$ forces $f=\frac bc$, that is, $BF=BE$. Then $F=(b,\;b-\frac{b^2}c)$.

The point $K$. $NB$ is the $y$-axis, so $KF\parallel NB$ means that $K$ has $x$-coordinate $b$. The points of line $DN$ are $\lambda(c-b,-c)$. If $c=b$ this line is the $y$-axis and contains no point with $x=b$; so $c>b$, and $K$ corresponds to $\lambda=\frac b{c-b}$: \[K=\Bigl(b,\;-\frac{bc}{c-b}\Bigr),\qquad K-B=\Bigl(b,\;-\frac{b(2c-b)}{c-b}\Bigr).\] Since $K$ lies on the arc centred at $B$ with radius $BC=1=b^2+c^2$, \[b^2+\frac{b^2(2c-b)^2}{(c-b)^2}=b^2+c^2\iff b(2c-b)=c(c-b),\] taking positive square roots ($c>b>0$). This is $c^2-3bc+b^2=0$, so $\frac cb=\frac{3+\sqrt5}2$ (the root greater than $1$), and \[BE=BF=\frac bc=\frac{2}{3+\sqrt5}=\frac{3-\sqrt5}2 .\] Conversely these values work: $\lambda=\frac b{c-b}=\frac{\sqrt5-1}2\in(0,1)$, so $K$ is on segment $DN$. Therefore \[\frac{BE+BF}{AB}=\boxed{3-\sqrt5}.\]

Show solution 2 – The altitude of the right triangle $KNF$

Take $AB=1$, let $\theta=\angle BCE$ and $t=\tan\theta=BE$, so $0<t\le1$.

Step 1: $BF=BE$. Use coordinates $B=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(0,1)$, $D=(1,1)$, $E=(0,t)$, $F=(f,0)$. The foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to line $CE$ is $N=\frac1{1+t^2}(t^2,t)$; write $N=(n_1,n_2)$ and note $n_1^2+n_2^2=\frac{t^2}{1+t^2}=n_1$. Then \[\overrightarrow{NF}\cdot\overrightarrow{ND}=(f-n_1)(1-n_1)-n_2(1-n_2)=f(1-n_1)-n_2=\frac{f-t}{1+t^2},\] so $\angle FND=90^\circ$ gives $f=t$: $BF=BE=t$. Also $t<1$: if $t=1$ then $F=C$, $N$ is the centre of the square, and the line through $F$ parallel to $NB$ (a diagonal) would be parallel to line $DN=DB$, so $K$ would not exist.

Step 2: locating $K$. Let $P$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $F$ to line $CE$. Both $FP$ and $KF$ are parallel to $NB$ (all are perpendicular to $CE$), so $K,F,P$ are collinear. In triangle $KNF$ the angle at $N$ is $90^\circ$ ($K$ lies on $DN$ and $\angle FND=90^\circ$), and $NP\perp KF$ with $P$ on line $KF$. So $P$ is the foot of the altitude from the right angle; it lies between $K$ and $F$, and \[NP^2=PK\cdot PF .\] Now compute with $CF=1-t$: $PF=(1-t)\sin\theta$, $CP=(1-t)\cos\theta$, $CN=BC\cos\theta=\cos\theta$, so $NP=CN-CP=t\cos\theta=\sin\theta$. Also $NB=\sin\theta$, and \[PK=\frac{NP^2}{PF}=\frac{\sin\theta}{1-t}.\]

Step 3: the condition $BK=BC$. The point $F$ is on the same side of line $CE$ as $B$, and $K$ is beyond $P$ on ray $FP$, so $K$ is on the other side. Measuring along $CE$ and perpendicular to it, the feet of $B$ and $K$ are $N$ and $P$, at distance $NP=\sin\theta$, and the perpendicular distances are $NB=\sin\theta$ and $PK$ on opposite sides. Hence \[BK^2=NP^2+(NB+PK)^2=\sin^2\theta+\sin^2\theta\Bigl(\frac{2-t}{1-t}\Bigr)^2 .\] Setting $BK^2=1=\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta$ gives $\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\,\frac{(2-t)^2}{(1-t)^2}$, i.e. $1=t^2\frac{(2-t)^2}{(1-t)^2}$, so $t(2-t)=1-t$ (both sides positive). Thus $t^2-3t+1=0$ and, as $t<1$, $t=\frac{3-\sqrt5}2$.

Step 4: this value really occurs. Take $t=\frac{3-\sqrt5}2$ and $F=(t,0)$, so $\angle FND=90^\circ$ by Step 1. Let $K$ be the point on ray $FP$ beyond $P$ with $PK=\frac{\sin\theta}{1-t}$. Then $NP^2=PK\cdot PF$ with $NP\perp KF$, so the triangles $NPK$ and $FPN$ are similar (right angles at $P$, $\frac{NP}{PK}=\frac{PF}{NP}$), and $\angle KNF=\angle KNP+\angle PNF=\angle NFP+\angle PNF=90^\circ$. Hence $K$ lies on the line through $N$ perpendicular to $NF$, which is line $DN$; it is on the side of line $CE$ away from $B$, like $D$, so $K$ is on ray $ND$. Since $t(2-t)=1-t$, the computation of Step 3 read backwards gives $BK=1$. On ray $ND$ there is only one point at distance $1$ from $B$ (as $BN=\sin\theta<1$, $N$ is inside the circle with centre $B$ and radius $1$), and since $BN<1<\sqrt2=BD$ that point lies on segment $DN$. So $K$ is on segment $DN$, inside the square, and on the arc, and $KF\parallel NB$ by construction. Therefore \[\frac{BE+BF}{AB}=2t=\boxed{3-\sqrt5}.\]

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

Let $P$ be a configuration of $n\ge3$ points in the plane, no three collinear and let $K$ be a maximal set of pairwise nonparallel lines, each joining two of these points. Let $M(P)$ be the largest possible size of $K$. What is the minimum value of $M(P)$ over all possible configurations $P$?

Show the answer

$n$

Show solution 1 – A vertex of the convex hull

$M(P)$ is the number of different directions of the lines joining two points of $P$.

Lower bound $M(P)\ge n$. Let $A$ be a vertex of the convex hull of $P$. All other points lie in the angle at $A$ formed by its two hull neighbours $B$ and $Z$, and this angle is less than $180^\circ$. The $n-1$ lines $AQ$ ($Q\in P$, $Q\ne A$) are pairwise nonparallel: two of them pass through $A$, so if they were parallel they would coincide and three points would be collinear.

We show that the line $BZ$ is parallel to none of them, which gives $n$ directions. Write $\vec u=\overrightarrow{AB}$, $\vec v=\overrightarrow{AZ}$. Every point $Q\ne A$ of $P$ lies in the angle $BAZ$, so $\overrightarrow{AQ}=\alpha\vec u+\beta\vec v$ with $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ not both $0$. If $AQ\parallel BZ$, then $\overrightarrow{AQ}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{BZ}=\lambda(\vec v-\vec u)$ for some $\lambda\ne0$; since $\vec u,\vec v$ are not parallel, comparing coefficients gives $\alpha=-\lambda$ and $\beta=\lambda$, which cannot both be $\ge0$. So $BZ$ gives a new direction and $M(P)\ge n$.

A configuration with exactly $n$ directions. Let $P$ be the vertex set $V_0,\dots,V_{n-1}$ of a regular $n$-gon with centre $O$, $V_k$ at angle $\frac{2\pi k}n$ (no three vertices of a convex polygon are collinear). For an integer $m$, the reflection in the line through $O$ at angle $\frac{\pi m}n$ sends the angle $\frac{2\pi k}n$ to $\frac{2\pi(m-k)}n$, i.e. $V_k\mapsto V_{m-k}$ (indices mod $n$). So if $i+j\equiv m\pmod n$, this reflection swaps $V_i$ and $V_j$, and the chord $V_iV_j$ is perpendicular to its axis. Hence all chords with the same value of $i+j\bmod n$ are parallel, and there are at most $n$ directions. Together with the lower bound, this configuration has exactly $n$.

So the minimum value of $M(P)$ is $\boxed{n}$.

Show solution 2 – Each direction class is a matching

$M(P)$ is the number of directions of the $\binom n2$ lines joining two points of $P$; group these segments into classes of parallel ones.

Each class is a matching. Two parallel segments cannot share an endpoint (they would lie on one line, giving three collinear points). So a class has at most $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ segments, and with $d=M(P)$ classes, \[\binom n2\le d\Bigl\lfloor\frac n2\Bigr\rfloor .\] For odd $n$ this gives $d\ge\frac{n(n-1)/2}{(n-1)/2}=n$. For even $n$ it gives $d\ge n-1$, with equality only if every class has exactly $n/2$ segments, i.e. every class is a perfect matching: then every point is an endpoint of a segment in every one of the $d$ directions.

Excluding $d=n-1$ for even $n$. Suppose this happens. Let $A$ be a vertex of the convex hull with hull neighbours $B$ and $Z$; all points lie in the angle $BAZ$, which is less than $180^\circ$. The direction of $BZ$ is one of the $d$ directions, so some segment $AQ$ is parallel to $BZ$. Write $\overrightarrow{AQ}=\alpha\overrightarrow{AB}+\beta\overrightarrow{AZ}$ with $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ (as $Q$ is in the angle) and also $\overrightarrow{AQ}=\lambda(\overrightarrow{AZ}-\overrightarrow{AB})$ with $\lambda\ne0$; then $\alpha=-\lambda$, $\beta=\lambda$, a contradiction. Hence $d\ge n$ for all $n$.

Equality. Take the regular $n$-gon with vertices $V_k=(\cos\frac{2\pi k}n,\sin\frac{2\pi k}n)$, $k=0,\dots,n-1$. With $\gamma=\frac{\pi(i+j)}n$ and $\eta=\frac{\pi(j-i)}n$, the sum-to-product formulas give \[\overrightarrow{V_iV_j}=\bigl(\cos(\gamma+\eta)-\cos(\gamma-\eta),\ \sin(\gamma+\eta)-\sin(\gamma-\eta)\bigr)=2\sin\eta\,\bigl(-\sin\gamma,\ \cos\gamma\bigr).\] So the line $V_iV_j$ is perpendicular to the direction of angle $\gamma=\frac{\pi(i+j)}n$, and this direction (taken modulo $\pi$) depends only on $i+j\bmod n$. Hence the regular $n$-gon determines at most $n$ directions.

Hence the minimum of $M(P)$ is $\boxed{n}$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes · Infinite Descent · also Singapore Junior MO 2026, Problem 5

Determine all integers $a,b,c$ satisfying \[a^2+b^2+c^2=13(ab+bc+ca).\]

Show the answer

only $a=b=c=0$

Show solution 1 – Descent modulo $5$ via $a+b+c$

Let $s=a+b+c$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. Adding $2q$ to both sides of the equation gives \[s^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2q=15q .\] Everything is divisible by $5$. Since $5\mid s^2$ and $5$ is prime, $5\mid s$; then $25\mid s^2=15q$, so $5\mid q$. Modulo $5$ we have $c\equiv-a-b$, hence \[q=ab+c(a+b)\equiv ab-(a+b)^2=-(a^2+ab+b^2)\pmod5,\] so $a^2+ab+b^2\equiv0\pmod 5$. Multiply by $4$: $(2a+b)^2+3b^2\equiv0$, i.e. $(2a+b)^2\equiv2b^2\pmod5$. The squares modulo $5$ are $0,1,4$. If $5\nmid b$ then $b^2\equiv1$ or $4$, so $2b^2\equiv2$ or $3$, which is not a square modulo $5$. Hence $5\mid b$, then $(2a)^2\equiv0$ gives $5\mid a$, and $c\equiv-a-b\equiv0$. So $a,b,c$ are all divisible by $5$.

Descent. The equation is homogeneous, so $(a/5,b/5,c/5)$ is again an integer solution. Repeating, $a,b,c$ are divisible by $5^k$ for every $k$, which is only possible for $a=b=c=0$. Conversely $(0,0,0)$ is a solution. The only solution is $\boxed{a=b=c=0}$.

Show solution 2 – The differences and the form $x^2+xy+y^2$

Put $x=a-b$, $y=b-c$ (so $c-a=-(x+y)$) and $s=a+b+c$, $q=ab+bc+ca$. From the equation, $a^2+b^2+c^2=13q$, and \[s^2=13q+2q=15q,\qquad x^2+y^2+(x+y)^2=2(a^2+b^2+c^2)-2q=24q .\] Since $x^2+y^2+(x+y)^2=2(x^2+xy+y^2)$, we get $x^2+xy+y^2=12q=\frac45s^2$, that is \[5(x^2+xy+y^2)=4s^2.\qquad(\ast)\] Lemma. If $5\mid x^2+xy+y^2$ then $5\mid x$ and $5\mid y$. Indeed $4(x^2+xy+y^2)=(2x+y)^2+3y^2$, so $(2x+y)^2\equiv2y^2\pmod5$; as $2$ and $3$ are not squares modulo $5$ (squares are $0,1,4$), this forces $5\mid y$ and then $5\mid x$.

Descent on $(\ast)$. Let $(x,y,s)$ be an integer solution of $(\ast)$ that is not $(0,0,0)$, with $|x|+|y|+|s|$ as small as possible. From $(\ast)$, $5\mid4s^2$, so $5\mid s$; write $s=5s_1$. Then $x^2+xy+y^2=20s_1^2$ is divisible by $5$, so by the lemma $x=5x_1$, $y=5y_1$. Substituting, $125(x_1^2+x_1y_1+y_1^2)=100s_1^2$, i.e. $(x_1,y_1,s_1)$ again satisfies $(\ast)$, is not $(0,0,0)$, and is smaller. This contradiction shows that $(\ast)$ has only the solution $x=y=s=0$.

So $a=b=c$ and $3a=s=0$, giving $\boxed{a=b=c=0}$, which is indeed a solution.

Problem 4

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Reformulation of Real Problems

Fix a positive integer $N$. Let $a_k,b_k$ $(0\le k\le N)$ and $c_k$ $(1\le k\le N)$ be sequences of real numbers such that for all $1\le k\le N$, we have \[a_k=\frac{4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1}}{5}+c_k,\qquad b_k=\frac{4b_{k-1}-3a_{k-1}}{5}.\] If $a_0=b_0=0$ and $a_N\ge1$, determine the smallest possible value of \[|c_1|+|c_2|+\cdots+|c_N|.\]

Show the answer

$1$

Show solution 1 – The distance from the origin

Let $r_k=\sqrt{a_k^2+b_k^2}$. Put $u=\frac{4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1}}5$ and $w=\frac{4b_{k-1}-3a_{k-1}}5$. Then \[u^2+w^2=\frac{(16+9)(a_{k-1}^2+b_{k-1}^2)+(24-24)a_{k-1}b_{k-1}}{25}=r_{k-1}^2,\] and $(a_k,b_k)=(u+c_k,w)$. By the triangle inequality (the distance from the origin to $(u,w)+(c_k,0)$ is at most the sum of the lengths), \[r_k\le\sqrt{u^2+w^2}+|c_k|=r_{k-1}+|c_k| .\] Since $r_0=0$, adding these for $k=1,\dots,N$ gives $r_N\le|c_1|+\dots+|c_N|$, and therefore \[|c_1|+\dots+|c_N|\ge r_N\ge a_N\ge1 .\] The value $1$ is attained: take $c_1=\dots=c_{N-1}=0$ and $c_N=1$. Then $a_k=b_k=0$ for $k<N$, and $a_N=0+1=1$. The smallest possible value is $\boxed{1}$.

Show solution 2 – A complex closed form

Let $z_k=a_k+ib_k$ and $\omega=\frac{4-3i}5$, a complex number with $|\omega|=1$. Then \[\omega z_{k-1}=\frac{(4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1})+i(4b_{k-1}-3a_{k-1})}5,\] so the two recurrences say exactly $z_k=\omega z_{k-1}+c_k$. With $z_0=0$ this gives, by induction, \[z_N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_k\,\omega^{N-k}.\] Taking real parts and using $|\operatorname{Re}w|\le|w|$ and $|\omega^{N-k}|=1$: \[1\le a_N=\operatorname{Re}z_N\le|z_N|\le\sum_{k=1}^N|c_k|\,|\omega|^{N-k}=\sum_{k=1}^N|c_k| .\] Equality is possible: with $c_N=1$ and all other $c_k=0$ we get $z_N=1$, so $a_N=1$. The minimum is $\boxed{1}$.

Problem 5

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of positive real numbers. Prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that \[\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_{n+1}}{x_n}\ge3.99.\]

Show solution 1 – Ratios of consecutive partial sums

Let $c=3.99$ and $S_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$, so $S_n>0$ is increasing. Suppose the statement is false. Then there is $N\ge2$ with \[S_{n+1}<c\,x_n=c\,(S_n-S_{n-1})\qquad\text{for all }n\ge N .\] Let $r_n=\dfrac{S_{n+1}}{S_n}$; then $r_n>1$ for all $n$. Dividing by $S_n$ gives, for $n\ge N$, \[r_n<c-\frac c{r_{n-1}}=r_{n-1}-\frac{r_{n-1}^2-c\,r_{n-1}+c}{r_{n-1}} .\] The numerator satisfies $r^2-cr+c=\bigl(r-\frac c2\bigr)^2+\frac{c(4-c)}4\ge\mu$, where $\mu=\frac{c(4-c)}4>0$ because $c<4$. Hence $r_n<r_{n-1}-\frac{\mu}{r_{n-1}}<r_{n-1}$ for $n\ge N$. So $r_{N-1}>r_N>r_{N+1}>\cdots$, all of them at most $R=r_{N-1}$, and therefore \[r_n<r_{n-1}-\frac\mu R\qquad(n\ge N).\] After $m$ steps, $r_{N-1+m}<R-\frac{m\mu}R$, which is less than $1$ once $m>\frac{R(R-1)}\mu$. This contradicts $r_n>1$. Hence there are infinitely many $n$ with $\dfrac{x_1+\dots+x_{n+1}}{x_n}\ge3.99$.

Show solution 2 – Comparison with a sine sequence

Let $S_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$ and write $3.99=4\cos^2\varphi$ with $0<\varphi<\frac\pi2$. Suppose the statement is false: there is $N$ such that $S_{n+1}<3.99\,x_n$ for all $n\ge N$. For $n\ge N+1$ (so $n\ge2$) this reads $S_{n+1}<3.99\,(S_n-S_{n-1})$. Put $w_n=S_n/(2\cos\varphi)^n>0$. Dividing by $(2\cos\varphi)^{n+1}$ we get \[w_{n+1}+w_{n-1}<2\cos\varphi\;w_n\qquad(n\ge N+1).\qquad(1)\]

Let $s_k=\sin(k\varphi)$. Then $s_{k+1}+s_{k-1}=2\cos\varphi\,s_k$ for all $k$, $s_0=0$. Let $K\ge1$ be the integer with $K\varphi<\pi\le(K+1)\varphi$ (it exists since $\varphi<\frac\pi2$); then $s_1,\dots,s_K>0$ and $s_{K+1}\le0$.

Put $W_j=w_{N+j}$. By (1) with $n=N+k$, $k=1,\dots,K$, each bracket below is negative while $s_k>0$, so \[\Sigma:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_k\bigl(W_{k+1}+W_{k-1}-2\cos\varphi\,W_k\bigr)<0 .\] On the other hand, using $2\cos\varphi\,s_k=s_{k+1}+s_{k-1}$ and $T_k=s_kW_{k+1}-s_{k+1}W_k$, \[\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^K\bigl(s_kW_{k+1}-s_{k+1}W_k\bigr)-\sum_{k=1}^K\bigl(s_{k-1}W_k-s_kW_{k-1}\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^K(T_k-T_{k-1})=T_K-T_0,\] so \[\Sigma=s_KW_{K+1}-s_{K+1}W_K+s_1W_0>0,\] because $s_K>0$, $s_1>0$, $-s_{K+1}\ge0$ and all $W_j>0$. This contradiction proves that there are infinitely many $n$ with $\dfrac{x_1+\dots+x_{n+1}}{x_n}\ge3.99$.
