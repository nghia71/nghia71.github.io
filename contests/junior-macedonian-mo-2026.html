Problem 1

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Cyclic Quadrilaterals

In the acute $\triangle ABC$ point $D$ on side $BC$ is such that $\angle ACB=2\angle DAC$. Ray $AD$ intersects the circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$ at $E$, and $D'$ is the reflection of $D$ with respect to the line $BE$. Let the line through $D'$ parallel to $AC$ meet $AD$ at $F$, and let the second intersection point of $BE$ and the circumcircle of $\triangle BFD$ be $G\ne B$. Prove that the points $D'$, $E$, $G$, and $F$ lie on a common circle.

Show the solution – The line $BD^\prime$ is parallel to $AC$

Let $\gamma=\angle DAC$, so $\angle ACB=2\gamma$.

Step 1: $BD'\parallel AC$. Since $D$ lies inside the segment $BC$, the ray $AD$ crosses $BC$ and meets the circumcircle at a point $E$ of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$. The inscribed angles on the arc $EC$ give $\angle CBE=\angle CAE=\gamma$, so the ray $BE$ lies on the other side of $BC$ than $A$ and makes the angle $\gamma$ with the ray $BC$. Reflecting the ray $BD$ (which is the ray $BC$) in the line $BE$, we get the ray $BD'$, on the same side of $BC$ as $E$, with \[\angle CBD'=2\gamma=\angle ACB .\] These are alternate angles at the transversal $BC$ ($D'$ and $A$ lie on opposite sides of $BC$), so $BD'\parallel AC$.

Hence the line through $D'$ parallel to $AC$ is the line $BD'$, and $F$ is the intersection of $BD'$ with $AD$. In particular $B,D',F$ are collinear, and $A,D,F,E$ are collinear.

Step 2: the circle. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ modulo $180^\circ$. The reflection in the line $BE$ fixes $G$ and $B$ and maps $D$ to $D'$, and it reverses angles; hence $\measuredangle(GD',GB)=\measuredangle(GB,GD)$. As $B,F,D,G$ are concyclic, $\measuredangle(GB,GD)=\measuredangle(FB,FD)$. Using the collinearities, \[\measuredangle(GD',GE)=\measuredangle(GD',GB)=\measuredangle(GB,GD)=\measuredangle(FB,FD)=\measuredangle(FD',FE).\] So $G$ and $F$ see the segment $D'E$ under the same directed angle, and $D',E,G,F$ lie on a circle.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Coloring Arguments

Find all positive integers $n$ for which an $n\times n$ square board can be tiled completely and without overlap using “T-tetrominoes” (as shown below).

Comment: We say that a tiling is complete and without overlap if every unit square of the board is covered by a unit square of exactly one tetromino and every tetromino is fully inside the board.

Show the answer

all $n$ divisible by $4$

Show the solution – Chessboard colouring

$4\mid n$ suffices. The $4\times4$ board can be tiled by four T-tetrominoes (figure), and an $n\times n$ board with $4\mid n$ splits into $4\times4$ blocks.

$4\mid n$ is necessary. Every tetromino covers $4$ cells, so $4\mid n^2$ and $n$ is even. Colour the board like a chessboard; as $n$ is even, there are $\frac{n^2}2$ black and $\frac{n^2}2$ white cells. A T-tetromino always covers $3$ cells of one colour and $1$ of the other (the centre cell of the T and its three neighbours). If $x$ tetrominoes cover $3$ black cells and $y$ cover $1$ black cell, then counting black and white cells, \[3x+y=\frac{n^2}2=x+3y,\] so $x=y$, and the number of tetrominoes $\frac{n^2}4=x+y=2x$ is even. Writing $n=2m$, this says $m^2$ is even, so $m$ is even and $4\mid n$.

Answer: $\boxed{n\equiv0\pmod 4}$.

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Prove that for all non-negative real numbers $x_1,x_2,x_3$, and $x_4$, such that $x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=1$ and $x_3^2+x_4^2,\ x_4^2+x_1^2,\ x_1^2+x_2^2\neq0$, the following inequality holds: \[\frac{x_1+x_2}{x_3^2+x_4^2}+\frac{x_2+x_3}{x_4^2+x_1^2}+\frac{x_3+x_4}{x_1^2+x_2^2}\geqslant 4.\] Determine all cases of equality.

Show the answer

proof; equality only for $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(\tfrac12,0,0,\tfrac12)$

Show solution 1 – Drop the middle term, then AM-GM

Let $a=x_1+x_2$ and $b=x_3+x_4$, so $a+b=1$. Since $x_1^2+x_2^2\ne0$ and $x_3^2+x_4^2\ne0$, both $a,b>0$. For non-negative numbers, $x_3^2+x_4^2\le(x_3+x_4)^2=b^2$ and $x_1^2+x_2^2\le a^2$. The middle term is non-negative. Hence \[\text{LHS}\ \ge\ \frac{a}{b^2}+0+\frac{b}{a^2}\ \ge\ 2\sqrt{\frac{a}{b^2}\cdot\frac b{a^2}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{ab}}\ \ge\ \frac{2}{\frac{a+b}2}=4 .\]

Equality. We need equality everywhere: the middle term is $0$, so $x_2=x_3=0$; then $x_3^2+x_4^2=b^2$ and $x_1^2+x_2^2=a^2$ hold automatically; and $a=b=\frac12$. So $x_1=x_4=\frac12$, $x_2=x_3=0$, and indeed the value is $\frac{1/2}{1/4}+0+\frac{1/2}{1/4}=4$. Equality holds only for $\boxed{(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(\tfrac12,0,0,\tfrac12)}$.

Show solution 2 – One variable after the same reduction

Put $a=x_1+x_2>0$, $b=x_3+x_4>0$ (both are positive because of the conditions $x_1^2+x_2^2\ne0$, $x_3^2+x_4^2\ne0$), $a+b=1$, and $t=ab\in(0,\frac14]$. For non-negative numbers $x_3^2+x_4^2\le(x_3+x_4)^2=b^2$ and $x_1^2+x_2^2\le a^2$, and the middle term is at least $0$, so \[\text{LHS}\ge\frac{a}{b^2}+\frac{b}{a^2}=\frac{a^3+b^3}{a^2b^2}=\frac{(a+b)^3-3ab(a+b)}{(ab)^2}=\frac{1-3t}{t^2}.\] The function $g(t)=\frac{1-3t}{t^2}=\frac1{t^2}-\frac3t$ satisfies $g(t)-4=\frac{(1-4t)(1+t)}{t^2}\ge0$ for $0<t\le\frac14$. Hence $\text{LHS}\ge4$.

Equality needs $t=\frac14$, i.e. $a=b=\frac12$, $x_3^2+x_4^2=b^2$ and $x_1^2+x_2^2=a^2$ (so $x_3x_4=0$, $x_1x_2=0$), and a zero middle term ($x_2=x_3=0$). So equality holds exactly for $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(\frac12,0,0,\frac12)$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

We call a positive integer exactly divisible if it is divisible by each of its digits, but not by any of their squares. What is the maximum number of distinct digits that can appear in an exactly divisible number? Is the set of all exactly divisible numbers with as many distinct digits finite? Justify your answer.

Show the answer

$5$ distinct digits (the digits $3,4,6,7,8$); infinitely many such numbers

Show solution 1 – Ruling out digits, then a repeatable block

Let $D$ be the set of digits of an exactly divisible number $n$.

Which digits can occur. We collect the facts we need.

  • $0\notin D$, because no number is divisible by $0$; and $1\notin D$, because every number is divisible by $1^2=1$.

  • $5$ and an even digit cannot both occur: then $10\mid n$, so the last digit of $n$ would be $0$.

  • $3$ and $9$ cannot both occur: the digit $9$ needs $9\mid n$, while the digit $3$ forbids $3^2=9\mid n$.

  • $2$ cannot occur together with $4$ or $8$: each of $4$, $8$ needs $4\mid n$, while the digit $2$ forbids $2^2=4\mid n$.

  • If $6$ and $9$ both occur, then $4$ and $8$ do not: $4\mid n$ and $9\mid n$ would give $36=6^2\mid n$.

Now we count, according to the odd digits $5$ and $9$.

  • $5\in D$. There is no even digit, so $D\subseteq\{3,5,7,9\}$, and $3,9$ are not both in $D$: at most $3$ digits.

  • $5\notin D$, $9\in D$. Then $3\notin D$, so the odd digits are among $7,9$. If $2\in D$, the even digits are among $2,6$. If $2\notin D$ and $6\in D$, the only even digit is $6$ (no $4$, $8$). If $2,6\notin D$, the even digits are among $4,8$. In every case there are at most $2$ even digits, so at most $4$ digits.

  • $5,9\notin D$. The odd digits are among $3,7$. If $2\in D$, the even digits are among $2,6$; otherwise among $4,6,8$. So there are at most $2+3=5$ digits, and $5$ only for $D=\{3,4,6,7,8\}$.

So at most $\boxed{5}$ distinct digits occur.

Five digits occur. For $D=\{3,4,6,7,8\}$ the conditions are $8\mid n$, $16\nmid n$, $3\mid n$, $9\nmid n$, $7\mid n$, $49\nmid n$ (then also $4,6\mid n$ and $36,64\nmid n$). The number $n_0=3467688$ works: $n_0=168\cdot20641=2^3\cdot3\cdot7\cdot20641$, and $20641$ is odd, has digit sum $13$ (not divisible by $3$), and $20641=7\cdot2948+5$.

Infinitely many. Let $X=38367=441\cdot87=9\cdot49\cdot87$; its digits are in $D$. For $t\ge1$ let $N_t$ be the number written as $t$ copies of $X$ followed by $n_0$: \[N_t=X\bigl(1+10^5+\dots+10^{5(t-1)}\bigr)\cdot10^7+n_0 .\] The first summand is divisible by $9\cdot49$ and by $2^7$, so $N_t\equiv n_0$ modulo $16$, $9$ and $49$. Hence $8\mid N_t$, $16\nmid N_t$, $3\mid N_t$, $9\nmid N_t$, $7\mid N_t$, $49\nmid N_t$, and the digits of $N_t$ are exactly $3,4,6,7,8$. So there are $\boxed{\text{infinitely many}}$ exactly divisible numbers with five distinct digits.

Show solution 2 – Even and odd digits separately, then a prefix of threes

At most five digits. Let $n$ be exactly divisible.

  • The digit $0$ cannot occur (no number is divisible by $0$), and neither can $1$ (every number is divisible by $1^2$).

  • If $5$ occurs, then $5\mid n$ and the last digit of $n$ is $0$ or $5$; it is not $0$, so it is $5$ and $n$ is odd. Then no even digit occurs, and the digits are among $3,5,7,9$. The digits $3$ and $9$ cannot both occur: the digit $9$ needs $9\mid n$, while the digit $3$ forbids $3^2=9\mid n$. So there are at most $3$ digits.

  • If $5$ does not occur: the digit $2$ forbids $4\mid n$, while $4$ and $8$ each need $4\mid n$; so $2$ excludes both $4$ and $8$, and at most $3$ of the even digits $2,4,6,8$ occur. The odd digits are among $3,7,9$, and $3,9$ exclude each other, so at most $2$ odd digits occur. In total at most $3+2=5$.

So an exactly divisible number has at most $5$ distinct digits.

Five digits are possible. Take $N=3467688$, with digits $3,4,6,7,8$. We have $N=168\cdot 20641=2^3\cdot3\cdot7\cdot20641$, and $20641$ is odd, has digit sum $13$ (so $3\nmid 20641$) and leaves remainder $5$ when divided by $7$. Hence $N$ is divisible by $8$ but not by $16$, by $3$ but not by $9$, by $7$ but not by $49$. Therefore $N$ is divisible by $3,4,6,7,8$, but not by $9$, $16$, $36$ (as $9\nmid N$), $49$ or $64$ (as $16\nmid N$): it is exactly divisible.

Infinitely many. Write $R_h=11\ldots1$ ($h$ ones). Among the $442$ numbers $R_1,R_2,\dots,R_{442}$ two leave the same remainder when divided by $441$, say $R_p$ and $R_q$ with $p>q$. Their difference is $R_p-R_q=R_{p-q}\cdot10^{q}$, a multiple of $441$; since $10^q$ has no common factor with $441=9\cdot49$, the number $R_{p-q}$ is a multiple of $441$. Put $h=p-q\ge1$. (In fact the smallest such $h$ is $126$.)

For $t=1,2,3,\dots$ let $M_t$ be the number written with $th$ threes followed by the digits of $N$: \[M_t=3\,R_{th}\cdot 10^{7}+N .\] The number $R_{th}$ consists of $t$ blocks $R_h$, so $R_{th}=R_h\,(1+10^h+10^{2h}+\dots+10^{(t-1)h})$ is a multiple of $441$; and $10^7$ is a multiple of $2^7$. Hence $M_t-N$ is divisible by $9$, $49$ and $16$, so $M_t$ leaves the same remainders as $N$ modulo $9$, $49$ and $16$. These remainders decide all the conditions above: $M_t$ is divisible by $8$ but not $16$, by $3$ but not $9$, by $7$ but not $49$. The digits of $M_t$ are again exactly $3,4,6,7,8$. So every $M_t$ is exactly divisible with $5$ distinct digits, and the set of such numbers is infinite.

The maximum is $\boxed{5}$, and there are $\boxed{\text{infinitely many}}$ exactly divisible numbers with $5$ distinct digits.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 26: Double Counting and Combinatorial Proof · Handshake and Degree Logic

This year's JBMO has an even number of participants. Each participant is acquainted with fewer than half of the other participants. Prove that the organisers can arrange all the participants to sit in pairs such that no pair consists of two acquaintances.

Comment. The “acquaintance” relation is symmetric.

Show solution 1 – A largest set of good pairs

Let there be $2m$ participants. Each knows fewer than $\frac{2m-1}2$ others, i.e. at most $m-1$; so each has at least $(2m-1)-(m-1)=m$ strangers. Call a pair of strangers good.

Choose as many disjoint good pairs as possible, say $k$ pairs. Suppose $k<m$. Then two participants $u,v$ are left unpaired. They are acquainted (otherwise $\{u,v\}$ could be added). Every stranger of $u$ is paired (otherwise we could add $u$ with that stranger), and the same holds for $v$.

Look at one of the $k$ pairs, $\{a,b\}$. If $u$ is a stranger of $a$ and $v$ a stranger of $b$ (or $u$ of $b$ and $v$ of $a$), we could replace $\{a,b\}$ by the two good pairs $\{u,a\},\{v,b\}$ and get $k+1$ disjoint good pairs, which is impossible. Among the four possible stranger relations $u$–$a$, $u$–$b$, $v$–$a$, $v$–$b$, any three contain one of these two patterns; so at most two of them hold. Hence the pairs account for at most $2k\le2(m-1)$ strangers of $u$ and $v$ together. But $u$ and $v$ together have at least $2m$ strangers, all of them paired. This contradiction shows $k=m$: all participants sit in good pairs.

Show solution 2 – Through a Hamiltonian cycle (Dirac)

Let there be $2m$ participants and form the graph $H$ whose edges join strangers. Each participant knows at most $m-1$ others (fewer than half of the other $2m-1$), so every vertex of $H$ has degree at least $m$, half the number of vertices. By Dirac's theorem, $H$ has a Hamiltonian cycle $w_1w_2\dots w_{2m}w_1$. Its alternate edges $\{w_1,w_2\},\{w_3,w_4\},\dots,\{w_{2m-1},w_{2m}\}$ split all participants into pairs of strangers.

(Dirac's theorem: a graph with $N\ge3$ vertices and minimum degree at least $N/2$ has a Hamiltonian cycle. For $2m=2$ the two participants know fewer than $\frac12$ of the other one, i.e. nobody, and they form the pair.)
