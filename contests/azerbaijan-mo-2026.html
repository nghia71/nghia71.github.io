Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Let $x,y,a,b,c$ be real numbers such that \[\begin{cases} a+b+c=1,\\ a^2+b^2+c^2=3,\\ bc-x-2ya=0,\\ ca-x-2yb=0,\\ ab-x-2yc=0.\end{cases}\] Prove that $abc\in\left\{-1,\frac{5}{27}\right\}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Subtracting the equations)

Subtracting the fourth equation from the third gives $bc-ca-2y(a-b)=0$, i.e. $(b-a)(c+2y)=0$. In the same way \[(b-a)(c+2y)=0,\qquad(c-b)(a+2y)=0,\qquad(a-c)(b+2y)=0 .\] Two of $a,b,c$ are equal. If $a,b,c$ were pairwise distinct, we would get $a=b=c=-2y$, a contradiction.

Not all three are equal. Otherwise $3a=1$ and $3a^2=3$, impossible.

So exactly two are equal. The system is unchanged when $a,b,c$ are permuted (together with the last three equations), so we may assume $a=b\ne c$. Then $2a+c=1$ and $2a^2+c^2=3$; substituting $c=1-2a$ gives $6a^2-4a-2=0$, i.e. $(3a+1)(a-1)=0$.

  • $a=1$: $c=-1$ and $abc=-1$;

  • $a=-\frac13$: $c=\frac53$ and $abc=\frac19\cdot\frac53=\frac5{27}$.

Hence $abc\in\{-1,\frac5{27}\}$. (Both values occur: with $a=b$, $y=-\frac a2$ and $x=a^2+ac$ all equations hold.)

Solution 2 (A quadratic with three roots)

From $a+b+c=1$ and $a^2+b^2+c^2=3$ we get $ab+bc+ca=\frac{1-3}2=-1$. Let $p=abc$; then $a,b,c$ are the roots of \[g(t)=t^3-t^2-t-p .\] Multiplying the equation $bc=x+2ya$ by $a$ gives $p=xa+2ya^2$; similarly $p=xb+2yb^2$ and $p=xc+2yc^2$. So $a,b,c$ are roots of $R(t)=2yt^2+xt-p$.

If $R$ is the zero polynomial, then $x=y=p=0$, and the original equations give $bc=ca=ab=0$; then two of $a,b,c$ vanish and the third is $1$, contradicting $a^2+b^2+c^2=3$. So $R$ is a non-zero polynomial of degree at most $2$, and it has at most two distinct roots. Hence two of $a,b,c$ coincide, i.e. $g$ has a double root $r$, which satisfies $g(r)=g'(r)=0$: \[g'(r)=3r^2-2r-1=(3r+1)(r-1)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ r\in\{1,-\tfrac13\},\qquad p=r^3-r^2-r\in\Bigl\{-1,\ \frac{5}{27}\Bigr\}.\] So $abc\in\{-1,\frac5{27}\}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Two players $A$ and $B$ play the following game on an $8\times 8$ chessboard with one knight. A knight's “direction” is defined as the direction he moves two cells. For example: If a knight moves two cells upward and one cell left, then his “direction” is up. If it moves two cells right and one cell down then his “direction” is right. First, $A$ places the knight on some cell of the board and every round goes as follows: Firstly $B$ advises a “direction” for the move, if there exist two possible moves with that “direction” then $A$ chooses one of them and changes the knight's position, if there is only one possible move then $A$ can either reject or accept to do that move. It is known that $B$ cannot advise the same “direction” twice in a round and $A$ cannot reject $B$'s propositions infinitely many times. The round ends when the knight's position has been changed. When a knight moves to a cell that cell gets marked (including the beginning cell). Find the least positive integer $k$, such that regardless of the advices of $B$, $A$ can make the number of marked cells in the board at most $k$.

Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution

Solution (A residue class modulo $5$, and three cells in every quarter)

Reading of the rules. In a round, $B$ advises different directions one after another (a direction with no legal move is not advised). If the advised direction has two moves, $A$ must make one of them, of his choice. If it has one move, $A$ may reject it, and then $B$ advises another direction; $A$ must accept when $B$ has no further direction left (he cannot reject forever).

Self-safe sets. Call a set $T$ of cells self-safe if for every $u\in T$:

  • (i) whenever a direction has two moves from $u$, at least one of them ends in $T$, and

  • (ii) at least one move from $u$ ends in $T$.

If the knight stands on a cell of a self-safe set $T$, then $A$ can keep it in $T$ for the whole round: a two-move direction is answered by a move into $T$; a one-move direction is accepted if it ends in $T$ and rejected otherwise. $A$ is never forced to accept a move out of $T$, because the direction from (ii) (whose move into $T$ is accepted, or chosen) is still available to $B$ as long as $A$ has only rejected moves out of $T$. Conversely, if (i) fails at $u$, then $B$ advises that direction; if (ii) fails, $B$ advises directions until $A$ must accept; either way the knight leaves $T$.

Upper bound $12$. Let $T$ be the set of the $12$ cells $(r,c)$ ($0\le r,c\le7$, row $r$ from the top, column $c$ from the left) with \[r+2c\equiv3\pmod5 .\] The knight moves $(+1,+2),(-1,-2),(+2,-1),(-2,+1)$ keep $r+2c$ modulo $5$, and each direction (right, left, down, up) contains exactly one of them: right $=(\pm1,+2)$, left $=(\pm1,-2)$, down $=(+2,\pm1)$, up $=(-2,\pm1)$. So in every direction with two moves, one of them stays in the class of $r+2c$ modulo $5$, i.e. in $T$; this is (i). Each of the $12$ cells has a class-preserving move inside the board, which is (ii): \[\begin{array}{llll} (0,4)\to(2,3), & (1,1)\to(2,3), & (1,6)\to(3,5), & (2,3)\to(1,1),\\ (3,0)\to(4,2), & (3,5)\to(4,7), & (4,2)\to(3,0), & (4,7)\to(3,5),\\ (5,4)\to(6,6), & (6,1)\to(4,2), & (6,6)\to(5,4), & (7,3)\to(5,4). \end{array}\] So $A$ puts the knight on a cell of $T$ (this first cell is marked too, and it is in $T$) and keeps it in $T$ forever: at most $12$ cells are marked (see the left part of the figure).

Reduction of the lower bound. Suppose $A$ can guarantee at most $k$ marked cells. Call a position (knight's cell $v$, set $M$ of marked cells) winning if from it $A$ can guarantee that at most $k$ cells are ever marked. Choose a winning position $(v,M)$ with $|M|$ as large as possible. From a winning position $A$ can force the next position to be winning; from $(u,M)$ a move to a new cell $w\notin M$ would give a winning position with more marked cells, which is impossible, so $A$ can force the knight to stay in $M$ and on a cell $w$ with $(w,M)$ winning. Hence $T=\{u\in M:(u,M)\text{ winning}\}$ is non-empty and self-safe, with $|T|\le k$. So $k$ is at least the size of the smallest self-safe set.

Every self-safe set has at least $12$ cells. Let $T$ be a non-empty self-safe set. Cells are $(r,c)$ with row $r$ counted from the top; “up” from $(r,c)$ means the moves to $(r-2,c\pm1)$, “left” the moves to $(r\pm1,c-2)$, and so on. We use (i) in this form: if a direction has two moves from $u$ and neither target is in $T$, then $u\notin T$. The reflections of the board in its two axes and in a diagonal permute the four directions, so they map self-safe sets to self-safe sets.

For $1\le r,c\le6$ let $X(r,c)$ be the set of the five cells $(r,c)$, $(r\pm1,c\pm1)$ (a cell and its four diagonal neighbours).

Lemma 1. $T$ contains a cell of every $X(m)$. Suppose $T\cap X(m)=\emptyset$, $m=(m_r,m_c)$. For a cell $v$ let $d(v)=(v_r-m_r)^2+(v_c-m_c)^2$, and write $m+(x,y)$ for the cell $(m_r+x,m_c+y)$. Choose $u\in T$ with $d(u)$ as small as possible. After reflections (which keep the form of the sets $X$) we may assume $u=m+(x,y)$ with $x\ge y\ge0$. Since $u\notin X(m)$, $(x,y)\ne(0,0),(1,1)$, so $(x,y)=(1,0)$ or $x\ge2$. Note $d(m+(x',y'))-d(u)=x'^2+y'^2-x^2-y^2$.

  • $(x,y)=(1,0)$. “Up” from $u$ has the two moves to $m+(-1,\pm1)$, both in $X(m)$, so $u\notin T$: contradiction.

  • $x\ge2$ and $u$ is not in the last column. “Up” from $u$ has two moves, to $m+(x-2,y-1)$ and $m+(x-2,y+1)$, and one of them is in $T$. Their values of $d$ are $d(u)-(4x+2y-5)$ and $d(u)-(4x-2y-5)$. The first is smaller than $d(u)$; the second is smaller than $d(u)$ unless $(x,y)=(2,2)$ (for $x\ge3$, $4x-2y-5\ge2x-5>0$; for $x=2$, $y\le1$, $3-2y>0$). By the choice of $u$ we must have $(x,y)=(2,2)$ and $w=m+(0,3)\in T$. But “left” from $w$ has the two moves to $m+(\pm1,1)$, both in $X(m)$: contradiction.

  • $x\ge2$ and $u$ is in the last column (so $y=7-m_c\ge1$). If $u$ is the corner $(7,7)$, by (ii) one of its two moves, to $m+(x-2,y-1)$ or $m+(x-1,y-2)$, ends in $T$; the values of $d$ are $d(u)-(4x+2y-5)$ and $d(u)-(2x+4y-5)$, both smaller than $d(u)$: contradiction. Otherwise “left” from $u$ has two moves, so $w=m+(x+\varepsilon,y-2)\in T$ for some $\varepsilon=\pm1$. The cell $w$ is in column $5$ and in a row $\ge m_r+1\ge2$, so “up” from $w$ has two moves and $z=m+(x+\varepsilon-2,\ y-2+\delta)\in T$ for some $\delta=\pm1$. The four possibilities for $z$ are \[m+(x-1,y-1),\quad m+(x-1,y-3),\quad m+(x-3,y-1),\quad m+(x-3,y-3),\] with $d(z)-d(u)=-(2x+2y-2),\ -(2x+6y-10),\ -(6x+2y-10),\ -(6x+6y-18)$. For $x\ge2$, $y\ge1$ these are all negative, except that the second and the fourth vanish for $(x,y)=(2,1)$. So $(x,y)=(2,1)$, hence $m_c=6$ and $d(u)=5$. Now $\varepsilon=-1$ would give $w=m+(1,-1)\in X(m)$, so $w=m+(3,-1)$, and $z=m+(1,0)$ (with $d=1$, impossible) or $z=m+(1,-2)\in T$. This cell is in column $4$ and in row $m_r+1\le6$, so “right” from it has two moves, to $m+(0,0)\in X(m)$ and to $m+(2,0)$ with $d=4<5$. Neither is in $T$: contradiction.

This proves Lemma 1.

Lemma 2. $T$ contains a cell of $R=\{(1,0),(1,3),(3,0),(3,1),(3,2),(3,3)\}$. Suppose not. “Down” from $(1,2)$ has the two moves to $(3,1),(3,3)$, so $(1,2)\notin T$; “right” from $(2,1)$ has the two moves to $(1,3),(3,3)$, so $(2,1)\notin T$. Then $T$ contains no cell of $X(2,1)=\{(2,1),(1,0),(1,2),(3,0),(3,2)\}$, against Lemma 1.

Counting. The top-left $4\times4$ quarter of the board is the disjoint union of \[X(1,1)=\{(1,1),(0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(2,2)\},\qquad X(1,2)=\{(1,2),(0,1),(0,3),(2,1),(2,3)\},\qquad R\] (see the right part of the figure), so by the two lemmas it contains at least $3$ cells of $T$. By reflection the same holds for the other three quarters. Hence every self-safe set has at least $12$ cells, so $k\ge12$, and with the upper bound the answer is $\boxed{12}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Find all nonnegative integers $a,b,c$ such that \[20^a+b^4=2024^c.\]

Show the answer

$(a,b,c)=(0,0,0)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Fourth powers modulo $11$)

If $c=0$, then $20^a+b^4=1$, so $a=0$ (otherwise $20^a\ge20$) and then $b=0$. This gives $(a,b,c)=(0,0,0)$.

Let $c\ge1$. Since $2024=8\cdot11\cdot23$, we get $20^a+b^4\equiv0\pmod{11}$.

  • Modulo $11$, the fourth powers of $0,1,2,\dots,10$ are $0,1,5,4,3,9,9,3,4,5,1$; so $b^4\bmod 11$ lies in $\{0,1,3,4,5,9\}$.

  • $20\equiv-2\pmod{11}$ and $(-2)^5=-32\equiv1$, so $20^a\bmod11$ runs through $1,9,4,3,5$ (period $5$), and $-20^a\bmod11\in\{10,2,7,8,6\}$.

The two sets $\{0,1,3,4,5,9\}$ and $\{2,6,7,8,10\}$ are disjoint, so $b^4\equiv-20^a\pmod{11}$ is impossible. Hence the only solution is $(a,b,c)=\boxed{(0,0,0)}$.

Solution 2 (Raising to the fifth power)

If $c=0$, the equation is $20^a+b^4=1$, which forces $a=0$ and then $b=0$: the solution $(0,0,0)$.

Let $c\ge1$; as $11\mid2024$, we get $b^4\equiv-20^a\pmod{11}$. Here $11\nmid b$, since otherwise $11\mid20^a$. Raise both sides to the fifth power: \[b^{20}\equiv(-20^a)^5=-\bigl(20^5\bigr)^a\pmod{11}.\] By Fermat's little theorem $b^{20}=(b^{10})^2\equiv1$, and $20^5\equiv(-2)^5=-32\equiv1\pmod{11}$. So $1\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, which is false. Hence $(a,b,c)=\boxed{(0,0,0)}$ is the only solution.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

A Sudoku is a puzzle played on a $9\times 9$ grid. The grid is divided into nine $3\times 3$ subgrids. The cells are filled with the numbers $\{1,2,3,\dots,9\}$ in such a way that each row, each column, and each $3\times 3$ subgrid contains each of these digits exactly once. Let $M$ and $N$ denote the products of the numbers on the two main diagonals.

  • (a) Prove that there exists such a Sudoku for which $2025^3\mid MN$.

  • (b) Prove that there does not exist such a Sudoku for which $2025^4\mid MN$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting factors $3$ and $5$ on the diagonals)

Note $2025=3^4\cdot5^2$, so $2025^3=3^{12}5^{6}$ and $2025^4=3^{16}5^{8}$. The main diagonal passes through the three $3\times3$ boxes top-left, centre and bottom-right, three cells in each; the other diagonal through top-right, centre and bottom-left. The centre cell lies on both diagonals, so its digit is counted twice in $MN$.

(a) The following Sudoku works: \[\begin{array}{|ccc|ccc|ccc|}\hline 5&8&1&7&3&2&4&6&9\\ 7&3&6&9&8&4&1&5&2\\ 4&2&9&6&1&5&3&8&7\\\hline 2&7&4&1&6&8&5&9&3\\ 1&6&3&2&5&9&7&4&8\\ 9&5&8&3&4&7&2&1&6\\\hline 6&4&5&8&7&3&9&2&1\\ 8&9&7&5&2&1&6&3&4\\ 3&1&2&4&9&6&8&7&5\\\hline \end{array}\] (each row, column and box contains $1,\dots,9$). The main diagonal reads $5,3,9,1,5,7,9,3,5$ and the other diagonal $9,5,3,8,5,3,5,9,3$. So $M=3^6\cdot5^3\cdot7$ and $N=3^7\cdot5^3\cdot8$, hence $MN=3^{13}5^6\cdot56$ is divisible by $3^{12}5^6=2025^3$.

(b) Count the factors $5$ of $MN$; only the digit $5$ contributes, one factor each time it is counted. Each box contains exactly one $5$. The boxes top-left, bottom-right, top-right and bottom-left each meet only one diagonal, in three cells, so each contributes at most one factor $5$. In the centre box the two diagonals use five cells, among which there is at most one $5$; it contributes $2$ factors if it is the centre cell and at most $1$ otherwise. Hence $MN$ contains at most $4+2=6$ factors $5$, while $2025^4$ needs $8$. So no Sudoku has $2025^4\mid MN$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle, and let $D$ be a point on $BC$. The lines through $D$ parallel to $AB$ and $AC$ meet $AC$ and $AB$ at $E$ and $F$, respectively. Let the circumcircles of triangles $AEF$ and $ABC$ intersect again at $X$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. The line $XM$ meets the circumcircle of $ABC$ again at $Y$. Let $AY$ intersect $BC$ at $K$. Prove that $MD=MK$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Complex numbers)

The point $X$. The lines $FB$ and $EC$ meet at $A$, so the second intersection $X$ of the circles $AFE$ and $ABC$ is the centre of the spiral similarity that maps $F\mapsto B$ and $E\mapsto C$. (If $D=M$ the two circles are tangent at $A$ and there is no $X$; so $D\ne M$.)

Use complex numbers with $BC$ on the real axis, $M=0$, $B=-1$, $C=1$, $D=d$ real with $-1<d<1$, $d\ne0$, and $A=a$ not real. Since $DF\parallel AC$ and $DE\parallel AB$, Thales gives $BF:BA=BD:BC=\frac{1+d}2$ and $CE:CA=CD:CB=\frac{1-d}2$, so \[f=-1+\tfrac{1+d}{2}(a+1),\qquad e=1+\tfrac{1-d}{2}(a-1),\qquad e+f=a+d,\qquad f-e=da-1 .\] The spiral similarity condition reads $\frac{b-x}{f-x}=\frac{c-x}{e-x}$ with $b=-1$, $c=1$, i.e. $(-1-x)(e-x)=(1-x)(f-x)$, which is linear in $x$: \[x=\frac{e+f}{2+f-e}=\frac{a+d}{1+da}\] (here $1+da\ne0$, because $a$ is not real). The point $Y$. $M=0$ is inside the circumcircle and its power is $-MB\cdot MC=-1$, so $Y$ is on the line $XM$, on the other side of $M$, with $MX\cdot MY=1$: that is, $y=-\frac{x}{|x|^2}=-\frac1{\bar x}=-\frac{1+d\bar a}{\bar a+d}$.

The point $K$. We show that $-d$ lies on the line $AY$: \[y+d=\frac{-1-d\bar a+d\bar a+d^2}{\bar a+d}=\frac{d^2-1}{\bar a+d},\qquad \frac{y-(-d)}{a-(-d)}=\frac{d^2-1}{(\bar a+d)(a+d)}=\frac{d^2-1}{|a+d|^2}\in\mathbb R .\] Since $a$ is not real, the line $AY$ meets the real axis in a single point, so $K=-d$. Therefore $MK=|d|=MD$.

Solution 2 (Spiral similarity and a midline)

Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AD$. Since $AEDF$ is a parallelogram ($DE\parallel AF$, $DF\parallel AE$), $N$ is also the midpoint of $EF$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$. We may assume $D\ne M$ (for $D=M$ the circles $AFE$ and $ABC$ are tangent at $A$ and $X$ does not exist).

The spiral similarity. The lines $FB$ and $EC$ meet at $A$, so $X$, the second common point of the circles $AFE$ and $ABC$, is the centre of the spiral similarity $s$ with $s(F)=B$ and $s(E)=C$. A spiral similarity maps midpoints to midpoints, so $s(N)=M$. Since $s$ maps $F\mapsto B$ and $N\mapsto M$ with centre $X$, the triangles $XFB$ and $XNM$ are directly similar (same rotation angle, $XB:XF=XM:XN$), so \[\measuredangle(MX,MN)=\measuredangle(BX,BF)=\measuredangle(BX,BA).\] Parallel lines. $A,B,X,Y$ lie on the circumcircle, so $\measuredangle(BX,BA)=\measuredangle(YX,YA)$. The lines $MX$ and $YX$ coincide (as $X,M,Y$ are collinear), hence \[\measuredangle(YX,MN)=\measuredangle(YX,YA),\] so $MN\parallel YA$, i.e. $MN\parallel AK$.

Midline. In triangle $ADK$ the line through the midpoint $N$ of $AD$ parallel to $AK$ meets $DK$ (the line $BC$) at the midpoint of $DK$. That point is $M$, because $M$ lies on $BC$ and on this line. Hence $MD=MK$.

Solution 3 (Oblique coordinates and the reflected point)

We may assume that $D$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$ and that $D\ne M$ (for $D=M$ the circles $AEF$ and $ABC$ are tangent at $A$ and $X$ does not exist).

Coordinates. Let $\mathbf p=\overrightarrow{AB}$, $\mathbf q=\overrightarrow{AC}$, and give the point $A+\xi\mathbf p+\eta\mathbf q$ the coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$. Then $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(0,1)$, $M=(\frac12,\frac12)$, and $D=(1-t,t)$ with $0<t<1$, $t\ne\frac12$. Since $DE\parallel AB$ and $DF\parallel AC$, we get $E=(0,t)$ and $F=(1-t,0)$. Put $b=AB^2$, $c=AC^2$, $g=\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$. The squared distance from $A$ to $(\xi,\eta)$ is \[\varphi(\xi,\eta)=b\xi^2+2g\xi\eta+c\eta^2,\] which is positive for $(\xi,\eta)\ne(0,0)$.

Powers of a point. If a circle with centre $O$ passes through $A$, put $\mathbf w=\overrightarrow{AO}$; the power of a point $P$ is $|\overrightarrow{AP}-\mathbf w|^2-|\mathbf w|^2=AP^2-2\,\mathbf w\cdot\overrightarrow{AP}$. For the circle $ABC$, the points $B$ and $C$ give $2\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf p=b$ and $2\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf q=c$; for the circle $AEF$, the points $F=A+(1-t)\mathbf p$ and $E=A+t\mathbf q$ give $2\mathbf w'\cdot\mathbf p=(1-t)b$ and $2\mathbf w'\cdot\mathbf q=tc$. So the two powers are \[f_1=\varphi(\xi,\eta)-b\xi-c\eta,\qquad f_2=\varphi(\xi,\eta)-(1-t)b\xi-tc\eta .\] At $X$ both vanish, so $f_1-f_2=-tb\xi-(1-t)c\eta=0$ there: $X$ lies on the line through $A$ with direction $U=\bigl(c(1-t),\,-bt\bigr)$.

Second intersections. For a direction $w=(w_1,w_2)\ne(0,0)$, the point $\lambda w$ is on the circle $ABC$ exactly when $\lambda^2\varphi(w)-\lambda(bw_1+cw_2)=0$. So the line through $A$ with direction $w$ meets the circle again at \[R(w)=\frac{bw_1+cw_2}{\varphi(w)}\,w .\] For $U$ we have $L_U:=bU_1+cU_2=bc(1-2t)\ne0$, so $X=R(U)=\frac{L_U}{D_U}U$ with $D_U=\varphi(U)$.

The reflected point. Let $K'=(t,1-t)$; it lies on $BC$ (its coordinates add up to $1$), and $D+K'=(1,1)$ in coordinates, so $K'$ is the reflection of $D$ in $M$. Let $V=(t,1-t)$, $L_V=bt+c(1-t)>0$, $D_V=\varphi(V)$, and $Z=R(V)=\frac{L_V}{D_V}V$: the second intersection of the line $AK'$ with the circle $ABC$; $Z\ne A$ as $L_V\ne0$. We show that $Z$ lies on the line $XM$.

Write $[P,Q]=P_1Q_2-P_2Q_1$; then $P,M,Q$ are collinear exactly when $[P-M,Q-M]=0$, and \[[P-M,Q-M]=[P,Q]-[P,M]+[Q,M],\qquad [P,M]=\tfrac12(P_1-P_2).\] Let $\Delta=[U,V]=c(1-t)^2+bt^2>0$. Directly, \[D_U=bc^2(1-t)^2-2gbc\,t(1-t)+b^2c\,t^2,\qquad D_V=bt^2+2g\,t(1-t)+c(1-t)^2,\] so the terms with $g$ cancel in \[D_U+bc\,D_V=2bc\bigl(c(1-t)^2+bt^2\bigr)=2bc\,\Delta. \tag{1}\] Moreover $U_1-U_2=c(1-t)+bt=L_V$ and $V_1-V_2=2t-1$. Hence \[[X-M,Z-M]=\frac{L_UL_V\Delta}{D_UD_V}-\frac{L_UL_V}{2D_U}+\frac{L_V(2t-1)}{2D_V},\] and, using $L_U=bc(1-2t)$ and (1), \[2D_UD_V\,[X-M,Z-M]=(1-2t)L_V\bigl(2bc\,\Delta-bc\,D_V-D_U\bigr)=0 .\] So $X$, $M$, $Z$ are collinear.

Conclusion. $M$ is inside the circle $ABC$ (it is an interior point of the chord $BC$), so $X\ne M$ and the line $XM$ meets the circle in exactly the two points $X$ and $Y$. The point $Z$ is on the circle and on this line. Also $Z\ne X$: both differ from $A$, and they lie on the lines through $A$ with directions $U$ and $V$, which are different lines since $[U,V]=\Delta\ne0$. Hence $Z=Y$, and the line $AY$ is the line $AK'$. This line is not parallel to $BC$ (its direction $V$ satisfies $[V,(-1,1)]=1\ne0$), so it meets $BC$ only at $K'$. Therefore $K=K'$, the reflection of $D$ in $M$, and $MD=MK$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation

Prove that there exist polynomials $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ with real coefficients such that $\deg P\ge 2026$, $\deg Q\ge 2$, and for all real numbers $x$, \[P(Q(x)-x-1)=Q(P(x)).\]

Show the answer

e.g. $Q(x)=\frac{x^2+5x}{2}$ and $P=U^{\circ11}-1$ with $U(x)=\frac{x^2+3x-2}{2}$ ($\deg P=2048$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Conjugating by a translation)

Idea. Write $U(x)=Q(x)-x-1$; the equation is $P\circ U=Q\circ P$. If $Q$ were conjugate to $U$ by a translation $L(x)=x-1$, that is $Q\circ L=L\circ U$, then $P=L\circ U^{\circ m}$ (with $U^{\circ m}=U\circ\dots\circ U$, $m$ times) would work for every $m$. The condition $Q(x-1)=U(x)-1=Q(x)-x-2$ says $Q(x)-Q(x-1)=x+2$, which a quadratic $Q$ can satisfy.

The example. Let \[Q(x)=\frac{x^2+5x}{2},\qquad U(x)=Q(x)-x-1=\frac{x^2+3x-2}{2},\qquad P(x)=U^{\circ11}(x)-1 .\] First, \[Q(x-1)+1=\frac{(x-1)^2+5(x-1)}{2}+1=\frac{x^2+3x-2}{2}=U(x),\qquad\text{so}\quad Q(z-1)=U(z)-1\ \text{for all }z. \tag{1}\] Now for every real $x$, using (1) with $z=U^{\circ11}(x)$, \[Q(P(x))=Q\bigl(U^{\circ11}(x)-1\bigr)=U\bigl(U^{\circ11}(x)\bigr)-1=U^{\circ12}(x)-1,\] \[P\bigl(Q(x)-x-1\bigr)=P(U(x))=U^{\circ11}\bigl(U(x)\bigr)-1=U^{\circ12}(x)-1 .\] So $P(Q(x)-x-1)=Q(P(x))$. Finally $\deg Q=2$, and since $U$ has degree $2$, $\deg P=2^{11}=2048\ge2026$.

Solution 2 (A monomial ansatz)

Finding the example. Try $Q(x)=kx^2+x+1$, so that $Q(x)-x-1=kx^2$, and $P(x)=p+qx^m$ with $m$ even. Then $P(Q(x)-x-1)=p+qk^mx^{2m}$ is $p$ plus a monomial, and $Q(P(x))=Q(p+qx^m)$ has the same shape if $Q(p+u)=p+ku^2$ for all $u$, that is, if the vertex of the parabola $y=Q(x)$ is at $x=p$ and $Q(p)=p$. The vertex is at $-\frac1{2k}$, so $k=-\frac1{2p}$, and $Q(p)=kp^2+p+1=p$ gives $-\frac p2+1=0$: $p=2$, $k=-\frac14$.

The example. Let $m=2048$, $\gamma=4^{1-m}$ and \[Q(x)=1+x-\frac{x^2}{4},\qquad P(x)=2-\gamma x^{m}.\] Then $Q(x)-x-1=-\frac{x^2}{4}$, and for every real $u$ \[Q(2+u)=1+(2+u)-\frac{4+4u+u^2}{4}=2-\frac{u^2}{4}.\] Since $m$ is even, \[P\bigl(Q(x)-x-1\bigr)=2-\gamma\Bigl(-\frac{x^2}{4}\Bigr)^{m}=2-\gamma\,4^{-m}x^{2m},\] \[Q(P(x))=Q\bigl(2+(-\gamma x^m)\bigr)=2-\frac{\gamma^2}{4}x^{2m}.\] These are equal because $\frac{\gamma^2}{4}=\gamma\cdot\frac{4^{1-m}}{4}=\gamma\,4^{-m}$. So $P(Q(x)-x-1)=Q(P(x))$ for all real $x$, with $\deg P=2048\ge2026$ and $\deg Q=2$. (Any even $m\ge2026$ works with $\gamma=4^{1-m}$.)
