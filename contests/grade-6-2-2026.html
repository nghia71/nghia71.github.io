Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Absolute Value as Distance

The sum of two different positive integers is $8$ more than the absolute difference between the two integers. What is the least possible value of the sum of the two integers?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick two different positive integers as a test pair. Write their sum and their absolute difference separately, keeping track of which integer is larger.
Hint 2: key idea
Label the smaller number $a$ and the larger one $b$, so the absolute difference is $b-a$. Compare this expression with the sum and look for a term that cancels. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Write the given relationship as an equation and cancel the larger number to determine the smaller one. Then decide how small the larger integer can be while keeping the two numbers different.
Common trap
Choosing two equal numbers would violate the problem even if they satisfied the equation.
Show the answer

$9$

Show solution 1 – Sum minus difference

Call the smaller number $a$ and the larger number $b$. The absolute difference is $b-a$. We are told \[a+b=(b-a)+8.\] Take $b$ away from both sides: $a=-a+8$, so $2a=8$ and $a=4$.

So the smaller number must be $4$, and the larger number can be any whole number bigger than $4$. The smallest choice is $b=5$, which gives the sum $4+5=9$. Check: $9$ is $8$ more than the difference $5-4=1$. The least possible sum is $\boxed{9}$.

Show solution 2 – Search by the sum

Suppose the sum is $s$. Then the difference is $s-8$. The two numbers are different, so the difference is at least $1$; hence $s-8\ge 1$ and $s\ge 9$.

For $s=9$ the difference must be $1$: two numbers adding to $9$ and differing by $1$ are $4$ and $5$. They are different positive integers, so $s=9$ really happens. The least possible sum is $\boxed{9}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Weighted Average and Mixture Ratios

Mirko the mouse has three different-sized blocks of cheese, each made up of identically-sized small cubes, as shown in the diagram. He ate $40\%$ of the first block, $40\%$ of the second and $20\%$ of the third. What percentage of the total amount of cheese did Mirko eat?

(A) $21\%$ (B) $22\%$ (C) $23\%$ (D) $24\%$ (E) $25\%$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Look at one block in the diagram and count the small cubes along an edge. Think about how many layers the whole block has.
Hint 2: key idea
Use one small cube as the unit of volume for all three blocks. Find each block's volume from its edge length, and apply its own percentage to that volume. (LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change)
Hint 3: approach
Work out the original amount of cheese and the amount eaten for each block in small-cube units. Add the amounts in each group separately and set up the ratio of the total eaten to the total originally present.
Common trap
Averaging the three percentages ignores that the blocks contain different amounts of cheese.
Show the answer

(E) $25\%$

Show solution 1 – Count small cubes

The blocks are cubes with edges of $1$, $2$ and $3$ small cubes, so they contain \[1,\qquad 2\times2\times2=8,\qquad 3\times3\times3=27\] small cubes: $36$ small cubes in all. Mirko ate \[40\%\text{ of }1=0.4,\qquad 40\%\text{ of }8=3.2,\qquad 20\%\text{ of }27=5.4,\] which is $0.4+3.2+5.4=9$ small cubes. As a part of the total this is $\frac{9}{36}=\frac14=25\%$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 25\%}$.

Show solution 2 – Group the blocks

The blocks hold $1$, $8$ and $27$ small cubes, $36$ in all. The first two blocks together hold $9$ cubes, which is $\frac{9}{36}=\frac14$ of the cheese, and Mirko ate $40\%$ of both of them. The third block holds $27$ cubes, which is $\frac34$ of the cheese, and he ate $20\%$ of it.

So he ate $40\%$ of one quarter and $20\%$ of three quarters of the cheese: \[\tfrac14\times40\%+\tfrac34\times20\%=10\%+15\%=25\%.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 25\%}$.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Ratio Interpretation

On Iyla's island there are some lions, some tigers and some jaguars. Two-thirds of the animals are not lions; three-quarters of the animals are not tigers; $84$ of the animals are not jaguars. How many animals are on Iyla's island?

(A) $88$ (B) $108$ (C) $120$ (D) $144$ (E) $160$

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the three kinds of animals. For each clue, mark which kinds are included in the stated fraction or count.
Hint 2: key idea
Subtract the fraction that are not lions from one to get the fraction that are lions, and do the same for tigers. The jaguars make up the part left over after those two fractions. (LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change)
Hint 3: approach
Put the lion and tiger fractions over a common denominator and use them to find the jaguar fraction. Find its complement and match that fraction of the whole island to the given headcount. Use the match to set up the value of one equal part.
Common trap
The stated headcount excludes jaguars, so it cannot be matched to the jaguar fraction.
Show the answer

(D) $144$

Show solution 1 – Fractions of the whole

If two-thirds of the animals are not lions, then the lions are $\frac13$ of the animals. If three-quarters are not tigers, the tigers are $\frac14$ of the animals. The jaguars are the rest: \[1-\tfrac13-\tfrac14=\tfrac{12-4-3}{12}=\tfrac{5}{12}.\] So the animals that are not jaguars make up $\frac{7}{12}$ of all the animals, and there are $84$ of them. One twelfth of the animals is $84\div7=12$, so there are $12\times12=144$ animals. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 144}$.

Show solution 2 – Test the options

The lions are $\frac13$ of the animals and the tigers $\frac14$, so the number of animals divides by both $3$ and $4$, that is, by $12$. Among the options only $108$, $120$ and $144$ do.

  • $108$ animals: $36$ lions and $27$ tigers, so $63$ are not jaguars.

  • $120$ animals: $40$ lions and $30$ tigers, so $70$ are not jaguars.

  • $144$ animals: $48$ lions and $36$ tigers, so $84$ are not jaguars.

Only the last one gives $84$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 144}$.

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations · Composite Angle Chases

The diagram shows kite $FGHI$, in which $IF=FG$ and $GH=HI$, and parallelogram $HIJK$. Points $G$, $H$ and $K$ lie on a straight line. Angle $JKH$ is $80^\circ$ and angle $FGH$ is $85^\circ$. What is the size of angle $IFG$?

(A) $100^\circ$ (B) $110^\circ$ (C) $120^\circ$ (D) $125^\circ$ (E) $150^\circ$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the two given angles in the diagram and trace the straight line through $G$, $H$, and $K$. Label the angle on each side of $HI$ at $H$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use adjacent angles in the parallelogram and the straight line through $H$ to transfer the given angle at $K$ into the kite. The equal sides of the kite give a line of symmetry that relates the angles at $G$ and $I$. (LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations)
Hint 3: approach
Find $\angle IHK$ from $\angle JKH$, then use the straight line to find $\angle IHG$. Use the kite's symmetry about $FH$ to fill in the angle at $I$, and write the sum of the kite's four interior angles.
Common trap
The angle at $H$ inside the parallelogram is different from the angle at $H$ inside the kite.
Show the answer

(B) $110^\circ$

Show solution 1 – Angles of the kite

In parallelogram $HIJK$ the angles at $H$ and $K$ are next to each other, so they add up to $180^\circ$: $\angle IHK=180^\circ-80^\circ=100^\circ$. Since $G$, $H$, $K$ lie on a straight line, $\angle IHG=180^\circ-100^\circ=80^\circ$.

The kite $FGHI$ is symmetric about the line $FH$ (because $FI=FG$ and $HI=HG$), so its angles at $G$ and $I$ are equal: $\angle FIH=\angle FGH=85^\circ$. The four angles of a quadrilateral add up to $360^\circ$, so \[\angle IFG=360^\circ-85^\circ-85^\circ-80^\circ=110^\circ.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 110^\circ}$.

Show solution 2 – Cut the kite in half

In parallelogram $HIJK$ the opposite angles are equal, so $\angle JIH=80^\circ$. Because $IJ$ is parallel to $KH$, which lies along the line $GH$, the angles $\angle JIH$ and $\angle IHG$ are alternate angles, so $\angle IHG=80^\circ$.

Draw the line $FH$. Triangles $FGH$ and $FIH$ have equal sides ($FG=FI$, $HG=HI$, $FH$ shared), so they are congruent, and $FH$ cuts the angles at $H$ and at $F$ in half. Hence $\angle FHG=40^\circ$. In triangle $FGH$: \[\angle GFH=180^\circ-85^\circ-40^\circ=55^\circ,\] and so $\angle IFG=2\times55^\circ=110^\circ$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 110^\circ}$.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

The units digit of a number is $1$. Julio removes this digit to obtain a new number that is $2026$ less than the original number. What is the sum of the digits of the original number?

(A) $10$ (B) $12$ (C) $14$ (D) $16$ (E) $18$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try removing the last digit from a simple number ending in one. Look at how the place values of the remaining digits change.
Hint 2: key idea
Call the shortened number $a$. To rebuild the original, shift every digit of $a$ one place to the left and then add the given units digit. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Write the original number in terms of $a$ and subtract $a$ to match the stated difference. Solve for the shortened number and put the units digit back before working out the digit sum.
Common trap
The stated difference is the amount by which the whole number changes, not the digit that was removed.
Show the answer

(A) $10$

Show solution 1 – Place value

Let $a$ be the new number (the original number with its last digit removed). Putting the digit $1$ back on the end multiplies $a$ by $10$ and adds $1$, so the original number is $10a+1$. We are told \[(10a+1)-a=2026,\qquad\text{so}\qquad 9a=2025,\qquad a=225.\] The original number is $2251$, and its digit sum is $2+2+5+1=10$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 10}$.

Show solution 2 – Rebuild the number digit by digit

Call the new number $X$. Then $X+2026$ is the original number, which is $X$ with a $1$ written after it.

  • The units digit of $X+2026$ is $1$, so the units digit of $X$ is $5$ (as $5+6=11$; we carry $1$).

  • The tens digit of the original number is the units digit of $X$, which is $5$. The tens digit of $X+2026$ is (tens digit of $X$) $+2+1$, so the tens digit of $X$ is $2$.

  • The hundreds digit of the original number is then $2$; adding the $0$ of $2026$ gives the hundreds digit of $X$ as $2$.

  • The thousands digit of the original number is $2$, and $2026$ has $2$ thousands (with no carry from the hundreds), so $X$ has thousands digit $0$.

  • The ten-thousands digit of the original number is the thousands digit of $X$, which is $0$. There is no carry and $2026$ has no ten-thousands digit, so the ten-thousands digit of $X$ is $0$ too. In the same way every further digit of $X$ to the left is $0$.

So $X=225$, the original number is $2251$ (and indeed $2251-225=2026$), and its digit sum is $10$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 10}$.

Problem 6

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Percent Increase and Decrease

In an orchard, $80\%$ of the trees were apple trees and the rest were pear trees. Last summer, the apple weevil killed some of the apple trees but no pear trees. Now $50\%$ of the trees are apple trees. What percentage of the apple trees were killed by the apple weevil?

(A) $30\%$ (B) $40\%$ (C) $50\%$ (D) $60\%$ (E) $75\%$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Make a before-and-after list for apple and pear trees. Which tree count stays the same, and which percentages describe the whole orchard?
Hint 2: key idea
Imagine starting with $100$ trees so the original percentages give direct counts. The pear count stays fixed and now accounts for half of the remaining trees, which lets you find the new apple count. (LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change)
Hint 3: approach
Find the original counts and carry the pear count unchanged into the new orchard. Use the new percentage split to find the surviving apples, then set up the fraction of the original apple trees that were lost.
Common trap
The percentage killed is measured against the original apple count, not against all the trees.
Show the answer

(E) $75\%$

Show solution 1 – Imagine 100 trees

Only percentages matter, so the answer does not depend on the size of the orchard. Suppose there were $100$ trees: $80$ apple trees and $20$ pear trees. No pear tree died, so there are still $20$ pear trees. Now the apple trees are $50\%$ of the trees, so the pear trees are the other $50\%$; the orchard now has $40$ trees, of which $20$ are apple trees.

So $80-20=60$ of the $80$ apple trees were killed, which is $\frac{60}{80}=75\%$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 75\%}$.

Show solution 2 – Measure everything in pear trees

The number of pear trees never changes, so use it as the unit. Before, pear trees were $\frac15$ of the orchard, so there were $4$ apple trees for every pear tree. Afterwards, apple trees and pear trees are equal in number, so there is $1$ apple tree for every pear tree.

For every $4$ apple trees, $3$ died: that is $\frac34=75\%$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 75\%}$.

Problem 7

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Substitution and Elimination

Mariam has $13$ dollars less than the total amount that Ria and Emma have. Ria has $5$ dollars more than the total amount that Mariam and Emma have. How many dollars does Emma have?

(A) $18$ (B) $12$ (C) $8$ (D) $7$ (E) $4$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Give each person's amount a letter. In each statement, underline which two amounts are added before making the comparison.
Hint 2: key idea
Write one equation for each comparison, keeping the stated dollar adjustments on the correct sides. If you add the equations, Mariam's and Ria's amounts occur on both sides and can be cancelled. (LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints)
Hint 3: approach
Put Mariam's amount on the left of the first equation and Ria's on the left of the second. Add the equations and cancel the common terms so that only Emma's amount remains to be found.
Common trap
Each comparison uses the combined amount of two people, not either person's amount by itself.
Show the answer

(E) $4$

Show solution 1 – Add the two facts

Let Mariam, Ria and Emma have $m$, $r$ and $e$ dollars. The two facts say \[m=r+e-13\qquad\text{and}\qquad r=m+e+5.\] Add the two equations: \[m+r=r+m+2e-8.\] Take $m+r$ away from both sides: $0=2e-8$, so $e=4$. Emma has $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 4}$ dollars.

(Any $m$ works with $r=m+9$; for example $m=10$, $r=19$, $e=4$ satisfies both facts.)

Show solution 2 – Compare two gaps

Look at Ria compared with Mariam.

  • Ria has $5$ dollars more than Mariam and Emma together, so Ria is ahead of Mariam by $5$ dollars plus Emma's money.

  • Ria and Emma together have $13$ dollars more than Mariam, so Ria is ahead of Mariam by $13$ dollars minus Emma's money.

Both describe the same gap, so $5+e=13-e$, where $e$ is Emma's money. Then $2e=8$ and $e=4$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 4}$.

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

In Kush's Cake shop, there are chocolate cakes, cherry cakes, cream cakes and coffee cakes. For every seven chocolate cakes, there are six cherry cakes. For every five cherry cakes, there are four cream cakes. For every three cream cakes, there are two coffee cakes. Which of these could be the total number of cakes in Kush's shop?

(A) $60$ (B) $150$ (C) $240$ (D) $330$ (E) $420$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the three pairs of cake types in order and label each pair with its given ratio. Notice where the same cake type appears in two comparisons.
Hint 2: key idea
Scale neighbouring ratios so they give the same number of parts for the cake type they share. Common multiples let you join the ratios without changing any of the original comparisons. (LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction)
Hint 3: approach
Match the cherry terms first, then match the cream terms, to get one ratio for all the cake types. Use the coprime chocolate and coffee terms to justify that the common scale factor is an integer. Add the four terms to set up a divisibility test for the offered totals.
Common trap
Using the given ratios side by side without rescaling may assign two different counts to the same cake type.
Show the answer

(E) $420$

Show solution 1 – Join the ratios

Write the three ratios with matching middle terms.

  • chocolate : cherry $=7:6=35:30$;

  • cherry : cream $=5:4=30:24$;

  • cream : coffee $=3:2=24:16$.

So chocolate : cherry : cream : coffee $=35:30:24:16$. In particular chocolate : coffee $=35:16$, and $35$ and $16$ have no common factor larger than $1$, so this ratio cannot be simplified: there are $35k$ chocolate cakes and $16k$ coffee cakes for some whole number $k$. Then there are $30k$ cherry cakes and $24k$ cream cakes, and the total is $35k+30k+24k+16k=105k$, a multiple of $105$ (and every multiple of $105$ can happen). Among the options only $420=4\times105$ is a multiple of $105$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 420}$.

Show solution 2 – Start from the cherry cakes

The number of cherry cakes is $\frac67$ of the number of chocolate cakes, so it is a multiple of $6$; it is also cut into fifths (four cream cakes for every five cherry cakes), so it is a multiple of $5$. Hence there are $30t$ cherry cakes for some whole number $t$. Then \[\text{chocolate}=\tfrac76\cdot30t=35t,\quad \text{cream}=\tfrac45\cdot30t=24t,\quad \text{coffee}=\tfrac23\cdot24t=16t,\] all whole numbers. The total is $35t+30t+24t+16t=105t$. Of $60,150,240,330,420$ only $420$ (with $t=4$) has this form. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 420}$.

Problem 9

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime and Composite Structure

Five children each writes down a different positive integer. One writes down a square, one writes down a cube, one writes down a prime, one writes down an odd integer and one writes down an even integer. What is the smallest possible total of the five integers?

(A) $15$ (B) $17$ (C) $19$ (D) $21$ (E) $23$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the five roles next to five empty spaces. Try a few small positive integers and mark every role each one could fill.
Hint 2: key idea
Start with the smallest set of five distinct positive integers you could use. Its total gives a lower bound, and the roles tell you whether that bound can be reached. (LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization)
Hint 3: approach
Decide which numbers in that set could serve as the square and which as the cube. Then check whether the numbers left over can cover the prime, odd and even roles, one number per role.
Common trap
A number can qualify for more than one role, but it can appear in only one child's space.
Show the answer

(A) $15$

Show the solution – Smallest possible, then check it works

Five different positive integers add up to at least $1+2+3+4+5=15$. So the total cannot be less than $15$.

Can $1,2,3,4,5$ be shared out to fit the five roles? The only cube among them is $1$ ($=1^3$), so the cube-writer takes $1$. The square-writer then takes $4$ ($=2^2$). The other three numbers $2,3,5$ are all prime, so give $3$ to the prime-writer, $5$ to the odd-writer and $2$ to the even-writer. Every child has a different number that fits, and the total is $15$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 15}$.

Problem 10

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition · Additive and Subtractive Area

Rafael uses three squares to create a shape. The first has side-length $3$ cm. The second has side-length $5$ cm and one of its vertices is placed in the centre of the first square. The third square has side-length $7$ cm and one of its vertices is placed in the centre of the second square, as shown in the diagram. The top edges of all three squares are parallel. What is the total shaded area in cm$^2$?

(A) $66$ (B) $74.5$ (C) $75$ (D) $77.5$ (E) $83$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Trace the three square outlines and mark the two centres used in the construction. Shade the places where one square lies over another.
Hint 2: key idea
A larger square that starts at the centre of a smaller one covers a square with half the smaller side length. When adding the full square areas, keep track of the overlap regions counted twice. (LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Find the side lengths of the overlaps for the first two squares and for the last two squares. Use the offsets in the diagram to check whether the first and third squares touch, then keep the full areas and the overlap areas in separate lists.
Common trap
Check whether the first and third squares overlap before deciding how many overlap areas to account for.
Show the answer

(B) $74.5$

Show solution 1 – Add the squares, remove the overlaps

The second square has a corner at the centre of the first square, and its sides are parallel to the sides of the first. It is bigger than the first square, so it covers exactly the quarter of the first square next to that corner: a square of side $\frac32$ cm, with area $\frac94=2.25$ cm$^2$. In the same way the third square covers a quarter of the second square: a square of side $\frac52$ cm, area $\frac{25}{4}=6.25$ cm$^2$. The first and third squares do not meet (the first square ends $3$ cm from its top-left corner, and the third starts $1.5+2.5=4$ cm across and down).

Adding the three areas counts each overlap twice, so the shaded area is \[9+25+49-2.25-6.25=74.5\ \text{cm}^2.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 74.5}$.

Show solution 2 – Count what can be seen

Look at the shape as three cards laid one on top of the next. The third square ($49$ cm$^2$) is seen whole. The second square shows everything except the quarter hidden under the third square, so it shows $\frac34\times25=18.75$ cm$^2$. The first square shows everything except the quarter hidden under the second square, so it shows $\frac34\times9=6.75$ cm$^2$. (Each hidden part is a quarter because a corner of the larger square sits at the centre of the smaller one, with sides parallel. The third square hides nothing of the first: measured from the top-left corner of the first square, the third square starts $1.5+2.5=4$ cm across and down, beyond the first square's $3$ cm sides.)

Total: $49+18.75+6.75=74.5$ cm$^2$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 74.5}$.

Problem 11

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work · Relative Motion

On Planet Mathian, the Mathinian folk measure time on an $8$ “hour” clock with $90$ “minutes” per “hour”. How many degrees are in the measure of the smaller angle formed by the hands of this clock when the time is $2{:}24$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a clock face with eight equally spaced hour marks. Mark the direction of each hand at $2{:}00$, then think about how each one moves over the next $24$ minutes.
Hint 2: key idea
Measure both hands clockwise from the top of the dial. In an hour the minute hand makes a full turn, while the hour hand moves through one gap between hour marks. (LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work)
Hint 3: approach
Find the angle between neighboring hour marks and use it to locate the hour hand at $2{:}00$. Apply the fraction $24/90$ to the distance each hand turns in one hour, then record the two resulting directions from the top.
Common trap
The hour hand keeps moving during the $24$ minutes after $2{:}00$.
Show the answer

$6$

Show solution 1 – Degrees for each hand

The clock has $8$ hours around $360^\circ$, so each hour mark is $360^\circ\div8=45^\circ$ apart. Measure both hands from the top (the $8$), clockwise.

Minute hand. It goes once round in $90$ minutes. After $24$ minutes it has turned $\frac{24}{90}\times360^\circ=96^\circ$.

Hour hand. At $2{:}00$ it points at the $2$, which is $2\times45^\circ=90^\circ$. In $24$ of the $90$ minutes of an hour it moves $\frac{24}{90}$ of the next $45^\circ$, which is $12^\circ$. So it is at $102^\circ$.

The angle between the hands is $102^\circ-96^\circ=$ $\boxed{6}$ degrees.

Show solution 2 – Fractions of a full turn

Count the time since the clock last showed $8{:}00$, when both hands pointed to the top, in Mathian minutes. A whole turn of the hour hand takes $8\times90=720$ minutes, and it is now $2\times90+24=204$ minutes after the top. So the hour hand has made $\frac{204}{720}$ of a turn.

The minute hand makes a whole turn in $90$ minutes, and $24$ minutes have passed since the hour began, so it has made $\frac{24}{90}=\frac{192}{720}$ of a turn.

The hands are $\frac{204-192}{720}=\frac{12}{720}=\frac{1}{60}$ of a turn apart, and $\frac1{60}\times360^\circ=6^\circ$. The answer is $\boxed{6}$ degrees.

Problem 12

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Factorization

The five numbers $95$, $96$, $97$, $98$, $99$ are each paired with one of the five numbers $1$, $3$, $5$, $7$, $9$ (each number used once). The larger number of each pair is then divided by the smaller, giving five integer answers. What is the difference between the smallest and the largest of the five integer answers?

(A) $96$ (B) $86$ (C) $76$ (D) $66$ (E) $56$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Put the five larger numbers in one list and the five possible partners in another. For one larger number, try a few partners and record which divisions have no remainder.
Hint 2: key idea
An integer quotient means the partner must be a divisor of the larger number. Use prime factorization to narrow the list of possible partners for each number. (LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization)
Hint 3: approach
Find which large number is prime and reserve its sole possible partner. Assign the forced partners next, check the remaining numbers for divisibility by $3$ and $9$, then identify the smallest and largest quotients from your completed pairing.
Common trap
Cross out each partner as soon as you use it, since every partner can be used only once.
Show the answer

(B) $86$

Show the solution – The pairing is forced

Each big number must be divisible by its partner. Factorise the big numbers: \[95=5\times19,\quad 96=2^5\times3,\quad 97\ \text{is prime},\quad 98=2\times7^2,\quad 99=3^2\times11.\]

  • $97$ is prime, so its only possible partner from $1,3,5,7,9$ is $1$.

  • With $1$ used, $95$ must take $5$ and $98$ must take $7$ (their only other divisors in the list).

  • $96$ is divisible by $3$ but not by $9$, so $96$ takes $3$, and $99$ takes $9$.

The answers are $95\div5=19$, $96\div3=32$, $97\div1=97$, $98\div7=14$ and $99\div9=11$. The difference between the largest and the smallest is $97-11=86$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 86}$.

Problem 13

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting Principles · Unordered Selections

Nell wants to write the numbers $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ and $5$ in the five cells in the diagram so that any number directly below another number is greater than that number and any number directly to the right of another number is greater than that number. In how many ways can she do this?

(A) $3$ (B) $4$ (C) $5$ (D) $6$ (E) $8$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Copy the shape and draw arrows from each cell to its neighbor on the right or below, wherever there is one. Each arrow should point from a smaller entry to a larger one.
Hint 2: key idea
Find the cell from which you can reach every other cell by following the arrows. After that entry is fixed, focus on which numbers belong in each arm of the shape. (LPS VI, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting Principles)
Hint 3: approach
Put the smallest number in the common corner, then choose which remaining numbers go in the horizontal arm. Decide how the increasing rule places those numbers and where the unused numbers must go.
Common trap
Choosing the same numbers for an arm in a different order does not produce a new increasing arrangement.
Show the answer

(D) $6$

Show solution 1 – Choose the numbers for the row

The top-left cell has a number to its right and a number below it, and every other number is reached from it by moving right or down, so it holds the smallest number, $1$.

The remaining numbers $2,3,4,5$ go into two arms: two cells to the right of $1$ and two cells below $1$. Each arm must increase, and the two arms do not affect each other. So Nell only has to choose which two numbers go in the row; they are then written in increasing order, and the other two go down the column in increasing order. The number of ways to choose $2$ numbers out of $4$ is $\frac{4\times3}{2}=6$: \[\{2,3\},\ \{2,4\},\ \{2,5\},\ \{3,4\},\ \{3,5\},\ \{4,5\}.\] The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 6}$.

Show solution 2 – Place the largest number first

Place the numbers from $5$ down to $1$. At each step the largest remaining number must go at the end of an arm (the right end of the row or the bottom of the column, among the cells still empty), since nothing in that arm may come after it.

The top-left cell must be the last one filled (it gets $1$). So we fill the two row cells and the two column cells, always taking the outermost empty cell of the arm we choose. A filling is just the order in which we choose the arms, for example row, column, column, row. We need two row choices and two column choices, in some order: \[RRCC,\ RCRC,\ RCCR,\ CRRC,\ CRCR,\ CCRR,\] which is $6$ orders. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 6}$.

Problem 14

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · Contextual Integer Models

In Poppy's pond there are frogs and snakes. Four-fifths of the frogs are green frogs and six-sevenths of the snakes are black snakes. Which of the following could not be the total number of creatures in Poppy's pond?

(A) $22$ (B) $23$ (C) $24$ (D) $26$ (E) $27$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Suppose there is just one complete group of frogs in which four-fifths are green. How big would that group be, and what would the corresponding group of snakes look like?
Hint 2: key idea
The fractions are in lowest terms, and the numbers of green frogs and black snakes are whole numbers. Use each denominator to determine what multiples the total of that kind of creature must be. (LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations)
Hint 3: approach
For each proposed total, list the positive multiples of $7$ smaller than that total as possible snake counts. Subtract each from the total and check whether the remainder is a positive multiple of $5$.
Common trap
The pond must contain at least one frog and at least one snake.
Show the answer

(B) $23$

Show solution 1 – Check each option

Four-fifths of the frogs is a whole number, so the number of frogs is a multiple of $5$. Six-sevenths of the snakes is a whole number, so the number of snakes is a multiple of $7$. There are frogs and there are snakes, so neither number is $0$.

Try to write each option as (a multiple of $5$) $+$ (a multiple of $7$): \[22=15+7,\qquad 24=10+14,\qquad 26=5+21,\qquad 27=20+7.\] For $23$: subtract $7$, $14$ or $21$ snakes and we get $16$, $9$ or $2$ frogs, none a multiple of $5$. So the total cannot be $23$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 23}$.

Show solution 2 – Use remainders on division by 5

The frogs come in fives, so the total has the same remainder on division by $5$ as the number of snakes. The number of snakes is $7$, $14$, $21$, $28$, …, with remainders $2$, $4$, $1$, $3$, … on division by $5$.

The total $23$ has remainder $3$, so it would need at least $28$ snakes, which is more than $23$ creatures. The other options are fine: $22$ and $27$ (remainder $2$) use $7$ snakes; $24$ (remainder $4$) uses $14$ snakes; $26$ (remainder $1$) uses $21$ snakes, and the rest are frogs in each case. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(B)}\ 23}$.

Problem 15

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

In the addition shown, each letter represents a single digit and different letters represent different digits. What is the value of $L+M+N$? \[\begin{array}{cccc} & L & M & N\\ + & L & N & M\\ \hline & N & 4 & L\end{array}\]

(A) $16$ (B) $17$ (C) $18$ (D) $19$ (E) $20$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Rewrite the addition with the digits lined up by place value. Mark the two places where a carry could enter from the column to the right.
Hint 2: key idea
The units and tens columns both start with the same sum, $M+N$. The carry from the units is the only extra amount the tens column can receive. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Try the case with no carry from the units, and check what the tens and hundreds columns would then require. For the case with a carry, use the tens digit and the possible range of $M+N$ to set up the remaining digit calculations.
Common trap
The result has only three digits, so the hundreds column cannot carry out.
Show the answer

(A) $16$

Show solution 1 – Column by column

Write $s=M+N$. The units column and the tens column both add $M$ and $N$.

Units: $N+M=s$ ends in $L$.

Tens: $s$ plus the carry from the units ends in $4$.

Hundreds: $L+L$ plus the carry from the tens is $N$ (no carry out, since the answer has three digits).

If there were no carry from the units, then $s=L$ and $s$ would end in $4$, so $L=4$ and $s=4$. But then $N\ge 2L=8$, which is more than $s=M+N=4$. Impossible.

So the units carry $1$: $s=L+10$, and $s+1$ ends in $4$, so $s=13$ (since $s$ is between $10$ and $18$). Then $L=3$, there is a carry of $1$ into the hundreds, and $N=2\times3+1=7$, $M=13-7=6$. Check: $367+376=743$. So $L+M+N=3+13=16$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 16}$.

Show solution 2 – Try the few possible values of L

The hundreds column says $N=2L$ or $N=2L+1$ (depending on a carry), and $N$ is a digit, so $L$ is $1$, $2$, $3$ or $4$. Also, the units column says that the units digit of $N+M$ is $L$.

  • $L=1$: $N$ is $2$ or $3$, and $N+M$ ends in $1$, so $N+M=11$ and $M$ is $9$ or $8$. Then the tens column is $N+M+1=12$, which does not end in $4$.

  • $L=2$: $N$ is $4$ or $5$; $N+M=12$ ($N+M=2$ is too small), and the tens give $13$. No.

  • $L=3$: $N$ is $6$ or $7$; $N+M=13$, and the tens give $14$, ending in $4$ with a carry, so $N=2\cdot3+1=7$ and $M=6$. This works: $367+376=743$.

  • $L=4$: $N$ is $8$ or $9$; $N+M=14$, and the tens give $15$. No.

So $L=3$, $M=6$, $N=7$, and $L+M+N=16$. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(A)}\ 16}$.

Problem 16

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Worst-Case Thresholds

A bag contains only red marbles and green marbles. For every five marbles that are chosen, at least one will be red. For every six marbles that are chosen, at least one will be green. What is the largest number of marbles the bag can contain?

(A) $11$ (B) $10$ (C) $9$ (D) $8$ (E) $7$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Picture two piles, one red and one green. For each of the two draw sizes, ask what a handful would look like if it broke the rule.
Hint 2: key idea
Turn each guarantee into a restriction on how many marbles of a single colour the bag can hold. A draw made entirely of the other colour is the case to test. (LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence)
Hint 3: approach
Use the rule for draws of five to bound the number of green marbles, and the rule for draws of six to bound the number of red marbles. Check whether a bag reaching both bounds can obey both rules.
Common trap
Each rule applies to every possible draw of that size.
Show the answer

(C) $9$

Show solution 1 – Bound each colour

If the bag had $5$ or more green marbles, we could choose $5$ green marbles, and there would be no red one among them. So there are at most $4$ green marbles.

If the bag had $6$ or more red marbles, we could choose $6$ red marbles with no green one. So there are at most $5$ red marbles.

Hence the bag has at most $4+5=9$ marbles. A bag with $5$ red and $4$ green marbles works: any $5$ marbles cannot all be green (there are only $4$ green), and any $6$ cannot all be red (there are only $5$ red). The largest number is $\boxed{\text{(C)}\ 9}$.

Show solution 2 – Rule out the larger options

Suppose the bag had $10$ or more marbles. It has at most $4$ green marbles (otherwise $5$ green marbles chosen together would contain no red one). So it has at least $10-4=6$ red marbles, and choosing $6$ of them gives six marbles with no green: a contradiction. So $10$ and $11$ are impossible.

With $9$ marbles, $5$ red and $4$ green, both rules hold: any five marbles include a red one (only four are green), and any six include a green one (only five are red). The answer is $\boxed{\text{(C)}\ 9}$.

Problem 17

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Monovariants

Eight kangaroos stood in a line, as shown. At some point, two kangaroos standing next to each other and facing each other exchanged places. This was repeated until no further exchanges were possible. How many exchanges were made?

(A) $8$ (B) $10$ (C) $12$ (D) $13$ (E) $17$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Copy the line using arrows for the directions the kangaroos face. Try one allowed swap and keep each kangaroo facing the same way as before.
Hint 2: key idea
Look at every pair with a right-facing kangaroo somewhere to the left of a left-facing one. Swapping neighbors changes the order of just that pair, so track how many such pairs remain. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Describe the final order by ruling out adjacent kangaroos that face each other. In the starting line, go through the left-facing kangaroos one at a time and record how many right-facing kangaroos begin to the left of each.
Common trap
Pairs may start far apart and become neighbors only after earlier swaps.
Show the answer

(D) $13$

Show solution 1 – Count the pairs that must pass

Write $R$ for a kangaroo facing right and $L$ for one facing left. From left to right the line is \[R\,R\,R\,L\,R\,R\,L\,L.\] Two neighbours face each other exactly when an $R$ stands just left of an $L$. When they swap, the $R$ moves one place right and the $L$ one place left, and they now face away from each other. An $R$ only ever moves right and an $L$ only ever moves left, so each $R$ and $L$ pass each other at most once.

The exchanges stop only when no $R$ stands just left of an $L$, that is, when all the $L$s are to the left of all the $R$s. So every pair ($R$, $L$) with the $R$ starting on the left must pass, exactly once, and each exchange is one such passing. Count those pairs: the $L$ in place $4$ has $3$ $R$s to its left, and each of the $L$s in places $7$ and $8$ has $5$. In total $3+5+5=13$ exchanges. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 13}$.

Show solution 2 – Follow the left-facing kangaroos

Number the places $1$ to $8$ from the left. Each exchange moves exactly one left-facing kangaroo one place to the left (and one right-facing kangaroo one place to the right).

At the end no right-facing kangaroo stands just left of a left-facing one, so the three left-facing kangaroos stand in places $1$, $2$, $3$. They never pass each other, so the one starting in place $4$ ends in place $1$, and those from places $7$ and $8$ end in places $2$ and $3$. Their moves add up to \[(4-1)+(7-2)+(8-3)=3+5+5=13,\] and each exchange contributes exactly one of these moves. So $13$ exchanges were made, whatever the order. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(D)}\ 13}$.

Problem 18

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting Principles · Ordered Arrangements

Julia has four different coloured pencils and wants to use some or all of them to colour the map of four regions shown in the diagram. She wants to ensure that two regions that share a common border are shaded in different colours. In how many different ways can she colour the map?

(A) $12$ (B) $18$ (C) $24$ (D) $36$ (E) $48$

Give each region a short label and write down the regions it shares a border with. Use the border list to check your reading of the map.
Hint 2: key idea
Start with the two regions that each border the most other regions. Their colour choices set the restrictions for the remaining two. (LPS VI, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting Principles)
Hint 3: approach
Work out how many choices there are for the first of those two regions and then for its neighbor. With those colours set, record the choices for each remaining region and check whether choosing one affects the other.

The instruction allows a colour to go unused, even when there are as many pencils as regions.
Show the answer

(E) $48$

Show solution 1 – Colour the busiest regions first

Name the regions $A$ (large, top left), $B$ (right), $C$ (small, bottom left) and $D$ (bottom middle). From the map: $A$ borders $B$, $C$ and $D$; $D$ borders $A$, $B$ and $C$; but $B$ and $C$ do not touch.

  • $A$: any of the $4$ colours.

  • $D$: it touches $A$, so $3$ choices.

  • $B$: it touches $A$ and $D$, which have different colours, so $2$ choices.

Together $24+24=48$ ways. The answer is $\boxed{\text{(E)}\ 48}$.

Problem 19

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Invariants

A magic coin exchange machine can exchange coins as follows:

  • $3$ silver coins $\to$ $2$ gold $+$ $1$ bronze

  • $3$ gold coins $\to$ $1$ silver $+$ $4$ bronze

Johnny starts with $10$ gold coins. After exchanging coins as many times as possible, how many coins will he have in total?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write down how many silver, gold, and bronze coins Johnny starts with. For each rule, point to the metal that must go into the machine.
Hint 2: key idea
Track the three coin counts, using only the silver and gold counts to decide which exchange is available. Bronze coins can accumulate without enabling a new exchange. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Check the current gold and silver counts against the two rules. Apply an available exchange, update all three counts, then check again until neither rule applies.
Common trap
Keep the bronze coins in your record even though they can never be exchanged.
Show the answer

$18$

Show solution 1 – Follow the machine

Bronze coins are never exchanged, so only silver and gold matter. At every moment at most one exchange is possible (with $10$ gold coins there are never $3$ silver and $3$ gold at the same time, as we will see), so the process is forced. Write (silver, gold, bronze):

  • start: $(0,10,0)$;

  • three times $3$ gold $\to$ $1$ silver $+$ $4$ bronze: $(1,7,4)$, $(2,4,8)$, $(3,1,12)$;

  • $3$ silver $\to$ $2$ gold $+$ $1$ bronze: $(0,3,13)$;

  • $3$ gold $\to$ $1$ silver $+$ $4$ bronze: $(1,0,17)$.

Now there are fewer than $3$ silver and fewer than $3$ gold coins, so no more exchanges are possible. Johnny has $1+0+17=$ $\boxed{18}$ coins.

Show solution 2 – A value that never changes

Give each coin a number of points: a bronze coin $7$ points, a silver coin $11$ points and a gold coin $13$ points. Then \[3\times11=33=2\times13+7\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3\times13=39=11+4\times7,\] so neither exchange changes the total number of points. Johnny starts with $10\times13=130$ points, so he always has $130$ points.

The exchanges must stop at some point: every exchange adds at least one bronze coin, and he can never have more than $130\div7<19$ bronze coins. When they stop he has at most $2$ silver and at most $2$ gold coins. Try each of the nine cases: the points from bronze, $130-11s-13g$, must be a multiple of $7$. Only $s=1$, $g=0$ works ($130-11=119=17\times7$). So he ends with $1$ silver, $0$ gold and $17$ bronze coins: $\boxed{18}$ coins in total. This method also shows that the answer does not depend on the order of the exchanges.

Problem 21

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

Liam is reading two books, one with $100$ pages and one with $200$ pages. He can read up to $20$ pages a night, but refuses to read from the same book two nights in a row. What is the fewest number of days it could take Liam to finish both books?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a row of spaces for nights and mark each space with the book Liam reads that night. What restriction applies to two neighbouring spaces?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the nightly page limit to find how many nights the longer book needs. Any two successive nights devoted to that book must have a gap between them. (LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles)
Hint 3: approach
Sketch the long-book nights with the smallest allowed gaps. Count how many short-book slots that leaves and check their page capacity before assigning any pages.
Common trap
A gap is needed between long-book nights, with no extra gap required after the last one.
Show the answer

$19$

Show solution 1 – The long book sets the pace

The $200$-page book needs at least $200\div20=10$ nights. Liam never reads it two nights in a row, so between any two of these nights there is at least one other night. Ten nights with a gap between each two need at least $10+9=19$ nights.

Nineteen nights are enough: read $20$ pages of the long book on nights $1,3,5,\ldots,19$ (ten nights, $200$ pages) and the short book on nights $2,4,\ldots,18$ (nine nights; for example $12$ pages on each of the first eight of them and $4$ pages on the last). Each night he reads at most $20$ pages and never the same book twice in a row. The fewest number of days is $\boxed{19}$.

Show solution 2 – Look at nights in pairs

Group the nights in pairs: nights $1$ and $2$, nights $3$ and $4$, and so on. In each pair Liam reads the long book on at most one night (never two nights in a row), so at most $20$ pages of it per pair.

In $18$ nights there are $9$ pairs, so at most $9\times20=180$ pages of the $200$-page book: not enough. So he needs at least $19$ nights.

He can do it in $19$: long book on the odd nights $1,3,\ldots,19$ ($10\times20=200$ pages) and the short book on the even nights $2,4,\ldots,18$ (nine nights are plenty for $100$ pages). The answer is $\boxed{19}$ days.
