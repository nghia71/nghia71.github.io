Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

Barron Nürremberg claims he chooses two distinct non-opposite half-lines $(OA$ and $(OB$ sharing the same origin. Then, he claims he can choose $20$ pairwise distinct points, $10$ on each half-line, labeled $K_1,\dots,K_5,L_1,\dots,L_5\in(OA$ and $M_1,\dots,M_5,N_1,\dots,N_5\in(OB$ such that, for each pair of distinct indices $(i,j)$, with $1\le i,j\le 5$, the distances $K_iM_j$ and $L_iN_j$ are equal. Can all of Barron's claims be true?

Show the answer

Yes (and only with perpendicular half-lines)

Show the solution – Perpendicular half-lines

Construction. Let the half-lines be perpendicular: put $O$ at the origin, $(OA$ along the positive $x$-axis and $(OB$ along the positive $y$-axis. For $i,j=1,\dots,5$ let \[K_i=(i+1,\,0),\quad L_i=\bigl(\sqrt{(i+1)^2-1},\,0\bigr),\quad M_j=(0,\,j),\quad N_j=\bigl(0,\,\sqrt{j^2+1}\bigr).\] The numbers $(i+1)^2-1\in\{3,8,15,24,35\}$ and $j^2+1\in\{2,5,10,17,26\}$ are not perfect squares, so the $L$'s and $N$'s have irrational coordinates, different from the integer coordinates of the $K$'s and $M$'s; hence the ten points on each half-line are pairwise distinct. By Pythagoras, \[L_iN_j^2=\bigl((i+1)^2-1\bigr)+\bigl(j^2+1\bigr)=(i+1)^2+j^2=K_iM_j^2\] for all $i,j$. So all of Barron's claims can be true: the answer is Yes.

Remark: the angle must be right. Let $\theta\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ be the angle between the half-lines, $c=\cos\theta$, and let $k_i,l_i,m_j,n_j>0$ be the distances of $K_i,L_i,M_j,N_j$ from $O$. By the law of cosines the conditions read \[k_i^2-l_i^2+m_j^2-n_j^2=2c\,(k_im_j-l_in_j)\qquad(1\le i,j\le5).\] Suppose $c\ne0$. Subtracting the equations for $(i,j)$ and $(i',j)$ with $i\ne i'$ shows that all points $(m_j,n_j)$ satisfy one linear equation $2c(k_i-k_{i'})m_j-2c(l_i-l_{i'})n_j=\text{const}$; since $k_i\ne k_{i'}$ and the $m_j$ are distinct, this gives $n_j=pm_j+q$ for constants $p,q$. In the same way $l_i=p'k_i+q'$. Substituting, the equation becomes a polynomial identity in $k=k_i$ and $m=m_j$ of degree at most $2$ in each variable, valid on a $5\times5$ grid of distinct values, hence identically. Comparing coefficients of $k^2$, $m^2$, $km$, $k$, $m$: $p'^2=1$, $p^2=1$, $pp'=1$, $q'=cq$ and $q=cq'$. Since $|c|<1$, $q=q'=0$, so $l_i=\pm k_i$, which is impossible ($l_i=k_i$ contradicts distinctness and $l_i>0$). So the half-lines must be perpendicular.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation · Diophantine Approximation

We consider a positive integer $n$ for which there exist positive integers $a$ and $b$ for which $\lfloor a\sqrt{10}\rfloor=n=\lfloor b\sqrt{11}\rfloor$. Prove there exists a positive integer $c$ for which $n=\lfloor c(11\sqrt{10}-10\sqrt{11})\rfloor$.

Show the solution – Adding two intervals

Put $x=11\sqrt{10}-10\sqrt{11}>0$. Since $\sqrt{110}\cdot\sqrt{11}=11\sqrt{10}$ and $\sqrt{110}\cdot\sqrt{10}=10\sqrt{11}$, we have $x=\sqrt{110}\,(\sqrt{11}-\sqrt{10})$, and as $(\sqrt{11}-\sqrt{10})(\sqrt{11}+\sqrt{10})=1$, \[\frac1x=\frac{\sqrt{11}+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{110}}=\frac1{\sqrt{10}}+\frac1{\sqrt{11}} .\]

Now $n=\lfloor a\sqrt{10}\rfloor$ means $n\le a\sqrt{10}<n+1$; equality on the left is impossible because $a\sqrt{10}$ is irrational. Hence \[\frac{n}{\sqrt{10}}<a<\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{10}},\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad \frac{n}{\sqrt{11}}<b<\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{11}} .\] Adding, with $c=a+b$ (a positive integer), \[\frac nx=n\Bigl(\frac1{\sqrt{10}}+\frac1{\sqrt{11}}\Bigr)<c<(n+1)\Bigl(\frac1{\sqrt{10}}+\frac1{\sqrt{11}}\Bigr)=\frac{n+1}x,\] that is, $n<cx<n+1$. Therefore $\lfloor c(11\sqrt{10}-10\sqrt{11})\rfloor=n$.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $m>k>1$ be fixed positive integers. Find the greatest value of the positive integer $N$ for which the following statement is true:

Consider an $N\times N$ grid of unit squares. We can place $N$ tokens in the unit squares of the grid, such that each row and each column has exactly one token, and there do not exist a pair of lines among the gridlines of the square, one horizontal and one vertical, such that among the four rectangles the square is split into, we can find two of them that do not share a side, such that one has at least $m$ tokens, and the other at least $k$ tokens.

Show the answer

$N=2m+2k-4$

Show the solution – Two neighbouring rows, and a block construction

Number the rows $1,\dots,N$ from top to bottom and the columns from left to right. A horizontal and a vertical gridline split the board into four rectangles $NW$, $NE$, $SW$, $SE$; the pairs that do not share a side are $(NW,SE)$ and $(NE,SW)$. Call a pair of token counts bad if one is at least $m$ and the other at least $k$, and call a placement good if no choice of lines gives a bad pair. (Whenever we exhibit a bad pair below, both rectangles contain tokens, so the lines are genuine interior gridlines.) Put $a=k-1$, $b=m-1$, so $1\le a<b$.

Step 1: if an $N\times N$ good placement exists, so does an $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ one. Delete the last row and the column of its token. Any two lines of the small board correspond to two lines of the big board whose four rectangles contain the same tokens, except that one rectangle may also contain the deleted token. Counts only grow, so a bad pair in the small board would give a bad pair in the big one.

Step 2: there is no good placement for $N=2L+1$, where $L=m+k-2$. Suppose there is one. Let $\tau$ be the token in row $L+1$.

The line below row $L$. Above it there are $L$ tokens, below it $L+1$. List the upper tokens from left to right as $u_1,\dots,u_L$ and let $\alpha_j$ be the number of lower tokens to the left of $u_j$. Using the vertical line just left or just right of $u_j$:

  • just left of $u_{k-1}$: $NE=L-k+2=m$ and $SW=\alpha_{k-1}$, so $\alpha_{k-1}\le k-1$;

  • just right of $u_k$: $NW=k$ and $SE=L+1-\alpha_k$, so $L+1-\alpha_k\le m-1$, i.e. $\alpha_k\ge k$;

  • just left of $u_{m-1}$: $NE=L-m+2=k$ and $SW=\alpha_{m-1}$, so $\alpha_{m-1}\le m-1$.

The line below row $L+1$. Now $L+1$ tokens are above, $L$ below. List the upper tokens as $v_1,\dots,v_{L+1}$ and let $\beta_j$ be the number of lower tokens to the left of $v_j$:

  • just left of $v_k$: $NE=L+2-k=m$, $SW=\beta_k$, so $\beta_k\le k-1$; just right of $v_k$: $NW=k$, $SE=L-\beta_k$, so $L-\beta_k\le m-1$, i.e. $\beta_k\ge k-1$. Hence $\beta_k=k-1$.

  • just left of $v_m$: $NE=L+2-m=k$, $SW=\beta_m$, so $\beta_m\le m-1$; just right of $v_m$: $NW=m$, $SE=L-\beta_m$, so $L-\beta_m\le k-1$, i.e. $\beta_m\ge m-1$. Hence $\beta_m=m-1$.

Comparing. Passing from the first line to the second, only $\tau$ changes sides. Let $j_0$ be the number of $u$'s to the left of $\tau$. Then $v_j=u_j$ and $\beta_j=\alpha_j$ for $j\le j_0$; $v_{j_0+1}=\tau$; and $v_j=u_{j-1}$, $\beta_j=\alpha_{j-1}-1$ for $j\ge j_0+2$ (the token $\tau$, which was lower and to the left of $u_{j-1}$, is no longer lower).

  • If $j_0\ge k$: $\beta_k=\alpha_k\ge k$, contradicting $\beta_k=k-1$.

  • If $j_0\le k-2$: $\beta_k=\alpha_{k-1}-1\le k-2$, contradicting $\beta_k=k-1$.

  • If $j_0=k-1$: since $m\ge k+1=j_0+2$, $\beta_m=\alpha_{m-1}-1\le m-2$, contradicting $\beta_m=m-1$.

So no good placement exists for $N=2m+2k-3$, and by Step 1 none for any larger $N$.

Step 3: a good placement for $N=2a+2b=2m+2k-4$. Let $c=b-a\ge1$ and place the tokens (row, column) as follows:

  • $X$: $(i,i)$ for $1\le i\le a$;  $Z$: $(i,i)$ for $a+2b<i\le N$;

  • $U_1$: $(a+j,\ a+2b+1-j)$ and $U_2$: $(2a+t,\ a+b+t)$, for $1\le j\le a$, $1\le t\le c$;

  • $V_1$: $(a+b+t,\ 2a+t)$ and $V_2$: $(a+b+c+j,\ 2a+1-j)$, for $1\le t\le c$, $1\le j\le a$.

Let $U=U_1\cup U_2$ (rows $a+1,\dots,a+b$, columns $a+b+1,\dots,a+2b$) and $V=V_1\cup V_2$ (rows $a+b+1,\dots,a+2b$, columns $a+1,\dots,a+b$); each row and each column gets one token. Say that $P$ is NW of $Q$ if $P$ is in a higher row and a column further left, and similarly for NE, SW, SE. We use:

  • (i) no token is SW or NE of a token of $X$, and none is SW or NE of a token of $Z$; every token of $X$ is NW of every token outside $X$, and every token of $Z$ is SE of every token outside $Z$;

  • (ii) every token of $U$ is NE of every token of $V$;

  • (iii) inside $U$, a token NW of another occurs only within $U_2$ (because $U_1$ goes down-left and lies NE of $U_2$); inside $V$, only within $V_1$;

  • (iv) each token of $U$ has at most $a$ tokens of $U$ to its NE, and each token of $V$ has at most $a$ tokens of $V$ to its SW.

The pair $(NW,SE)$. Suppose $NW\ge a+1$ and $SE\ge a+1$. Every token of $NW$ is NW of every token of $SE$. As $|X|=|Z|=a$, $NW$ contains a token $u\notin X$ and $SE$ a token $v\notin Z$; by (i) $u\notin Z$ and $v\notin X$, and by (ii), (iii) $u$ and $v$ both lie in $U_2$ or both in $V_1$. Applying this to every such pair, all tokens of $NW$ outside $X$ and all tokens of $SE$ outside $Z$ lie in one group $G\in\{U_2,V_1\}$ with $|G|=c$. So $NW\le a+t$ and $SE\le a+t'$ with $t,t'\ge1$, $t+t'\le c$, giving $NW,SE\le a+c-1=b-1<m$: the pair is not bad.

The pair $(NE,SW)$. Suppose $NE\ge a+1$ and $SW\ge a+1$; we show that both are at most $b$, so the pair is not bad. By (i), $NE$ contains no token of $X$ and $SW$ none of $Z$. If the horizontal line were above row $a+1$, $NE$ would contain only tokens of $X$; if the vertical line were left of column $a+1$, $SW$ would contain only tokens of $X$ lying below the line, i.e. none. Symmetrically for $Z$. Hence $NE,SW\subseteq U\cup V$.

  • The horizontal line is above row $a+b+1$ (so all of $V$ is below it). If the vertical line is left of column $a+b+1$, all of $U$ is to its right, so $NE\subseteq U$ and $SW\subseteq V$, both of size at most $b$. Otherwise all of $V$ is in $SW$ and $NE\subseteq U$; if $SW$ also contains a token $w\in U$, then all of $NE$ is NE of $w$ inside $U$, so $NE\le a$ by (iv); if not, $SW=V$ has $b$ tokens and $NE\le|U|=b$.

  • The horizontal line is below row $a+b$ (so all of $U$ is above it). If the vertical line is right of column $a+b$, then $NE\subseteq U$ and $SW\subseteq V$. Otherwise all of $U$ is in $NE$, $SW\subseteq V$, and either $NE$ contains a token $w\in V$, so that $SW\le a$ by (iv), or $NE=U$ has $b$ tokens and $SW\le b$.

So the placement is good, and the greatest $N$ is $\boxed{2m+2k-4}$.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

Let $v\ge 4$ be a fixed positive integer. Find the smallest positive integer $e$ such that for any connected graph $G$ on $v$ vertices and $e$ edges, there exists a cycle whose removal doesn't disconnect $G$. (We remove only the edges of the cycle, not the vertices.)

Show the answer

$e=2v-2$

Show solution 1 – Spanning trees and a vertex of small degree

Graphs are simple. Removing the edges of a cycle $C$ leaves $G$ connected exactly when $G-E(C)$ contains a spanning tree; call such a cycle good. For $e<v-1$ there is no connected graph at all, so the condition holds vacuously; the question is about the least $e\ge v-1$, equivalently the least $e$ such that every connected graph on $v$ vertices with at least $e$ edges has a good cycle.

Fewer edges are not enough. For $v-1\le e\le 2v-3$ put $t=e-v+1\in\{0,\dots,v-2\}$. Take adjacent vertices $x,y$, join both of them to $t$ further vertices $z_1,\dots,z_t$, and attach the remaining $v-2-t$ vertices as a path hanging from $x$. This connected graph has $1+2t+(v-2-t)=e$ edges. The path edges are bridges, so every cycle lies among $x,y,z_1,\dots,z_t$; a cycle has at least $3$ vertices, so it passes through some $z_i$, and since $z_i$ has degree $2$ the cycle uses both of its edges. Removing the cycle isolates $z_i$. So no cycle is good.

$2v-2$ edges are enough. If $e\ge 2v-1$, take any spanning tree $T$: the graph $G-E(T)$ has at least $v$ edges on $v$ vertices, so it contains a cycle, which is good because $T$ survives.

Now let $e=2v-2$ and suppose no cycle is good. Then for every spanning tree $T$, the graph $G-E(T)$ has $v-1$ edges and no cycle, so it is itself a spanning tree. In particular every vertex meets an edge of $T$ and an edge of $G-E(T)$, so every degree is at least $2$. The degree sum is $4v-4<4v$, so some vertex $w$ has degree $2$ or $3$.

Degree $2$, edges $wx,wy$: these two edges form no cycle, so they extend to a spanning tree $T$. Then $w$ is isolated in $G-E(T)$, a contradiction.

Degree $3$, edges $wx,wy,wz$: extend $\{wx,wy\}$ to a spanning tree $T$. As $G-E(T)$ is a spanning tree, it contains $wz$, and $w$ is a leaf of it. In $T$ the vertex $w$ has exactly the edges $wx,wy$; let $T_x$ and $T_y$ be the (disjoint) branches of $T$ at $w$ containing $x$ and $y$.

Consider $S=(G-E(T))-wz+wx$; it is a spanning tree ($w$ is re-attached through $x$). Its complement is $T-wx+wz$. The forest $T-wx$ has two components, $T_x$ and the rest; if $z\notin T_x$, adding $wz$ creates a cycle in $G-E(S)$, which is good because $S$ survives. So $z\in T_x$. In the same way, using $(G-E(T))-wz+wy$, we get $z\in T_y$. But $T_x\cap T_y=\varnothing$, a contradiction.

Hence a good cycle always exists when $e\ge 2v-2$, and the smallest $e$ is $\boxed{2v-2}$.

Show solution 2 – A spanning tree containing a whole star

Graphs are simple. Removing the edges of a cycle $C$ leaves $G$ connected exactly when $G-E(C)$ still contains a spanning tree; call such a cycle good. For $e<v-1$ there is no connected graph at all, so the condition holds vacuously; the question is about the least $e\ge v-1$, equivalently the least $e$ such that every connected graph on $v$ vertices with at least $e$ edges has a good cycle.

$2v-2$ edges are enough. Let $G$ be connected with $v$ vertices and $e\ge2v-2$ edges, and fix a vertex $w$. The edges at $w$ form a star, which contains no cycle; since $G$ is connected, this star can be extended to a spanning tree $T$ of $G$ (keep adding an edge of $G$ that joins two different components of the current forest, until it is connected). Then $T$ contains every edge at $w$, so the $e-(v-1)\ge v-1$ edges of $G-E(T)$ all join vertices of the set $V\setminus\{w\}$, which has only $v-1$ vertices. A graph on $v-1$ vertices with at least $v-1$ edges is not a forest (a forest on $v-1$ vertices has at most $v-2$ edges), so $G-E(T)$ contains a cycle $C$. Removing the edges of $C$ leaves all of $T$, so $G$ stays connected: $C$ is good.

Fewer edges are not enough. Let $v-1\le e\le2v-3$. Take vertices $x,y,z_1,\dots,z_{v-2}$ and let $H$ consist of the edge $xy$ and the edges $xz_i$, $yz_i$ for all $i$; it has $2v-3$ edges and is connected. The star of $x$ (edges $xy$, $xz_1,\dots,xz_{v-2}$) is a spanning tree of $H$; add to it any $e-(v-1)$ further edges of $H$ (there are $v-2\ge e-v+1$ of them) to get a connected graph $G$ with $v$ vertices and $e$ edges. Every vertex $z_i$ has degree at most $2$ in $G$. A cycle of $G$ has at least $3$ vertices, so it passes through some $z_i$; then $z_i$ has degree exactly $2$ and the cycle uses both edges at $z_i$, so removing the cycle isolates $z_i$. Hence $G$ has no good cycle.

The smallest $e$ is $\boxed{2v-2}$.

Problem 5

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry · Complex Reformulation of Olympiad Geometry

Let $O$ and $H$ be the circumcenter and orthocenter of an acute-angled triangle $ABC$ respectively. Let line $OH$ intersect $AB$ and $AC$ at points $B'$ and $C'$. The circles $(AB'C')$ and $(ABC)$ intersect for the second time at point $S$. Point $K$ lies on $(ABC)$ such that $AK$ is tangent to $(AB'C')$. Finally, let $AH$ cross $(ABC)$ for the second time at point $M$.

Prove that the lines $BC$, $SM$ and $KH$ are concurrent.

Show the solution – Reduce to one angle, then complex numbers

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$. The triangle is not equilateral (otherwise $O=H$), and we assume the generic situation in which all points named are well defined.

Reduction. $M$ is the reflection of $H$ in $BC$ (well known; it also follows from the formula for $m$ below). Let $\rho$ be the reflection in $BC$. It fixes the line $AH$ (which is perpendicular to $BC$), swaps $H$ and $M$, and reverses directed angles. Suppose we prove \[\angle(HA,HK)=\angle(KS,KA).\tag{$*$}\] Since $A,K,M,S$ lie on one circle, $\angle(KS,KA)=\angle(MS,MA)$. Then $\rho(MS)$ is a line through $H$ with \[\angle(HA,\rho(MS))=-\angle(MA,MS)=\angle(MS,MA)=\angle(HA,HK),\] so $\rho(MS)=HK$. Two lines that are mirror images in $BC$ meet on $BC$ (or are both parallel to it), so $BC$, $SM$, $KH$ are concurrent (at the point $X$ of the figure). It remains to prove $(*)$.

Lemma: $AK$ is also tangent to the circle $(AOH)$. The lines $AO$ and $AH$ are isogonal in angle $A$: $\angle(AO,AB)=\angle(AC,AH)$. Let $t$ be the tangent at $A$ to $(AOH)$. By the tangent-chord angle, $\angle(t,AO)=\angle(HA,HO)$. Hence \[\angle(t,AB)=\angle(t,AO)+\angle(AO,AB)=\angle(HA,HO)+\angle(AC,AH)=\angle(AC,OH)=\angle(C'A,C'B'),\] which says that $t$ is tangent to $(AB'C')$ at $A$. So $t=AK$.

Coordinates. Take the circumcircle as the unit circle, $o=0$, and write $\sigma_1=a+b+c$, $\sigma_2=ab+bc+ca$, $\sigma_3=abc$. Then $h=\sigma_1\ne0$, $\bar a=1/a$ (and so on), $\bar\sigma_1=\sigma_2/\sigma_3$, $\bar\sigma_2=\sigma_1/\sigma_3$. Put \[P_a=a\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\quad P_b=b\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\quad P_c=c\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\quad Q=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2 .\] Note $\overline{P_a}=P_a/(a\sigma_3)$, and similarly for $P_b,P_c$.

$M$. Reflection in the chord $BC$ is $z\mapsto b+c-bc\bar z$, so the reflection of $H$ is $b+c-bc\left(\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c\right)=-\frac{bc}{a}$, a point of the unit circle on line $AH$; so $m=-bc/a$.

$K$. By the lemma, $\angle(AK,AH)=\angle(OA,OH)$, i.e. $\dfrac{(k-a)\,h}{(h-a)\,a}=\dfrac{(k-a)\sigma_1}{a(b+c)}$ is real. The point $k=-\sigma_2/\sigma_1$ lies on the unit circle ($|\sigma_2|=|\sigma_3\bar\sigma_1|=|\sigma_1|$) and gives $k-a=-P_a/\sigma_1$, so the quotient is $-P_a/(a(b+c))$, whose conjugate is $-\dfrac{P_a/(a\sigma_3)}{(b+c)/\sigma_3}$, the same number. As the line through $A$ meets the circle only once more, $k=-\sigma_2/\sigma_1$.

$B'$ and $C'$. A point $z=t\sigma_1$ ($t$ real) of line $OH$ lies on line $AB$ iff $z+ab\bar z=a+b$, i.e. $t(\sigma_1+\sigma_2/c)=a+b$. So \[b'=\frac{c(a+b)\sigma_1}{P_c},\qquad c'=\frac{b(a+c)\sigma_1}{P_b}.\]

$S$. As $B'\in AB$ and $C'\in AC$, the second intersection $S$ of $(AB'C')$ and $(ABC)$ is the center of the spiral similarity taking $B'$ to $B$ and $C'$ to $C$; so $\frac{b-s}{b'-s}=\frac{c-s}{c'-s}$, which gives $s=\dfrac{b'c-bc'}{b'+c-b-c'}$. Substituting, \[b'c-bc'=-\frac{(b-c)\,\sigma_1(\sigma_2^2+\sigma_1\sigma_3)}{P_bP_c},\qquad b'+c-b-c'=-\frac{(b-c)\,\sigma_2\,Q}{P_bP_c},\qquad\text{so}\qquad s=\frac{\sigma_1(\sigma_2^2+\sigma_1\sigma_3)}{\sigma_2\,Q}.\]

Proof of $(*)$. The relation $(*)$ says that $q=\dfrac{k-h}{a-h}\cdot\dfrac{s-k}{a-k}$ is real. We have $k-h=-Q/\sigma_1$, $a-h=-(b+c)$, $a-k=P_a/\sigma_1$, and \[s-k=\frac{\sigma_1^2(\sigma_2^2+\sigma_1\sigma_3)+\sigma_2^2Q}{\sigma_1\sigma_2Q}=\frac{2\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2+\sigma_1^3\sigma_3+\sigma_2^3}{\sigma_1\sigma_2Q}=\frac{P_aP_bP_c}{\sigma_1\sigma_2Q},\] because expanding $P_aP_bP_c=\prod(a\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$ gives $\sigma_1^3\sigma_3+\sigma_1^2\sigma_2\cdot\sigma_2+\sigma_1\sigma_2^2\cdot\sigma_1+\sigma_2^3$. Therefore \[q=\frac{Q}{\sigma_1(b+c)}\cdot\frac{P_aP_bP_c}{\sigma_1\sigma_2Q}\cdot\frac{\sigma_1}{P_a}=\frac{P_bP_c}{(b+c)\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}.\] Its conjugate is \[\bar q=\frac{\dfrac{P_b}{b\sigma_3}\cdot\dfrac{P_c}{c\sigma_3}}{\dfrac{b+c}{bc}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_3}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_3}}=\frac{P_bP_c}{(b+c)\sigma_1\sigma_2}=q,\] so $q$ is real, $(*)$ holds, and the three lines are concurrent.

Problem 6

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities · Factorization by Structure

Find all real polynomials $P$ for which there exist pairwise distinct, non-constant polynomials with real coefficients $f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4$, whose leading coefficients are all positive, for which: \[f_1(x)\cdot f_2(x)=f_3(x)\cdot f_4(x)\quad\text{and}\quad P(f_1(x))\cdot P(f_2(x))=P(f_3(x))\cdot P(f_4(x)).\]

Show the answer

$P(x)=cx^n$ with $c\in\mathbb R$, $n\ge0$ (this includes $P\equiv0$ and the constants)

Show solution 1 – Leading terms after inverting

These work. For $P=cx^n$, $P(f_1)P(f_2)=c^2(f_1f_2)^n=c^2(f_3f_4)^n=P(f_3)P(f_4)$ for any $f$'s with $f_1f_2=f_3f_4$, for example $f_1=x(x+1)$, $f_2=(x+2)(x+3)$, $f_3=x(x+2)$, $f_4=(x+1)(x+3)$.

Nothing else works. Let $P\ne0$ work with $f_1,\dots,f_4$, and write $P=x^nR$ with $R(0)\ne0$. Since $P(f_1)P(f_2)=(f_1f_2)^nR(f_1)R(f_2)$ and similarly for $f_3,f_4$, we get $R(f_1)R(f_2)=R(f_3)R(f_4)$. We show that $R$ is constant.

Step 1 (factorization). Let $A=\gcd(f_1,f_3)$, normalised to be monic, and $B=f_1/A$, $C=f_3/A$; these have positive leading coefficients and $\gcd(B,C)=1$. From $Bf_2=f_1f_2/A=f_3f_4/A=Cf_4$ we get $C\mid f_2$; write $f_2=CD$, so $f_4=BD$. Thus \[f_1=AB,\quad f_2=CD,\quad f_3=AC,\quad f_4=BD,\] with all leading coefficients positive, $B\ne C$ (as $f_1\ne f_3$) and $A\ne D$ (as $f_1\ne f_4$). Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ be the degrees of $A,B,C,D$, $\sigma=\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=\deg(f_1f_2)$ and $\mu=\max(\alpha,\delta)+\max(\beta,\gamma)$. Then $\sigma-\mu=\min(\alpha,\delta)+\min(\beta,\gamma)>0$: otherwise one of $A,D$ and one of $B,C$ would be constant, and one of $f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4$ would be constant.

Step 2 (inverting). Suppose $r=\deg R\ge1$, $R(t)=\sum_{i=0}^r c_it^i$. Put $a_i=c_{r-i}/c_r$, so $a_0=1$, $a_r=c_0/c_r\ne0$, and $R(t)=c_rt^rS(1/t)$ with $S(u)=\sum_{i=0}^r a_iu^i$. With $g_i=1/f_i$ and $\Pi=f_1f_2=f_3f_4$ the relation becomes $S(g_1)S(g_2)=S(g_3)S(g_4)$. For any $p,q$, \[S(p)S(q)=\sum_i a_i^2(pq)^i+\sum_{k\ge1}(p^k+q^k)\,W_k(pq),\qquad W_k(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-k}a_ia_{i+k}z^i .\] Since $g_1g_2=g_3g_4=1/\Pi$, subtracting gives $\sum_{k=1}^r(g_1^k+g_2^k-g_3^k-g_4^k)\,W_k(1/\Pi)=0$. Here \[g_1^k+g_2^k-g_3^k-g_4^k=\frac{(AB)^k+(CD)^k-(AC)^k-(BD)^k}{\Pi^k}=\frac{(A^k-D^k)(B^k-C^k)}{\Pi^k}.\] Write $A^k-D^k=(A-D)h_k(A,D)$ with $h_k(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}X^iY^{k-1-i}$, divide by $(A-D)(B-C)\ne0$ and multiply by $\Pi^r$: \[\sum_{k=1}^r h_k(A,D)\,h_k(B,C)\,V_k=0,\qquad V_k=\sum_{i=0}^{r-k}a_ia_{i+k}\,\Pi^{\,r-k-i}.\]

Step 3 (degrees). All terms of $h_k(A,D)$ have positive leading coefficients, so the top ones add up: $\deg h_k(A,D)=(k-1)\max(\alpha,\delta)$, with positive leading coefficient; similarly for $h_k(B,C)$. If $V_k\ne0$, let $j_k$ be the least $i$ with $a_ia_{i+k}\ne0$; then $\deg V_k=(r-k-j_k)\sigma$. So the $k$-th term has degree $(k-1)\mu+(r-k-j_k)\sigma$.

Let $k_0$ be the least $k\ge1$ with $a_k\ne0$ (it exists, as $a_r\ne0$). Then $j_{k_0}=0$ and the $k_0$-th term has degree $D_0=(k_0-1)\mu+(r-k_0)\sigma$ and a nonzero leading coefficient. Every other term has smaller degree:

  • for $k>k_0$: degree at most $(k-1)\mu+(r-k)\sigma=D_0-(k-k_0)(\sigma-\mu)<D_0$;

  • for $k<k_0$: $a_k=0$, so $j_k\ge1$, and $a_{j_k}\ne0$ forces $j_k\ge k_0$; the degree is at most $(k-1)\mu+(r-k-k_0)\sigma=D_0-(k_0-k)\mu-k\sigma<D_0$.

So the sum has degree $D_0$ and cannot vanish, a contradiction. Hence $R$ is a constant $c\ne0$ and $P=cx^n$.

Answer: exactly the polynomials $\boxed{P(x)=cx^n}$ with $c\in\mathbb R$ and $n\ge0$ an integer.

Show solution 2 – Strict convexity of $\log|P(e^t)|$

These work. For $P=cx^n$ (including $c=0$), $P(f_1)P(f_2)=c^2(f_1f_2)^n=c^2(f_3f_4)^n=P(f_3)P(f_4)$ whenever $f_1f_2=f_3f_4$, for example with $f_1=x$, $f_2=6x$, $f_3=2x$, $f_4=3x$.

Nothing else works. Suppose $P$ has at least two nonzero terms and works with $f_1,\dots,f_4$. Write \[P(y)=cy^d\bigl(1+b_1y^{-k_1}+b_2y^{-k_2}+\dots+b_sy^{-k_s}\bigr),\qquad 1\le k_1<k_2<\dots<k_s,\] with $c\ne0$, $s\ge1$ and all $b_j\ne0$.

Step 1: a strictly monotone derivative. For real $t$ put $E(t)=\sum_jb_je^{-k_jt}$, so that $P(e^t)=ce^{dt}(1+E(t))$. For $t$ large, $|E(t)|<\frac12$, and \[\Lambda(t)=\log\bigl|P(e^t)\bigr|=\log|c|+dt+\log\bigl(1+E(t)\bigr)\] is defined and smooth. Its second derivative is \[\Lambda''(t)=\frac{E''(t)\bigl(1+E(t)\bigr)-E'(t)^2}{\bigl(1+E(t)\bigr)^2}.\] Here $E''(t)=k_1^2b_1e^{-k_1t}+O(e^{-(k_1+1)t})$, while $E''(t)E(t)$ and $E'(t)^2$ are $O(e^{-2k_1t})$. Hence $\Lambda''(t)=k_1^2b_1e^{-k_1t}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)$, which has the sign of $b_1\ne0$ for all $t\ge t_0$, for a suitable $t_0$. So $\Lambda'$ is strictly monotone on $[t_0,\infty)$.

Step 2: the two pairs coincide. The $f_i$ are non-constant with positive leading coefficients, so $f_i(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$; take $x_0$ such that $f_i(x)>e^{t_0}$ for all $i$ and all $x>x_0$. Fix $x>x_0$ and put $t_i=\log f_i(x)\ge t_0$. From $f_1f_2=f_3f_4$, \[t_1+t_2=t_3+t_4=:\tau,\] and taking $\log|\cdot|$ of $P(f_1)P(f_2)=P(f_3)P(f_4)$ (both sides are nonzero, as $P(e^t)\ne0$ for $t\ge t_0$) gives \[\Lambda(t_1)+\Lambda(t_2)=\Lambda(t_3)+\Lambda(t_4).\] Let $\varphi(u)=\Lambda(u)+\Lambda(\tau-u)$ for $t_0\le u\le\frac\tau2$. Then $\varphi'(u)=\Lambda'(u)-\Lambda'(\tau-u)$, and for $u<\frac\tau2$ we have $u<\tau-u$, both in $[t_0,\infty)$, so $\varphi'(u)\ne0$ with one and the same sign throughout. Hence $\varphi$ is strictly monotone on $[t_0,\frac\tau2]$. The numbers $\min(t_1,t_2)$ and $\min(t_3,t_4)$ lie in this interval and $\varphi$ takes the same value at them, so they are equal; then $\{t_1,t_2\}=\{t_3,t_4\}$, and in particular $f_1(x)\in\{f_3(x),f_4(x)\}$.

Step 3: contradiction. So the polynomial $(f_1-f_3)(f_1-f_4)$ vanishes at every $x>x_0$, hence is the zero polynomial, and $f_1=f_3$ or $f_1=f_4$. This contradicts the assumption that the $f_i$ are pairwise distinct.

Therefore $P$ has at most one nonzero term: $\boxed{P(x)=cx^n}$ with $c\in\mathbb R$ and $n\ge0$ an integer.
