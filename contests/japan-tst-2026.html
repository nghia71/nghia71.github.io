Problem 1

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Monovariants and Directional Change

Let $n\ge 2$ be an integer. There are two blackboards, A and B, each with $n$ distinct integers. The integers on one blackboard need not be different from those on the other blackboard. Now, consider repeating the following operation.

Let $X=\{a+b : a \text{ is on blackboard A, and } b \text{ is on blackboard B}\}$. Replace the $n$ numbers on blackboard A with the $n$ smallest elements of $X$, and the $n$ numbers on blackboard B with the $n$ largest elements of $X$.

Prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that for every integer $k\ge N$, after the $k$-th operation, the $n$ numbers on A form an arithmetic progression when they are arranged in increasing order, and the $n$ numbers on B also form an arithmetic progression when they are arranged in decreasing order.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Profiles from the bottom and from the top)

For a set $T=\{t_1<t_2<\dots<t_n\}$ of integers let its span be $t_n-t_1$, its lower profile $L(T)=(t_1-t_1,t_2-t_1,\dots,t_n-t_1)$ and its upper profile $U(T)=(t_n-t_n,\,t_n-t_{n-1},\dots,t_n-t_1)$. Note that \[L(T)_i+U(T)_{n+1-i}=\operatorname{span}(T).\tag{1}\] Write $A_k,B_k$ for the boards after $k$ operations ($A_0,B_0$ the initial boards); $A_{k+1}$ consists of the $n$ smallest and $B_{k+1}$ of the $n$ largest elements of $X_k=A_k+B_k$.

Step 1: two inequalities. Let $\alpha=L(A_k)$ and $\beta=L(B_k)$, viewed as sets containing $0$. The least element of $X_k$ is $\min A_k+\min B_k$, and $X_k-(\min A_k+\min B_k)=\alpha+\beta$. Since $0\in\beta$, the set $\alpha+\beta$ contains $\alpha$, so its $i$-th smallest element is at most the $i$-th smallest element of $\alpha$; likewise it contains $\beta$. Hence, coordinatewise, \[L(A_{k+1})\le\min\big(L(A_k),L(B_k)\big),\qquad\text{and symmetrically}\qquad U(B_{k+1})\le\min\big(U(A_k),U(B_k)\big).\tag{2}\]

Step 2: the spans stabilize. Taking the last coordinates in (2), both $A_{k+1}$ and $B_{k+1}$ have span at most $m_k=\min(\operatorname{span}A_k,\operatorname{span}B_k)$. So $m_{k+1}\le m_k$. These are positive integers, so from some $K$ on $m_k=s$ is constant; then for $k\ge K+1$ both spans are at most $m_{k-1}=s$ and at least $m_k=s$, i.e. $\operatorname{span}A_k=\operatorname{span}B_k=s$.

Step 3: the profiles stabilize. For $k\ge K+1$, (1) and (2) give \[L(B_{k+1})_i=s-U(B_{k+1})_{n+1-i}\ge s-\min\big(U(A_k),U(B_k)\big)_{n+1-i}=\max\big(L(A_k)_i,L(B_k)_i\big).\] So for $k\ge K+1$ the vectors $L(A_k)$ decrease and the vectors $L(B_k)$ increase coordinatewise. They are integer vectors with entries in $[0,s]$, so from some point on $L(A_k)=\alpha$ and $L(B_k)=\beta$ are constant. Then each $A_k$ is a translate of $\alpha$ and each $B_k$ a translate of $\beta$, and the operation says: \[\text{the $n$ smallest elements of }\alpha+\beta\text{ form }\alpha,\qquad\text{the $n$ largest form }s+\beta .\tag{3}\] (The largest element of $\alpha+\beta$ is $2s$, and $B_{k+1}$, a translate of $\beta$ with span $s$, must be $s+\beta$ after the shift.)

Step 4: $\alpha=\beta$ is an arithmetic progression. The $n$ largest elements of $\alpha+\beta$ are $s+\beta$, whose smallest element is $s$; so the elements of $\alpha+\beta$ that are $\ge s$ are exactly $s+\beta$. But $s+\alpha\subseteq\alpha+\beta$ (as $s\in\beta$) consists of $n$ elements $\ge s$. Hence $s+\alpha=s+\beta$ and $\alpha=\beta$. Now (3) says: the elements of $\alpha+\alpha$ that are $\le s$ are exactly those of $\alpha$, and those that are $\ge s$ are exactly those of $s+\alpha$. Reduce modulo $s$: the set $\bar\alpha=\{a\bmod s: a\in\alpha\}$ has $n-1$ elements ($0$ and $s$ coincide). For $a,a'\in\alpha$: if $a+a'\le s$ then $a+a'\in\alpha$, and if $a+a'\ge s$ then $a+a'-s\in\alpha$. So $\bar\alpha$ is closed under addition modulo $s$; a nonempty finite subset of $\mathbb Z/s\mathbb Z$ closed under addition is a subgroup, here of order $n-1$, namely the multiples of $d=\frac{s}{n-1}$. Therefore $\alpha=\{0,d,2d,\dots,(n-1)d\}$.

So from some step $N$ on, $A_k$ and $B_k$ are translates of the arithmetic progression $\{0,d,\dots,(n-1)d\}$; in particular the numbers on A in increasing order, and those on B in decreasing order, form arithmetic progressions.

Solution 2 (Measure A from its minimum and B from its maximum)

Write $A_k,B_k$ for the boards after $k$ operations and $X_k=A_k+B_k$, so $A_{k+1}$ consists of the $n$ smallest and $B_{k+1}$ of the $n$ largest elements of $X_k$. Measure board A from its minimum and board B from its maximum: \[U_k=\{a-\min A_k: a\in A_k\},\qquad V_k=\{\max B_k-b: b\in B_k\}.\] Both are sets of $n$ nonnegative integers containing $0$; let $u_k=\max U_k$ and $v_k=\max V_k$ (so $v_k\in V_k$ and $0\in v_k-V_k$).

Step 1: the new sets. The least element of $X_k$ is $\min A_k+\min B_k$, and \[X_k-(\min A_k+\min B_k)=U_k+\{b-\min B_k: b\in B_k\}=U_k+(v_k-V_k).\] Hence $U_{k+1}$ is the set of the $n$ smallest elements of $U_k+(v_k-V_k)$. In the same way, the largest element of $X_k$ is $\max A_k+\max B_k$, and $\max A_k+\max B_k-X_k=(u_k-U_k)+V_k$, so $V_{k+1}$ is the set of the $n$ smallest elements of $V_k+(u_k-U_k)$.

Step 2: stabilization. Since $0\in v_k-V_k$, the set $U_k+(v_k-V_k)$ contains $U_k$. The $i$-th smallest element of a set is at most the $i$-th smallest element of any $n$-element subset, so, listing elements in increasing order, every entry of $U_{k+1}$ is at most the corresponding entry of $U_k$. Likewise for $V_{k+1}$ and $V_k$. These are nonnegative integers, so they can decrease only finitely often: there is $N$ with $U_k=U$ and $V_k=V$ for all $k\ge N$. Let $u=\max U$ and $v=\max V$. By Step 1, \[U=\text{the $n$ smallest elements of }U+(v-V),\qquad V=\text{the $n$ smallest elements of }V+(u-U).\tag{1}\]

Step 3: the last gaps are equal. Since $n\ge2$, let $u-a$ be the second largest element of $U$ and $v-b$ the second largest element of $V$, with $a,b>0$. Then $b\in v-V$. If $x\in U$ and $x+b\le u$, then $x+b$ is an element of $U+(v-V)$ not exceeding the largest of its $n$ smallest elements, so by (1) it lies in $U$. Thus \[x\in U,\ x+b\le u\ \Longrightarrow\ x+b\in U.\tag{2}\] If $b<a$, apply (2) to $x=u-a$: the number $u-a+b$ lies strictly between $u-a$ and $u$, yet no element of $U$ does. Hence $b\ge a$. The same argument with the roles of $U$ and $V$ exchanged gives $a\ge b$. So $a=b=:d$.

Step 4: $U$ and $V$ are arithmetic progressions. Take $x\in U$ and add $d$ repeatedly as long as the result stays at most $u$; by (2) all these numbers lie in $U$. The last one lies in $(u-d,u]$, and the only element of $U$ in this interval is $u$. Hence every element of $U$ is congruent to $u$ modulo $d$. Starting from $x=0\in U$ we get $0,d,2d,\dots,u\in U$; so $d\mid u$, and $U$ consists of exactly the multiples of $d$ in $[0,u]$, i.e. $U=\{0,d,\dots,(n-1)d\}$. The same argument shows that $V$ is an arithmetic progression.

So for every $k\ge N$, the numbers on A, which are $\min A_k+U$, form an arithmetic progression in increasing order, and the numbers on B, which are $\max B_k-V$, form an arithmetic progression in decreasing order.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Hybrid Counting and Structure · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test, AIMO 6, Problem 2

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ be any $n$-tuple of distinct integers. An inversion is a pair $(i,j)$ of integers satisfying $1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n$ and $a_i>a_j$. An inversion is called odd if $j-i$ is odd. Let $A$ be the number of all inversions, and let $B$ be the number of odd inversions.

  • (a) Prove that $A\leqslant 5B$ for all $n$ and all $n$-tuples $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$.

  • (b) Prove that there exist an $n$ and an $n$-tuple $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ for which $A>4.99B$.

Show the answer

proof (TST wording: the largest $t$ with $B\geqslant tA$ is $t=\frac15$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Charging each even inversion to a middle point)

Call an inversion $(i,j)$ even if $j-i$ is even; let $E=A-B$ be the number of even inversions.

(a) We show $E\le4B$, which is equivalent to $A\le5B$. Let $(i,j)$ be an even inversion, $j-i=2d$. Put \[k=\begin{cases}i+d,& d\text{ odd},\\ i+d-1,& d\text{ even}.\end{cases}\] Then $i<k<j$ and $k-i$, $j-k$ are odd. Since $a_i>a_j$, we have $a_i>a_k$ or $a_k>a_j$; so $(i,k)$ or $(k,j)$ is an odd inversion. Assign $(i,j)$ to $(i,k)$ if $a_i>a_k$, and to $(k,j)$ otherwise.

Now fix an odd inversion $(u,v)$, $h=v-u$ (odd), and count the even inversions assigned to it.

  • As $(i,k)=(u,v)$: then $i=u$ and $k-i=h$ equals $d$ (if $d$ is odd) or $d-1$ (if $d$ is even), so $d\in\{h,h+1\}$ and $j=u+2d$ takes at most $2$ values.

  • As $(k,j)=(u,v)$: then $j=v$ and $j-k=2d-(k-i)=h$ equals $d$ ($d$ odd) or $d+1$ ($d$ even), so $d\in\{h,h-1\}$ and $i=v-2d$ takes at most $2$ values.

Hence every odd inversion receives at most $4$ even inversions, and $E\le4B$, i.e. $A=E+B\le5B$.

(b) Let $m$ and $C$ be even positive integers, $n=Cm$, and use positions $x=0,1,\dots,n-1$ (shift by $1$ for the indexing of the problem). For a position $x$ let $c(x)=\lfloor x/m\rfloor$ be its block and define its band \[\beta(x)=\begin{cases}c(x),& x\equiv c(x)\pmod 2,\\ c(x)-1,& \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad a_x=n\,\beta(x)-x .\] Properties. Always $x\equiv\beta(x)\pmod2$; band $b$ consists of the positions of parity $b$ in blocks $b$ and $b+1$. Since $0\le x<n$, $a_x>a_y$ holds exactly when $\beta(x)>\beta(y)$, or $\beta(x)=\beta(y)$ and $x<y$; in particular the $a_x$ are distinct.

Which pairs $x<y$ are inversions? All pairs in the same band (they are at even distance). If $\beta(x)>\beta(y)$ with $x<y$, then $c(x)-1\le c(y)-1\le\beta(y)<\beta(x)\le c(x)$ forces $c(y)=c(x)=:c$, $\beta(x)=c$, $\beta(y)=c-1$: $x$ and $y$ lie in the same block, $x$ has parity $c$, $y$ the other parity (odd distance). So \[E=\sum_{\text{bands}}\binom{|\text{band}|}{2},\qquad B=\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\#\{x<y\text{ in block }c:\ x\equiv c,\ y\not\equiv c\pmod 2\}.\] Bands $0,\dots,C-2$ have $m$ elements, bands $-1$ and $C-1$ have $\frac m2$, so $E=(C-1)\frac{m(m-1)}2+\frac{m(m-2)}4$. A block starts at an even position; for even $c$ the pairs counted are (even offset, later odd offset), $\frac{m}{2}\cdot\frac{m/2+1}{2}$ of them, and for odd $c$ they are $\frac m2\cdot\frac{m/2-1}2$. As $C$ is even, $B=\frac C2\cdot\frac{m^2}{4}=\frac{Cm^2}8$. Therefore \[\frac AB=1+\frac EB=1+\frac{4(C-1)(m-1)+2(m-2)}{Cm}=5-\frac4m-\frac2C .\] With $m=C=1000$ (so $n=10^6$) this is $4.994>4.99$, as required.

The TST wording. In the Japan TST the problem asked for a real number $t$ such that $B\geqslant tA$ for all $n$ and all $n$-tuples. Part (a) shows that $t=\frac15$ works. No larger $t$ works: the example above gives $\frac BA=\big(5-\frac4m-\frac2C\big)^{-1}$, which tends to $\frac15$ as $m,C\to\infty$, so it is below any $t>\frac15$ for $m,C$ large. Hence the best constant is $t=\boxed{\tfrac15}$ (and every $t\leqslant\frac15$ works).

Solution 2 (A periodic example built from residues modulo $2m+1$)

Call an inversion $(i,j)$ even if $j-i$ is even; let $E=A-B$ be the number of even inversions.

(a) We show $E\le4B$, which is equivalent to $A\le5B$. Let $(i,j)$ be an even inversion, $j-i=2s$, and put $c=i+s$ if $s$ is odd and $c=i+s-1$ if $s$ is even. Then $i<c<j$, and $c-i$ and $j-c$ are odd. As $a_i>a_j$, we have $a_i>a_c$ or $a_c>a_j$; assign $(i,j)$ to the odd inversion $(i,c)$ in the first case and to $(c,j)$ in the second. Fix an odd inversion $(x,y)$, $y-x=g$. If $(i,j)$ is assigned to it as $(i,c)$, then $i=x$ and $g=c-i\in\{s,s-1\}$, so $s\in\{g,g+1\}$ and $j=x+2s$ takes at most $2$ values. If it is assigned as $(c,j)$, then $j=y$ and $g=j-c\in\{s,s+1\}$, so $s\in\{g,g-1\}$ and $i=y-2s$ takes at most $2$ values. So each odd inversion receives at most $4$ even inversions, $E\le4B$ and $A\le5B$.

(b) Let $m,T$ be positive integers, $k=2m+1$ and $n=kT$; we index the terms by $i=0,1,\dots,n-1$ (shift by $1$ for the problem's indexing). As $k$ is odd, there is a unique $r_i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}$ with $2r_i\equiv-i\pmod k$. Put \[a_i=n\,(i+3r_i)+i .\] These are distinct, since $a_i\equiv i\pmod n$.

Criterion. Let $0\le i<j\le n-1$, $d=j-i$ and $\delta=r_i-r_j$. If $i+3r_i>j+3r_j$, then $a_i-a_j\ge n+i-j>0$; otherwise $a_i-a_j\le i-j<0$. So $(i,j)$ is an inversion if and only if $3\delta>d$. Since $2\delta\equiv j-i=d$ and $2(m+1)=k+1\equiv1\pmod k$, we have $\delta\equiv(m+1)d\pmod k$. Let $\rho_d\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ be the remainder of $(m+1)d$ modulo $k$. As $|\delta|<k$, either $\delta=\rho_d$ (when $r_i\ge\rho_d$) or $\delta=\rho_d-k<0$ (when $r_i<\rho_d$). Hence \[(i,i+d)\text{ is an inversion}\iff 3\rho_d>d\ \text{ and }\ r_i\ge\rho_d .\]

The distances that occur. Note $\rho_d\le2m$, so $3\rho_d>d$ forces $d<6m$.

  • $d=2s$: $(m+1)2s=s(k+1)\equiv s$. For $1\le s\le2m$, $\rho_d=s$ and $3s>2s$: these distances occur. For $k\le s<2k$, $\rho_d=s-k$ and $3(s-k)>2s$ would need $s>3k$; larger $s$ give $d\ge4k>6m$. So no other even distance occurs.

  • $d=2s+1$: $(m+1)(2s+1)\equiv s+m+1$. For $0\le s\le m-1$, $\rho_d=s+m+1$ and $3(s+m+1)>2s+1$: these occur. For $m\le s\le3m$, $\rho_d=s-m$ and $3(s-m)>2s+1$ would need $s>3m+1$; larger $s$ give $d>6m$. So no other odd distance occurs.

All occurring distances are less than $2k$.

Counting. The map $i\mapsto r_i$ is a bijection from any $k$ consecutive integers onto $\{0,\dots,k-1\}$, so among $k$ consecutive values of $i$ exactly $k-\rho$ have $r_i\ge\rho$. For an occurring odd distance $d=2s+1$, the starting points $i$ lie in $[0,n-d)\subset[0,n)$, which consists of $T$ blocks of $k$ consecutive integers; so there are at most $T(k-\rho_d)=T(m-s)$ inversions at distance $d$, and \[B\le T\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}(m-s)=T\,\frac{m(m+1)}2 .\] Let $T\ge2$. For every occurring distance $d<2k$, the interval $[0,n-d)$ contains the $T-2$ blocks of $[0,k(T-2))$, so \[A\ge(T-2)\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{2m}(k-s)+\frac{m(m+1)}2\Bigr)=(T-2)\Bigl(m(2m+1)+\frac{m(m+1)}2\Bigr).\] Hence $A\ge\bigl(1-\frac2T\bigr)\bigl(1+\frac{2(2m+1)}{m+1}\bigr)B=\bigl(1-\frac2T\bigr)\bigl(5-\frac2{m+1}\bigr)B$. For $m=250$ and $T=20000$ (so $n=10\,020\,000$), $\bigl(1-\frac1{10000}\bigr)\bigl(5-\frac2{251}\bigr)>0.9999\cdot4.992>4.9915$, and $B>0$ (for instance $(i,i+1)$ is an inversion whenever $r_i\ge m+1$). So $A>4.99B$.

Problem 3

SGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Dot Product and Metric Recovery

Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram with $AB<AD$. Let $E$ be a point on segment $BD$ with $BE>DE$, and let $F$ be a point on segment $AE$ (other than the endpoints) with $AF=BD$. $X$ and $Y$ lie on the angle bisectors of $\angle AEB$ and $\angle AED$, respectively, such that $XY\perp CF$ and $BM=DM$, where $M$ is the midpoint of segment $XY$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of segment $EM$. The circumcircles of triangles $ABD$ and $BEF$ intersect at $P$ other than $B$, and the circumcircles of triangles $ABD$ and $DEF$ intersect at $Q$ other than $D$. Let $K$ be the intersection of lines $BP$ and $AD$, and let $L$ be the intersection of lines $DQ$ and $AB$. Prove that there exists a point $Z$, depending only on the parallelogram $ABCD$, such that $Z$ always lies on the line passing through $N$ and perpendicular to $KL$, regardless of the choice of $E$.

Show the answer

proof ($Z$ is the circumcenter of $ABD$)

Show the solution

Solution (Radical axes, a rotation, and dot products)

We show that $Z$ can be taken to be the circumcenter $O$ of triangle $ABD$, i.e. that $ON\perp KL$.

Put $a=EA$, $b=EB$, $d=ED$. Since $F$ is an interior point of segment $AE$ with $AF=BD=b+d$, we have $a>b+d$; also $b>d$. Let $\omega_0$, $\omega_1$, $\omega_3$ be the circles $ABD$, $BEF$, $DEF$.

Step 1: $K$ lies on the bisector of $\angle AEB$. Let $W$ be the midpoint of the arc $AED$ of the circle $AED$ (the arc containing $E$); then $WA=WD$, and $W\ne E$ because $EA\ne ED$. As $ED<EA$, going around this circle we meet $A,W,E,D$ in this order, so $A$ and $D$ lie on the same side of the chord $WE$ and $\angle WAE=\angle WDE$. The triangles $WAF$ and $WDB$ have $WA=WD$, $AF=DB$ and equal angles $\angle WAF=\angle WAE=\angle WDE=\angle WDB$ (the ray $AF$ is the ray $AE$, and the ray $DB$ is the ray $DE$). So they are congruent, with the same orientation (the directed angles $\measuredangle(AW,AE)$ and $\measuredangle(DW,DE)$ are equal, as $A,D$ lie on the same side of $WE$), and the rotation about $W$ that maps $A$ to $D$ maps $F$ to $B$. The lines $AF$ and $DB$ meet at $E$; by the standard property of the center of a spiral similarity, $W$ lies on the circle $EFB=\omega_1$ (and on the circle $EAD$).

The midpoint $W$ of the arc $AED$ lies on the external bisector of $\angle AED$, which is the bisector of $\angle AEB$. This line is not parallel to $AD$ (because $EA\ne ED$); let it meet line $AD$ at $K_0$. Using signed lengths, \[\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_0}(K_0)=K_0A\cdot K_0D=K_0E\cdot K_0W=\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_1}(K_0),\] the middle equality being the power of $K_0$ with respect to the circle $AEDW$, and the last one holding because $E,W\in\omega_1$. So $K_0$ is on the radical axis of $\omega_0$ and $\omega_1$, which is the line $BP$. Hence $K_0=BP\cap AD=K$: the point $K$ lies on the bisector of $\angle AEB$.

In the same way, with $V$ the midpoint of the arc $AEB$ of the circle $AEB$ (the rotation about $V$ mapping $A$ to $B$ maps $F$ to $D$, because $AF=BD$), $V\in\omega_3$, and $L=DQ\cap AB$ lies on the line $EV$, the bisector of $\angle AED$.

Step 2: vectors. Put the origin at $E$ and let $\alpha,\beta$ be the unit vectors along $EA$ and $EB$. Then \[A=a\alpha,\quad B=b\beta,\quad D=-d\beta,\quad C=B+D-A=(b-d)\beta-a\alpha,\quad F=(a-b-d)\alpha .\] The bisectors of $\angle AEB$ and $\angle AED$ are the lines $\mathbb R(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\mathbb R(\alpha-\beta)$. The point $K$ is on line $AD$ and on $\mathbb R(\alpha+\beta)$, and $L$ is on line $AB$ and on $\mathbb R(\alpha-\beta)$; one checks that \[K=\frac{aD-dA}{a-d}=-\frac{ad}{a-d}(\alpha+\beta),\qquad L=\frac{aB-bA}{a-b}=\frac{ab}{a-b}(\beta-\alpha)\] (both are affine combinations of the two points of the respective line, and $a\ne b$, $a\ne d$). Hence \[L-K=p\,\beta+q\,\alpha,\qquad p=\frac{ab}{a-b}+\frac{ad}{a-d}=\frac{a(ab+ad-2bd)}{(a-b)(a-d)},\qquad q=-\frac{ab}{a-b}+\frac{ad}{a-d}=\frac{a^2(d-b)}{(a-b)(a-d)} .\]

The point $M$. $X$ lies on $\mathbb R(\alpha+\beta)$ and $Y$ on $\mathbb R(\alpha-\beta)$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the line $\mathbb R(\alpha+\beta)$; it swaps $\alpha$ and $\beta$, fixes $X$ and sends $Y$ to $-Y$. So $\sigma(Y-X)=-(X+Y)=-2M$, and $XY\perp CF$ gives \[0=(Y-X)\cdot CF=\sigma(Y-X)\cdot\sigma(CF)=-2\,M\cdot\big((2a-b-d)\beta-(b-d)\alpha\big),\] because $CF=F-C=(2a-b-d)\alpha-(b-d)\beta$. The condition $MB=MD$ reads $2M\cdot(D-B)=|D|^2-|B|^2$, i.e. $M\cdot\beta=\frac{b-d}{2}$. Since $b\ne d$, it follows that $M\cdot\alpha=\frac{2a-b-d}{2}$. And $N=\frac M2$.

The point $O$. $OB=OD$ gives $O\cdot\beta=\frac{b-d}{2}$, and $OA=OB$ gives $2O\cdot(B-A)=|B|^2-|A|^2$, i.e. $O\cdot\alpha=\frac{a^2-b^2+b(b-d)}{2a}=\frac{a^2-bd}{2a}$.

Conclusion. \[(O-N)\cdot(L-K)=p\Big(\frac{b-d}{2}-\frac{b-d}{4}\Big)+q\Big(\frac{a^2-bd}{2a}-\frac{2a-b-d}{4}\Big)=\frac{p(b-d)}{4}+\frac{q\,(ab+ad-2bd)}{4a}.\] Substituting $p$ and $q$, the numerator becomes $a(ab+ad-2bd)(b-d)+a(d-b)(ab+ad-2bd)=0$. Hence $ON\perp KL$: the line through $N$ perpendicular to $KL$ always passes through the circumcenter $O$ of triangle $ABD$, a point that depends only on the parallelogram. (The hypothesis $AB<AD$ is not needed.)

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test, AIMO 1, Problem 2

There are $2025$ white balls and $2025$ black balls arranged in a row. A balanced segment is a contiguous nonempty segment of balls that contains the same number of white balls as black balls. A balanced removal is the operation of selecting a balanced segment $S$, removing all the balls in $S$, and shifting left the balls that were to the right of $S$ so that the remaining balls form a contiguous row. Determine the smallest positive integer $N$ satisfying the following property: for every configuration of balls and for every integer $k$ satisfying $0\leq k\leq 2025$, there exists a sequence of at most $N$ balanced removals after which there are exactly $2k$ remaining balls.

Show the answer

$N=2$

Show the solution

Solution (Cut along the zero-level walk)

Let $n=2025$. Number the gaps between balls $0,1,\dots,2n$ (gap $t$ is just after the $t$-th ball) and let $P_t$ be the number of white minus the number of black balls among the first $t$ balls. So $P_0=P_{2n}=0$, consecutive values differ by $1$, and the segment of balls between gaps $i<j$ is balanced exactly when $P_i=P_j$.

$N\ge2$. Take the row $BB\,\underbrace{W\cdots W}_{n}\,\underbrace{B\cdots B}_{n-2}$ and $k=1$. One balanced removal leaving $2$ balls would remove all balls except either the first two, or the last two, or the first and the last. These pairs are $BB$, $BB$ and $BB$ (as $n-2\ge2$), never one white and one black, while the removed segment, and hence the remaining pair, would have to be balanced. So one removal is not enough.

$N\le2$. Fix a row and $k$, and put $L=2n-2k$, the number of balls to remove. If $L=0$ do nothing. Otherwise let $z$ be the last gap with $z<L$ and $P_z=0$ (it exists, since $P_0=0$), and let $z'$ be the first gap with $z'\ge L$ and $P_{z'}=0$ (it exists, since $P_{2n}=0$). Between $z$ and $z'$ the walk does not return to $0$, so $P_t$ has constant sign for $z<t<z'$; say $P_t\ge0$ on $[z,z']$ (the other case is symmetric).

Put $\ell=L-z$; it is even and $0<\ell\le z'-z$. For $z\le i\le z'-\ell$ consider $g(i)=P_{i+\ell}-P_i$. Then \[g(z)=P_{z+\ell}\ge0,\qquad g(z'-\ell)=-P_{z'-\ell}\le0,\] all values of $g$ are even (since $\ell$ is even), and $g(i+1)-g(i)\in\{-2,0,2\}$. Hence $g(i)=0$ for some $i$: the balls between gaps $i$ and $i+\ell$ form a balanced segment of length $\ell$ lying between gaps $z$ and $z'$.

Now remove this segment, and then (if $z>0$) the first $z$ balls, which form a balanced segment because $P_z=P_0$ and are still contiguous at the start of the row. Altogether $\ell+z=L$ balls are removed in at most two balanced removals, leaving $2k$ balls.

Hence the smallest $N$ is $\boxed{2}$.

Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{Z}_+\to\mathbb{Z}_+$ such that \[f^{n+2}(1)=f(n)+2\] for every positive integer $n$. Here, $f^k(n)$ denotes the $k$-th iteration of $f(n)$.

Show the solution

Solution (The orbit of 1 covers everything)

Step 4: $c_k=k+1$. Problem 6

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Euler Totient and Related Functions

Let $c$ be a positive real number. Now, prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that, for each integer $n\ge N$ and every pair of integers $a,b$ satisfying $1\le a,b\le n^c$, the following proposition holds: \[\text{If } \varphi(a+k)=\varphi(b+k) \text{ holds for every integer } k \text{ with } 1\le k\le n, \text{ then } a=b.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Comparing the small prime factors of $m$ and $m+h$)

Setup. For a prime $p$ put $w_p=\ln\frac{p}{p-1}>0$, and for a positive integer $x$ let \[g(x)=\sum_{p\mid x}w_p\qquad(\text{sum over the primes dividing }x),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \varphi(x)=x\prod_{p\mid x}\Big(1-\frac1p\Big)=x\,e^{-g(x)} .\] Suppose $a\ne b$; the hypothesis is symmetric in $a$ and $b$, so we may assume $a<b$. Put $h=b-a$, so $1\le h\le n^c-1$, and let $I=\{a+1,\dots,a+n\}$. For $m\in I$ the hypothesis gives $m\,e^{-g(m)}=\varphi(m)=\varphi(m+h)=(m+h)\,e^{-g(m+h)}$, hence \[g(m+h)-g(m)=\ln\Big(1+\frac hm\Big)>0\qquad\text{for every }m\in I.\tag{1}\] In words: the larger number $m+h$ must have “more” small prime factors than $m$. We show that this fails on average over the multiples of a suitable prime $q$.

Step 1: a small prime $q$ with $q\nmid h$. By Bertrand's postulate, for each $j\ge1$ there is a prime in $(2^{j-1},2^j]$. Let $J=\lfloor\frac13\log_2 n\rfloor$. These primes for $j=1,\dots,J$ are distinct, at most $2^J\le n^{1/3}$, and their product is at least $2^{0+1+\dots+(J-1)}=2^{J(J-1)/2}$. For $n$ large, $J(J-1)/2\ge c\log_2 n$, so this product is at least $n^c>h$; hence not all of these primes divide $h$. Fix a prime $q\le n^{1/3}$ with $q\nmid h$.

Step 2: summing (1) over the multiples of $q$. Let $S=\{m\in I: q\mid m\}$; it has $|S|\ge\lfloor n/q\rfloor>\frac nq-1$ elements. By (1), \[0<\sum_{m\in S}\big(g(m+h)-g(m)\big)=\sum_{p}w_p\,\big(A_p-B_p\big),\qquad A_p=\#\{m\in S: p\mid m+h\},\quad B_p=\#\{m\in S: p\mid m\}.\tag{2}\] We estimate each $A_p-B_p$.

  • $p=q$: since $q\mid m$ and $q\nmid h$, $q\nmid m+h$; so $A_q=0$ and $B_q=|S|$.

  • $p\ne q$, $p\mid h$: $p\mid m+h\iff p\mid m$, so $A_p=B_p$.

  • $p\ne q$, $p\nmid h$: by the Chinese remainder theorem, $A_p$ counts the elements of $I$ in one residue class modulo $pq$ (namely $m\equiv0\pmod q$, $m\equiv-h\pmod p$), and $B_p$ the elements of $I$ in another one ($m\equiv0\pmod{pq}$). A block of $n$ consecutive integers contains either $\lfloor n/pq\rfloor$ or $\lfloor n/pq\rfloor+1$ elements of any residue class modulo $pq$, so $A_p-B_p\le1$. Moreover, if $p>b+n$ then $p$ divides no element of $I$ and no element of $I+h$ (all are positive and at most $b+n$), so $A_p=B_p=0$.

Putting these into (2), \[0<-w_q|S|+\sum_{p\le b+n}w_p,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad w_q\,|S|<E:=\sum_{p\le b+n}w_p .\tag{3}\]

Step 3: the two sides of (3). On the left, $w_q=-\ln\big(1-\frac1q\big)\ge\frac1q$, so \[w_q|S|>\frac1q\Big(\frac nq-1\Big)\ge\frac n{q^2}-1\ge n^{1/3}-1 .\] On the right, $w_p=\ln\big(1+\frac1{p-1}\big)\le\frac1{p-1}$, and the numbers $p-1$ are distinct positive integers at most $b+n-1$, so \[E\le\sum_{k=1}^{b+n-1}\frac1k\le1+\ln(b+n)\le1+\ln(n^c+n)\le1+\ln2+\max(c,1)\ln n .\] Since $n^{1/3}$ grows faster than $\ln n$, there is $N$ (depending only on $c$) such that for $n\ge N$ both $J(J-1)/2\ge c\log_2 n$ in Step 1 and $n^{1/3}-1\ge1+\ln2+\max(c,1)\ln n$. For such $n$, (3) is false. This contradiction shows that $a=b$ whenever $n\ge N$, $1\le a,b\le n^c$ and $\varphi(a+k)=\varphi(b+k)$ for $1\le k\le n$.

Show the solution

Solution (Deleting the largest number)

We prove a more flexible statement. Consider a cyclic arrangement $a_1,\dots,a_L$ ($L\ge2$, indices modulo $L$; for $L=2$ both neighbours of a number are the other number) of integers greater than $2^{2025}$, together with positive integers $e_1,\dots,e_L$ such that \[a_i\mid a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}-e_i\quad\text{for all }i,\qquad e_i\le2^{2025-L}.\tag{$\ast$}\] The problem is the case $L=2025$, $e_i=1$. We show that ($\ast$) with $L\ge3$ leads to ($\ast$) with $L-1$, and that ($\ast$) with $L=2$ is impossible.

From $L$ to $L-1$ ($L\ge3$). Let $a_i$ be a largest number. Since $1\le e_i\le2^{2025-L}<a_{i-1}$, \[0<a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}-e_i<2a_i ,\] and this number is a multiple of $a_i$, so $a_i=a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}-e_i$. (For $L=3$ the indices $i-2$ and $i+1$ coincide, and the formulas still describe the $2$-cycle.) The other conditions are unchanged. The new constants are at most $2\cdot2^{2025-L}=2^{2025-(L-1)}$, so ($\ast$) holds for the shorter cycle.

$L=2$ is impossible. Let the numbers be $x\ge y>2^{2025}$ with $x\mid2y-e$ and $y\mid2x-e'$, where $1\le e,e'\le2^{2023}$. Since $0<2y-e<2x$, we get $x=2y-e$. Then $y\mid2x-e'=4y-(2e+e')$, so $y\mid2e+e'$. But $0<2e+e'\le3\cdot2^{2023}<2^{2025}<y$, a contradiction.

Problem 9

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test, AIMO 7, Problem 3

On a $45\times 45$ square grid there is an echidna. From any cell, the echidna can move to any other cell that shares a side with that cell. The echidna starts in one of the cells and makes a sequence of $2024$ moves, after which it has visited each cell exactly once. Prove that it is possible to write the numbers from $1$ to $2025$, one in each cell, in such a way that:

  • For any pair of adjacent cells in the same row, the cell containing the larger number was visited earlier.

  • For any pair of adjacent cells in the same column, the cell containing the larger number was visited later.

Show the solution

Solution (No directed cycle: a switch count on the enclosed region)

Let $t(c)\in\{0,1,\dots,2024\}$ be the time at which cell $c$ is visited. Form a directed graph $D$ on the cells: for two cells $u,v$ in the same row and adjacent, draw $u\to v$ if $t(u)>t(v)$; for two cells $u,v$ in the same column and adjacent, draw $u\to v$ if $t(u)<t(v)$. A labelling as required is exactly a numbering in which every arrow goes from a smaller to a larger number. Such a numbering exists if $D$ has no directed cycle (number the cells along a topological order: repeatedly give the next number to a cell with no incoming arrow from unnumbered cells). So it suffices to show that $D$ is acyclic.

We picture cells as their centres (lattice points) and adjacencies as unit segments. The echidna's route is a simple lattice path through all points; for times $p<q$ write $\Gamma[p,q]$ for the part of the route between times $p$ and $q$. We use repeatedly: two grid paths with no common cell do not meet at all, since unit segments of the lattice meet only at lattice points.

Lemma 1 (no directed unit square). Suppose the four cells at the corners of a unit square form a directed cycle. Around the square the arrows alternate between horizontal and vertical, so at every corner one arrow is horizontal and one vertical. A horizontal arrow points to the earlier cell and a vertical arrow to the later one. Hence at a corner whose incoming arrow is horizontal and outgoing arrow vertical, the corner is earlier than both its neighbours on the square, and the two corners of this type are opposite; call them $b,d$ and the other two $a,c$. So $\max(t(b),t(d))<\min(t(a),t(c))$. Then the route segment $\Gamma_1$ between $b$ and $d$ uses only times below $\min(t(a),t(c))$, and the segment $\Gamma_2$ between $a$ and $c$ only times above; they are disjoint and avoid the other two corners, so neither uses a side of the square. Close $\Gamma_1$ by the diagonal $bd$ to a closed curve $J$. Near the diagonal, $a$ and $c$ lie on different sides of $J$, but $\Gamma_2$ joins them without meeting $\Gamma_1$ or the diagonal (it stays outside the open square). This contradicts the Jordan curve theorem.

Lemma 2 (no source or sink with four neighbours). Let $x$ have neighbours $L,R$ in its row and $U,W$ in its column. If all four arrows at $x$ point out of $x$, then $t(L),t(R)<t(x)<t(U),t(W)$; if all point in, the inequalities are reversed. In the first case the route segment $\Gamma_1$ between $L$ and $R$ uses only times $<t(x)$, and the segment $\Gamma_2$ between $U$ and $W$ only times $>t(x)$ (the second case is the same with the roles exchanged). Close $\Gamma_1$ by the segments $Lx$ and $xR$ to a closed curve $J$. Near $x$, $J$ is a straight horizontal line, so $U$ and $W$ lie on different sides of $J$; yet $\Gamma_2$ joins them without meeting $\Gamma_1$, $x$, or the segments $Lx,xR$. Contradiction.

The counting. Suppose $D$ has a directed cycle; take a simple one, $C$. It is a simple closed lattice polygon. Let $H$ be the plane graph formed by all lattice points on or inside $C$ (all are cells of the board) and all unit segments between them lying inside or on $C$, with the orientations of $D$. Its bounded faces are unit squares and its outer face is bounded by $C$.

For a vertex $v$ let $s_v$ be the number of switches at $v$: consecutive edges around $v$ of which one points in and one points out. For a face $f$ let $s_f$ be the number of switches along its boundary: consecutive boundary edges that point in opposite directions along the boundary walk. Each angle (a pair of consecutive edges at a vertex $v$, lying in a face $f$) is a switch at $v$ or a switch of $f$, but not both: if one edge enters $v$ and the other leaves, the boundary of $f$ passes through $v$ in a consistent direction; if both enter or both leave, the direction reverses at $v$. There are $2E$ angles, so $\sum_vs_v+\sum_fs_f=2E$, and Euler's formula $V-E+F=2$ gives \[\sum_v\Bigl(1-\frac{s_v}2\Bigr)+\sum_f\Bigl(1-\frac{s_f}2\Bigr)=2 .\] All $s$-values are even. Now estimate each term:

  • the outer face: $C$ is directed, so $s=0$ and the term is $1$;

  • a unit-square face: by Lemma 1 it is not a directed cycle, so $s_f\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$;

  • a vertex on $C$: it has an incoming and an outgoing edge of $C$, so $s_v\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$;

  • a vertex strictly inside $C$: all its four neighbours are in $H$, and by Lemma 2 it is neither a source nor a sink, so $s_v\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$.

The left side is therefore at most $1$, a contradiction. Hence $D$ is acyclic, and the required numbering exists.

Solution (Residues as a bijection, and two large primes)

$f(n)=-n$ works: for $d\mid n$ take $e=d$; then $d+f(e)=0$.

Now let $f$ satisfy the condition; write $D(n)$ for the set of positive divisors of $n$ and $\tau(n)=|D(n)|$.

Step 1: a bijection. Fix $n$. The numbers $-d$ $(d\in D(n))$ are $\tau(n)$ different residues modulo $n$ (the divisors below $n$ are distinct and nonzero modulo $n$, and $-n\equiv0$). Each of them must be congruent to some $f(e)$ with $e\in D(n)$, and there are only $\tau(n)$ such values. Hence \[\{f(e)\bmod n:\ e\in D(n)\}=\{-d\bmod n:\ d\in D(n)\},\quad\text{and the }f(e)\ (e\in D(n))\text{ are pairwise incongruent mod }n. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $f(m)=0$ or $f(m)=-d$ for some $d\in D(m)$. Let $a=f(m)$ and pick a prime $q>|a|+m$; then $q\nmid m$. By (1) for $n=mq$, $a\equiv-d\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid mq$, i.e. $d=d'$ or $d=d'q$ with $d'\mid m$. In the first case $mq\mid a+d'$ and $|a+d'|\le|a|+m<mq$, so $a=-d'$. In the second case $q\mid a$, so $a=0$ (as $q>|a|$).

Step 3: $f(m)=-m$ for all $m$, by strong induction. Let $m\ge1$ and assume $f(d)=-d$ for all $d<m$ (nothing is assumed for $m=1$). By (1) for $n=m$, the values $f(d)=-d$ $(d\in D(m),\ d<m)$ already occupy all the required residues except $0$, so $f(m)\equiv0\pmod m$, and by Step 2, $f(m)\in\{0,-m\}$.

Suppose $f(m)=0$. Let $q$ be any prime not dividing $m$ and $n=mq$, so $D(n)=D(m)\cup qD(m)$. The residue $-m$ is required by (1), and $-m\not\equiv0$. It is not $f(d)=-d$ for $d<m$, $d\mid m$, nor $f(m)=0$. So $f(dq)\equiv-m\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid m$. By Step 2, $f(dq)$ is $0$ or $-c$ with $c\mid dq$, $0<c\le dq\le mq$; the congruence then forces $f(dq)=-m$ and $m\mid dq$, hence $m\mid d$ and $d=m$. So \[f(mq)=-m\quad\text{for every prime } q\nmid m.\] Take two different such primes $q,r$ and $N=mqr$. Both $mq$ and $mr$ divide $N$ and $f(mq)=f(mr)=-m$, contradicting the incongruence in (1) for $N$. Hence $f(m)=-m$, completing the induction.

The only function is $\boxed{f(n)=-n}$.

Solution 1 ($T$ is the reflection of $Q$, then a harmonic division)

The point $X$ lies on line $QQ^*$, i.e. $X=(m,x)$, and on line $RD$: \[x=d\,\frac{m-r}{e-r}.\] The altitude from $C$ is line $CH$; it meets line $QQ^*$ at $X'=(m,x')$ with $x'=-d\,\dfrac{m-c}{e-c}$. Solution 3 (Coordinates: $T$ is the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $B=(u,v)$ with $v>0$, and let $r=u^2+v^2=AB^2$ and $n=(1-u)^2+v^2=BC^2=r+1-2u$. Since $AB<AC$, $r<1$. The angles at $A$ and $C$ are acute, so $0<u<1$, and the angle at $B$ is acute: $\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=r-u>0$. Put $w=\frac{r-u}{v}>0$. We shall use the identity \[rn=(r-u)^2+v^2,\tag{$*$}\] which holds because both sides equal $r^2+r-2ur$ (use $u^2+v^2=r$).

$\Gamma$, $D$, $R$, $Q$. The circle $\Gamma$ is $x^2+y^2=x+wy$ (it passes through $A$, $C$, and through $B$ as $u+wv=r$). The perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$ is $x=u$; on it $\Gamma$ gives $y^2-wy+u^2-u=0$, with roots $v$ and $w-v$, so $D=(u,w-v)$. For a point of $\Gamma$ we have $AZ^2=x^2+y^2=x+wy$; so the points of $\Gamma$ at distance $\sqrt r$ from $A$ are its points on the line $x+wy=r$. Thus $BQ$ is the line $x+wy=r$, and $R=(r,0)$, with $0<r<1$.

Let $Q=(q,z)$. Substituting $x=r-wy$ in $x^2+y^2=r$ gives $(1+w^2)y^2-2rwy+r^2-r=0$, with roots $v$ and $z$; so $vz=-\frac{r(1-r)}{1+w^2}$. By $(*)$, $1+w^2=\frac{v^2+(r-u)^2}{v^2}=\frac{rn}{v^2}$, hence \[z=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}<0,\qquad q=r-wz .\]

The point $T$. Let $T'=(q,-z)$ be the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$. The line $BC$ is $vx+(1-u)y=v$, and since $vw+1-u=r-u+1-u=n$, \[vq-(1-u)z=vr-(vw+1-u)z=vr-nz=vr+v(1-r)=v,\] so $T'$ lies on line $BC$. Its height $-z=\frac{v(1-r)}{n}$ lies strictly between $0$ and $v$, because $1-r<n$ is equivalent to $u<r$. So $T'$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$.

The circle $\omega$ through $Q,R,T'$. As $Q$ and $T'$ are symmetric in $AC$, the centre of $\omega$ is on $AC$, say $(c_0,0)$, and $\omega$ is $f(x,y):=x^2+y^2-2c_0x+2c_0r-r^2=0$ (it passes through $R$). Using $q^2+z^2=AQ^2=r$, the condition $f(Q)=0$ reads $r-r^2=2c_0(q-r)=-2c_0wz$, so \[2c_0=\frac{r(1-r)}{-wz}=\frac{rn}{vw}=\frac{rn}{r-u}.\]

$S$ lies on $\omega$. Let $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-x-wy$, so $\Gamma$ is $g=0$, and $\ell=f-g=(1-2c_0)x+wy+2c_0r-r^2$, an affine function. Since $S\in\Gamma$, we have $S\in\omega$ if and only if $\ell(S)=0$. Write the points of line $DR$ as $R+\mu(D-R)$. Then $g(R+\mu(D-R))$ is a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $|D-R|^2$, constant term $g(R)=r^2-r$, and roots $\mu=1$ (the point $D$) and $\mu_S$ (the point $S$); so $\mu_S=\frac{r^2-r}{|D-R|^2}$. As $\ell$ is affine, $\ell(S)=\ell(R)+\mu_S\bigl(\ell(D)-\ell(R)\bigr)$. Here $\ell(R)=r-r^2$ and, using $2c_0(u-r)=-rn$ and $wv=r-u$, \[\ell(D)-\ell(R)=(1-2c_0)(u-r)+w(w-v)=(u-r)+rn+w^2-wv=rn+w^2-2wv .\] By $(*)$, $|D-R|^2=(u-r)^2+(w-v)^2=rn+w^2-2wv$ as well. Hence $\ell(S)=r-r^2+(r^2-r)=0$, and $S\in\omega$. So $\omega$ is the circle $QRS$.

$T=T'$. The power of $C$ with respect to $\omega$ is $f(1,0)=(1-r)(1+r-2c_0)$, where \[1+r-2c_0=\frac{(1+r)(r-u)-r(r+1-2u)}{r-u}=-\frac{u(1-r)}{r-u}<0;\] so $C$ lies inside $\omega$. Hence $C$ lies between the two points where line $BC$ meets $\omega$; one of them is $T'$, on the side of $B$, so the other one is beyond $C$. Thus $T'$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on $\omega$, and $T=T'=(q,-z)$.

The point $X$. The line $TQ$ is $x=q$. The line $RS$ is the line $RD$, which is not vertical as $u\ne r$; its point with $x=q$ is $R+\mu(D-R)$ with $\mu=\frac{q-r}{u-r}=\frac{-wz}{-vw}=\frac zv$, so $X=\bigl(q,\frac{z(w-v)}{v}\bigr)$.

Conclusion. We have $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(q-1)+z(w-v)$. With $q-1=r-1-wz$ this is $u(r-1)+z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)$. Now $v\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=(r-u)(1-u)-v^2=r-u-ur+u^2-(r-u^2)=-u(1+r-2u)=-un$, so \[z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{un}{v}\Bigr)=u(1-r),\] and $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(r-1)+u(1-r)=0$. Hence $CX$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

Problem 12

SAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Invariant-Based Game Analysis

Tim and Tam play a game. To start the game, Tim writes some nonzero real numbers not necessarily distinct, on a blackboard. In each round, the following happens.

  • First, Tam chooses a polynomial $P_k(x)=a_kx^k+a_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0$ whose coefficients $a_k,a_{k-1},\ldots,a_1,a_0$ are all of the numbers currently written on the blackboard in some order. (For example, if the numbers on the blackboard are $4,-3,4$, then $k=2$ and Tam may choose the polynomial $4x^2-3x+4$ or $-3x^2+4x+4$ but not $4x-3$ or $-3x^2+4$.)

  • Then, if the equation $P_k(x)=0$ has no real solutions, the game is stopped. Otherwise, Tim chooses a real number $r$ such that $P_k(r)=0$ and writes it on the blackboard, so there is now one more number on the blackboard.

Determine whether Tim can ensure that the game is never stopped, no matter what Tam does.

Show the answer

not determined

Show the solution

Solution (Partial progress: what each player can force)

This is partial progress only: the question whether Tim can keep the game going forever is not settled here.

Throughout, $m$ is the number of numbers on the board, so Tam's polynomial has degree $k=m-1$.

(1) Only rounds with $m$ odd matter. If all numbers on the board are nonzero, every arrangement has a nonzero constant term, so $0$ is never a root and the board never contains $0$. If $m$ is even, $k$ is odd and every arrangement has a real root. Hence the game can stop only in a round in which $m$ is odd.

(2) A winning criterion for Tam. Let $m=2s+1$, and suppose that the $s+1$ numbers of largest absolute value (ties broken arbitrarily) all have the same sign. Then Tam stops the game at once. Since a polynomial and its negative have the same roots, we may assume that these numbers $p_0,\dots,p_s$ are positive; call the remaining numbers $q_1,\dots,q_s$, so that $|q_j|\le\min_ip_i$ for every $j$. Tam chooses \[P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_jx^{2j-1}.\] For real $x$ and $1\le j\le s$, the AM-GM inequality gives \[\tfrac12p_{j-1}x^{2j-2}+\tfrac12p_jx^{2j}\ \ge\ \sqrt{p_{j-1}p_j}\,|x|^{2j-1}\ \ge\ |q_j|\,|x|^{2j-1}.\] Adding these $s$ inequalities, and noting that the left sides add up to $\sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}-\tfrac12p_0-\tfrac12p_sx^{2s}$, we get \[P(x)\ \ge\ \sum_{j=0}^{s}p_jx^{2j}-\sum_{j=1}^{s}|q_j|\,|x|^{2j-1}\ \ge\ \tfrac12p_0+\tfrac12p_sx^{2s}\ >\ 0,\] so $P$ has no real root. In particular Tam wins at once whenever $m$ is odd and all numbers have the same sign; once both signs are present, they stay present.

(3) Two situations in which every arrangement has a real root. Let $\Sigma$ be the sum of the numbers; $P(1)=\Sigma$ for every arrangement.

  • If $\Sigma=0$, then $1$ is a root of every arrangement.

  • If $\Sigma<0$ and exactly one number $N$ is negative, then every arrangement, of any degree, has a positive root: if $N$ is not the constant term, then $P(0)>0>P(1)$; if it is, the leading coefficient is positive and $P(1)<0<P(x)$ for large $x$. The same holds with all signs reversed.

These are not the only safe boards: for $1,4,-\tfrac92$ (positive sum, two positive numbers) every arrangement has a root, because $-\tfrac92$ must stand in the middle and $(\tfrac92)^2>4\cdot1\cdot4$.

(4) The second situation cannot be kept forever. Suppose the board has exactly one negative number $N$ and $\Sigma<0$, and Tam makes $N$ the constant term. For $0<|x|\le1$ every other term satisfies $a_ix^i\le a_i|x|^i\le a_i$, so $P(x)\le N+(\Sigma-N)=\Sigma<0$. Hence every real root $r$ has $|r|>1$. If Tim writes a positive root (to keep a single negative number), $\Sigma$ increases by more than $1$; if he writes a negative root, the board gets a second negative number of absolute value greater than $1$. So if Tam always does this, Tim can stay in this situation only for finitely many rounds.

(5) Experiments. An exhaustive search of the game tree for small boards (Tam tries every arrangement, Tim every real root, roots computed numerically) shows: from the two-number starts $1,-c$ ($c=4,5,8,20$) Tam wins by the round with $5$ numbers; from random three-number starts the only boards found from which Tim survives up to $7$ numbers were of the type in (3), for example $-13.94,\,3.06,\,3.88$, which by (4) lose that property after a number of rounds that grows with the size of the negative number. This is consistent with the guess that Tam can always stop the game, but it proves nothing.

What is missing. A strategy for Tam that works against every initial board (presumably the answer is that Tim cannot ensure that the game never stops), or an invariant for Tim more robust than those in (3).
