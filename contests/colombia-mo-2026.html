Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

A group of $11$ students is participating in a tournament. The tournament consists of a series of $11$ games selected from a list of $22$ possible games. The $11$ chosen games are revealed only on the day of the tournament, and each student must participate in exactly one of the games. To win the competition, the students must win each of the $11$ chosen games. To ensure they win the tournament, the students decide to prepare in advance of the tournament day. It is known that it takes each student $1$ hour of preparation to become an expert at one of the $22$ games and, therefore, guarantee their victory in that game. Each student prepares separately but they agree on which games each will practice. Determine the minimum number of preparation hours required among all of them to guarantee winning the tournament, regardless of which games they have to play on the day of the tournament.

Show the answer

$132$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Each student needs $12$ games; private games plus common games)

For each student $s$ let $K_s$ be the set of games $s$ prepares; the total number of hours is $\sum_s |K_s|$. The students are guaranteed to win exactly when, for every set $G$ of $11$ chosen games, the students can be assigned to the games of $G$, one student per game, each student to a game in his own $K_s$.

Lower bound. Suppose some student $s$ prepares at most $11$ games. Then at least $11$ games are outside $K_s$, and if $11$ of them are the chosen games, $s$ must play a game he has not prepared, so victory is not guaranteed. Hence $|K_s|\ge 12$ for all $11$ students, and the total is at least $11\cdot 12=132$ hours.

Construction with $132$ hours. Number the games $g_1,\dots,g_{22}$ and the students $s_1,\dots,s_{11}$. Let $s_i$ prepare his “private” game $g_i$ and the eleven “common” games $g_{12},\dots,g_{22}$: that is $12$ hours each, $132$ in total. Given the chosen set $G$, every student $s_i$ with $g_i\in G$ plays $g_i$. If $j$ students are used in this way, then $G$ contains exactly $11-j$ common games, and the remaining $11-j$ students play them, since everybody has prepared every common game. So the tournament is always won.

The minimum is $\boxed{132}$ hours.

Solution 2 (Sliding windows of $12$ consecutive games)

As in any solution, describe a plan by the sets $K_s$ of games each student prepares; the plan works when, for every set of $11$ chosen games, each student can be given a different chosen game from his own set.

Lower bound by counting choices. If a student prepares $h$ games, the number of $11$-element sets of games avoiding all of them is $\binom{22-h}{11}$, and for each such choice that student cannot be used. So $\binom{22-h}{11}=0$, i.e. $22-h\le 10$ and $h\ge 12$. Summing over the $11$ students, at least $132$ hours are needed.

A different construction. Number the games $1,2,\dots,22$ and the students $1,2,\dots,11$, and let student $i$ prepare the $12$ consecutive games $i,i+1,\dots,i+11$ (a “window”). Total: $132$ hours. Let the chosen games be $c_1<c_2<\dots<c_{11}$ and let student $j$ play $c_j$. Since $c_1,\dots,c_j$ are $j$ distinct positive integers, $c_j\ge j$; since $c_j<c_{j+1}<\dots<c_{11}\le 22$ are $12-j$ distinct integers, $c_j\le 22-(11-j)=j+11$. So $c_j$ lies in the window of student $j$, and every game is played by an expert.

Therefore the minimum is $\boxed{132}$ hours.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB < AC$ and let $M$ be the midpoint of the segment $AC$. Let $D$ be the point on $AC$ such that $DB = DC$ and let $E$ be the intersection between the circumcircle of triangle $ABM$ and the line $BD$ (distinct from $B$). Let $P$ and $Q$ be the intersection points of the line $BC$ with the lines $EM$ and $AE$ respectively. Prove that $P$ is the midpoint of the segment $BQ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two similar triangles on the segment $EB$)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles of $ABC$ at $A,B,C$, and let $\omega$ be the circle $ABM$.

Configuration. The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ separates the points nearer to $B$ from those nearer to $C$. Since $AB<AC$, it separates $A$ from $C$, so $D$ lies strictly between $A$ and $C$. In the isosceles triangle $DBC$, $DC=\dfrac{BC}{2\cos\gamma}$, while $MC=\dfrac{AC}{2}$. Now $AC\cos\gamma$ is the distance from $C$ to the foot of the altitude from $A$, which lies inside $BC$ because the triangle is acute; so $AC\cos\gamma<BC$, i.e. $DC>MC$. Hence $D$ lies strictly between $A$ and $M$, inside the chord $AM$ of $\omega$. Therefore the second intersection $E$ of line $BD$ with $\omega$ lies beyond $D$ (so $D$ is between $B$ and $E$), on the arc $AM$ not containing $B$, and $A,B,M,E$ lie on $\omega$ in this order. Consequently \[ \angle BEM=\angle BAM=\alpha,\qquad \angle AEB=\angle AMB, \] (angles on the same arc), and $\angle EBC=\angle DBC=\angle DCB=\gamma$ because $DB=DC$.

Triangle $EBP$. The points $M$ and $C$ lie on the same side of line $BE$ (the line $BD$ meets line $AC$ only at $D$, and $M,C$ are on the same side of $D$). The ray from $E$ through $M$ and the ray from $B$ through $C$ make angles $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ with $EB$, on the same side, and $\alpha+\gamma<180^\circ$; so they meet, at the point $P$, and triangle $EBP$ has angles $\alpha$ at $E$, $\gamma$ at $B$, hence $\beta$ at $P$. Thus $\triangle EBP\sim\triangle ACB$ (with $E\leftrightarrow A$, $B\leftrightarrow C$, $P\leftrightarrow B$), and \[ \frac{BP}{CB}=\frac{EB}{AC},\qquad\text{so}\qquad BP=\frac{BC\cdot EB}{AC}. \]

Triangle $EBQ$. Since $D$ is between $A$ and $C$, the points $A$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of line $BE$, so the ray from $E$ opposite to $EA$ lies on the side of $C$. It makes with $EB$ the angle $180^\circ-\angle AEB=180^\circ-\angle AMB=\angle BMC$, and the ray $BC$ makes the angle $\gamma$; as $\angle BMC+\gamma<180^\circ$ (two angles of triangle $BMC$), these rays meet, at $Q$. Triangle $EBQ$ has angles $\angle BMC$ at $E$ and $\gamma$ at $B$, so $\triangle EBQ\sim\triangle MCB$ (with $E\leftrightarrow M$, $B\leftrightarrow C$, $Q\leftrightarrow B$), and \[ \frac{BQ}{CB}=\frac{EB}{MC},\qquad\text{so}\qquad BQ=\frac{BC\cdot EB}{MC}=\frac{2\,BC\cdot EB}{AC}=2\,BP . \] Since $P$ and $Q$ both lie on the ray $BC$, $P$ is the midpoint of $BQ$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Choose coordinates with $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and $A$ in the upper half-plane. The point $D$ with $DB=DC$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=0$ of $BC$; write $D=(0,d)$. As $A$ lies on line $CD$, we can write \[ A=C+s(D-C)=(1-s,\ sd) \] for some real $s$. Since $AB<AC$, $A$ is nearer to $B$, i.e. its $x$-coordinate is negative: $s>1$ (so $D$ lies between $A$ and $C$), and then $sd>0$ gives $d>0$. The angle at $B$ is acute, so $A$ lies to the right of the line $x=-1$: $1-s>-1$, i.e. $s<2$. The midpoint of $AC$ is $M=\left(1-\tfrac s2,\ \tfrac{sd}{2}\right)$.

The point $E$. Points of line $BD$ are $B+u(D-B)=(-1+u,\ ud)$, and points of line $CD$ are $C+\sigma(D-C)$. Both direction vectors have length $\ell=\sqrt{1+d^2}$ (as $DB=DC=\ell$), so such points are at distance $|u-1|\ell$, respectively $|\sigma-1|\ell$, from $D$. On line $CD$, $A$ has $\sigma=s$ and $M$ has $\sigma=s/2<1$, so $D$ lies inside the chord $AM$, with $DA=(s-1)\ell$ and $DM=(1-\tfrac s2)\ell$. Hence $D$ is inside the circle $ABM$, the second intersection $E$ of line $BD$ has a parameter $u>1$, and the power of the point $D$ gives $DB\cdot DE=DA\cdot DM$: \[ \ell\cdot(u-1)\ell=(s-1)\left(1-\tfrac s2\right)\ell^2,\qquad u=1+\frac{(s-1)(2-s)}{2}=\frac{s(3-s)}{2}. \] So $E=\left(-1+\tfrac{s(3-s)}{2},\ \tfrac{s(3-s)d}{2}\right)$.

The points $P$ and $Q$. We claim $P=(2-s,0)$ and $Q=(5-2s,0)$. Indeed, with these points, \[ M-P=\tfrac12\,(s-2,\ sd),\qquad E-P=\left(\tfrac{(3-s)(s-2)}{2},\ \tfrac{(3-s)sd}{2}\right)=(3-s)(M-P), \] so $E,M,P$ are collinear; and \[ A-Q=(s-4,\ sd),\qquad E-Q=\left(\tfrac{(3-s)(s-4)}{2},\ \tfrac{(3-s)sd}{2}\right)=\tfrac{3-s}{2}\,(A-Q), \] so $A,E,Q$ are collinear. (The lines $EM$ and $AE$ are not parallel to $BC$: $E$ and $M$ have equal heights only if $s=2$, and $E$ and $A$ only if $s=1$.) Hence $P$ and $Q$ are the intersections of line $BC$ with $EM$ and $AE$. Finally \[ P-B=(3-s,\ 0),\qquad Q-B=(6-2s,\ 0)=2(P-B), \] so $P$ is the midpoint of $BQ$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $a \ge 2$ be a positive integer. For a positive integer $m$ we define $c(m)$ as the largest square that divides $m$. We say that the sequence $a_0, a_1, \ldots$ is Colombian if it satisfies $a_0 = a$ and for $n \ge 0$ \[a_{n+1} = \begin{cases} \sqrt{a_n} & \text{if } \sqrt{a_n} \text{ is an integer,}\\ a_n + c(a_n) & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\] Determine all integers that are in all Colombian sequences.

Show the answer

$2,\ 3,\ 4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squarefree parts and a descent)

Notation. Every positive integer $x$ can be written uniquely as $x=c(x)\,m(x)$ with $c(x)$ the largest square dividing $x$ and $m(x)$ squarefree (take each prime to the largest even power not exceeding its exponent). Then $x$ is a square iff $m(x)=1$, and for a square $s$ we have $c(sy)=s\,c(y)$ and $m(sy)=m(y)$.

Only $2,3,4$ can work. For $a=2$ the sequence is $2,3,4,2,3,4,\dots$ ($c(2)=c(3)=1$ and $\sqrt4=2$). So an integer lying in all Colombian sequences is one of $2,3,4$. We show that every Colombian sequence contains $2$; then it also contains $3$ and $4$, the next terms.

(a) All terms are at least $2$. If $a_n\ge 2$ is a square then $a_n\ge 4$ and $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_n}\ge 2$; otherwise $a_{n+1}>a_n$.

(b) Lemma: starting from a non-square $x$, the sequence reaches a square $S$ after finitely many steps, and $S<9x$. Let $x_0=x,x_1,\dots$ be the terms as long as they are non-squares, and write $c_i=c(x_i)$, $m_i=m(x_i)\ge 2$. Then $x_{i+1}=x_i+c_i=c_i(m_i+1)$, so \[ x_{i+1}=x_i\Big(1+\frac1{m_i}\Big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{i+1}=m(m_i+1). \] Call step $i$ a climb if $m_i+1$ is squarefree (then $m_{i+1}=m_i+1$) and a drop otherwise (then $m_i+1=j^2m_{i+1}$ with $j\ge2$, so $m_{i+1}\le (m_i+1)/4$). Group the steps into runs: some climbs followed by one drop. If a run starts with the value $p\ge 2$, its values are $p,p+1,\dots,q$, where $q+1$ is the first non-squarefree number after $p$; one of $p+1,\dots,p+4$ is divisible by $4$, so $q+1\le p+4$. Over the run the term is multiplied by \[ \prod_{m=p}^{q}\Big(1+\frac1m\Big)=\frac{q+1}{p}\le 1+\frac4p , \] and the next run starts with $p'=m(q+1)\le\frac{q+1}{4}\le\frac{p+4}{4}<p$. So the starting values $p_0=m_0>p_1>p_2>\cdots$ decrease, which cannot go on for ever: some run $R$ ends with $m=1$, i.e. at a square $S$. From $p_{r+1}\le(p_r+4)/4$ we get $p_r\ge 4p_{r+1}-4$, and $p_R\ge 2$; with $b_0=2$, $b_{i+1}=4b_i-4$, i.e. $b_i=\frac{2\cdot 4^i+4}{3}$, induction gives $p_{R-i}\ge b_i$. Therefore \[ \frac Sx\le\prod_{r=0}^{R}\Big(1+\frac{4}{p_r}\Big)\le\prod_{i=0}^{R}\Big(1+\frac4{b_i}\Big) =\prod_{i=0}^{R}\frac{4^i+8}{4^i+2}=\prod_{i=0}^{R}\frac{4(4^i+8)}{4^{i+1}+8}=\frac{9\cdot4^{R+1}}{4^{R+1}+8}<9 . \]

(c) Every term $x\ge 3$ is followed later by a smaller term. If $x$ is a square, then $x\ge4$ and the next term is $\sqrt x<x$. If $x$ is not a square and $x\ge 9$, the lemma gives a later square $S<9x\le x^2$, followed by $\sqrt S<x$. The remaining non-squares are $3,5,6,7,8$: $3\to4\to2$, and $5\to6\to7\to8\to12\to16\to4$, so each of them is followed by $2$ or $4$, which is smaller.

(d) Conclusion. Let $\mu$ be the smallest term of the sequence. By (c), $\mu\le 2$, and by (a), $\mu=2$. So $2$, and hence $3$ and $4$, appear in every Colombian sequence.

The integers in all Colombian sequences are exactly $\boxed{2,\ 3,\ 4}$.

Solution 2 (A quantity that never grows from one square jump to the next)

Notation. Every positive integer $x$ can be written uniquely as $x=s^2b$ with $s\ge1$ and $b$ squarefree (take each prime to the largest even power not exceeding its exponent in $x$). Then $c(x)=s^2$, and $x$ is a square iff $b=1$. Put \[ \varphi(x)=s\,b . \] Note that $\varphi(x)\le s^2b=x$, with equality only if $s=1$. Also, for a square $s^2$ and any $y$ we have $c(s^2y)=s^2c(y)$.

Only $2,3,4$ can work. For $a=2$ the sequence is $2,3,4,2,3,4,\dots$ (as $c(2)=c(3)=1$ and $\sqrt4=2$). So an integer lying in all Colombian sequences is one of $2,3,4$. We show that every Colombian sequence contains $2$; then it also contains the next two terms $3$ and $4$.

All terms are at least $2$. If $a_n\ge2$ is a square, then $a_n\ge4$ and $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_n}\ge2$; otherwise $a_{n+1}>a_n$.

Lemma. Let $x=s^2b$ be a non-square, so $b\ge2$. Then the sequence starting from $x$ reaches a square $S$, and $\sqrt S\le s\,b$, with strict inequality unless $b=2$.

Proof. Let $j\ge1$ be the least integer such that $b+j$ is not squarefree. For $0\le i<j$ the number $s^2(b+i)$ is a non-square with square part $s^2c(b+i)=s^2$, so it is followed by $s^2(b+i+1)$. Hence the terms after $x$ are $s^2(b+1),\dots,s^2(b+j)$. Since $b$ is squarefree, $4\nmid b$, so one of $b+1,b+2,b+3$ is a multiple of $4$, and $j\le3$. Write $b+j=t^2b_1$ with $t\ge2$ and $b_1$ squarefree. The term reached is $x_1=(st)^2b_1$, and \[ \varphi(x_1)=s\,t\,b_1=\frac{s(b+j)}{t}\le\frac{s(b+3)}{2}. \] If $b\ge4$, then $(b+3)/2<b$, so $\varphi(x_1)<\varphi(x)$. If $b=2$ or $b=3$, the first non-squarefree number after $b$ is $4$, so $t=2$, $b_1=1$ and $\varphi(x_1)=2s\le sb$, with strict inequality for $b=3$. In every case $\varphi(x_1)\le\varphi(x)$, strictly unless $b=2$; moreover $b_1=(b+j)/t^2\le(b+3)/4<b$.

If $b_1=1$, then $x_1$ is a square and $\sqrt{x_1}=st=\varphi(x_1)$. Otherwise repeat the argument from $x_1$. The squarefree parts $b>b_1>\cdots$ strictly decrease, so after finitely many such stages the squarefree part is $1$: the sequence reaches a square $S$ with $\sqrt S=\varphi(S)\le\cdots\le\varphi(x_1)\le\varphi(x)=sb$, and the first inequality $\varphi(x_1)\le\varphi(x)$ is strict unless $b=2$. $\square$

Every term $x\ge3$ is followed later by a smaller term. If $x$ is a square, then $x\ge4$ and the next term is $\sqrt x<x$. If $x=s^2b$ is not a square, the Lemma gives a later square $S$ with $\sqrt S\le sb\le s^2b=x$. Equality throughout would need both $b=2$ and $s=1$, that is $x=2$, which is excluded. So $\sqrt S<x$, and $\sqrt S$ is the term after $S$.

Conclusion. Let $\mu$ be the smallest term of the sequence. Then $\mu\ge2$, and $\mu\ge3$ is impossible by the previous paragraph, so $\mu=2$. Hence $2$, and therefore $3$ and $4$, appear in every Colombian sequence.

The integers in all Colombian sequences are exactly $\boxed{2,\ 3,\ 4}$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Let $a$ and $b$ be two positive real numbers such that $b \ne 1$. Felipe performs the following procedure. In his first step, he writes the number $a$ on a sheet of paper. From then on, he writes on the sheet the number from the previous step multiplied by $b$. Felipe stops writing numbers when the sheet contains a number greater than $100$ or a number that is not an integer. What is the largest number of numbers the sheet can contain?

Show the answer

$8$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The ratio in lowest terms)

Felipe writes $x_j=ab^j$ for $j=0,1,2,\dots$ and stops right after writing the first number that is greater than $100$ or not an integer. So if the sheet contains $N$ numbers, then $x_0,\dots,x_{N-2}$ are positive integers at most $100$.

$8$ numbers are possible: $a=1$, $b=2$ gives $1,2,4,8,16,32,64,128$, and Felipe stops at $128>100$.

$9$ numbers are impossible. Then $x_0,\dots,x_7$ would be eight positive integers at most $100$ forming a geometric progression with ratio $b\ne1$. The ratio $b=x_1/x_0$ is rational. If $b<1$, read the progression backwards: $x_7,x_6,\dots,x_0$ has ratio $1/b>1$. So we may assume the ratio is $p/q>1$ in lowest terms, with $p\ge 2$. Then $x_7=x_0p^7/q^7$ is an integer and $\gcd(p^7,q^7)=1$, so $q^7\mid x_0$ and \[ x_7=\frac{x_0}{q^7}\,p^7\ge p^7\ge 2^7=128>100, \] a contradiction. The largest number of numbers is $\boxed{8}$.

Solution 2 (Repeated differences)

Felipe writes $x_j=ab^j$ and stops right after writing the first number that is greater than $100$ or not an integer. So if the sheet contains $N$ numbers $x_0,\dots,x_{N-1}$, then $x_0,\dots,x_{N-2}$ are positive integers at most $100$; and $a=1$, $b=2$ gives $N=8$ ($1,2,\dots,64$ and then $128$).

Suppose $N\ge 9$, so $x_0,\dots,x_7$ are positive integers at most $100$. Reversing the order if necessary, we may assume the common ratio is $r>1$. Form the difference tables $\Delta^0_j=x_j$ and $\Delta^{k+1}_j=\Delta^k_{j+1}-\Delta^k_j$. Each $\Delta^k_j$ is an integer combination of the $x_i$, hence an integer, and by induction $\Delta^k_j=x_0r^j(r-1)^k>0$. So $\Delta^k_0$ is a positive integer, $\Delta^k_0\ge 1$, for $k=0,1,\dots,7$ (it only uses $x_0,\dots,x_k$). By the binomial theorem, \[ x_7=x_0r^7=x_0\big(1+(r-1)\big)^7=\sum_{k=0}^{7}\binom7k x_0(r-1)^k=\sum_{k=0}^{7}\binom 7k\Delta^k_0\ge\sum_{k=0}^7\binom7k=2^7=128, \] contradicting $x_7\le 100$. Hence the answer is $\boxed{8}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Find all real numbers $x_1, \ldots, x_{100}$ such that \[\begin{aligned} x_3^{100} &= x_1 + x_2\\ x_4^{100} &= x_2 + x_3\\ &\ \,\vdots\\ x_{100}^{100} &= x_{98} + x_{99}\\ x_1^{100} &= x_{99} + x_{100}\\ x_2^{100} &= x_{100} + x_1 \end{aligned}\]

Show the answer

$x_1=\dots=x_{100}=0$ or $x_1=\dots=x_{100}=\sqrt[99]{2}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Looking at the smallest and the largest unknown)

Take indices modulo $100$; the system says $x_{i+2}^{100}=x_i+x_{i+1}$ for every $i$. In particular \[ x_i+x_{i+1}\ge 0\qquad\text{for every } i. \tag{1} \]

Step 1: no unknown is negative. Suppose not, and let $m<0$ be the smallest value, attained at $x_j=m$. By (1), $x_{j+1}\ge -m=|m|>0$, so $x_{j+1}^{100}\ge m^{100}$. On the other hand, using $x_{j-2}\ge m$, \[ m^{100}=x_j^{100}=x_{j-2}+x_{j-1}\ \ge\ x_j+x_{j-1}=x_{j+1}^{100}\ \ge\ m^{100}. \] So equality holds throughout; in particular $x_{j-2}=m$. Thus $j-2$ is also an index of the minimum, and the same argument for $j-2$ (in place of $j$) gives $x_{j-1}^{100}=m^{100}$ with $x_{j-1}\ge |m|>0$, i.e. $x_{j-1}=|m|$. But then $x_{j+1}^{100}=x_{j-1}+x_j=|m|+m=0$, so $x_{j+1}=0$, contradicting $x_{j+1}\ge|m|>0$.

Step 2: all unknowns are equal. Now all $x_i\ge 0$. Let $m$ be the smallest value, at $x_j$, and $M$ the largest, at $x_k$.

  • If $m=0$: $0=x_j^{100}=x_{j-2}+x_{j-1}$ with both terms $\ge0$, so $x_{j-1}=0$. Repeating this with $j-1$ in place of $j$, and so on, all unknowns are $0$.

  • If $m>0$: $m^{100}=x_{j-2}+x_{j-1}\ge 2m$ gives $m^{99}\ge 2$, and $M^{100}=x_{k-2}+x_{k-1}\le 2M$ gives $M^{99}\le 2$. Hence $M\le\sqrt[99]2\le m\le M$, and all unknowns equal $\sqrt[99]2$.

Both candidates work: $0=0+0$, and $\big(\sqrt[99]2\big)^{100}=2\sqrt[99]2=\sqrt[99]2+\sqrt[99]2$. So the solutions are \[ \boxed{(x_1,\dots,x_{100})=(0,\dots,0)\quad\text{or}\quad(x_1,\dots,x_{100})=\big(\sqrt[99]{2},\dots,\sqrt[99]{2}\big)} . \]

Solution 2 (Signs of consecutive differences)

Take indices modulo $100$; the system says $x_{i+2}^{100}=x_i+x_{i+1}$ for every $i$ (call this equation $(E_i)$). In particular \[ x_i+x_{i+1}\ge0\qquad\text{for every } i. \tag{1} \] Put $d_i=x_{i+1}-x_i$ and $D_i=x_{i+1}^{100}-x_i^{100}$. Note $d_0+d_1+\dots+d_{99}=0$.

Lemma. If $D_i>0$ then $d_i>0$, and if $D_i<0$ then $d_i<0$. Indeed, $u\mapsto u^{50}$ is strictly increasing for $u\ge0$, so $D_i$ has the same sign as $x_{i+1}^2-x_i^2=d_i\,(x_i+x_{i+1})$. If $D_i>0$, this product is positive; its second factor is $\ge0$ by (1), hence positive, and so $d_i>0$. The case $D_i<0$ is the same.

A recursion. Subtracting $(E_i)$ from $(E_{i+1})$ gives \[ D_{i+2}=x_{i+3}^{100}-x_{i+2}^{100}=x_{i+2}-x_i=d_i+d_{i+1}. \tag{2} \]

Step 1: the differences are all $0$ or strictly alternate in sign. Suppose $d_i\ge0$ and $d_{i+1}\ge0$, not both $0$. By (2), $D_{i+2}>0$, so $d_{i+2}>0$ by the Lemma; now the pair $d_{i+1},d_{i+2}$ has the same property, and by induction $d_k>0$ for all $k\ge i+2$. Going once around the cycle, all $100$ differences are positive, contradicting $\sum d_k=0$. In the same way (with all signs reversed) we cannot have $d_i\le0$, $d_{i+1}\le0$, not both $0$. Consequently, if some $d_i=0$, then $d_{i+1}=0$ (otherwise the pair $d_i,d_{i+1}$ is of a forbidden kind), and then all differences are $0$. If no difference is $0$, any two consecutive differences have opposite signs.

Step 2: alternating signs are impossible. Suppose the $d_i$ are nonzero and alternate in sign. Then the unknowns with indices of one parity are each larger than both neighbours; let $H$ be this set of indices ($d_{h-1}>0>d_h$ for $h\in H$). The map $h\mapsto h+1$ is a bijection from $H$ onto the other parity class. For $h\in H$ we have $d_h<0$, so the Lemma rules out $D_h>0$; that is, \[ x_h^{100}\ge x_{h+1}^{100}\qquad (h\in H). \tag{3} \] On the other hand, adding the equations $(E_i)$ over all even $i$ gives $\sum_{k\text{ even}}x_k^{100}=\sum_{i\text{ even}}(x_i+x_{i+1})=\sum_k x_k$, and over all odd $i$ gives $\sum_{k\text{ odd}}x_k^{100}=\sum_k x_k$. So the two parity classes have the same sum of $100$th powers, that is, $\sum_{h\in H}x_h^{100}=\sum_{h\in H}x_{h+1}^{100}$. Together with (3), every inequality in (3) is an equality: $|x_h|=|x_{h+1}|$. As $x_h\ne x_{h+1}$, we get $x_{h+1}=-x_h$, and $(E_h)$ gives $x_{h+2}^{100}=x_h+x_{h+1}=0$. Since $h+2$ runs over all of $H$ as $h$ does, $x_h=0$ for every $h\in H$, and then $x_{h+1}=-x_h=0$ too. So all unknowns are $0$, contradicting $d_i\ne0$.

Step 3: the constant solutions. By Steps 1 and 2 all unknowns are equal to some $t$, and $t^{100}=2t$. So $t=0$ or $t^{99}=2$; the latter has the single real solution $t=\sqrt[99]2$, as $u\mapsto u^{99}$ is strictly increasing. Both work: $0=0+0$ and $\big(\sqrt[99]2\big)^{100}=2\sqrt[99]2$. The solutions are \[ \boxed{(x_1,\dots,x_{100})=(0,\dots,0)\quad\text{or}\quad(x_1,\dots,x_{100})=\big(\sqrt[99]{2},\dots,\sqrt[99]{2}\big)} . \]

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Initially, all cells of a $n \times n$ board are painted white. On each turn, it is allowed to paint one cell of the board black, as long as for every white cell there exist at least two other white cells that share a side with it. Find the greatest amount of cells that might be painted black.

Show the answer

$n^2-2n$ for even $n$, $n^2-2n-1$ for odd $n\ge 3$ (and $1$ for $n=1$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The perimeter of the white region)

We read the rule as: after each painting, every white cell must have at least two white neighbours (cells sharing a side). For $n=1$ the single cell can be painted (no white cell remains), so the answer is $1$; from now on $n\ge2$.

Perimeter. For a set $X$ of cells let $\operatorname{per}(X)$ be the number of unit edges that separate a cell of $X$ from a cell outside $X$ or from the outside of the board. If $\deg_X(z)$ denotes the number of neighbours of $z$ in $X$, then $\operatorname{per}(X)=\sum_{z\in X}\big(4-\deg_X(z)\big)$, and $\operatorname{per}(\text{board})=4n$.

Monovariant. If $z\notin X$ has $h\ge2$ neighbours in $X$, then $\operatorname{per}(X\cup\{z\})-\operatorname{per}(X)=(4-h)-h\le 0$: the $h$ edges between $z$ and $X$ stop being boundary edges and the other $4-h$ edges of $z$ become boundary edges.

Upper bound. Let $W_0\supset W_1\supset\dots\supset W_k=W$ be the white sets during a legal painting, $W_0$ the whole board. For each $t$, $W_t=W_{t+1}\cup\{z_t\}$ where $z_t$ has at least two neighbours in $W_t$ (for $t=0$ because every cell of the board has at least two neighbours, for $t\ge1$ by the rule), and they all lie in $W_{t+1}$. By the monovariant, $\operatorname{per}(W_t)\le\operatorname{per}(W_{t+1})$, so \[ 4n=\operatorname{per}(W_0)\le\operatorname{per}(W)=\sum_{z\in W}\big(4-\deg_W(z)\big)\le 2|W|, \] because $\deg_W(z)\ge 2$ for all $z\in W$ (if $k=0$ nothing was painted). Hence $|W|\ge 2n$ and at most $n^2-2n$ cells are black.

Odd $n$: $|W|=2n$ is impossible. Let $n$ be odd and suppose $|W|=2n$. Then all the inequalities above are equalities, so every cell of $W$ has exactly two neighbours in $W$, and $\operatorname{per}(W)=4n$. Hence each connected piece of $W$ is a cycle of cells, and different pieces have no adjacent cells. If every piece were a $2\times2$ square, $|W|$ would be divisible by $4$, but $2n\equiv 2\pmod 4$. So some piece $C$, with $L$ cells, is not a $2\times 2$ square. Let its bounding box (the smallest rectangle of cells containing $C$) have $a$ columns and $b$ rows. A cycle cannot lie in one row or column, so $a,b\ge2$; and if $a=b=2$, then $C$, having at least $4$ cells, would fill the $2\times2$ box. So $a+b\ge5$.

(i) $L\ge 2(a+b)-4$. Take one of the $b-1$ grid lines between two consecutive rows of the box. Both sides contain cells of $C$ (the top and bottom rows of the box do). Going once around the cycle $C$ we cross this line each time we use a vertical step between those two rows; we return to the start, so the number of crossings is even, and it is positive, so it is at least $2$. The same holds for the $a-1$ lines between consecutive columns. Each of the $L$ steps of the cycle crosses exactly one such line, so $L\ge 2(b-1)+2(a-1)$.

(ii) The box can be filled. Call it legal to add to a set a cell with at least two neighbours in the set. If a rectangle $R$ is contained in the set and a cell $z$ just outside one side of $R$ is in the set, we can legally add the whole row (or column) of cells along that side: going from $z$ in both directions, each next cell has a neighbour in $R$ and a neighbour just added. Now list the cells of $C$ as $z_1,z_2,\dots,z_L$ around the cycle, and let $R_i$ be the bounding box of $z_1,\dots,z_i$. Each $z_{i+1}$ is adjacent to $z_i\in R_i$, so it is either in $R_i$ or just outside one side of it, and the previous remark lets us fill $R_{i+1}$. In the end the whole box $B_C=R_L$ is filled.

(iii) Contradiction. So $X=W\cup B_C$ is obtained from $W$ by legal additions. Every boundary edge of $X$ is a boundary edge of $W\setminus C$ or of $B_C$, and, as $C$ has no neighbours in $W\setminus C$, $\operatorname{per}(W)=\operatorname{per}(W\setminus C)+\operatorname{per}(C)$. With $\operatorname{per}(C)=2L$ and $\operatorname{per}(B_C)=2(a+b)$, and $L\ge2(a+b)-4>a+b$: \[ \operatorname{per}(X)\le \operatorname{per}(W)-2L+2(a+b)<\operatorname{per}(W)=4n . \] But reversing the painting adds the cells $z_{k-1},\dots,z_0$ legally to $W$; doing the same additions starting from $X\supseteq W$ (skipping cells already present) is still legal and fills the board. By the monovariant, $4n=\operatorname{per}(\text{board})\le\operatorname{per}(X)<4n$, a contradiction. So for odd $n$, $|W|\ge 2n+1$ and at most $n^2-2n-1$ cells are black.

Constructions. If $W$ satisfies the rule and the whole board can be obtained from $W$ by legal additions, then painting the added cells in reverse order is legal: each intermediate white set is obtained from $W$ by legal additions, and a legal addition never lowers any cell's number of neighbours, so every white cell keeps at least two white neighbours.

  • $n$ even: let $W$ consist of the $n/2$ squares $\{2i-1,2i\}\times\{2i-1,2i\}$ on the diagonal; each of its cells has two white neighbours. If the square $Q$ of rows and columns $1,\dots,2i$ is filled, the cell $(2i,2i+1)$ has the neighbours $(2i,2i)$ and $(2i+1,2i+1)$; with it added, the cells $(2i-1,2i+1),\dots,(1,2i+1)$ of column $2i+1$ can be added one by one (each has a neighbour in $Q$ and the previous one), and likewise the cells $(2i+1,2i),\dots,(2i+1,1)$ of row $2i+1$, starting next to $(2i+1,2i+1)$. Now rows and columns $1,\dots,2i+1$ are filled and the cells $(2i+1,2i+2)$, $(2i+2,2i+1)$, $(2i+2,2i+2)$ are white, so column $2i+2$ (upwards from $(2i,2i+2)$) and row $2i+2$ (leftwards from $(2i+2,2i)$) are filled in the same way. This fills rows and columns $1,\dots,2i+2$. So the board is filled, $|W|=2n$, and $n^2-2n$ cells are black.

  • $n$ odd, $n\ge3$: take $(n-3)/2$ such squares in rows and columns $1,\dots,n-3$, together with the $3\times3$ square in rows and columns $n-2,n-1,n$ without its corners $(n-2,n)$ and $(n,n-2)$ (these $7$ cells each have at least two of them as neighbours). The two missing corners can be added legally, which fills the $3\times 3$ square, and then the board is filled exactly as before. Here $|W|=2(n-3)+7=2n+1$.

Therefore the greatest number of black cells is \[ \boxed{\begin{cases} n^2-2n, & n \text{ even},\\ n^2-2n-1, & n\ge 3 \text{ odd},\\ 1, & n=1.\end{cases}} \]

Solution 2 (Counting edges, and cycles with nothing inside)

We read the rule as: after each painting, every white cell must have at least two white neighbours (cells sharing a side). For $n=1$ the single cell can be painted, so the answer is $1$; from now on $n\ge2$.

Edge count. Join two cells by an edge when they share a side; the board has $2n(n-1)$ edges. For a set $X$ of cells let $e(X)$ be the number of edges with both ends in $X$. Let $W_0\supset W_1\supset\dots\supset W_k=W$ be the white sets during a legal painting, $W_0$ the whole board, $W_t=W_{t+1}\cup\{z_t\}$. The cell $z_t$ has at least two neighbours in $W_t$ (for $t=0$ because $n\ge2$, for $t\ge1$ by the rule), and they lie in $W_{t+1}$; so $e(W_t)\ge e(W_{t+1})+2$. Also every cell of $W$ has at least two neighbours in $W$, so $e(W)=\frac12\sum_{z\in W}\deg_W(z)\ge|W|$. With $w=|W|$, \[ 2n(n-1)=e(W_0)\ge e(W)+2(n^2-w)\ge w+2n^2-2w,\qquad\text{so}\qquad w\ge 2n . \] Hence at most $n^2-2n$ cells are black.

Odd $n$: $w=2n$ is impossible. Suppose $w=2n$. Then all the inequalities above are equalities: every cell of $W$ has exactly two neighbours in $W$, and every $z_t$ has exactly two neighbours in $W_{t+1}$. Represent each cell by its centre, a lattice point, and each edge by the unit segment joining the centres. Each connected piece of $W$ is then a cycle, drawn as a simple closed lattice polygon $\Gamma$. Two different unit lattice segments meet at most at a common endpoint, so a segment joining two adjacent cells crosses $\Gamma$ only at a lattice point of $\Gamma$. Consequently a neighbour of a cell whose centre is strictly inside $\Gamma$ is itself strictly inside $\Gamma$ or on $\Gamma$.

(i) No cell centre lies strictly inside $\Gamma$. Let $I$ be the set of cells with centres strictly inside $\Gamma$, and suppose $I\ne\varnothing$.

  • If some cell of $I$ is not in $W$, let $z$ be the one among them restored last when the painting is reversed ($z=z_t$ with $t$ smallest). All four neighbours of $z$ are in $I$ or on $\Gamma$; those on $\Gamma$ and those in $I\cap W$ lie in $W$, and the others were restored earlier. So $z$ has four neighbours in $W_{t+1}$, not two, a contradiction.

  • If $I\subseteq W$, start at a cell of $I$ and walk to the right along its row. The walk eventually leaves the inside of $\Gamma$, so at some step it goes from a cell $y\in I$ to a cell $y'$ not in $I$, and $y'$ lies on $\Gamma$. Then $y'$ has three neighbours in $W$: its two neighbours on the cycle and $y\notin\Gamma$, a contradiction.

(ii) $\Gamma$ is a unit square. The region bounded by $\Gamma$ is a union of unit squares of the lattice. If it consisted of two or more of them, then, as its inside is connected and contains no lattice point by (i), two of these unit squares share a side $\sigma$. The points of $\sigma$ other than its endpoints are inside the region, so $\sigma$ is not a side of $\Gamma$; its endpoints are lattice points of the closed region, so by (i) they lie on $\Gamma$. Thus two cells of the cycle are adjacent through an edge that is not a cycle edge, and they have three neighbours in $W$, a contradiction. So $\Gamma$ is a single unit square, i.e. the piece is a $2\times2$ block of cells.

Hence $W$ consists of $2\times2$ blocks and $w$ is a multiple of $4$; but for odd $n$, $2n\equiv2\pmod4$. So $w\ge2n+1$, and at most $n^2-2n-1$ cells are black.

Constructions. Write $(r,c)$ for the cell in row $r$ and column $c$. Call it legal to add to a set of cells a cell with at least two neighbours in the set. Suppose $W$ satisfies the rule (every cell of $W$ has at least two neighbours in $W$) and the whole board can be obtained from $W$ by legal additions. Then painting the added cells in the reverse order is a legal painting that ends with white set $W$: each intermediate white set $Y$ is obtained from $W$ by legal additions, and adding cells never lowers a cell's number of neighbours in the set, so each cell of $Y$ has at least two neighbours in $Y$ (a cell of $W$ already has two in $W$; an added cell had two when it was added).

Filling the board. Let $Q_m$ be the square of rows and columns $1,\dots,m$. Suppose a set contains $Q_m$ and the cell $(m+1,m+1)$, with $m<n$. The cell $(m,m+1)$ has the neighbours $(m,m)$ and $(m+1,m+1)$, so it can be added. Going up the column, each of $(m-1,m+1),\dots,(1,m+1)$ has a neighbour in $Q_m$ and the neighbour just added, so it can be added. Going left along row $m+1$ from $(m+1,m+1)$, each of $(m+1,m),\dots,(m+1,1)$ has a neighbour in $Q_m$ and the neighbour just added. Now the set contains $Q_{m+1}$. (Cells already in the set are simply skipped.) Hence, if a set contains every diagonal cell $(i,i)$, $1\le i\le n$, then starting from $Q_1=\{(1,1)\}$ and repeating this step fills the whole board by legal additions.

  • $n$ even: let $W$ be the union of the $n/2$ blocks $\{2i-1,2i\}\times\{2i-1,2i\}$, $1\le i\le n/2$. Each cell of $W$ has two neighbours in its block, and $W$ contains the whole diagonal. So $W$ can be reached, $w=2n$, and $n^2-2n$ cells are black.

  • $n$ odd, $n\ge3$: let $W$ be the union of the $(n-3)/2$ blocks $\{2i-1,2i\}\times\{2i-1,2i\}$, $1\le i\le(n-3)/2$, and the $3\times3$ square of rows and columns $n-2,n-1,n$ without its corners $(n-2,n)$ and $(n,n-2)$. Each of these $7$ cells has at least two neighbours among them (the centre has four; each of the other six has two, for example $(n-2,n-1)$ has $(n-2,n-2)$ and $(n-1,n-1)$), and $W$ contains the whole diagonal. So $W$ can be reached, $w=4\cdot\frac{n-3}2+7=2n+1$, and $n^2-2n-1$ cells are black.

Therefore the greatest number of black cells is \[ \boxed{\begin{cases} n^2-2n, & n \text{ even},\\ n^2-2n-1, & n\ge 3 \text{ odd},\\ 1, & n=1.\end{cases}} \]
