Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Pythagorean Theorem

Let $ABCD$ be a rhombus and $E$ be a point on the diagonal $AC$. Let $F$ be the point on the side $BC$ such that $BF=DE$. Prove that \[(AB+BF)\cdot FC=AE\cdot EC.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw both diagonals and label their intersection $O$. Try placing $E$ on either side of $O$ and mark the segments in the proposed equality.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the perpendicular bisector of $BD$ to relate $DE$ to $BE$. Express $FC$ as a difference of lengths so the product on the left becomes a difference of squares. (LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework)
Hint 3: approach
Write $AE$ and $EC$ using $AO$ and $OE$, checking their order on both sides of $O$. Apply Pythagoras in $AOB$ and $EOB$ and look for the common squared leg when comparing the two differences.
Common trap
The formulas for $AE$ and $EC$ exchange roles when $E$ crosses the midpoint.
Show solution 1 – Pythagoras around the centre

Let $O$ be the point where the diagonals meet. In a rhombus the diagonals cut each other in half and are perpendicular, so $AO=OC$, $BO=OD$ and $\angle AOB=90^\circ$.

Step 1: $BF=BE$. The diagonal $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $BD$, and every point of a perpendicular bisector is equally far from the two ends. As $E$ lies on $AC$, $BE=DE$. Hence $BF=DE=BE$.

Step 2: the left side. All sides of a rhombus are equal, so $AB=BC$, and $F$ lies on side $BC$, so $FC=BC-BF$. Therefore \[(AB+BF)\cdot FC=(BC+BF)(BC-BF)=BC^2-BF^2=AB^2-BE^2 .\]

Step 3: the right side. Let $m=AO=OC$ and $e=OE$. If $E$ lies between $A$ and $O$, then $AE=m-e$ and $EC=m+e$; if it lies between $O$ and $C$, the two are swapped. In both cases \[AE\cdot EC=(m-e)(m+e)=m^2-e^2 .\] In the right triangles $AOB$ and $EOB$ (right angle at $O$), Pythagoras gives $m^2=AB^2-BO^2$ and $e^2=BE^2-BO^2$. So \[AE\cdot EC=(AB^2-BO^2)-(BE^2-BO^2)=AB^2-BE^2 .\] Steps 2 and 3 give the same value, so $(AB+BF)\cdot FC=AE\cdot EC$.

Show solution 2 – Two chords of one circle

Let $r=AB$. All sides of the rhombus equal $r$, so the circle $\omega$ with centre $B$ and radius $r$ passes through $A$ and $C$.

$BF=BE$. The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular and bisect each other, so $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $BD$. Points of $AC$ are equally far from $B$ and $D$; hence $BE=DE=BF$. Call this length $d$. If $E$ is $A$ or $C$, then $d=r$, $F=C$ and both sides of the claim are $0$; so assume $E$ lies strictly between $A$ and $C$, and then $d<r$ ($E$ is inside $\omega$).

Both sides equal $r^2-d^2$. If two chords $XY$ and $ZW$ of a circle cross at a point $T$, then $TX\cdot TY=TZ\cdot TW$. We use it for the chord $AC$ and the diameter of $\omega$ through $E$; for the other side of the claim a diameter alone suffices.

  • The line $BE$ meets $\omega$ at two points $X,Y$, with $X$ on ray $BE$ and $Y$ on the opposite ray. Then $EX=r-d$ and $EY=r+d$. The chord $AC$ also passes through $E$, so \[AE\cdot EC=EX\cdot EY=(r-d)(r+d)=r^2-d^2 .\]

  • The line $BC$ meets $\omega$ at $C$ and at the point $C'$ with $B$ the midpoint of the diameter $CC'$. The point $F$ lies on this diameter, with $FC=BC-BF=r-d$ and $FC'=FB+BC'=d+r=AB+BF$. So $(AB+BF)\cdot FC=(r+d)(r-d)=r^2-d^2$.

Therefore $(AB+BF)\cdot FC=r^2-d^2=AE\cdot EC$.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Difference of Squares and Related Forms

Prove that every integer $n$ can be written as $n=a^2+b^2-c^2$ where $a,b,c$ are integers.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try representing a few small integers, including a negative one. See how much you can simplify the search by choosing one of the two added squares first.
Hint 2: key idea
Expand $(k+1)^2-k^2$ for an arbitrary integer $k$. Determine which parity of integer this difference can represent. (LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Split into odd and even inputs and use a first square of either zero or one to leave an odd remainder. Match that remainder to the difference of consecutive squares and work out the index $k$ in each case.
Common trap
The square values are nonnegative, but the integer bases of the squares can be negative.
Show solution 1 – Two consecutive squares

The key fact is that consecutive squares differ by an odd number: $(k+1)^2-k^2=2k+1$ for every integer $k$.

  • If $n$ is odd, write $n=2k+1$ with $k$ an integer (positive, zero or negative). Then \[n=0^2+(k+1)^2-k^2 .\]

  • If $n$ is even, write $n=2k$. Then $n-1=2k-1=k^2-(k-1)^2$, so \[n=1^2+k^2-(k-1)^2 .\]

For example $7=0^2+4^2-3^2$, $10=1^2+5^2-4^2$ and $-6=1^2+(-3)^2-(-4)^2$. So every integer has the required form.

Show solution 2 – Which numbers are differences of two squares

Differences of two squares. If $n$ is odd, $n=2m+1$, then $n=(m+1)^2-m^2$. If $n$ is a multiple of $4$, $n=4m$, then \[(m+1)^2-(m-1)^2=(m^2+2m+1)-(m^2-2m+1)=4m=n .\] So every integer that is not of the form $4m+2$ is a difference $b^2-c^2$, and we may take $a=0$.

The remaining case $n=4m+2$. Then $n-1=4m+1$ is odd, so $n-1=(2m+1)^2-(2m)^2$, and \[n=1^2+(2m+1)^2-(2m)^2 .\] (Numbers of the form $4m+2$ really need the extra square: a square is $0$ or $1$ more than a multiple of $4$, so $b^2-c^2$ is never $2$ more than a multiple of $4$.) Hence every integer $n$ can be written as $a^2+b^2-c^2$.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

Lines are drawn inside a unit square partitioning it into squares, not necessarily of the same size. Choose one of the main diagonals of the unit square. A square is said to intersect the chosen main diagonal if it has at least one point in common with the diagonal. Is it possible to draw the lines so that the sum of the perimeters of the squares that intersect the chosen main diagonal exceeds $2026$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Divide the unit square into four equal squares and draw the chosen diagonal. Check each small square against the stated definition of intersection.
Hint 2: key idea
When quartering a square whose diagonal lies on the chosen line, distinguish the children that contain a diagonal segment from those that meet it only at a corner. Consider which children can be quartered in the same way again. (LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Investigate repeating the quartering on the children whose diagonal lies on the line while leaving the corner-touching children intact. In each round, compare the number of new corner-touching squares with their side length to find the perimeter retained from that round. Decide from this whether the total can be made as large as you like.
Common trap
Touching the chosen diagonal at only one corner still qualifies as an intersection.
Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – Repeated quartering along the diagonal

Yes. The trick is that a square “intersects” the diagonal even if it only touches it at one corner, and many such squares can be squeezed along the diagonal. The “lines” of the statement are segments drawn inside the square (lines running all the way across could only cut it into rectangles of a grid), so we may cut one square of the current partition at a time.

Let the chosen diagonal run from the bottom left corner to the top right corner (for the other diagonal, use the mirror image of the construction). Call a square of the partition a diagonal square if its own bottom left to top right diagonal lies on the chosen diagonal; the unit square itself is one.

One quartering. Cut a diagonal square of side $s$ into four squares of side $\frac s2$ by its two midlines. The bottom left and top right quarters are again diagonal squares. The other two quarters (top left and bottom right) have the centre of the old square as a corner, and this centre lies on the diagonal, so both of them intersect the diagonal. Their perimeters add up to $2\cdot4\cdot\frac s2=4s$.

The construction. Do $L$ rounds. In round $1$ quarter the unit square; in each later round quarter every diagonal square obtained in the previous round, and never cut the other squares again. In round $k$ there are $2^{k-1}$ diagonal squares of side $\frac1{2^{k-1}}$ to be quartered, so round $k$ creates off-diagonal squares with total perimeter \[2^{k-1}\cdot4\cdot\frac1{2^{k-1}}=4 .\] After round $L$ there remain $2^L$ diagonal squares of side $\frac1{2^L}$, with total perimeter $2^L\cdot\frac4{2^L}=4$. All the cuts are midlines of squares, so the unit square is partitioned into squares, and every square of this partition intersects the diagonal. The sum of their perimeters is \[\underbrace{4+4+\dots+4}_{L\text{ rounds}}+4=4L+4 .\] With $L=506$ this is $2028>2026$. So it is possible.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Strategic State Tracking

Alex and Bob take turns to select one number with no repetition allowed from $0$ to $14$, starting with Alex. Alex wins if he can form a $4$-term arithmetic progression among the numbers in his selection. Does Alex have a winning strategy?

Hint 1: understand the problem
List a few four-term progressions with different common differences inside the allowed range. Mark which entries are still free after a few example picks.
Hint 2: key idea
Start with the middle number and use reflection of the range to reduce the opponent's first replies. Aim for two progressions with three entries already held and two different free numbers needed to complete them. (LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy)
Hint 3: approach
After reflecting when needed, split the first reply by its position relative to the middle. For each case, look for a second pick that shares progressions with your first and leaves few replies that prevent an immediate double threat. In the remaining branches, look for a single threat that forces a particular reply before you try a double threat again.
Common trap
Always check that the missing entries of a threat are still unclaimed.
Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – Start in the middle, then build double threats

Yes, Alex can always win; in fact he wins with at most five picks.

Words we use. A threat is a moment when Alex holds three numbers of some $4$-term progression whose fourth number is still free: Bob must take that fourth number at once, otherwise Alex takes it and wins. A double threat is two threats with different missing numbers: Bob can block only one, and Alex completes the other on his next pick.

First pick: $7$. Let $b$ be Bob's first pick. Replacing every number $x$ by $14-x$ turns $4$-term progressions into $4$-term progressions and keeps $7$ fixed. So if $b<7$, Alex plays the mirror image (every number $x$ below replaced by $14-x$) of the strategy below. From now on $b\ge8$.

Case A: $b\in\{10,11,12,13,14\}$. Alex takes $5$ (he holds $5,7$). Let $c$ be Bob's next pick.

  • If $c\notin\{4,6,8\}$: Alex takes $6$. Now $4,5,6,7$ misses only $4$, and $5,6,7,8$ misses only $8$, both free: a double threat.

  • If $c\in\{4,6,8\}$: then $1,3,9$ are free. Alex takes $3$: now $1,3,5,7$ misses only $1$ and $3,5,7,9$ misses only $9$: a double threat.

Case B: $b=8$. Alex takes $5$ (he holds $5,7$). Let $c$ be Bob's next pick.

  • $c\notin\{1,3,9\}$: Alex takes $3$; double threat at $1$ ($1,3,5,7$) and $9$ ($3,5,7,9$).

  • $c=1$: Alex takes $9$; double threat at $3$ ($3,5,7,9$) and $11$ ($5,7,9,11$).

  • $c=3$: Alex takes $4$; threat at $6$ ($4,5,6,7$), so Bob must take $6$. Alex takes $10$; double threat at $1$ ($1,4,7,10$) and $13$ ($4,7,10,13$).

  • $c=9$: Alex takes $1$; threat at $3$ ($1,3,5,7$), so Bob must take $3$. Alex takes $4$; double threat at $6$ ($4,5,6,7$) and $10$ ($1,4,7,10$).

Case C: $b=9$. Alex takes $4$ (he holds $4,7$). Let $c$ be Bob's next pick.

  • $c\notin\{1,10,13\}$: Alex takes $10$; double threat at $1$ ($1,4,7,10$) and $13$ ($4,7,10,13$).

  • $c=1$: Alex takes $6$; threat at $5$ ($4,5,6,7$), so Bob takes $5$. Alex takes $2$; double threat at $0$ ($0,2,4,6$) and $8$ ($2,4,6,8$).

  • $c=10$: Alex takes $5$; threat at $6$ ($4,5,6,7$), so Bob takes $6$. Alex takes $3$; double threat at $2$ ($2,3,4,5$) and $1$ ($1,3,5,7$).

  • $c=13$: Alex takes $1$; threat at $10$ ($1,4,7,10$), so Bob takes $10$. Alex takes $3$; double threat at $2$ ($1,2,3,4$) and $5$ ($1,3,5,7$).

In every line, the numbers needed by the threats are still free when Alex creates them (Bob's picks so far are listed in the case), so Alex completes a $4$-term arithmetic progression by his fifth pick. Hence Alex has a winning strategy.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 51: Counterexamples, Contradiction, and Infinite Descent · Infinite Descent · also Singapore Senior MO 2026, Problem 3

Determine all integers $a,b,c$ satisfying \[a^2+b^2+c^2=13(ab+bc+ca).\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Expand the square of $a+b+c$ and compare its terms with the given equation. Look for a way to replace the sum of squares.
Hint 2: key idea
Write the resulting equation in terms of the sum and the sum of pairwise products. Work modulo $5$ and determine what the equation forces about both quantities. (LPS VI, Chapter 51: Counterexamples, Contradiction, and Infinite Descent)
Hint 3: approach
Eliminate $c$ from the pairwise-product congruence and complete a square in $a,b$ modulo $5$. Compare the possible square residues to test whether any of the variables can escape divisibility by $5$. Then check what happens to the original equation when all three variables are divided by the same factor.
Common trap
Dividing the variables by a common factor preserves this equation because both sides have degree two.
Show the answer

only $a=b=c=0$

Show solution 1 – Divisibility by $5$ and infinite descent

Clearly $a=b=c=0$ is a solution. We show there is no other.

Step 1: a square. Let $s=a+b+c$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. Since $s^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2q$, the equation gives \[s^2=13q+2q=15q .\]

Step 2: $5$ divides $s$ and $q$. $s^2=15q$ is a multiple of $5$; since $5$ is prime, $s$ is a multiple of $5$. Then $s^2$ is a multiple of $25$, so $15q$ is, and $q$ is a multiple of $5$.

Step 3: $5$ divides $a,b,c$. Work with remainders modulo $5$. From $s\equiv0$ we get $c\equiv-a-b$, so \[q=ab+c(a+b)\equiv ab-(a+b)^2=-(a^2+ab+b^2)\pmod 5 .\] Thus $a^2+ab+b^2\equiv0$, and multiplying by $4$: $(2a+b)^2+3b^2\equiv0$, i.e. $(2a+b)^2\equiv2b^2\pmod5$. A square leaves remainder $0$, $1$ or $4$ when divided by $5$ (check $0^2,1^2,2^2,3^2,4^2$). If $b$ is not a multiple of $5$, then $b^2$ leaves remainder $1$ or $4$, so $2b^2$ leaves remainder $2$ or $3$, which no square does. Hence $5\mid b$; then $(2a)^2\equiv0$, so $5\mid a$; and $c\equiv-a-b\equiv0$.

Step 4: descent. Write $a=5a_1$, $b=5b_1$, $c=5c_1$. Dividing the equation by $25$ shows that $(a_1,b_1,c_1)$ is again a solution. By Step 3, $a_1,b_1,c_1$ are multiples of $5$ too, and so on: $a,b,c$ are divisible by $5,25,125,\dots$, by every power of $5$. The only integer divisible by every power of $5$ is $0$. So the only solution is $\boxed{a=b=c=0}$.

Show solution 2 – The differences $a-b$ and $b-c$

Clearly $a=b=c=0$ is a solution; suppose there is another one. Put $x=a-b$, $y=b-c$, so that $c-a=-(x+y)$, and $s=a+b+c$.

An equation for $x,y,s$. With $q=ab+bc+ca$ the equation says $a^2+b^2+c^2=13q$. Expanding, \[s^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2q=15q,\qquad x^2+y^2+(x+y)^2=2(a^2+b^2+c^2)-2q=24q .\] Since $x^2+y^2+(x+y)^2=2(x^2+xy+y^2)$, we get $x^2+xy+y^2=12q$. Comparing with $s^2=15q$ (multiply the first by $5$ and the second by $4$): \[5(x^2+xy+y^2)=4s^2 .\qquad(\ast)\]

A fact about $5$. If $x^2+xy+y^2$ is a multiple of $5$, then so are $x$ and $y$. Indeed $4(x^2+xy+y^2)=(2x+y)^2+3y^2$, so $(2x+y)^2$ leaves the same remainder as $-3y^2$, i.e. as $2y^2$, on division by $5$. Squares leave remainders $0,1,4$, while $2y^2$ leaves $2$ or $3$ unless $5\mid y$. So $5\mid y$, then $5\mid 2x$ and $5\mid x$.

Descent. If $(x,y,s)\ne(0,0,0)$ solves $(\ast)$, then $5\mid4s^2$, so $5\mid s$, $s=5s_1$; then $x^2+xy+y^2=20s_1^2$ is a multiple of $5$, so $x=5x_1$, $y=5y_1$. Putting these into $(\ast)$ and dividing by $25$ gives $5(x_1^2+x_1y_1+y_1^2)=4s_1^2$: a smaller solution of the same equation, not all zero. Repeating this forever is impossible (the numbers $|x|+|y|+|s|$ would decrease forever while staying positive whole numbers). Hence $x=y=s=0$.

Then $a=b=c$ and $3a=s=0$, so $\boxed{a=b=c=0}$ is the only solution.
