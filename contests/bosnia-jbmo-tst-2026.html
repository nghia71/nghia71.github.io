Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Elementary Symmetric Substitution

Let $x$ and $y$ be real numbers such that $x^2+xy+y^2=1$. Determine the minimum and maximum value of the expression \[W=x^3y+xy^3.\]

Show the answer

minimum $-2$, maximum $\frac29$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The product xy as the only variable)

Put $p=xy$. The condition gives $x^2+y^2=1-p$, so \[W=xy\,(x^2+y^2)=p(1-p).\] Range of $p$. From $x^2+y^2\ge 2xy$ we get $1-p\ge2p$, i.e. $p\le\frac13$; from $x^2+y^2\ge-2xy$ we get $1-p\ge-2p$, i.e. $p\ge-1$.

The function $p\mapsto p-p^2$ is increasing for $p\le\frac12$, hence on $[-1,\frac13]$. Therefore \[-2=(-1)\cdot2\ \le\ W\ \le\ \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac29 .\] Both bounds are attained: $x=1$, $y=-1$ satisfies $1-1+1=1$ and gives $W=-1-1=-2$; $x=y=\frac1{\sqrt3}$ satisfies $\frac13+\frac13+\frac13=1$ and gives $W=2\cdot\frac19=\frac29$.

The minimum is $\boxed{-2}$ and the maximum is $\boxed{\tfrac29}$.

Solution 2 (Sum and difference)

Put $u=x+y$ and $v=x-y$. Then $xy=\frac{u^2-v^2}4$, $x^2+y^2=\frac{u^2+v^2}2$, and the condition becomes \[x^2+xy+y^2=\frac{3u^2+v^2}{4}=1,\qquad\text{so}\qquad v^2=4-3u^2,\quad 0\le u^2\le\tfrac43 .\] Every $s=u^2\in[0,\frac43]$ occurs (take $u=\sqrt s$, $v=\sqrt{4-3s}$). Now \[W=xy(x^2+y^2)=\frac{u^4-v^4}{8}=\frac{s^2-(4-3s)^2}{8}=-s^2+3s-2=-(s-1)(s-2).\] This quadratic in $s$ has its vertex at $s=\frac32$, so it is increasing on $[0,\frac43]$. Its minimum is $W(0)=-2$ (at $u=0$, $v=\pm2$, e.g. $x=1$, $y=-1$) and its maximum is $W(\frac43)=-\frac13\cdot(-\frac23)=\frac29$ (at $v=0$, $x=y=\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}$).

The minimum is $\boxed{-2}$ and the maximum is $\boxed{\tfrac29}$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Divisibility Chains and Factor Relations

Let \[I=(2^2+1)\cdot(6^2+1)\cdot(10^2+1)\cdots(2022^2+1)\cdot(2026^2+1).\] If $p$ is a prime number such that $p^2\mid I$, prove that $p<1013$.

Show the solution

Solution (One factor or two factors)

The factors of $I$ are $n^2+1$ for $n\in S=\{2,6,10,\dots,2026\}$, the numbers $n\equiv2\pmod 4$ with $2\le n\le2026$. Each factor is odd, so $p$ is odd. Since $p^2\mid I$, one of two cases occurs.

Case 1: $p$ divides two different factors $n_1^2+1$ and $n_2^2+1$ with $n_1>n_2$ in $S$. Then $p$ divides their difference $(n_1-n_2)(n_1+n_2)$. Both $n_1-n_2$ and $n_1+n_2$ are multiples of $4$, and \[\frac{n_1-n_2}{4}\le\frac{2024}4=506,\qquad \frac{n_1+n_2}{4}\le\frac{2026+2022}{4}=1012 .\] As $p$ is odd, it divides one of these two positive quotients, so $p\le1012$.

Case 2: $p$ divides only one factor. Then $p^2\mid n^2+1$ for one $n\in S$; write $n^2+1=kp^2$, where $k$ is odd because $n^2+1$ is odd. Suppose $p\ge1013$. Then \[k=\frac{n^2+1}{p^2}\le\frac{2026^2+1}{1013^2}=4+\frac1{1013^2},\] so $k\in\{1,3\}$. If $k=1$, then $p^2-n^2=1$, impossible for positive integers ($p^2-n^2=(p-n)(p+n)$ with $p+n\ge2$). If $k=3$, then $n^2+1=3p^2$ gives $n^2\equiv2\pmod3$, impossible since squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$. Hence $p<1013$.

In both cases $p<1013$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Power of a Point

In triangle $ABC$, where $AB<AC$, the angle bisector of $\angle A$ intersects side $BC$ at point $L$, and the circumcircle $\Omega$ at point $W$. On the circumcircle of triangle $WLC$, a point $P\ne W$ is chosen such that $BP=CP$. The tangent to $\Omega$ at $A$ intersects $BC$ at point $T$. On line $BW$, a point $Q\ne B$ is chosen such that $TB=TQ$. Prove that the points $Q$, $A$, $P$, $W$ lie on the same circle.

Show the solution

Solution (Powers of B with respect to two circles)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$, and write $\angle A,\angle B,\angle C$ for the angles of the triangle. Since $AB<AC$, the point $L$ lies between $B$ and $M$ ($LB=\frac{ac}{b+c}<\frac a2$), and the tangent at $A$ meets line $BC$ at a point $T$ beyond $B$ (on ray $CB$). Indeed, the tangent is not parallel to $BC$, since otherwise $A$ would be the midpoint of an arc $BC$ and $AB=AC$. By the tangent–chord angle, $\angle TAB=\angle TCA$, so triangles $TAB$ and $TCA$ (common angle at $T$) are similar and $\frac{TB}{TA}=\frac{TA}{TC}=\frac{AB}{AC}$; hence $\frac{TB}{TC}=\bigl(\frac{AB}{AC}\bigr)^2<1$. As $T$ lies on the tangent, it is outside $\Omega$, hence not on the segment $BC$; with $TB<TC$ it lies beyond $B$. The point $W$ is the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$, so $WB=WC$ and the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ is the line $WM$. We use $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for directed angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$, and signed powers of points.

Step 1: $Q$ lies on the circle $CWT$. Here $Q\ne B$ (for $Q=B$ the claim is false: the circle through $B,A,W$ is $\Omega$, and $P$ is not on $\Omega$, because the different circles $\Omega$ and $WLC$ already share the two points $W$ and $C$, and $P\ne W$, $P\ne C$). Since $\angle WBC=\angle WAC=\frac{\angle A}2<90^\circ$, line $BW$ is not perpendicular to $TB$, so it meets the circle with centre $T$ and radius $TB$ at a second point; $Q$ is this point, the reflection of $B$ in the foot of the perpendicular from $T$ to $BW$. Also $Q\ne W$: as $WM\perp BC$ and $B$ lies between $T$ and $M$, we have $TW>TM>TB$. Triangle $TBQ$ is isosceles with $TB=TQ$, and triangle $WBC$ is isosceles with $WB=WC$, so (base angles, directed) \[\measuredangle(QT,QW)=\measuredangle(QT,QB)=\measuredangle(BQ,BT)=\measuredangle(BW,BC)=\measuredangle(CB,CW)=\measuredangle(CT,CW).\] Hence $Q,W,C,T$ are concyclic. The point $B$ lies between $C$ and $T$, so it is inside this circle; thus $B$ lies between $W$ and $Q$, and \[BW\cdot BQ=BC\cdot BT. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $2\,BT\cdot ML=LB^2$. By the tangent–chord angle, $\angle TAB=\angle C$, so $\angle TAL=\angle C+\frac{\angle A}2$; also $\angle TLA=\angle ALB=180^\circ-\angle B-\frac{\angle A}{2}=\angle C+\frac{\angle A}{2}$. So $TA=TL$. With $t=BT$ and $\ell=LB$, the power of $T$ with respect to $\Omega$ gives $t(t+a)=TA^2=TL^2=(t+\ell)^2$, so $t(a-2\ell)=\ell^2$. Since $a-2\ell=2\left(\frac a2-\ell\right)=2\,ML$, \[2\,BT\cdot ML=LB^2. \tag{2}\]

Step 3: the power of $B$ with respect to $\omega=(AWP)$. The point $P$ is the second intersection of line $WM$ with the circle $WLC$. The point $M$ lies inside that circle (between $L$ and $C$ on a chord), so $M$ lies between $W$ and $P$ and $MW\cdot MP=ML\cdot MC$. The points $A,W,P$ are not collinear ($A$ is not on line $WM$ because $AB\ne AC$), so they define a circle $\omega$.

For a point $X$, put $f(X)=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(X)-\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(X)$. If the circles have centres $O_1,O_2$ and radii $r_1,r_2$, then $f(X)=|X-O_1|^2-r_1^2-|X-O_2|^2+r_2^2=2X\cdot(O_2-O_1)+\text{const}$, so $f$ is a linear function of the position of $X$; in particular it is linear along line $BC$. We evaluate it at three points of $BC$:

  • $f(L)=0$: $\omega$ and $\Omega$ both pass through $A$ and $W$, and $L$ lies on line $AW$, so both powers equal $\overline{LA}\cdot\overline{LW}$.

  • $f(M)=-MW\cdot MP+MB\cdot MC=-ML\cdot MC+MC^2=MC\,(MC-ML)=\frac a2\cdot LB$, since $M$ is inside both circles.

  • Hence, by linearity, with $B$ on the other side of $L$ from $M$ at distance $LB$ from $L$, \[f(B)=-\frac{LB}{ML}\,f(M)=-\frac a2\cdot\frac{LB^2}{ML}=-a\cdot BT\] by (2).

As $B$ lies on $\Omega$, $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(B)=f(B)=-BC\cdot BT<0$. So $B$ is inside $\omega$, line $WB$ meets $\omega$ again at a point $Q^*$ with $B$ between $W$ and $Q^*$, and \[BW\cdot BQ^*=BC\cdot BT .\]

Step 4: conclusion. Comparing with (1), $BQ^*=BQ$, and $Q$, $Q^*$ both lie on the ray from $B$ opposite to $W$. Hence $Q=Q^*\in\omega$, i.e. $Q$, $A$, $P$, $W$ lie on one circle.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting

A board of dimensions $2026\times 2026$ is divided into unit squares. Then the numbers $1,2,\dots,2026^2$ are written into it such that each number is located in exactly one square and every two consecutive numbers are located in adjacent squares (squares are adjacent if they share a side). Prove that on the main diagonal (from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner of the board) there are two numbers whose difference is equal to $2$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting links to the cells next to the diagonal)

Let $N=2026$. Colour the board like a chessboard. The diagonal cells all have the same colour, and consecutive numbers lie in adjacent cells, which have different colours; hence all numbers on the diagonal have the same parity. As $1$ is odd and $N^2$ is even, at most one of $1$ and $N^2$ is on the diagonal.

Call a cell near-diagonal if it is directly to the right of or directly below a diagonal cell, i.e. in position $(i,i+1)$ or $(i+1,i)$. There are $2(N-1)=4050$ such cells. Every side-neighbour of a diagonal cell is near-diagonal, and every near-diagonal cell is adjacent to exactly two diagonal cells.

Suppose that no two diagonal numbers differ by $2$. Call a pair $(k,j)$ a link if $k$ is on the diagonal and $j=k\pm1$ with $1\le j\le N^2$.

  • Each diagonal number $k$ has two links, except that $k=1$ or $k=N^2$ has only one, and at most one of these is on the diagonal. So there are at least $2N-1=4051$ links.

  • For a link $(k,j)$, the cell of $j$ is adjacent to the cell of $k$, so it is near-diagonal.

  • A number $j$ belongs to at most one link: two links $(k,j)$, $(k',j)$ would need $\{k,k'\}=\{j-1,j+1\}$, two diagonal numbers differing by $2$.

So the links give at least $4051$ different numbers $j$ in different near-diagonal cells, but there are only $4050$ such cells. This contradiction shows that two numbers on the diagonal differ by $2$.
