Problem 1

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Transformation Chains in Geometry

Let $P_1$, $P_2$ and $P_3$ be distinct points on a circle $\Omega$. For all positive integers $1\le i\le23$, define $P_{i+3}$ as the reflection of $P_i$ over line $P_{i+1}P_{i+2}$. Prove that $P_{20}P_{26}$ is tangent to $\Omega$.

Show solution 1 – A glide reflection whose axis is parallel to the tangent at $P_2$

Lemma ($P_{k+3}=G(P_k)$ for all $k$). Write $s_a,s_b,s_c$ for the reflections in the lines $P_2P_3$, $P_3P_1$, $P_1P_2$, and $g_k$ for the reflection in $P_{k+1}P_{k+2}$, so $P_{k+3}=g_k(P_k)$. Since $g_1$ fixes $P_3$ and maps $P_1$ to $P_4$, the line $P_3P_4$ is $g_1(P_3P_1)$, hence $g_2=g_1s_bg_1$ and $g_2g_1=g_1s_b$. Next $g_2g_1$ maps $P_1\mapsto P_4$ (as $g_2$ fixes $P_4$) and $P_2\mapsto P_5$ (as $g_1$ fixes $P_2$), so the line $P_4P_5$ is $(g_2g_1)(P_1P_2)$ and $g_3=(g_2g_1)s_c(g_2g_1)^{-1}$. Therefore \[G:=g_3g_2g_1=(g_2g_1)s_c=g_1s_bs_c=s_as_bs_c .\] $G$ maps $P_1\mapsto P_4$, $P_2\mapsto P_5$, $P_3\mapsto P_6$ (each $g_i$ fixes the points on its mirror). The rule defining the sequence commutes with isometries, so by induction $P_{k+6}=\text{refl}_{P_{k+4}P_{k+5}}(P_{k+3})=\text{refl}_{G(P_{k+1})G(P_{k+2})}(G(P_k))=G(P_{k+3})$, given $P_{j+3}=G(P_j)$ for $j\le k+2$. Hence $P_{k+3}=G(P_k)$ for all $k\ge1$, and $P_{k+6}=G^2(P_k)$. (All triangles $P_kP_{k+1}P_{k+2}$ are congruent to $P_1P_2P_3$, so the lines are always defined.)

The glide. $G=s_as_bs_c$ is a product of three reflections, so it reverses orientation: it is a reflection or a glide reflection. It is not a reflection $s_\ell$: otherwise $s_as_b=s_\ell s_c$, but the left side is a rotation about $P_3=P_2P_3\cap P_3P_1$ by a nonzero angle, while the right side is a rotation about a point of line $P_1P_2$ or a translation, and $P_3\notin P_1P_2$. So $G$ is a glide reflection with a nonzero glide vector $v$ along its axis, and $G^2$ is the translation by $w=2v\ne0$.

Direction of the axis. The linear part of a reflection in a line of direction angle $\theta$ is the reflection $\rho_\theta$, and $\rho_\alpha\rho_\beta\rho_\gamma=\rho_{\alpha-\beta+\gamma}$ (as $\rho_\alpha\rho_\beta$ is the rotation by $2(\alpha-\beta)$). Put $P_1,P_2,P_3$ at angles $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3$ on $\Omega$; the chord between angles $x$ and $y$ has direction $\frac{x+y}2+90^\circ$. So the axis of $G$ has direction \[\Bigl(\tfrac{\alpha_2+\alpha_3}2+90^\circ\Bigr)-\Bigl(\tfrac{\alpha_3+\alpha_1}2+90^\circ\Bigr)+\Bigl(\tfrac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2}2+90^\circ\Bigr)=\alpha_2+90^\circ\pmod{180^\circ},\] the direction of the tangent to $\Omega$ at $P_2$.

Conclusion. $P_{2+6k}=P_2+kw$ with $w\ne0$ parallel to the tangent $t$ at $P_2$. So $P_{20}=P_2+3w$ and $P_{26}=P_2+4w$ are two distinct points of $t$, and the line $P_{20}P_{26}$ is $t$, tangent to $\Omega$.

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers for the glide

Lemma ($P_{k+3}=G(P_k)$ for all $k$). Write $s_a,s_b,s_c$ for the reflections in the lines $P_2P_3$, $P_3P_1$, $P_1P_2$, and $g_k$ for the reflection in $P_{k+1}P_{k+2}$, so $P_{k+3}=g_k(P_k)$. Since $g_1$ fixes $P_3$ and maps $P_1$ to $P_4$, the line $P_3P_4$ is $g_1(P_3P_1)$, hence $g_2=g_1s_bg_1$ and $g_2g_1=g_1s_b$. Next $g_2g_1$ maps $P_1\mapsto P_4$ (as $g_2$ fixes $P_4$) and $P_2\mapsto P_5$ (as $g_1$ fixes $P_2$), so the line $P_4P_5$ is $(g_2g_1)(P_1P_2)$ and $g_3=(g_2g_1)s_c(g_2g_1)^{-1}$. Therefore \[G:=g_3g_2g_1=(g_2g_1)s_c=g_1s_bs_c=s_as_bs_c .\] $G$ maps $P_1\mapsto P_4$, $P_2\mapsto P_5$, $P_3\mapsto P_6$ (each $g_i$ fixes the points on its mirror). The rule defining the sequence commutes with isometries, so by induction $P_{k+6}=\text{refl}_{P_{k+4}P_{k+5}}(P_{k+3})=\text{refl}_{G(P_{k+1})G(P_{k+2})}(G(P_k))=G(P_{k+3})$, given $P_{j+3}=G(P_j)$ for $j\le k+2$. Hence $P_{k+3}=G(P_k)$ for all $k\ge1$, and $P_{k+6}=G^2(P_k)$. (All triangles $P_kP_{k+1}P_{k+2}$ are congruent to $P_1P_2P_3$, so the lines are always defined.)

Computing $G$. Let $\Omega$ be the unit circle and $P_1,P_2,P_3=a,b,c$. The reflection in the chord through $u,v$ is $z\mapsto u+v-uv\bar z$. Hence \[s_c(z)=a+b-ab\bar z,\qquad s_bs_c(z)=c+a-ca\,\overline{s_c(z)}=a-\frac{ca}{b}+\frac{c}{b}z,\] \[G(z)=s_a\bigl(s_bs_c(z)\bigr)=b+c-bc\Bigl(\frac1a-\frac{b}{ca}+\frac bc\bar z\Bigr)=-b^2\bar z+\mu,\qquad \mu=b+c+\frac{b(b-c)}{a}.\] Then $G^2(z)=-b^2\,\overline{(-b^2\bar z+\mu)}+\mu=z+w$ with $w=\mu-b^2\bar\mu$.

Direction. $\dfrac wb=\dfrac\mu b-b\bar\mu=\dfrac\mu b-\overline{\Bigl(\dfrac\mu b\Bigr)}=2i\,\mathrm{Im}\dfrac\mu b$ is purely imaginary, so $w$ is a real multiple of $ib$, i.e. parallel to the tangent $t$ at $b=P_2$.

$w\ne0$. The map $G(z)=-b^2\bar z+\mu$ is an orientation-reversing isometry, i.e. a reflection or a glide reflection, and $G^2$ is the translation by $w$. If $w=0$, then $G^2=\mathrm{id}$ and $G$ is a reflection $s_\ell$. Then $s_as_b=Gs_c=s_\ell s_c$; the left side is a rotation about $c$ by a nonzero angle, the right side is a rotation about a point of the line $ab$ or a translation, and $c$ is not on line $ab$. This contradiction shows $w\ne0$.

Conclusion. $P_{2+6k}=G^{2k}(P_2)=b+kw$, so $P_{20}=b+3w$ and $P_{26}=b+4w$ are distinct points of the tangent $t=\{b+\lambda ib:\lambda\in\mathbb R\}$, and $P_{20}P_{26}=t$ is tangent to $\Omega$.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Algebraic Interaction

An $n\times n$ Latin square is an $n\times n$ grid of cells where each cell contains exactly one positive integer between $1$ and $n$, each row contains the numbers $1$ to $n$ in some order, and each column contains the numbers $1$ to $n$ in some order. Call four numbers stable if there are either exactly two or exactly four distinct values among them. For which positive integers $n$ is it possible to construct an $n\times n$ Latin square such that every quadruple of four distinct cells forming the corners of a grid rectangle has entries which are stable?

Show the answer

exactly the powers of $2$: $n=2^k$, $k\ge0$

Show the solution – Row quotients form a group of involutions

Answer: $n$ must be a power of $2$ ($n=1$ is included: there is no rectangle, so the condition holds vacuously).

Construction. For $n=2^k$ label rows and columns by $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ and put $L(i,j)=1+(i\oplus j)$ (bitwise XOR). For fixed $i$ the map $j\mapsto i\oplus j$ is a bijection of $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$, and similarly for fixed $j$, so this is a Latin square with entries $1,\dots,n$. For rows $i_1\ne i_2$ and columns $j_1\ne j_2$ the entries in the same row or column differ, and \[L(i_1,j_1)=L(i_2,j_2)\iff i_1\oplus i_2=j_1\oplus j_2\iff L(i_1,j_2)=L(i_2,j_1) .\] So either both diagonals have equal entries (two values) or the four entries are distinct.

Necessity. Let the square be good. Four corners can never show exactly one value or three values with a repeat in a row or column, so “stable” means: for rows $r\ne r'$ and columns $c\ne c'$, $L(r,c)=L(r',c')\iff L(r,c')=L(r',c)$. For rows $r,r'$ let $\pi_{r,r'}$ be the permutation of values with $\pi(L(r,c))=L(r',c)$ for every column $c$. With $x=L(r,c)$, $y=L(r,c')$ the condition reads $\pi(y)=x\iff\pi(x)=y$, for all $x\ne y$. Hence $\pi_{r,r'}$ is an involution, and it has no fixed point (columns are Latin).

Let $\sigma_r=\pi_{1,r}$, so $L(r,c)=\sigma_r(L(1,c))$ and $\sigma_r\sigma_{r'}^{-1}=\pi_{r',r}$. Each $\sigma_r$ $(r\ge2)$ is an involution and $\sigma_1=\mathrm{id}$. For $r\ne r'$, $\sigma_r\sigma_{r'}=\sigma_r\sigma_{r'}^{-1}$ is an involution, so $\sigma_r\sigma_{r'}=(\sigma_r\sigma_{r'})^{-1}=\sigma_{r'}\sigma_r$. Thus the group $H$ generated by $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$ is abelian and every element has order at most $2$.

$H$ acts transitively on the values: by the columns being Latin, $\{\sigma_r(x)\}_{r}$ is the set of all values for each $x$. An abelian transitive group acts freely: if $h(x)=x$, then for $y=g(x)$, $h(y)=hg(x)=gh(x)=y$, so $h=\mathrm{id}$. Hence $|H|=n$ (orbit-stabiliser). Finally an abelian group in which every element has order dividing $2$ has order a power of $2$: it is a vector space over $\mathbb F_2$ (or: choose generators one at a time; each new generator outside the current subgroup doubles its size). So $n=|H|$ is a power of $2$.

Problem 3

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Deep Structural Classification Problems

Determine all integers $n\ge2$ such that there exists an integer polynomial $P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ of degree at most $0.9n$ such that the range of $P$ modulo $n$ is the same as the set of zeros of $P$ modulo $n$.

The range of $P$ modulo $n$ is the set of all integers $0\le y\le n-1$ such that there exists a positive integer $x$ where $P(x)-y$ is a multiple of $n$.

The set of zeros of $P$ modulo $n$ is the set of all integers $0\le x\le n-1$ such that $P(x)$ is a multiple of $n$.

Show the answer

all $n\ge2$ with $n\not\equiv2\pmod 4$

Show the solution – Chinese remainder theorem and prime-power constructions

Answer: exactly the $n\ge2$ with $n\not\equiv2\pmod4$. For a modulus $q$ write $R_q(P)$ and $Z_q(P)$ for the range and the zero set of $P$ modulo $q$.

Splitting. If $n=q_1q_2$ with $\gcd(q_1,q_2)=1$, then $P(x)\bmod q_i$ depends only on $x\bmod q_i$, and $x\bmod q_1$, $x\bmod q_2$ vary independently. Hence, under the Chinese remainder bijection $\mathbb Z_n\cong\mathbb Z_{q_1}\times\mathbb Z_{q_2}$, \[R_n(P)=R_{q_1}(P)\times R_{q_2}(P),\qquad Z_n(P)=Z_{q_1}(P)\times Z_{q_2}(P).\]

$n\equiv2\pmod4$ fails. Let $n=2q$, $q$ odd, and suppose $R_n=Z_n$. The range is nonempty, so all four factors are nonempty and $R_2(P)=Z_2(P)$. Let $f(x)=P(x)\bmod2$ on $\{0,1\}$. Some value is a zero, so $0\in R_2=Z_2$, i.e. $f(0)=0$. If $f(1)=0$ then $R_2=\{0\}\ne\{0,1\}=Z_2$; if $f(1)=1$ then $R_2=\{0,1\}\ne\{0\}=Z_2$. Contradiction.

Constructions for prime powers $q=p^k\ne2$. Throughout, $x$ runs over residues mod $q$.

  • $k=2j$ even: $P(x)=p^jx$. Range: the multiples of $p^j$. Zeros: $p^{2j}\mid p^jx\iff p^j\mid x$. Equal; degree $1$.

  • $k=1$, $p$ odd: choose $c$ with $-2c$ a quadratic non-residue and let $P(x)=c\,x\,(x^{(p-1)/2}-1)$. Zeros: $0$ and the non-zero squares (Euler's criterion). For a non-residue $x$, $P(x)=-2cx$, a product of two non-residues, hence a non-zero square, and as $x$ runs over the non-residues $-2cx$ runs over all non-zero squares. So range $=\{0\}\cup\{\text{squares}\}=$ zeros. Degree $\frac{p+1}2\le0.9p$ (as $p\ge3$).

  • $k=2j+1\ge3$ (any prime $p$, including $p=2$): let $N=p^j(p-1)$ and $P(x)=p^j\,x\,(x^N-1+p)$, of degree $N+1\le p^{j+1}\le\frac12p^{2j+1}<0.9p^{2j+1}$ (as $p^j\ge2$). Note also $N\ge p^j\ge2^j\ge j+1$. If $p\nmid x$, then $x^N\equiv1\pmod{p^{j+1}}$ (Euler, $N=\varphi(p^{j+1})$), so $P(x)\equiv p^jx\cdot p=p^{j+1}x\pmod{p^{2j+1}}$; these are exactly the elements $p^{j+1}u$ with $u$ a unit. If $x=p^tu$ with $1\le t$ and $u$ a unit, then $tN\ge N\ge j+1$, so the term $p^{j+t}u\cdot x^N$ vanishes modulo $p^{2j+1}$ and $P(x)\equiv p^{j+t}u(p-1)\pmod{p^{2j+1}}$: this is $0$ iff $t\ge j+1$, and for $1\le t\le j$ it runs over all $p^{j+t}\cdot$unit. Hence both the range and the zero set equal the set of multiples of $p^{j+1}$.

In every case the degree is at most $0.9q$ (for $q=3$: degree $2\le2.7$).

All $n\not\equiv2\pmod 4$ work. Write $n=\prod q_i$ with pairwise coprime prime powers $q_i$; no $q_i$ equals $2$. Take the polynomials $P_i$ above and, coefficient by coefficient, an integer polynomial $P$ with $P\equiv P_i\pmod{q_i}$ for all $i$ (Chinese remainder theorem). Then $\deg P\le\max_i\deg P_i\le0.9\max_i q_i\le0.9n$, and by the splitting $R_n(P)=\prod R_{q_i}(P_i)=\prod Z_{q_i}(P_i)=Z_n(P)$.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

For an integer $n$, let $\tau(n)$ denote the number of positive integer divisors of $n$. For each positive integer $k$, let $S_k=\{\tau(k),\tau(2k),\tau(3k),\dots\}$. If $S_a=S_b$ for two positive integers $a$ and $b$, prove that $\tau(a^n)=\tau(b^n)$ for all positive integers $n\ge1$.

Two sets are equal if and only if every number which appears in one set also appears in the other set.

Show the solution – Reading the exponents off $S_a$ with large primes

Description of $S_a$. Let $a=\prod_{i=1}^rp_i^{e_i}$ and $u_i=e_i+1\ge2$, ordered so that $u_1\le\dots\le u_r$. For $m=\prod p_i^{g_i}\cdot m'$ with $\gcd(m',a)=1$ we have $\tau(am)=\prod_i(u_i+g_i)\cdot\tau(m')$, and $\tau(m')$ takes every positive value (e.g. $m'=q^{t-1}$ for a prime $q\nmid a$). Absorbing the factor $\tau(m')$ into one of the $u_i+g_i$: \[S_a=\{\,c_1c_2\cdots c_r:\ c_i\ge u_i\text{ integers}\,\}\quad(\text{and }S_1=\mathbb Z_{>0}).\] If $1\in S_a$, then $\tau(am)=1$ for some $m$, so $am=1$ and $a=1$. Hence if $S_a=S_b$ and one of $a,b$ equals $1$, both do; so we may assume $a,b>1$, i.e. $r\ge1$ for both. Since $\tau(a^n)=\prod_i(n(u_i-1)+1)$, it suffices to show that $S_a$ determines the multiset $\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}$.

Fix distinct primes $q_1<q_2<\cdots$ all larger than $\tau(a)$ (hence larger than every $u_i$).

$r$ is determined: $q_1\cdots q_s\in S_a\iff s\ge r$. Indeed, a factorisation $q_1\cdots q_s=c_1\cdots c_r$ with all $c_i\ge2$ needs $s\ge r$; conversely, if $s\ge r$, let $c_1,\dots,c_{r-1}$ be single primes and $c_r$ the product of the rest (each $\ge q_1>u_i$).

$\pi_k=u_1u_2\cdots u_k$ is determined for $1\le k\le r$, as the least $m<q_1$ with $m\,q_1\cdots q_{r-k}\in S_a$. We show that for $m<q_1$: \[m\,q_1\cdots q_{r-k}\in S_a\iff m=d_1\cdots d_k\ \text{with}\ d_j\ge u_j .\] ($\Leftarrow$) take $c_j=d_j$ for $j\le k$ and the $r-k$ primes as the other $c_i$. ($\Rightarrow$) In $m\,q_1\cdots q_{r-k}=c_1\cdots c_r$ each prime $q_t$ divides at most one $c_i$ (and $q_t\nmid m$ as $m<q_1$), so at least $k$ of the $c_i$, say $c_{i_1},\dots,c_{i_k}$ with $i_1<\dots<i_k$, are coprime to all $q_t$; their product divides $m$, say $m=c_{i_1}\cdots c_{i_k}\cdot t$. As $i_j\ge j$, $c_{i_j}\ge u_{i_j}\ge u_j$; take $d_j=c_{i_j}$ for $j<k$ and $d_k=c_{i_k}t$. The smallest product $d_1\cdots d_k$ with $d_j\ge u_j$ is $u_1\cdots u_k=\pi_k\le\tau(a)<q_1$, which proves the claim.

Conclusion. If $S_a=S_b$, use the same primes $q_t$ (larger than $\tau(a)$ and $\tau(b)$). Then $a$ and $b$ have the same $r$ and the same $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_r$, hence the same sorted $u_k=\pi_k/\pi_{k-1}$, i.e. the same multiset of exponents. Therefore $\tau(a^n)=\prod(ne_i+1)=\tau(b^n)$ for every $n$.

Problem 5

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Function Equations on Integers

Let $\mathbb{N}$ denote the set of all positive integers. Find all functions $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ such that \[f\bigl(f(n)^m\bigr)=2^{f(m)^n}\] for all $m,n\in\mathbb{N}$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=2^n$

Show solution 1 – Pin down $f(1)$, then a recursion

Answer: $\boxed{f(n)=2^n}$. It works: $f\bigl((2^n)^m\bigr)=2^{2^{nm}}=2^{(2^m)^n}$.

Let $Q(n,m)$ denote $f(f(n)^m)=2^{f(m)^n}$ and $c=f(1)$.

  • $Q(1,m)$: $f(c^m)=2^{f(m)}$. $\quad Q(n,1)$: $f(f(n))=2^{c^n}$.

  • $c\ne1$: otherwise $f(1)=f(1^m)=2^{f(m)}\ge2$. So $c\ge2$. Also $f(c)=2^{f(1)}=2^c$, hence $f(2^c)=f(f(c))=2^{c^c}$.

$c$ is a power of $2$. $Q(2^c,m)$: $f(2^{c^cm})=2^{f(m)^{2^c}}$. $Q(c,m')$ with $m'=c^{c-1}m$: $f(2^{c\cdot c^{c-1}m})=2^{f(c^{c-1}m)^c}$. Comparing, $f(m)^{2^c}=f(c^{c-1}m)^c$; at $m=1$, using $f(c^{c-1})=2^{f(c-1)}$ (valid as $c-1\ge1$): $c^{2^c}=2^{c\,f(c-1)}$. So $c$ is a power of $2$; write $c=2^s$, $s\ge1$.

$c=2$. By $Q(1,\cdot)$, $f(2^{sm})=f(c^m)=2^{f(m)}$; with $m=c$: $f(2^{sc})=2^{f(c)}=2^{2^c}$. By $Q(c,m)$, $f(2^{cm})=2^{f(m)^c}$; with $m=s$: $f(2^{cs})=2^{f(s)^c}$. Hence $f(s)^c=2^c$ and $f(s)=2$. Now $Q(s,m)$ gives $f(2^m)=2^{f(m)^s}$ for all $m$. Then $2^{f(m)}=f(2^{sm})=2^{f(sm)^s}$, so $f(m)=f(sm)^s$; at $m=s$: $2=f(s^2)^s$, which forces $s=1$. So $c=f(1)=2$.

All values. Now $Q(1,m)$ reads $f(2^m)=2^{f(m)}$; in particular $f(2)=2^{f(1)}=4$. Then $Q(2,m)$: $f(4^m)=2^{f(m)^2}$, while $f(4^m)=f(2^{2m})=2^{f(2m)}$; so $f(2m)=f(m)^2$. For $m\ge2$: \[2^{f(m)}=f(2^m)=f\bigl(2\cdot2^{m-1}\bigr)=f(2^{m-1})^2=\bigl(2^{f(m-1)}\bigr)^2=2^{2f(m-1)},\] so $f(m)=2f(m-1)$. With $f(1)=2$ this gives $f(m)=2^m$ for all $m$.

Show solution 2 – One recursion for all $n$ at once

Answer: $\boxed{f(n)=2^n}$. It works, since $f\bigl((2^n)^m\bigr)=2^{2^{nm}}=2^{(2^m)^n}$.

Let $Q(n,m)$ denote the equation $f(f(n)^m)=2^{f(m)^n}$ and put $c=f(1)$.

  • $Q(1,m)$: $f(c^m)=2^{f(m)}$ for all $m$. If $c=1$, this says $1=f(1)=2^{f(m)}\ge2$, which is impossible; so $c\ge2$. With $m=1$: $f(c)=2^c$.

  • $Q(n,1)$: $f(f(n))=2^{c^n}$ for all $n$.

A recursion. Let $n\ge2$. Applying $Q(\cdot,1)$ to the number $f(n)$ gives $f\bigl(f(f(n))\bigr)=2^{c^{f(n)}}$, while applying $f$ to $Q(n,1)$ gives $f\bigl(f(f(n))\bigr)=f\bigl(2^{c^n}\bigr)$. On the other hand, $Q(c,c^{n-1})$ together with $f(c)=2^c$ and $Q(1,n-1)$ gives \[f\bigl(2^{c^n}\bigr)=f\bigl((2^c)^{c^{n-1}}\bigr)=f\bigl(f(c)^{c^{n-1}}\bigr)=2^{f(c^{n-1})^c}=2^{(2^{f(n-1)})^c}=2^{2^{c\,f(n-1)}} .\] Comparing the exponents of $2$ twice, \[c^{f(n)}=2^{c\,f(n-1)}\qquad(n\ge2).\]

$f$ is geometric. The right side is a power of $2$ and $c\ge2$, so $c=2^t$ for an integer $t\ge1$. Then $2^{t f(n)}=2^{c f(n-1)}$, so $t f(n)=c f(n-1)$ for $n\ge2$, and therefore \[f(n)=c\Bigl(\frac ct\Bigr)^{n-1}\qquad\text{for all }n\ge1 .\]

$c=2$. Take $n=c\ge2$ and use $f(c)=2^c$: $c\,(c/t)^{c-1}=2^c$. Write $t=2^ju$ with $u$ odd. Then $c/t=2^{t-j}/u$ (note $t-j\ge0$, since $2^j\le t$), so \[2^{t+(t-j)(c-1)}=2^c\,u^{c-1}.\] The left side is a power of $2$ and $c-1\ge1$, so $u=1$ and $t=2^j$; comparing exponents, $t+(t-j)(c-1)=c$, i.e. $c\,(t-j-1)=-j$. Now $j<2^j=t<2^t=c$, so if $j\ge1$ the number $-j/c$ is not an integer, a contradiction. Hence $j=0$, $t=1$ and $c=2$.

Conclusion. With $c=2$ and $t=1$ the formula reads $f(n)=2\cdot2^{n-1}=2^n$ for every $n$, and this function was checked above. So $f(n)=2^n$ is the only solution.

Problem 6

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · High-Level Olympiad Synthesis

Let $n$ be a positive integer. There are $n(n+1)$ points chosen on the circumference of a circle, and each of these points is colored one of $n$ colors such that each color appears exactly $n+1$ times. Elmo wishes to choose $n$ non-overlapping arcs of the circle such that both endpoints of each arc are selected points of the same color, and this color is different for each of the $n$ chosen arcs.

Prove that Elmo can select the arcs in at least $n+1$ distinct ways.

Show the solution – Minimal arcs, a line lemma, and double counting

All arcs are read clockwise. An arc is a closed arc of the circle whose two endpoints are chosen points of the same colour, and a selection is a set of $n$ pairwise disjoint arcs, one of each colour. We show that there are at least $n+1$ selections.

Line lemma. Points of $m$ colours (and possibly points of other colours) lie on a line, and for every colour $\gamma$ we are given a family $F_\gamma$ of at least $m$ segments, each joining two points of colour $\gamma$, with pairwise disjoint interiors within each family. Then:

  • (a) one can choose pairwise disjoint segments, one from each family;

  • (b) if $r$ of the families have at least $m+1$ members, there are at least $r+1$ such choices.

Proof of (a) by induction on $m$; for $m=0$ the empty choice works. Among all segments of all families take one, $I\in F_\gamma$, whose right endpoint $e$ is leftmost. Let $J$ be a segment of another colour that meets $I$. The endpoints of $J$ differ from $e$ (different colours), the right endpoint of $J$ is not left of $e$, and $J$ meets $I$, so $J$ starts left of $e$ and ends right of $e$: $e$ is an interior point of $J$. As the segments of one family have disjoint interiors, each family other than $F_\gamma$ contains at most one segment meeting $I$. Delete $F_\gamma$ and delete from every other family its segment meeting $I$ (if any). We are left with $m-1$ colours and families of at least $m-1$ segments, none meeting $I$. By induction we choose disjoint segments from them, and $I$ completes the choice.

Proof of (b). Suppose there are only $s\le r$ choices. Assign to each choice its own family with at least $m+1$ members and delete from that family the segment the choice uses. Every family still has at least $m$ members, so by (a) there is a choice from the reduced families. It is also a choice from the original families, yet it differs from each of the $s$ choices, as each of those uses a deleted segment. This contradiction proves (b).

Minimal arcs. Call an arc from $X$ to $Y$ of colour $\gamma$ minimal if the chosen points strictly inside it have pairwise distinct colours, all different from $\gamma$. If it contains $\ell+1$ chosen points (so $\ell-1$ inside), then $1\le\ell\le n$, and exactly $n-\ell$ of the other $n-1$ colours do not occur inside it.

Claim 1: a minimal arc $A$ with $\ell+1$ points lies in at least $n-\ell+1$ selections. Remove $A$. The remaining chosen points lie on the open arc from $Y$ to $X$, which we read as a line. For each colour $\delta\ne\gamma$, let $F_\delta$ be the family of segments (arcs inside this open arc) joining consecutive remaining points of colour $\delta$; they have disjoint interiors. Colour $\delta$ keeps $n+1$ points if it does not occur inside $A$ and $n$ points if it occurs there (once), so $|F_\delta|\ge n-1$, and $|F_\delta|=n$ for the $n-\ell$ colours missing from $A$. Part (b) of the lemma with $m=n-1$ and $r=n-\ell$ gives at least $n-\ell+1$ choices; each of them together with $A$ is a selection, and different choices give different selections.

Claim 2: $\sum_A(n-\ell_A+1)\ge n(n+1)$, the sum over all minimal arcs $A$, where $\ell_A+1$ is the number of points of $A$. Start at any of the $n(n+1)$ chosen points $S$ and walk clockwise through the chosen points until the first point $Y$ whose colour has already been seen, at a point $X$. The arc from $X$ to $Y$ is minimal: the points strictly between them were seen before $Y$, so their colours are distinct and different from that of $Y$. So each start $S$ yields a minimal arc. Conversely, if $S$ yields the minimal arc $A$, then the points from $S$ to the one just before $Y$ have distinct colours, so there are at most $n+1$ points from $S$ to $Y$; as $A$ itself has $\ell_A+1$ of them, $S$ is one of the $n-\ell_A+1$ points from $X$ back to $n-\ell_A$ steps before $X$. Hence each minimal arc is yielded by at most $n-\ell_A+1$ starts, and counting the $n(n+1)$ starts proves Claim 2.

Conclusion. Count the pairs (selection, minimal arc belonging to it). By Claims 1 and 2 there are at least $\sum_A(n-\ell_A+1)\ge n(n+1)$ such pairs. A selection consists of $n$ arcs, so it belongs to at most $n$ pairs. Therefore there are at least $\frac{n(n+1)}{n}=n+1$ selections.
