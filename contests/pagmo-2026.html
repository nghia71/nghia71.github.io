Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with pairwise distinct side lengths. The perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $AC$ intersect segment $BC$ at $P$ and $Q$, respectively, and intersect each other at $O$. If $\angle BAQ=2\angle PAC$, determine $\angle AOC$.

Show the answer

$\angle AOC=120^\circ$

Show the solution

Solution (Two isosceles triangles)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of the triangle at $A,B,C$.

Since $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, $PA=PB$, so $\angle PAB=\angle PBA=\beta$. As $P$ lies on segment $BC$, the ray $AP$ is inside angle $BAC$ and $\angle PAC=\alpha-\beta$. In the same way $QA=QC$ gives $\angle QAC=\gamma$ and $\angle BAQ=\alpha-\gamma$.

The condition becomes $\alpha-\gamma=2(\alpha-\beta)$, i.e. $\alpha=2\beta-\gamma$. Adding $\beta+\gamma$ to both sides: $180^\circ=\alpha+\beta+\gamma=3\beta$, so $\beta=60^\circ$.

The triangle is acute, so $O$ lies inside it and the central angle $AOC$ is twice the inscribed angle $ABC$: $\angle AOC=2\beta=\boxed{120^\circ}$.

(Such triangles exist: the conditions $\beta<\alpha$, $\gamma<\alpha$ (so that $P$, $Q$ lie inside $BC$), $\alpha+\gamma=120^\circ$, $\alpha<90^\circ$ and distinct sides hold exactly for $30^\circ<\gamma<60^\circ$; the figure shows $\gamma=52^\circ$.)

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Some squares of a $2025\times2025$ board are colored black so that the following conditions hold:

  • (i) In every $3\times1$ or $1\times3$ block containing at least two black squares, the third square is also black.

  • (ii) At least one square of the board is left uncolored.

What is the maximum possible number of black squares?

Show the answer

$2\cdot675\cdot2025-675^2=2278125$

Show the solution

Solution (Full lines and sparse lines)

Call a row or column full if all its squares are black.

Fact 1. A row that is not full has its black squares pairwise at least $3$ apart. Suppose two black squares of a row are at distance $1$ or $2$. They lie in a common $1\times3$ block, which by (i) is then all black. Now the block shifted by one square contains two black squares, so it is all black too; repeating in both directions, the whole row is black. The same holds for columns.

Fact 2. Two full rows are at least $3$ apart. If two full rows were at distance $1$ or $2$, every column would contain two black squares at distance at most $2$, so by Fact 1 every column would be full, and the whole board would be black, contradicting (ii).

Upper bound. In a row that is not full, the black squares sit in columns $c_1<c_2<\dots<c_t$ with $c_{i+1}\ge c_i+3$, so $1+3(t-1)\le c_t\le 2025$ and $t\le 675$. By Fact 2 and the same argument, there are at most $675$ full rows. If there are $r$ full rows, the number of black squares is at most \[2025r+675(2025-r)=675\cdot2025+1350r\le 675\cdot2025+1350\cdot675=675\cdot3375=2278125 .\]

Construction. Color black every square whose row number or column number is $\equiv1\pmod3$ (rows and columns numbered $1,\dots,2025$). There are $675$ such rows and $675$ such columns, so the number of black squares is $675\cdot2025+675\cdot2025-675^2=2278125$, and the square in row $2$, column $2$ is uncolored. Any $1\times3$ block lies either in a full row (all black) or in a row whose black squares are exactly those in columns $\equiv1\pmod 3$, and then it contains exactly one black square; the same holds for $3\times1$ blocks. So (i) holds.

The maximum is $\boxed{2278125}$.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · GCD and LCM Structure

A positive integer $N$ is called a búcaro number if it can be written in the form \[N=\frac{(a+b)^{n+k}}{a^n\cdot b^k},\] where $a,b,n,k$ are positive integers. Find all búcaro numbers.

Show the answer

the powers $2^m$ with $m\ge2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Divide out the gcd)

Let $g=\gcd(a,b)$, $a=ga'$, $b=gb'$ with $\gcd(a',b')=1$. The powers of $g$ cancel: \[N=\frac{g^{n+k}(a'+b')^{n+k}}{g^{n}a'^{\,n}\,g^kb'^{\,k}}=\frac{(a'+b')^{n+k}}{a'^{\,n}\,b'^{\,k}} .\] Since $\gcd(a',a'+b')=\gcd(a',b')=1$, the number $a'^{\,n}$ is coprime to the numerator; as $N$ is an integer, $a'^{\,n}=1$, so $a'=1$. In the same way $b'=1$. Hence $N=2^{n+k}$ with $n,k\ge1$.

Conversely, for $m\ge2$, $a=b=1$, $n=1$, $k=m-1$ give $N=2^m$. So the búcaro numbers are exactly $\boxed{2^m\ (m\ge2)}$, i.e. $4,8,16,\dots$

Solution 2 (Compare prime exponents)

For a prime $p$ and a positive integer $m$, let $v_p(m)$ be the exponent of $p$ in $m$.

Claim: $a=b$. Suppose not. Then some prime $p$ has $v_p(a)\ne v_p(b)$; say $e=v_p(a)<f=v_p(b)$ (the other case is symmetric, with the roles of $n$ and $k$ exchanged). Then $p^e$ divides $a+b$, but $p^{e+1}$ does not, because $p^{e+1}\mid b$ and $p^{e+1}\nmid a$. So $v_p(a+b)=e$, and the exponent of $p$ in the fraction $N$ is \[(n+k)e-ne-kf=k(e-f)<0 .\] A fraction in which some prime appears with a negative exponent is not an integer, a contradiction. (If $v_p(a)>v_p(b)$, the same computation gives $n\bigl(v_p(b)-v_p(a)\bigr)<0$.) Hence $v_p(a)=v_p(b)$ for every prime $p$, that is, $a=b$.

Conclusion. With $a=b$, \[N=\frac{(2a)^{n+k}}{a^na^k}=2^{n+k},\] and $n+k\ge2$. Conversely, for $m\ge2$ the choice $a=b=1$, $n=1$, $k=m-1$ gives $N=2^m$. So the búcaro numbers are exactly $\boxed{2^m\ (m\ge2)}$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4,\dots$ be a sequence of real numbers such that $0<a_1\le1\le a_2\le3$ and \[a_{n+2}=a_n+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}\] for all $n\ge1$. For example, $a_3=a_1+\frac1{a_2}$ for $n=1$, $a_4=a_2+\frac1{a_3}$ for $n=2$, and so on.

Prove that $a_n\le\frac{3n}{2}$ for all $n\ge1$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An invariant product)

All terms are positive (by induction: $a_{n+2}=a_n+1/a_{n+1}>0$). Multiply the recurrence by $a_{n+1}$: \[a_{n+1}a_{n+2}=a_na_{n+1}+1 .\] So $a_na_{n+1}=c+n-1$ for all $n\ge1$, where $c=a_1a_2>0$. Hence $\frac1{a_{n+1}}=\frac{a_n}{c+n-1}$ and \[a_{n+2}=a_n+\frac{a_n}{c+n-1}=a_n\cdot\frac{c+n}{c+n-1}\qquad(n\ge1).\]

We prove $a_n\le\frac{3n}2$ by induction in steps of two. It holds for $n=1,2,3$: $a_1\le1$, $a_2\le3$, and $a_3=a_1+\frac1{a_2}\le1+1=2$.

Let $n\ge2$ and $a_n\le\frac{3n}2$. Then \[a_{n+2}\le\frac{3n}{2}\cdot\frac{c+n}{c+n-1}\le\frac{3(n+2)}{2}\iff n(c+n)\le(n+2)(c+n-1)\iff 0\le n-2+2c,\] which is true because $n\ge2$ and $c>0$. Starting from $n=2$ and $n=3$, this gives the bound for all even and all odd indices.

Solution 2 (Bounding the steps of the odd and even terms)

Positivity and monotonicity. All terms are positive: $a_1,a_2>0$, and if $a_n,a_{n+1}>0$ then $a_{n+2}=a_n+\frac1{a_{n+1}}>0$. Hence \[a_{n+2}-a_n=\frac1{a_{n+1}}>0,\] so the odd-indexed terms $a_1,a_3,a_5,\dots$ increase, and so do the even-indexed terms $a_2,a_4,a_6,\dots$.

Odd indices. Every even-indexed term satisfies $a_{2j}\ge a_2\ge1$, so each step of the odd terms is $a_{2j+1}-a_{2j-1}=\frac1{a_{2j}}\le1$. Adding $k$ such steps, \[a_{2k+1}\le a_1+k\le 1+k<\frac{3(2k+1)}{2}\qquad(k\ge0).\]

Even indices. Every odd-indexed term from $a_3$ on satisfies $a_{2j+1}\ge a_3=a_1+\frac1{a_2}>\frac1{a_2}\ge\frac13$, so each step of the even terms is $a_{2j+2}-a_{2j}=\frac1{a_{2j+1}}<3$. Adding $k-1$ such steps, \[a_{2k}\le a_2+3(k-1)\le 3+3(k-1)=3k=\frac{3\cdot 2k}{2}\qquad(k\ge1).\]

Together, $a_n\le\frac{3n}2$ for every $n\ge1$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · GCD and LCM Structure

Find all integers $n\ge2$ such that each of the following numbers divides $n+1$: \[\gcd(n,1)+\gcd(n,2),\quad \gcd(n,2)+\gcd(n,3),\quad \gcd(n,3)+\gcd(n,4),\quad\dots,\quad \gcd(n,n-1)+\gcd(n,n).\] Here, $\gcd(a,b)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$.

Show the answer

all primes $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Look next to the largest proper divisor)

Primes work. If $n=p$ is prime, then $\gcd(p,i)=1$ for $1\le i\le p-1$. The numbers are $1+1=2$ (for $i\le p-2$) and $\gcd(p,p-1)+\gcd(p,p)=1+p$. For $p=2$ the only number is $3$, which divides $3$; for odd $p$, both $2$ and $p+1$ divide $p+1$.

Composite numbers fail. Let $n$ be composite, $p$ its smallest prime factor and $q=n/p$, so $q\ge p\ge2$ and $2\le q\le n-1$. Consider the numbers with $i=q-1$ and $i=q$: \[d_1=\gcd(n,q-1)+q,\qquad d_2=q+\gcd(n,q+1).\] Let $g$ be $\gcd(n,q-1)$ or $\gcd(n,q+1)$. Then $g$ divides $n=pq$ and is coprime to $q$ (it divides $q\pm1$), so $g\mid p$, i.e. $g\in\{1,p\}$.

  • The value $q+1$ never divides $n+1$: indeed $n+1=pq+1=p(q+1)-(p-1)$ and $0<p-1<q+1$.

  • So both $d_1$ and $d_2$ must equal $q+p$, which forces $p\mid q-1$ and $p\mid q+1$, hence $p\mid2$ and $p=2$. Then $d_2=q+2$ divides $n+1=2q+1=2(q+2)-3$, so $q+2\mid3$, which is impossible for $q\ge2$.

So no composite $n$ works, and the answer is: $\boxed{n\text{ prime}}$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$ and circumcircle $\Gamma$. Let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ to $BC$. The circle with diameter $AD$ intersects $AB$ at $X\ne A$ and $\Gamma$ at $Y\ne A$. Let $O_1$, $O_2$, and $O_3$ be the circumcenters of triangles $ABC$, $ADX$, and $BXC$, respectively. Prove that triangles $XBY$ and $O_1O_2O_3$ are similar.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sides perpendicular to three radical axes)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$; the triangles will turn out to be oppositely similar, with $X\leftrightarrow O_1$, $B\leftrightarrow O_2$, $Y\leftrightarrow O_3$. Let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $AD$ (center $O_2$, the midpoint of $AD$; it is the circumcircle of $ADX$ because $\angle AXD=90^\circ$), and let $\omega_3$ be the circle $(BXC)$.

Step 1: the circle $\omega_3$. Let $X'$ be the second point of $\omega$ on $AC$, the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AC$. In the right triangles $ADB$ and $ADC$, $AX\cdot AB=AD^2=AX'\cdot AC$, so $B,C,X,X'$ are concyclic: $X'\in\omega_3$. Thus $XX'$ is the common chord of $\omega$ and $\omega_3$, and $O_2O_3\perp XX'$. Also $O_1O_3\perp BC$ (common chord of $\Gamma$ and $\omega_3$) and $O_1O_2\perp AY$ (common chord of $\Gamma$ and $\omega$).

Step 2: two lemmas on angles.

  • (a) $\angle(XY,XB)=\angle(AY,BC)$. Indeed $A,X,Y,D$ lie on $\omega$, so $\angle(XY,XA)=\angle(DY,DA)$, and since $DA\perp BC$ and $DY\perp AY$ (as $AD$ is a diameter), $\angle(DY,DA)=\angle(AY,BC)$. Finally $XA$ and $XB$ are the same line.

  • (b) $\angle(BX,BY)=\angle(XX',AY)$. Since $B,C,X,X'$ are concyclic, $XX'$ is antiparallel to $BC$ in angle $A$, hence parallel to the tangent $t$ to $\Gamma$ at $A$. By the tangent-chord angle, $\angle(t,AY)=\angle(BA,BY)$, and $BA=BX$ as lines.

Step 3. Rotating both lines of an angle by $90^\circ$ does not change it. By Step 1, \[\angle(O_1O_2,O_1O_3)=\angle(AY,BC)=\angle(XY,XB)=-\angle(XB,XY),\] \[\angle(O_2O_1,O_2O_3)=\angle(AY,XX')=-\angle(XX',AY)=-\angle(BX,BY).\] So the directed angles of triangle $O_1O_2O_3$ at $O_1$ and $O_2$ are the negatives of those of triangle $XBY$ at $X$ and $B$. Hence the mirror image of triangle $XBY$ has the same directed angles as $O_1O_2O_3$, and the triangles $XBY$ and $O_1O_2O_3$ are similar (with opposite orientation).

Solution 2 (Coordinates with the altitude as an axis)

Put $D$ at the origin, $BC$ on the real axis and $A$ on the imaginary axis, and use complex numbers: \[a=ih,\qquad b_0=-b,\qquad c_0=c\qquad(b,c,h>0,\ b<c),\] where $b_0$, $c_0$ denote the points $B$, $C$.

The points. $O_2$ is the midpoint of $AD$, $o_2=\frac{ih}2$. The center $O_1$ lies on the line $\operatorname{Re}z=\frac{c-b}2$; writing $o_1=\frac{c-b}2+iy_1$, the power of $D$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $|o_1|^2-|o_1-a|^2=2hy_1-h^2$, and it equals $-DB\cdot DC=-bc$; so $y_1=\frac{h^2-bc}{2h}$. Likewise $o_3=\frac{c-b}2+iy_3$, and the power of $A$ with respect to $(BXC)$ is $AX\cdot AB=AD^2=h^2$; computing it as $|a-o_3|^2-|b_0-o_3|^2=h^2-2hy_3-bc$ gives $y_3=-\frac{bc}{2h}$.

The foot $X$ of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AB$ is $x=\dfrac{ibh}{b-ih}$, and \[y=\frac{ibch}{bc+ih(b-c)} .\] One checks that $|y|^2=h\operatorname{Im}y$ (so $Y\in\omega$, whose equation is $|z|^2=h\operatorname{Im}z$) and that $y$ satisfies the equation $|z|^2+(b-c)\operatorname{Re}z+\frac{bc-h^2}{h}\operatorname{Im}z-bc=0$ of $\Gamma$; and $y\ne a$.

The two shapes. We get \[b_0-x=-\frac{b^2}{b-ih},\qquad y-x=\frac{b^2h^2}{(b-ih)\bigl(bc+ih(b-c)\bigr)},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{y-x}{b_0-x}=-\frac{h^2}{bc+ih(b-c)} .\] On the other hand $o_3-o_1=-\frac{ih}2$ and $o_2-o_1=\frac{b-c}2+\frac{ibc}{2h}=\frac{i\bigl(bc-ih(b-c)\bigr)}{2h}$, so \[\frac{o_3-o_1}{o_2-o_1}=-\frac{h^2}{bc-ih(b-c)}=\overline{\left(\frac{y-x}{b_0-x}\right)} .\] Equal shape ratios up to conjugation mean that triangle $XBY$ is similar to triangle $O_1O_2O_3$ ($X\leftrightarrow O_1$, $B\leftrightarrow O_2$, $Y\leftrightarrow O_3$), with opposite orientation.
