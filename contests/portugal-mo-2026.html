Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure

Francisca wrote the numbers from $1$ to $2026$ in the first row of a table as shown: \[\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline 1&2&3&4&5&\cdots&2026\\\hline &&&&&\cdots&\\\hline \end{array}\] Next, Francisca rewrote the numbers from $1$ to $2026$ in the second row of the table, ordered first by increasing sum of their digits and, in case of a tie, by increasing numerical value.

Which number is in the second row of the $2000$-th column?

Show the answer

$979$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting from the end)

Column $2000$ is the $27$th column from the end ($2026-2000=26$), so we look at the numbers with the largest digit sums.

Three-digit strings with a large digit sum. Let $x,y,z$ be digits ($0$ to $9$). Writing $x=9-x'$, $y=9-y'$, $z=9-z'$, the equation $x+y+z=27-t$ becomes $x'+y'+z'=t$ with $x',y',z'\ge0$; for $t\le3$ there are $\binom{t+2}{2}$ solutions, i.e. $1,3,6,10$ for $t=0,1,2,3$.

Numbers from $1$ to $2026$ with digit sum $s\ge25$. Numbers $2000,\dots,2026$ have digit sum at most $2+0+2+9=13$. A number up to $999$, written as a string $xyz$ (with leading zeros), has digit sum $x+y+z$; a number $1xyz$ from $1000$ to $1999$ has digit sum $1+x+y+z$. So the counts are \[\begin{array}{c|cccc} s & 25 & 26 & 27 & 28\\\hline \text{up to }999 & 6 & 3 & 1 & 0\\ 1000\text{ to }1999 & 10 & 6 & 3 & 1\\\hline \text{total} & 16 & 9 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\] In total $30$ numbers have digit sum at least $25$; they fill the last $30$ columns, $1997$ to $2026$, and the $16$ numbers with digit sum $25$ come first there, in increasing order, in columns $1997,\dots,2012$.

So column $2000$ holds the $4$th smallest number with digit sum $25$. The smallest ones are the three-digit numbers with digit sum $25$ (digits $\ge7$): $799,\ 889,\ 898,\ 979,\ 988,\ 997$. The fourth is $\boxed{979}$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry

In trapezoid $ABCD$, with $AB\parallel CD$, the midpoint $M$ of $BC$ satisfies $\angle BAM=\angle MAD$. Show that $AD=AB+CD$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Extend $AM$ to meet line $CD$)

In the trapezoid $ABCD$ the sides $AB$ and $DC$ are parallel and point in the same direction ($\vec{DC}$ is a positive multiple of $\vec{AB}$).

Let $E$ be the reflection of $A$ in $M$. The point reflection in $M$ swaps $B$ and $C$ (as $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$) and sends $A$ to $E$, so it maps segment $AB$ onto segment $CE$ and reverses vectors: $\vec{CE}=-\vec{BA}=\vec{AB}$, in particular $CE=AB$. Therefore $E$ lies on the line $DC$, beyond $C$, and \[DE=DC+CE=DC+AB .\] The reflection also maps the angle between rays $AB$ and $AM$ onto the angle between rays $EC$ and $EA$, so $\angle CEA=\angle BAM$. As $D$ lies on ray $EC$, $\angle DEA=\angle CEA=\angle BAM=\angle MAD=\angle EAD$ (the points $A,M,E$ are collinear). So triangle $DAE$ is isosceles with $DA=DE$, and \[AD=DE=AB+CD .\]

Solution 2 (The midline of the trapezoid)

Let $N$ be the midpoint of the leg $AD$; $M$ is the midpoint of the other leg $BC$. By the midline theorem for trapezoids, $NM\parallel AB$ and \[NM=\frac{AB+CD}{2}.\] The ray $AM$ lies inside the angle $BAD$, so $N$ (on $AD$) and $B$ are on opposite sides of the line $AM$; since $NM\parallel AB$, the alternate angles give $\angle NMA=\angle MAB$. With the hypothesis $\angle MAB=\angle MAN$ we get $\angle NMA=\angle NAM$, so triangle $NAM$ is isosceles: $NM=NA=\frac{AD}{2}$. Comparing, \[AD=2\,NM=AB+CD .\]

Solution 3 (Areas and equal distances)

Let $h>0$ be the distance between the parallel lines $AB$ and $CD$. The point $M$ lies on the side $BC$ of the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$, so the ray $AM$ lies inside the angle $BAD$; by the hypothesis it is the bisector of this angle.

Distances from $M$. As $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$, with $B$ on line $AB$ and $C$ on line $CD$, the point $M$ is at distance $\frac h2$ from each of the lines $AB$ and $CD$. Every point of the bisector of an angle is equally far from the two lines of the angle, so $M$ is also at distance $\frac h2$ from the line $AD$.

Areas. The segments $MA$ and $MD$ cut the trapezoid into the triangles $ABM$, $MCD$ and $AMD$. Taking $AB$, $CD$, $AD$ as bases, with the heights $\frac h2$ just found, \[[ABM]=\frac{AB\cdot h}{4},\qquad [MCD]=\frac{CD\cdot h}{4},\qquad [AMD]=\frac{AD\cdot h}{4}.\] Their sum is the area of the trapezoid, $\frac{(AB+CD)\,h}{2}$. Hence \[\frac{(AB+CD+AD)\,h}{4}=\frac{(AB+CD)\,h}{2},\] and dividing by $\frac h4$ gives $AB+CD+AD=2(AB+CD)$, that is, $AD=AB+CD$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

We want to connect twelve cities by a road network with the following condition: for any three distinct cities $X$, $Y$, and $Z$, it is possible to travel by road from $X$ to $Y$ without passing through $Z$.

What is the greatest value of $n$ such that, regardless of how the cities are connected under this condition, there exists a path along the roads passing through at least $n$ cities, in which one city is visited twice (at the start and at the end of the path) and each of the other cities in the path is visited exactly once?

Show the answer

$4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Enlarging a triangle)

Think of the cities as vertices and the roads as edges. The requested closed route is a cycle; we want the largest $n$ such that every admissible network has a cycle through at least $n$ cities (we count the different cities on the route; the city where the route starts and ends is counted once). The answer is $n=\boxed{4}$.

At most $4$. Take two cities $P,Q$ and join each of the other ten cities to both $P$ and $Q$ (and build no other roads). The condition holds: two of the ten cities are joined through $P$ or through $Q$, whichever is not $Z$; $P$ is joined directly to each of the ten; $P$ and $Q$ are joined through any of the ten except $Z$. Every road joins one of $P,Q$ to one of the ten, so along a cycle the cities alternate between $\{P,Q\}$ and the ten, and a cycle has at most $4$ cities.

At least $4$. First, every city $X$ has at least two roads. Indeed, $X$ has a road (it can reach other cities); if it had only one road, to $W$, then $X$ could not reach a third city $Y$ without passing through $W$.

Take a city $X$ with neighbours $Y$ and $W$. Using the condition with $Z=X$, there is a route from $Y$ to $W$ avoiding $X$; a shortest such route repeats no city, and together with the roads $XY,XW$ it forms a cycle through $X$. If this cycle has at least $4$ cities, we are done. Otherwise it is the triangle $XYW$.

There are $12>3$ cities and the network is connected, so some city $U\notin\{X,Y,W\}$ has a road to one of $X,Y,W$; the triangle is symmetric, so say to $X$. Using the condition with $Z=X$, there is a route from $U$ to $Y$ avoiding $X$; let $V$ be the first city of $\{Y,W\}$ on it, and let $R$ be the part of the route from $U$ to $V$ (shortened, if necessary, so that no city repeats). Then $R$ avoids $X,Y,W$ except at its end $V$, and \[X\to U\xrightarrow{\ R\ }V\to V'\to X,\qquad \{V,V'\}=\{Y,W\},\] is a cycle containing $X,U,Y,W$: at least $4$ cities.

Solution 2 (A longest path)

Cities are vertices, roads are edges; the requested closed route is a cycle, and we count the different cities on it. The two-hub network (cities $P,Q$, each joined to all of the other ten and no other roads) satisfies the condition and has no cycle with more than $4$ cities, because every road joins $\{P,Q\}$ to the other ten, so the cities of a cycle alternate between the two groups. It remains to show that every admissible network has a cycle with at least $4$ cities.

Every city has at least two roads: a city with a single road, to $W$, could not reach any third city without passing through $W$.

Let $v_1v_2\dots v_k$ be a longest path (no repeated cities). Since $v_2$ has a second neighbour, $k\ge3$. All neighbours of $v_1$ lie on the path, otherwise the path could be extended. If $v_1$ is adjacent to some $v_j$ with $j\ge4$, then $v_1v_2\dots v_jv_1$ is a cycle with $j\ge4$ cities.

Otherwise the neighbours of $v_1$ are exactly $v_2$ and $v_3$. Apply the condition with $Z=v_3$ and $X=v_1$, $Y$ any city outside $\{v_1,v_2,v_3\}$: a route from $v_1$ avoiding $v_3$ must start $v_1v_2$ and then leave $v_2$ along a road to some city $w\notin\{v_1,v_3\}$.

  • If $w$ is not on the path, then $w\,v_2\,v_1\,v_3\,v_4\dots v_k$ is a longer path, a contradiction.

  • If $w=v_j$ with $j\ge4$, then $v_1\,v_2\,v_j\,v_{j-1}\dots v_3\,v_1$ is a cycle through $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_j$, so it has at least $4$ cities.

Hence the greatest $n$ is $\boxed{4}$.

Day 2

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Pêro filled each cell of the following figure with a positive integer, obeying two rules:

  • 1. In each of the eight rows, all numbers are distinct;

  • 2. Each number not in the last row is equal to the sum of the two numbers immediately below it.

In this way, Pêro wrote $29$ distinct numbers in the $36$ cells.

Pêro realized that it is possible to fill the $36$ cells using fewer distinct numbers.

What is the minimum number of distinct numbers that Pêro can write?

Show the answer

$15$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Row maxima, and Fibonacci numbers)

Number the rows $1$ (top) to $8$ (bottom), and let $m_k$ be the largest number in row $k$.

Lower bound. Every cell of row $k+1$ is one of the two cells below some cell of row $k$, and that upper cell equals it plus a positive number. Applying this to a cell holding $m_{k+1}$ shows that row $k$ contains a number larger than $m_{k+1}$, so $m_k>m_{k+1}$. Hence \[m_1>m_2>\dots>m_7>m_8 .\] The eight numbers of the bottom row are distinct and all at most $m_8$; the seven numbers $m_1,\dots,m_7$ are distinct and all larger than $m_8$. So at least $8+7=15$ distinct numbers are written.

Construction. Let $F_1=F_2=1$, $F_{j+2}=F_{j+1}+F_j$ be the Fibonacci numbers, and write $F_2,F_3,\dots,F_9=1,2,3,5,8,13,21,34$ in the bottom row. Counting rows from the bottom as $r=0,1,\dots,7$ and positions from the left as $i=0,1,\dots$, the cell $(r,i)$ then holds $F_{2r+i+2}$: this is true for $r=0$, and if it holds for row $r$, the cell $(r+1,i)$ holds $F_{2r+i+2}+F_{2r+i+3}=F_{2r+i+4}=F_{2(r+1)+i+2}$. Since $F_2<F_3<F_4<\cdots$, the numbers in each row are distinct, and all numbers used are $F_2,F_3,\dots,F_{16}$: exactly $15$ distinct numbers (the top is $F_{16}=987$).

In the figure the row maxima $m_1,\dots,m_8$ are shaded.

So the minimum is $\boxed{15}$.

Solution 2 (Climbing from the largest bottom number)

Every number not in the bottom row is the sum of the two positive numbers below it, so it is larger than each of them.

Lower bound. Let $x$ be the largest number in the bottom row (row $8$). Start at a cell of the bottom row holding $x$ and go up one row at a time, each time to a cell of the row above that stands directly on the current cell (every cell below the top lies under at least one cell of the row above). After $7$ steps we reach the top. The $8$ cells visited hold strictly increasing numbers, because each is larger than the one below it; so we meet $7$ distinct numbers larger than $x$. The other $7$ cells of the bottom row hold numbers that are distinct (rule 1) and smaller than $x$ (since $x$ is the largest and the numbers of a row are distinct). Together with $x$ these are $7+1+7=15$ distinct numbers.

Construction. The Fibonacci filling of Solution 1 (bottom row $1,2,3,5,8,13,21,34$; the cell in row $r$ from the bottom and position $i$ holds $F_{2r+i+2}$) obeys both rules and uses exactly the $15$ numbers $F_2,\dots,F_{16}$.

So the minimum is $\boxed{15}$.

Solution 3 (Ranks grow by two per row)

Every number not in the bottom row is the sum of two positive numbers, so it is larger than each of them.

Lower bound. Let $v_1<v_2<\dots<v_N$ be the distinct numbers written, and call $i$ the rank of $v_i$. Number the rows from the bottom as $j=0,1,\dots,7$. We show that every number in row $j$ has rank at least $2j+1$. For $j=0$ this is clear. Suppose it holds for row $j$, and take a cell of row $j+1$ holding $x=a+b$, where $a$ and $b$ are the two cells below it, in row $j$. By rule 1, $a\ne b$, so $a$ and $b$ have different ranks, both at least $2j+1$; hence one of them, say $b$, has rank at least $2j+2$. Since $x>b$, the rank of $x$ is at least $2j+3=2(j+1)+1$. This completes the induction. The top cell is in row $7$, so its rank is at least $15$, and therefore $N\ge 15$.

Construction. Let $F_1=F_2=1$, $F_{k+2}=F_{k+1}+F_k$ be the Fibonacci numbers, and write $F_2,F_3,\dots,F_9=1,2,3,5,8,13,21,34$ in the bottom row, from left to right. Number the positions in each row from the left as $i=0,1,2,\dots$. Then the cell in row $j$ and position $i$ holds $F_{2j+i+2}$: this is true for $j=0$, and if it holds for row $j$, the cell in row $j+1$ and position $i$ holds $F_{2j+i+2}+F_{2j+i+3}=F_{2(j+1)+i+2}$. Since $F_2<F_3<F_4<\cdots$, the numbers in each row are distinct, and the numbers used are exactly $F_2,F_3,\dots,F_{16}$ (the top is $F_{16}=987$): $15$ distinct numbers.

So the minimum is $\boxed{15}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A set $S$ is called $n$-extremal if it consists of $n$ distinct integers and, for every integer $k$ with $1\leq k\leq n$, the sum of the $k$ smallest elements of $S$ or the sum of the $k$ largest elements of $S$ (or both) is a multiple of $n$.

Determine, for each $n$, the minimum possible number of multiples of $n$ that an $n$-extremal set can contain.

Show the answer

$1$ if $n$ is odd, $2$ if $n$ is even

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Prefix sums modulo $n$)

Let $S=\{s_1<s_2<\dots<s_n\}$, let $P_k=s_1+\dots+s_k$ ($P_0=0$) and $T=P_n$. The sum of the $k$ largest elements is $T-P_{n-k}$.

Reformulation. For $k=n$ both sums equal $T$, so $n\mid T$. Then for $1\le k\le n-1$ the condition reads: $n\mid P_k$ or $n\mid P_{n-k}$. So, with \[Z=\{k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}:\ n\mid P_k\},\] $S$ is $n$-extremal exactly when $0,n\in Z$ and, for every $1\le k\le n-1$, $k\in Z$ or $n-k\in Z$. Note that $s_k=P_k-P_{k-1}$, so \[k-1\in Z\ \text{and}\ k\in Z\ \Longrightarrow\ n\mid s_k. \tag{1}\] Lower bounds. For $k=1$: $1\in Z$ or $n-1\in Z$; together with $0,n\in Z$ and (1), $s_1$ or $s_n$ is a multiple of $n$. So there is always at least one multiple of $n$. Let $n=2m$ be even. If $m=1$, then $0,1,2\in Z$, so $s_1,s_2$ are both multiples of $2$. If $m\ge2$, then $m\in Z$ (take $k=m$, so $n-k=k$), and $k=m-1$ gives $m-1\in Z$ or $m+1\in Z$, so $s_m$ or $s_{m+1}$ is a multiple of $n$. The index sets $\{1,n\}$ and $\{m,m+1\}$ are disjoint, so there are at least two multiples of $n$.

Constructions. For $n=1$ any one-element set works (and its element is a multiple of $1$). For $n\ge2$ we use the following recipe. Choose $t_0,t_1,\dots,t_n\in\{0,1\}$ with $t_0=t_n=0$ and put \[s_k=3nk+t_k-t_{k-1}\qquad(1\le k\le n).\] Then $s_{k+1}-s_k\ge3n-2>0$, so the $s_k$ are increasing, and $P_k=3n\cdot\frac{k(k+1)}2+t_k\equiv t_k\pmod n$. Hence $k\in Z$ exactly when $t_k=0$, and, since $t_k-t_{k-1}\in\{-1,0,1\}$, $n\mid s_k$ exactly when $t_k=t_{k-1}$.

  • $n$ odd: $t_k=1$ for odd $k<n$, $t_k=0$ for even $k$ and for $k=n$. For $1\le k\le n-1$ the numbers $k$ and $n-k$ have different parities, so one of them is even, i.e. in $Z$. Consecutive values $t_{k-1},t_k$ differ except for $k=n$ ($t_{n-1}=t_n=0$), so exactly one element, $s_n$, is a multiple of $n$.

  • $n=2$: take $S=\{6,12\}$; the sums $6$ and $18$ are even, and both elements are multiples of $2$, as they must be.

  • $n=2m\ge4$ even: let $t_k=0$ exactly for $k\in Z_0:=\{0,n\}\cup\{1\le k\le m:\ k\equiv m\}\cup\{m+1\le k\le n-1:\ k\not\equiv m\}$ (congruences mod $2$); then $Z=Z_0$. For $1\le k\le m-1$, the numbers $k$ and $n-k$ have the same parity: if $k\equiv m$ then $k\in Z_0$, otherwise $n-k\in Z_0$; also $m\in Z_0$, and the cases $k\ge m+1$ are symmetric. Inside $[1,m]$ and inside $[m+1,n-1]$ membership in $Z_0$ alternates, so $t_{k-1}=t_k$ happens only for $k=m+1$ (both $m,m+1\in Z_0$) and for exactly one of $k=1$, $k=n$ (because $1\in Z_0$ exactly when $m$ is odd, and, as $m\ge2$, $n-1\in Z_0$ exactly when $m$ is even; here $1$, $m+1$ and $n$ are three different indices). So exactly two elements are multiples of $n$. (For instance $n=6$: $\{18,37,53,72,91,107\}$.)

Therefore the minimum is $\boxed{1}$ for odd $n$ and $\boxed{2}$ for even $n$.

Solution 2 (Counting blocks of zero residues)

Let $S=\{s_1<s_2<\dots<s_n\}$, let $P_k=s_1+\dots+s_k$ for $0\le k\le n$ (so $P_0=0$), and let $T=P_n$. The sum of the $k$ largest elements is $T-P_{n-k}$.

Reformulation. For $k=n$ both sums equal $T$, so $n\mid T$. Then, for $1\le k\le n-1$, the sum of the $k$ largest elements is a multiple of $n$ exactly when $n\mid P_{n-k}$. Put $z_k=1$ if $n\mid P_k$ and $z_k=0$ otherwise ($0\le k\le n$). So $S$ is $n$-extremal exactly when \[z_0=z_n=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad z_k=1\ \text{or}\ z_{n-k}=1\quad\text{for } 1\le k\le n-1. \tag{1}\] Since $s_k=P_k-P_{k-1}$, if $z_{k-1}=z_k=1$ then $n\mid s_k$. So the number of multiples of $n$ in $S$ is at least the number of adjacent pairs of ones in the string $z_0z_1\dots z_n$, that is, of indices $k$ with $z_{k-1}=z_k=1$.

Counting lemma. A string of zeros and ones with $Z$ ones and $W$ zeros has at least $Z-W-1$ adjacent pairs of ones. Indeed, the zeros cut the string into at most $W+1$ maximal blocks of consecutive ones; a block of $b$ ones contains $b-1$ adjacent pairs, so the total is $Z$ minus the number of blocks, which is at least $Z-(W+1)$.

Lower bounds. Let $n=2m+1$ be odd. The string $z_0\dots z_n$ has $2m+2$ entries. The pairs $\{k,n-k\}$ with $1\le k\le m$ are disjoint and contain one index with $z=1$ each, by (1); with $z_0=z_n=1$ this gives $Z\ge m+2$, so $W\le m$ and there are at least $(m+2)-m-1=1$ adjacent pairs of ones. Let $n=2m$ be even. The string has $2m+1$ entries. Taking $k=m$ in (1) gives $z_m=1$; the disjoint pairs $\{k,n-k\}$ with $1\le k\le m-1$ give $m-1$ further ones, and $z_0=z_n=1$; so $Z\ge m+2$, $W\le m-1$, and there are at least $(m+2)-(m-1)-1=2$ adjacent pairs of ones. Hence an $n$-extremal set contains at least one multiple of $n$, and at least two when $n$ is even.

Constructions. For $n=1$ any one-element set works, and its element is a multiple of $1$. Let $n\ge2$. Choose $t_0,t_1,\dots,t_n\in\{0,1\}$ with $t_0=t_n=0$, let $r_k\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$ be the remainder of $t_k-t_{k-1}$ modulo $n$, and put $s_k=nk+r_k$ for $1\le k\le n$. Then $s_{k+1}-s_k=n+r_{k+1}-r_k\ge n-(n-1)>0$, so the $s_k$ are increasing, and $P_k\equiv r_1+\dots+r_k\equiv t_k\pmod n$. As $n\ge2$ and $t_k\in\{0,1\}$, this means $z_k=1$ exactly when $t_k=0$, and $n\mid s_k$ exactly when $r_k=0$, that is, when $t_{k-1}=t_k$. So $S$ is $n$-extremal when $t_k=0$ or $t_{n-k}=0$ for every $1\le k\le n-1$, and its number of multiples of $n$ is the number of $k$ with $t_{k-1}=t_k$.

  • $n$ odd, $n\ge3$: $t_k=0$ for even $k$, $t_k=1$ for odd $k<n$, and $t_n=0$. For $1\le k\le n-1$ the numbers $k$ and $n-k$ have different parities, so one of $t_k,t_{n-k}$ is $0$. The values $t_0,\dots,t_{n-1}$ alternate, and $t_{n-1}=0=t_n$, so exactly one element is a multiple of $n$. (For $n=3$: $S=\{4,8,9\}$.)

  • $n=2$: $t_0=t_1=t_2=0$, giving $S=\{2,4\}$ with two multiples of $2$.

  • $n=2m$, $m\ge2$: $t_k=0$ for even $k$ and $t_k=1$ for odd $k$ when $0\le k\le m-1$; $t_m=0$; $t_{n-k}=1-t_k$ for $1\le k\le m-1$; $t_n=0$. The condition holds: $t_m=0$, and for $k\ne m$ one of $t_k$, $t_{n-k}=1-t_k$ is $0$. The values alternate along $t_0,\dots,t_{m-1}$ and along $t_{m+1},\dots,t_{n-1}$ (the second run is $1-t_{m-1},\dots,1-t_1$). At the centre, $t_{m-1}=t_m$ when $t_{m-1}=0$, and $t_m=t_{m+1}$ when $t_{m+1}=1-t_{m-1}=0$, i.e. when $t_{m-1}=1$: exactly one of these two happens. At the end, $t_{n-1}=1-t_1=0=t_n$. As $m\ge2$, the pair $(n-1,n)$ is not one of the central pairs, so there are exactly two multiples of $n$. (For $n=6$: $t=0,1,0,0,1,0,0$ and $S=\{7,17,18,25,35,36\}$.)

Therefore the minimum is $\boxed{1}$ for odd $n$ and $\boxed{2}$ for even $n$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

In the plane, $44$ vertices of a convex polygon and $2026$ additional points inside the polygon are marked, in such a way that no line passes through three or more of the $2070$ marked points.

Campos proposes the following challenge to Melo. First, Campos colors each of the $2070$ points either green or red. Melo's task is to draw some straight-line segments joining pairs of points of the same color, so that no two segments intersect except possibly at endpoints, and such that any two points of the same color are connected by a path formed by these segments.

In how many ways can Campos color the points so that it is possible for Melo to complete the task?

Show the answer

$1894\cdot2^{2026}=947\cdot2^{2027}$

Show the solution

Solution (The hull colours; triangles inside triangles)

Call the $44$ polygon vertices the hull vertices. We show that Melo can succeed exactly when, going around the polygon, the colour changes at most twice, i.e. the green hull vertices form one block of consecutive vertices (possibly empty or all of them). The interior points may be coloured arbitrarily. This gives the count \[2^{2026}\Bigl(2+2\binom{44}{2}\Bigr)=2^{2026}(2+44\cdot43)=\boxed{1894\cdot2^{2026}},\] because a cyclic colouring of the $44$ hull vertices with at most two colour changes is either constant ($2$ ways) or is given by the two sides of the polygon where the colour changes ($\binom{44}2$ ways) and the colour of one of the two arcs ($2$ ways).

Necessity. The number of colour changes around a cycle is even; suppose it is at least $4$. Then there are hull vertices $R_1,G_1,R_2,G_2$, in this cyclic order, with $R_1,R_2$ red and $G_1,G_2$ green. Suppose Melo succeeded. The red segments connect all red points, so there is a path of red segments from $R_1$ to $R_2$; it is a simple polygonal line $\pi$ inside the polygon $K$ (it may pass through other red hull vertices; the argument only uses that $\pi$ lies in $K$). Similarly there is a simple green polygonal line $\sigma$ from $G_1$ to $G_2$ in $K$. Add a point $\Omega$ outside $K$ and join it to $R_1,G_1,R_2,G_2$ by four arcs outside $K$ that do not cross each other; around $\Omega$ they leave in the order $R_1,G_1,R_2,G_2$. The arcs $\Omega R_1$, $\Omega R_2$ and $\pi$ form a closed curve $J$, and since the arcs towards $G_1$ and $G_2$ leave $\Omega$ on different sides of $J$, the points $G_1$ and $G_2$ lie on different sides of $J$ (Jordan curve theorem). The curve $\sigma$ lies in $K$ and does not meet the outer arcs, so it must meet $\pi$. But a red segment and a green segment have no common endpoint, so they would cross at an inner point, which is forbidden. Hence at most two colour changes are necessary.

Sufficiency. Call a set of red and green points with no three on a line solvable if Melo's task can be done for it (a colour with at most one point needs no segment). Only connectivity is asked, so we never need to worry about cycles among the drawn segments. We use one remark.

Gluing. Let a convex polygon whose vertices are marked points be cut into convex pieces by segments joining marked points, and suppose that no marked point lies inside a cutting segment. If the task is done separately in every piece (for the marked points in that piece, its boundary included), then the drawings together have no crossing. Indeed, segments drawn in different pieces lie in convex polygons without common interior points, so they can meet only on a cutting segment $s$; a drawn segment has only its endpoints on $s$ or is $s$ itself, because it ends at marked points and stays inside its piece. So two such segments meet only in a common endpoint or coincide.

Lemma (triangles). If the convex hull of the marked points is a triangle $abc$, the set is solvable.

Proof. Induction on the number $m$ of marked points inside the triangle. If $m=0$, draw the sides of $abc$ whose endpoints have the same colour. Let $m\ge1$. Choose an interior marked point $p$ (the choice is made below) and join it to $a,b,c$. No marked point lies on $pa,pb,pc$, so every other interior point is inside exactly one of the triangles $pab,pbc,pca$, and each of these has fewer than $m$ interior points. Solve the three triangles by the induction hypothesis and take all the segments drawn; by the gluing remark there is no crossing. In each small triangle all points of one colour are joined to each other, in particular to the vertices of that colour. It remains to choose $p$ so that each colour is connected in the union (see the figure).

  • $a,b,c$ have the same colour, say red. If there is a green point, let $p$ be green. Then every green point is joined to $p$. The red points of $pab$ are joined to $a$ and $b$, those of $pbc$ to $b$ and $c$, those of $pca$ to $c$ and $a$; so all red points are joined. If there is no green point, take any $p$: all points are red and every small triangle contains $p$.

  • Two colours, say $a$ red and $b,c$ green. If there is a red interior point, let $p$ be red. Then every red point is joined to $p$. The green points of $pab$ are joined to $b$, those of $pbc$ to $b$ and $c$, those of $pca$ to $c$; so all green points are joined. If there is no red interior point, $a$ is the only red point; take any $p$ (it is green), and every green point is joined to $p$.

This proves the Lemma.

One-coloured hull. Let all $44$ hull vertices $v_1,\dots,v_{44}$ be red. If there is a green point, choose one, $p$ (it is inside the polygon), and join it to all $v_i$. This cuts the polygon into the triangles $pv_iv_{i+1}$; solve each by the Lemma. All green points are joined to $p$; the red points of $pv_iv_{i+1}$ are joined to $v_i$ and $v_{i+1}$, and going around the polygon all red points are joined. If there is no green point, cut the polygon by the diagonals from $v_1$ into the triangles $v_1v_iv_{i+1}$ and solve each: all points are red and every triangle contains $v_1$. By the gluing remark there is no crossing.

Two-coloured hull. We show by induction on the number $h\ge3$ of hull vertices that a set is solvable if its hull vertices form a red block and a green block. For $h=3$ this is the Lemma. Let $h\ge4$. Then some red hull vertex $r$ and some green hull vertex $g$ are not adjacent. The diagonal $rg$ contains no other marked point and cuts the polygon into two convex polygons with fewer vertices; in each of them the vertices again form a red block and a green block. Solve both parts by the induction hypothesis. In the union the red points are joined (both parts contain $r$), the green points are joined (both contain $g$), and there is no crossing by the gluing remark.

So Melo can complete the task exactly when the colour changes at most twice around the polygon, and the number of colourings is \[2^{2026}\Bigl(2+2\binom{44}{2}\Bigr)=\boxed{1894\cdot2^{2026}}.\]
