Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{1000}$ be the integers from $1$ to $1000$ in some order. For which values of $a_1$ can we guarantee that there is a positive integer $k$, $1 \le k \le 1000$, such that $\underbrace{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k}_{k \text{ summands}}$ is a multiple of $3$?

Show the answer

exactly when $a_1\equiv 0$ or $a_1\equiv 2\pmod 3$ (i.e. $a_1\not\equiv1\pmod 3$)

Show the solution

Solution (Following the prefix sums modulo 3)

Answer. Exactly for $a_1\equiv0$ and $a_1\equiv2\pmod 3$.

Among $1,\dots,1000$ there are $333$ multiples of $3$, $334$ numbers $\equiv1$ and $333$ numbers $\equiv2\pmod3$. The total sum is $500500\equiv1\pmod3$. Let $S_k=a_1+\dots+a_k$. Terms divisible by $3$ do not change $S_k$ modulo $3$.

$a_1\equiv0$. Then $k=1$ works.

$a_1\equiv2$. Suppose no $S_k$ is divisible by $3$. List the $667$ terms not divisible by $3$ in their order in the permutation, with residues $r_1=2,r_2,\dots,r_{667}\in\{1,2\}$, and let $\sigma_j$ be the residue of the prefix sum right after the $j$-th of them; by our assumption $\sigma_j\in\{1,2\}$. Since $\sigma_j=\sigma_{j-1}+r_j\not\equiv0$, we must have $r_j\ne 3-\sigma_{j-1}$, i.e. $r_j=\sigma_{j-1}$, and then $\sigma_j\equiv2\sigma_{j-1}\equiv-\sigma_{j-1}$. Starting from $\sigma_1=2$ we get $\sigma_j\equiv(-1)^{j-1}\cdot2$, so $\sigma_{667}\equiv2$. But $\sigma_{667}$ is the residue of the total sum, which is $1$. Contradiction; so some $S_k$ is divisible by $3$.

$a_1\equiv1$. Then nothing can be guaranteed. After $a_1$ write the other $333$ numbers $\equiv1$ and the $333$ numbers $\equiv2$ alternately, starting with one $\equiv1$, and finally all multiples of $3$. The prefix sums are $\equiv1,2,1,2,\dots,2,1$ and then stay $\equiv1$; none is divisible by $3$.

Hence a suitable $k$ is guaranteed exactly when $\boxed{a_1\equiv 0 \text{ or } a_1\equiv 2 \pmod 3}$.

Remark (the same forcing as a count). If no prefix sum is divisible by $3$, the argument above shows that the $667$ residues $\not\equiv0$ must read $r,r,-r,r,-r,\dots,r,-r$ with $r$ the residue of $a_1$: the class of $a_1$ supplies $334$ of them and the other class $333$. Only the class $1$ has $334$ members, which explains at once why $a_1\equiv1$ can avoid a multiple of $3$ and $a_1\equiv2$ cannot.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $\mathbb{Z}^+$ denote the set of positive integers. Determine whether there exists a bijective function $f : \mathbb{Z}^+ \to \mathbb{Z}^+$ satisfying all of the following conditions:

  • (i) $n \mid f(1)+f(2)+\cdots+f(n)$ for every positive integer $n$;

  • (ii) $f(1)+f(2)+\cdots+f(n) \le n^2$ for every positive integer $n$;

  • (iii) $f(1)+f(2)+\cdots+f(n) = n^2$ for infinitely many positive integers $n$.

Note: A function $f : \mathbb{Z}^+ \to \mathbb{Z}^+$ is bijective if every positive integer appears exactly once in the infinite list $f(1), f(2), f(3), \ldots$.

Show the answer

No, no such bijection exists

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two special indices cannot both be large)

Answer: no. Suppose such an $f$ exists. Let $S_n=f(1)+\dots+f(n)$. By (i) and (ii), $S_n=nc_n$ with an integer $1\le c_n\le n$. Call $n$ special if $S_n=n^2$, i.e. $c_n=n$; by (iii) there are infinitely many special $n$. Let $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$.

(a) $c$ is non-decreasing. We have $f(n)=nc_n-(n-1)c_{n-1}$. If $c_n\le c_{n-1}-1$, then $f(n)\le c_{n-1}-n\le -1$, since $c_{n-1}\le n-1$.

(b) Flat and steep steps. With $\Delta_n=c_n-c_{n-1}\ge0$ we get $f(n)=c_{n-1}+n\Delta_n$. So either $\Delta_n=0$ and $f(n)=c_{n-1}$ (a flat step), or $f(n)\ge c_{n-1}+n$.

(c) No two consecutive flat steps: otherwise $f(n)=c_{n-1}=c_n=f(n+1)$.

(d) If $N$ is special, then $\{f(1),\dots,f(N)\}=\{1,\dots,N-1\}\cup\{T_N\}$. For $n>N$ we have $c_{n-1}\ge c_N=N$, so $f(n)\ge N$ by (b). Thus each of $1,\dots,N-1$ is among $f(1),\dots,f(N)$, and the remaining value is $N^2-(1+\dots+(N-1))=T_N$.

(e) If $M\ge2$ is special, then $\{f(1),\dots,f(M-1)\}=\{1,\dots,M-1\}$. Indeed $f(M)=M^2-(M-1)c_{M-1}\ge M^2-(M-1)^2=2M-1>M-1$, so by (d) $f(M)=T_M$ and the other values are $1,\dots,M-1$.

(f) There are no special $N<M$ with $N\ge3$. Suppose there are. By (d) and (e), $T_N\in\{f(1),\dots,f(N)\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M-1\}$, so $M-1\ge T_N\ge N+3$ (as $T_N-N-3=\frac{(N-3)(N+2)}2\ge0$). For $N<n\le M-1$ we have $f(n)\le M-1$; if step $n$ were steep, (b) would give $f(n)\ge c_{n-1}+n\ge N+n$ (as $c_{n-1}\ge c_N=N$ for $n-1\ge N$), i.e. $n\le M-1-N$. Hence every step $n$ with $M-1-N<n\le M-1$ is flat. Both $n=M-2$ and $n=M-1$ qualify (because $N\ge2$ and $M-2>N$), contradicting (c).

So at most one special number is $\ge3$, and there are only finitely many special numbers, contradicting (iii). No such bijection exists.

Solution 2 (One special index and a permutation lemma)

Suppose $f$ exists. Write $S_n=f(1)+\dots+f(n)$; by (i) and (ii), $S_n=nc_n$ with an integer $1\le c_n\le n$. Call $n$ special if $S_n=n^2$, i.e. $c_n=n$; by (iii) there are infinitely many special $n$.

Monotonicity. $f(n)=nc_n-(n-1)c_{n-1}=c_{n-1}+n(c_n-c_{n-1})$. If $c_n<c_{n-1}$, this is at most $c_{n-1}-n<0$, impossible. So $c_1\le c_2\le\cdots$ and $f(n)\ge c_{n-1}$ for every $n\ge2$.

A special $M\ge2$ resets the values. Let $M\ge2$ be special. For $n>M$ we have $f(n)\ge c_{n-1}\ge c_M=M$. Since $f$ is onto, each of $1,\dots,M-1$ is a value $f(n)$ with $n\le M$. Moreover $f(M)=M^2-(M-1)c_{M-1}\ge M^2-(M-1)^2=2M-1>M-1$, so each of $1,\dots,M-1$ is among $f(1),\dots,f(M-1)$. These are $M-1$ distinct numbers, hence $f(1),\dots,f(M-1)$ is a permutation of $1,\dots,M-1$, and by (i) its prefix sums satisfy $n\mid S_n$ for all $n\le M-1$.

Lemma. Let $m\ge1$ and let $a_1,\dots,a_m$ be a permutation of $1,\dots,m$ such that $n\mid a_1+\dots+a_n$ for every $n\le m$. Then $m=1$ or $m=3$.

Proof. The full sum is $\frac{m(m+1)}2$, and $m$ divides it, so $\frac{m+1}2$ is an integer: $m$ is odd. Suppose $m\ge5$ and put $h=\frac{m+1}2$, so that $a_1+\dots+a_m=mh$.

  • $(m-1)\mid a_1+\dots+a_{m-1}=mh-a_m$, and $m\equiv1\pmod{m-1}$, so $a_m\equiv h\pmod{m-1}$. In $[1,m]$ the only number $\equiv h$ is $h$ itself, because $h+(m-1)=\frac{3m-1}2>m$ and $h-(m-1)=\frac{3-m}2<1$. So $a_m=h$.

  • $(m-2)\mid a_1+\dots+a_{m-2}=mh-h-a_{m-1}=(m-1)h-a_{m-1}$, and $m-1\equiv1\pmod{m-2}$, so $a_{m-1}\equiv h\pmod{m-2}$. Again $h$ is the only such number in $[1,m]$, because $h+(m-2)=\frac{3m-3}2>m$ and $h-(m-2)=\frac{5-m}2<1$ for $m\ge5$. So $a_{m-1}=h=a_m$, a contradiction.

Hence $m\le3$ and $m$ is odd. (Both values occur: $1$ and $1,3,2$.) $\square$

Conclusion. For every special $M\ge2$ the lemma applies with $m=M-1$, so $M-1\in\{1,3\}$, i.e. $M\in\{2,4\}$. Thus every special number is $1$, $2$ or $4$, so there are at most three of them, contradicting (iii). No such bijection exists.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Mafalda dreams that she is on an interstellar journey with her friends. During the journey, she discovers a room in the shape of a cube, inside which $2026$ alfajores are floating motionless. She then chooses a vertex of the cube, where she places all the friends accompanying her. Each friend must travel to the opposite vertex of the cube, moving only in directions parallel to its edges and always moving away from the vertex from which they all started. Along the way, each friend collects the alfajores that lie on their path. What is the minimum number of friends Mafalda needs in order to be certain that all the alfajores that were in the cube are collected?

Note: Consider the alfajores and the friends to be points in space.

Show the answer

$1519$

Show the solution

Solution (Four orders, Dilworth, and a double count)

We read the problem as: the alfajores are in fixed known positions, Mafalda then picks the vertex and the routes, and we want the number of friends that suffices for every placement of the $2026$ points. The answer is $1519$.

Model. Let the cube be $[0,1]^3$. For a sign vector $\sigma=(1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3)$, $\sigma_2,\sigma_3\in\{\pm1\}$, write $p\preceq_\sigma q$ if $\sigma_i(q_i-p_i)\ge0$ for $i=1,2,3$; this is a partial order. A route from a vertex to the opposite one that only moves parallel to the edges and always away from the start changes each coordinate monotonically, so the points on it are pairwise comparable in one of these four orders (a vertex and its opposite vertex give opposite orders, hence the same chains). Conversely, the points of any chain (pairwise comparable set) of $\preceq_\sigma$ can be visited by one such route: sort them, go from the starting vertex to the first point, join consecutive points by axis-parallel segments, and go from the last point to the opposite vertex. So with the vertex corresponding to $\sigma$, the number of friends needed is the least number of chains covering all points, which by the lemma below (Dilworth's theorem) equals the largest size $w_\sigma$ of an antichain (a set of pairwise incomparable points).

Lemma (Dilworth). In a finite partially ordered set $Q$, the least number of chains that cover $Q$ equals the largest size of an antichain.

Proof. A chain contains at most one element of an antichain, so at least as many chains as the largest antichain are needed. For the converse we show by induction on $|Q|$ that $Q$ can be split into $w(Q)$ disjoint chains, where $w(Q)$ is the largest antichain size; $Q=\emptyset$ is clear. Let $a$ be a maximal element of $Q$ and $Q'=Q\setminus\{a\}$, $w'=w(Q')$. By induction $Q'$ is split into chains $C_1,\dots,C_{w'}$, and every antichain of size $w'$ in $Q'$ meets each $C_i$ in exactly one element. Each $C_i$ meets such an antichain (one exists), so we may let $x_i$ be the largest element of $C_i$ lying in some antichain of size $w'$ of $Q'$. Then $X=\{x_1,\dots,x_{w'}\}$ is an antichain: if $x_i<x_j$, take an antichain $B$ of size $w'$ containing $x_j$; it meets $C_i$ in some $y\le x_i$, and then $y<x_j$ with $y,x_j\in B$, impossible. If $X\cup\{a\}$ is an antichain, then $w(Q)=w'+1$ and the chains $C_1,\dots,C_{w'},\{a\}$ split $Q$. Otherwise $a$ is comparable with some $x_i$, and $x_i<a$ since $a$ is maximal. The set $K=\{a\}\cup\{z\in C_i: z\le x_i\}$ is a chain, and $Q\setminus K$ has no antichain of size $w'$: such an antichain would lie in $Q'$ and meet $C_i$ in an element above $x_i$, against the choice of $x_i$. By induction $Q\setminus K$ is split into at most $w'-1$ chains; with $K$ that is at most $w'\le w(Q)$ chains. $\square$ The answer is the maximum, over all placements, of $\min_\sigma w_\sigma$.

1519 friends always suffice. Fix a placement and, for each of the four orders, a largest antichain $A_\sigma$. Two distinct points $p,q$ are comparable in some order: choose $\sigma_i$ as the sign of $q_i-p_i$ (with $+$ for $0$), after swapping $p,q$ if necessary so that $\sigma_1=+1$. Hence no two distinct points lie in all four antichains: at most one point is counted four times and each of the other $2025$ points at most three times, so \[ \sum_\sigma w_\sigma=\sum_\sigma|A_\sigma|\le 3\cdot2025+4=6079 . \] So some $w_\sigma\le\lfloor 6079/4\rfloor=1519$; Mafalda chooses that vertex and $1519$ friends (one per chain) collect everything.

1519 friends may be necessary. Let $O=(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$ and $v_\sigma=(1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3)$. Choose $2025$ real numbers $t\ne0$ with pairwise different absolute values, all $|t|<\frac14$, and split them into four groups of sizes $506,506,506,507$, one group for each $\sigma$; the group of $\sigma$ gives the points $G_\sigma=\{O+tv_\sigma\}$. Together with $O$ these are $2026$ points of the cube.

Take $p=O+tv_\sigma$ and $q=O+uv_\tau$. In every coordinate the difference $q-p$ is $\pm(u-t)$ where $\sigma,\tau$ agree and $\pm(u+t)$ where they differ, never $0$; so $p,q$ are comparable in exactly one order $\rho$, given by $\rho_i=$ sign of $(q_i-p_i)/(q_1-p_1)$.

  • If $\sigma=\tau$, then $q-p=(u-t)v_\sigma$ and $\rho=\sigma$. The same holds for $p$ and $O$.

  • If $\sigma,\tau$ differ in exactly one of the coordinates $2,3$, then $\rho$ agrees with both in the other coordinate, so $\rho\in\{\sigma,\tau\}$.

  • If they differ in both, then $q_2-p_2=\tau_2(u+t)$ and $q_3-p_3=\tau_3(u+t)$, so $\rho_2\rho_3=\tau_2\tau_3=\sigma_2\sigma_3$; the only sign vectors with this product are $\sigma$ and $\tau$.

So two points of the configuration are comparable only in the order of one of their groups ($O$ belongs to no group). Consequently all points outside $G_\rho$ form an antichain for $\preceq_\rho$, of size at least $2026-507=1519$. A route meets an antichain in at most one point, so whichever vertex Mafalda chooses, she needs at least $1519$ friends.

Hence the minimum number of friends is $\boxed{1519}$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

A sequence $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ of positive integers is called porteña if it satisfies all of the following conditions:

  • (i) $a_1 < a_2 < a_3 < \cdots$;

  • (ii) $2a_n - n$ is a perfect square for every integer $n \ge 1$;

  • (iii) $a_{n-1} + a_n \le n^2$ for every integer $n \ge 2$.

Determine all porteña sequences.

Show the answer

only $a_n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Squeezing between consecutive triangular numbers)

Let $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ and $s_n=\sqrt{2a_n-n}\ge0$, an integer by (ii). Since $s_n^2=2a_n-n\equiv n\pmod2$, $s_n\equiv n\pmod 2$.

The sequence $a_n=T_n$ works: it is increasing, $2T_n-n=n^2$, and $T_{n-1}+T_n=n^2$. We show it is the only one, proving $a_n=T_n$ by induction.

$n=1$. By (i) and (iii), $2a_1<a_1+a_2\le4$, so $a_1=1=T_1$.

Step. Let $n\ge2$ and $a_{n-1}=T_{n-1}$. By (i) and (iii), \[ T_{n-1}<a_n\le n^2-T_{n-1}=T_n . \] Since $a_n=\frac{n+s_n^2}{2}$, $T_n=\frac{n+n^2}2$ and $T_{n-1}=\frac{n+n^2-2n}{2}$, this means $n^2-2n<s_n^2\le n^2$. So $s_n\le n$, and $s_n>n-2$ because $(n-2)^2\le n^2-2n$ for $n\ge2$. Thus $s_n\in\{n-1,n\}$, and the parity condition gives $s_n=n$, i.e. $a_n=T_n$.

So the only porteña sequence is $\boxed{a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$.

Solution 2 (An upper bound from the next term and a descent)

Let $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ and $s_n=\sqrt{2a_n-n}$, a non-negative integer with $s_n\equiv n\pmod 2$; then $a_n=\frac{n+s_n^2}{2}$ and $a_n=T_n\iff s_n=n$. The sequence $a_n=T_n$ is porteña ($2T_n-n=n^2$, $T_{n-1}+T_n=n^2$).

Upper bound $s_n\le n$. If $s_n>n$ then $s_n\ge n+2$ by parity, so $2a_n\ge n+(n+2)^2$. As $a_{n+1}\ge a_n+1$, \[ a_n+a_{n+1}\ge 2a_n+1\ge (n+2)^2+n+1>(n+1)^2 , \] contradicting (iii) for $n+1$. Hence $s_n\le n$ and $a_n\le T_n$ for all $n$.

Lower bound by descent. For $k=1$ we have $a_1\ge1=T_1$. Suppose $a_k<T_k$ for some $k\ge2$. Then $s_k<k$ (as $s_k\le k$ by the upper bound), so $s_k\le k-2$ by parity, and \[ a_k\le\frac{k+(k-2)^2}{2}=T_k-(2k-2)=T_{k-1}-(k-2)\le T_{k-1}, \] hence $a_{k-1}<a_k\le T_{k-1}$. Repeating, $a_1<T_1=1$, impossible. So $a_n\ge T_n$ for all $n$.

Therefore $a_n=T_n$ for all $n$: the only porteña sequence is $\boxed{a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$.

Solution 3 (A lower bound from (i) and (ii) alone)

Let $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ and $s_n=\sqrt{2a_n-n}$, a non-negative integer by (ii); then $a_n=\frac{n+s_n^2}{2}$. The sequence $a_n=T_n$ is porteña: it is increasing, $2T_n-n=n^2$, and $T_{n-1}+T_n=n^2$.

The square roots increase. By (i), $a_{n+1}-a_n\ge1$, so \[ s_{n+1}^2-s_n^2=2(a_{n+1}-a_n)-1\ge1 . \] Hence $s_{n+1}>s_n$, and since both are integers, $s_{n+1}\ge s_n+1$. Also $s_1^2=2a_1-1\ge1$, so $s_1\ge1$. By induction $s_n\ge n$ for every $n$, that is, \[ a_n=\frac{n+s_n^2}{2}\ge\frac{n+n^2}{2}=T_n\qquad\text{for all } n\ge1 . \]

Condition (iii) closes the gap. For $n\ge2$, \[ n^2\ge a_{n-1}+a_n\ge T_{n-1}+T_n=n^2 , \] so equality holds throughout, and as $a_{n-1}\ge T_{n-1}$ and $a_n\ge T_n$ separately, both are equalities. Taking $n=2,3,4,\dots$ gives $a_n=T_n$ for every $n\ge1$.

So the only porteña sequence is $\boxed{a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB > AC$, inscribed in a circle $\Gamma$. Let $X$ be a variable point on the arc $AC$ of $\Gamma$ not containing $B$, with $X$ different from $A$ and $C$. The line $AX$ meets the line $BC$ at $P$. The line through $P$ parallel to $AB$ and the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $B$ meet at $D$. The line through $P$ parallel to $AC$ and the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $C$ meet at $E$. Prove that the circumcenter of triangle $XDE$ lies on a fixed line as $X$ varies over the arc $AC$ of $\Gamma$.

Note: The circumcenter of a triangle is the center of the circle passing through its three vertices.

Show the answer

proof (the circumcentre lies on the perpendicular bisector of $TF$, where $T$ is the pole of $BC$ and $F$ a fixed point)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A second fixed point from a spiral similarity)

Let $t_B,t_C$ be the tangents to $\Gamma$ at $B,C$; they meet at a point $T$ (they are not parallel, since $\angle BAC<90^\circ$), and $T$ does not lie on line $BC$. We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ modulo $180^\circ$, and put $\theta=\angle(t_B,t_C)$.

Step 1: $\angle(XD,XE)=\theta$, and $T$ lies on the circle $XDE$. Because $PD\parallel AB$ and $P\in AX$, and by the tangent-chord angle at $B$, \[ \angle(PD,PX)=\angle(AB,AX)=\angle(t_B,BX)=\angle(BD,BX), \] so $P,B,D,X$ are concyclic. In the same way $\angle(PE,PX)=\angle(AC,AX)=\angle(CE,CX)$, so $P,C,E,X$ are concyclic. Hence \[ \angle(XD,XE)=\angle(XD,XP)+\angle(XP,XE)=\angle(BD,BP)+\angle(CP,CE)=\angle(t_B,BC)+\angle(BC,t_C)=\theta \] for every position of $X$. Here $X\ne D,E$ (a point of $\Gamma$ other than $B,C$ lies on neither tangent) and $D\ne E$ (otherwise $D=E=T$, so both lines through $P$ pass through $T$ and $P=T\in BC$, which is false); so $X,D,E$ form a triangle. If $D\ne T$ and $E\ne T$, then $\theta=\angle(TD,TE)$, and $T$ lies on the circle $XDE$; if $D=T$ or $E=T$, then $T$ lies on it trivially.

Step 2: a fixed spiral similarity. The map $P\mapsto D$ is the parallel projection of line $BC$ onto $t_B$ in the direction $AB$, and $P\mapsto E$ is the parallel projection onto $t_C$ in the direction $AC$; both are affine. Writing $P=B+\lambda(C-B)$, we get (in complex numbers) $D=B+\lambda u$ and $E=E_0+\lambda w$ with fixed non-zero $u\parallel t_B$, $w\parallel t_C$. Hence the map \[ \sigma(z)=E_0+\frac wu\,(z-B) \] sends $D$ to $E$ for every $\lambda$. Since $t_B\nparallel t_C$, $w/u$ is not real, so $\sigma$ is a spiral similarity with rotation angle $\theta=\angle(t_B,t_C)$ and a unique fixed point $F$. For every position, $D\ne F$ (since $\sigma(D)=E\ne D$) and $E\ne F$ (otherwise $\sigma(D)=F=\sigma(F)$ and $D=F$), so $\angle(FD,FE)=\theta=\angle(XD,XE)$ by Step 1, and $F$ lies on the circle $XDE$.

Moreover $F\ne T$. Take the $\lambda$ for which $D=T$ (the line through $P$ parallel to $AB$ passes through $T$). If $\sigma(T)=T$, then also $E=T$, so the lines through $P$ parallel to $AB$ and to $AC$ both pass through $T$, forcing $P=T\in BC$, which is false.

Step 3: conclusion. By Steps 1 and 2 the circle $XDE$ passes through the two fixed points $T$ and $F$. Hence its centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of $TF$, a fixed line.

Remark. Numerically $F$ is the second intersection of the median $AM$ ($M$ the midpoint of $BC$) with $\Gamma$; this is not needed for the proof.

Solution 2 (The centre moves affinely)

Let $t_B,t_C$ be the tangents at $B,C$ and $T=t_B\cap t_C$ ($t_B\nparallel t_C$ since $\angle BAC<90^\circ$). Directed angles are taken modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: the angle at $X$. From $PD\parallel AB$ and the tangent-chord angle, $\angle(PD,PX)=\angle(AB,AX)=\angle(BD,BX)$, so $P,B,D,X$ are concyclic; similarly $\angle(PE,PX)=\angle(AC,AX)=\angle(CE,CX)$ gives $P,C,E,X$ concyclic. Therefore \[ \angle(XD,XE)=\angle(XD,XP)+\angle(XP,XE)=\angle(BD,BP)+\angle(CP,CE)=\angle(t_B,t_C) \] for every position. Here $X\ne D,E$ ($X$ is on neither tangent) and $D\ne E$ (else $D=E=T$ and $P=T\in BC$, false). When $D\ne T$ and $E\ne T$, the right-hand side is $\angle(TD,TE)$, so $T$ lies on the circle $XDE$; trivially it does also when $D=T$ or $E=T$.

Step 2: $D$ and $E$ depend affinely on $P$. Write $P=B+\lambda(C-B)$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. The point $D$ is the image of $P$ under the parallel projection of line $BC$ onto $t_B$ along $AB$, an affine map; so $D=T+\delta(\lambda)\,e_1$ with a unit vector $e_1$ along $t_B$ and an affine function $\delta(\lambda)$. Likewise $E=T+\varepsilon(\lambda)\,e_2$ with a unit vector $e_2$ along $t_C$ and $\varepsilon$ affine.

Step 3: the centre. Let $O$ be the centre of circle $XDE$. By Step 1 this circle meets $t_B$ at $D$ and $T$ and $t_C$ at $E$ and $T$ (touching when the two points coincide). If $D\ne T$, the projection of $O$ onto the line $TD=t_B$ is the midpoint of $TD$, so $(O-T)\cdot e_1=\frac12\delta(\lambda)$. If $D=T$, Step 1 reads $\angle(XT,XE)=\angle(t_B,TE)$, so by the converse of the tangent-chord theorem $t_B$ touches the circle $XTE$ at $T$; then $OT\perp t_B$ and $(O-T)\cdot e_1=0=\frac12\delta(\lambda)$ still holds. In the same way $(O-T)\cdot e_2=\frac12\varepsilon(\lambda)$ in all cases (with $t_C$ when $E=T$). Since $e_1,e_2$ are linearly independent, these two linear equations determine $O-T$, and the solution depends linearly on the pair $\bigl(\delta(\lambda),\varepsilon(\lambda)\bigr)$, hence affinely on $\lambda$: $O=O_0+\lambda v$ for fixed $O_0,v$. Thus all centres lie on one fixed line ($v\ne0$, because $\delta$ is not constant).

Hence the circumcentre of $XDE$ always lies on a fixed line.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Determine all integers $n > 1$ for which there exist a permutation $d_1, \ldots, d_k$ of all the positive divisors of $n$ and a polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that $P(d_j) = d_{j+1}$ for every $1 \le j \le k-1$.

Show the answer

$n$ is a prime power, or $n=p(2p-1)$ with $p$ and $2p-1$ both prime ($n=6,15,91,703,\dots$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Differences divide differences)

Answer. $n=p^e$ ($p$ prime, $e\ge1$), or $n=p(2p-1)$ with $p$ and $2p-1$ both prime.

Constructions. For $n=p^e$ take $1,p,p^2,\dots,p^e$ and $P(x)=px$. For $n=pq$ with $q=2p-1$ prime take $1,p,q,pq$ and $P(x)=x^2-px+q$: $P(1)=p$, $P(p)=q$, $P(q)=q(q-p+1)=pq$.

Necessity. Let $n$ be divisible by two primes; let $p<q$ be its two smallest prime factors, $D$ its set of divisors, $k=|D|\ge4$, and $m=n/p$ its largest proper divisor ($m\ge q>p$). Put $\varphi(d_j)=d_{j+1}$ for $j\le k-1$; $\varphi$ is defined on $D'=D\setminus\{d_k\}$, injective, $\varphi(x)\ne x$, and there are no “returns” $\varphi(x)=y,\ \varphi(y)=x$ (that would repeat a divisor in the list). The key fact is \[ (\star)\qquad x-y\mid P(x)-P(y)\quad\text{for integers }x\ne y,\qquad\text{so } x-y\mid\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\ \text{ for } x\ne y\in D'. \] In particular $|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\ge|x-y|$. Let $e_1<e_2<\dots<e_k$ be the divisors in increasing order, and $g_j=d_{j+1}-d_j$.

Lemma (an ordering starting at $1$ is increasing). If $d_1=1$, then $d_i=e_i$ for all $i$.

Proof. By $(\star)$ for $d_{j+1},d_j$ we get $g_j\mid g_{j+1}$ for $j\le k-2$, so $g_j$ divides all later differences and $d_j,d_{j+1},\dots,d_k$ are congruent modulo $|g_j|$. We show $d_i=e_i$ for $i\le t$ by induction on $t$; for $t=1$ this is $d_1=1=e_1$. If $d_i=e_i$ for all $i\le t<k$, then the remaining divisors $d_{t+1},\dots,d_k$ all exceed $d_t=e_t$ and are $\equiv d_t\pmod{|g_t|}$, hence are $\ge d_t+|g_t|$. As $d_{t+1}=d_t+g_t\le d_t+|g_t|$, $d_{t+1}$ is the smallest of them, i.e. $d_{t+1}=e_{t+1}$. $\square$

Plan. We show: $d_k=n$ (Step 1); then $\varphi(1)=n$ or $\varphi(m)=n$ (Step 2). If $\varphi(1)=n$, i.e. $d_{k-1}=1$, the list is $q,p,1,pq$ with $q=2p-1$ (Case A). If $\varphi(m)=n$, then $d_1=1$ (Case B), so by the lemma the list is increasing, and the mirror symmetry $e_{k+1-i}=n/e_i$ finishes (Step 3).

Step 1: $d_k=n$. If $1,n\in D'$, then $|\varphi(1)-\varphi(n)|\ge n-1$ forces $\{\varphi(1),\varphi(n)\}=\{1,n\}$, so $\varphi(1)=n$, $\varphi(n)=1$: a return. If $d_k=1$, then $n,p\in D'$ and $|\varphi(n)-\varphi(p)|$ is a non-zero multiple of $n-p$, less than $2(n-p)$ (as $n\ge 2p$); two divisors at distance exactly $n-p$ must be $p$ and $n$ (if the larger one is $\le m\le\frac n2$, the smaller would be $\le p-\frac n2<0$), so again $\varphi(p)=n$, $\varphi(n)=p$: a return. Hence $d_k=n$ and $D'=D\setminus\{n\}$; all its elements are $\le m$.

Step 2: $\varphi(1)=n$ or $\varphi(m)=n$. Otherwise $\varphi(1),\varphi(m)\in[1,m]$ differ by a non-zero multiple of $m-1$, so $\{\varphi(1),\varphi(m)\}=\{1,m\}$; as $\varphi(1)\ne1$, this is a return. Note that $n=pm\equiv p\pmod{m-1}$.

Case A: $\varphi(1)=n$. By $(\star)$, $m-1\mid n-\varphi(m)$, so $\varphi(m)\equiv p\pmod{m-1}$. As $\varphi(m)\notin\{n,m\}$, $1\le\varphi(m)\le m-1$, and these are pairwise incongruent, so $\varphi(m)=p$. Next $m-p\mid\varphi(m)-\varphi(p)=p-\varphi(p)$, where $\varphi(p)\notin\{n,p,m\}$ ($m$ would be a return), so $1\le\varphi(p)<m=p+(m-p)$. Thus $\varphi(p)\le p-(m-p)$, i.e. $m\le 2p-1$. A composite divisor of $n$ is $\ge p^2\ge2p$, so $m$ is a prime: $m=q<2p$ and $n=pq$. The list is $q,p,1,pq$, and $(\star)$ gives $q-p\mid p-1$ and $p-1\mid pq-1=p(q-1)+(p-1)$, i.e. $p-1\mid q-1$ and hence $p-1\mid q-p$. So $q-p=p-1$: $n=p(2p-1)$.

Case B: $\varphi(m)=n$. Then $m-1\mid n-\varphi(1)$, so $\varphi(1)\equiv p\pmod{m-1}$ with $2\le\varphi(1)\le m$; hence $\varphi(1)=p$. If $1$ had a predecessor $z$ ($\varphi(z)=1$, $z\ne1,m$), then $z-1\mid1-p$ gives $z\le p$, so $z=p$, a return. Hence $d_1=1$.

Step 3: the increasing case. By the lemma, $d_i=e_i$ for all $i$. Now $g_i=e_{i+1}-e_i$, $g_i\mid g_{i+1}$; put $\rho_i=g_{i+1}/g_i\in\mathbb Z$. Since $e_{k+1-i}=n/e_i$, $g_{k-i}=\frac{n\,g_i}{e_ie_{i+1}}$, and $g_{k-i-1}\mid g_{k-i}$ says that $\frac{g_{k-i}}{g_{k-i-1}}=\frac{e_{i+2}}{\rho_ie_i}$ is an integer: \[ (\diamond)\qquad \rho_i\,e_i\mid e_{i+2}\qquad(1\le i\le k-2). \] Here $e_2=p$ and $e_3\in\{p^2,q\}$ (a divisor $>p$ is either a prime, hence $\ge q$, or composite, hence $\ge p^2$; a composite divisor below $q$ is a power of $p$).

  • $e_3=p^2$: let $j\ge2$ be largest with $e_{i+1}=p^i$ for all $i\le j$. As $n$ is not a prime power, $w=e_{j+2}$ exists and is not a power of $p$; also $w\le q$ (all divisors $\le p^j$ are powers of $p$, so $q>p^j$). By $(\diamond)$, $p^{j-1}\mid w$ with $j-1\ge1$; $w$ also has a prime factor $\ge q$, so $w\ge pq>q$, a contradiction.

  • $e_3=q$: $\rho_1=\frac{q-p}{p-1}$ and by $(\diamond)$ $\rho_1\mid q$, so $\rho_1=1$ ($\rho_1=q$ would give $q(2-p)=p$). Thus $q=2p-1$. If $n\ne pq$, then $q\mid m$, $m\ne q$, so $m\ge pq$. Apply $(\star)$ to $m=e_{k-1}$ and $p=e_2$, with images $n$ and $q$: $m-p\mid n-q\equiv p^2-q=(p-1)^2$ (mod $m-p$), so $m-p\le(p-1)^2$; but $m-p\ge pq-p=2p(p-1)>(p-1)^2$. Hence $n=pq=p(2p-1)$.

In all cases $n$ is a prime power or $n=p(2p-1)$ with $p,2p-1$ prime, and these all work: \[ \boxed{\,n=p^e\ (p\text{ prime},\ e\ge1)\quad\text{or}\quad n=p(2p-1)\ (p,\ 2p-1\text{ prime})\,} \]

Solution 2 (Blocks of multiples and a second difference)

Answer. $n=p^e$ ($p$ prime, $e\ge1$), or $n=p(2p-1)$ with $p$ and $2p-1$ both prime.

Constructions. For $n=p^e$ take the list $1,p,p^2,\dots,p^e$ and $P(x)=px$. For $n=pq$ with $q=2p-1$ prime take the list $1,p,q,pq$ and $P(x)=x^2-px+q$: then $P(1)=p$, $P(p)=q$ and $P(q)=q(q-p+1)=pq$.

Two facts about a polynomial $P$ with integer coefficients.

  • (a) For integers $x\ne y$, $x-y\mid P(x)-P(y)$, since $x-y\mid x^i-y^i$ for every $i\ge1$.

  • (b) For distinct integers $u,v,w$, the slopes $L_1=\frac{P(v)-P(u)}{v-u}$ and $L_2=\frac{P(w)-P(v)}{w-v}$ are integers by (a), and $w-u\mid L_2-L_1$. Indeed, dividing by the monic polynomial $x-u$ gives $P(x)-P(u)=(x-u)R(x)$ with $R$ having integer coefficients, and $R(v)=L_1$; dividing $R(x)-L_1$ by $x-v$ gives $R(x)-L_1=(x-v)Q(x)$ with $Q$ having integer coefficients. So $P(x)=P(u)+L_1(x-u)+(x-u)(x-v)Q(x)$, and substituting $x=w$ gives $L_2=L_1+(w-u)Q(w)$.

Set-up. Let $n$ have at least two distinct prime factors, let $p$ be its smallest prime factor and $m=n/p$ its largest proper divisor. Another prime factor $r>p$ of $n$ divides $m$, so $m>p$; also $n=pm\ge2m$, so $n-m\ge m$. Write $\varphi(d_j)=d_{j+1}$ for $j\le k-1$, where $k\ge4$ is the number of divisors. Since the $d_j$ are distinct, $\varphi(x)\ne x$, $\varphi$ is injective, and there is no return $\varphi(x)=y$, $\varphi(y)=x$ (it would repeat $x$ in the list). By (a), $x-y\mid\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)$ for any two divisors $x\ne y$ other than $d_k$.

Step 1: $d_k=n$. If neither $1$ nor $n$ is last, then $\varphi(1),\varphi(n)$ are distinct numbers in $[1,n]$ whose difference is a multiple of $n-1$, so $\{\varphi(1),\varphi(n)\}=\{1,n\}$; as $\varphi(1)\ne1$, this is the return $\varphi(1)=n$, $\varphi(n)=1$. Suppose $d_k=1$. If $\varphi(m)$ and $\varphi(n)$ were both $\le m$, their difference would be non-zero, smaller than $m\le n-m$, and a multiple of $n-m$, which is impossible. As $\varphi(n)\ne n$, we get $\varphi(m)=n$. Then $n-m\mid n-\varphi(n)$, so $\varphi(n)\equiv n\equiv m\pmod{n-m}$ with $1\le\varphi(n)\le m$; since $m-(n-m)\le0$, the only such number is $m$, and $\varphi(n)=m$ is a return. Hence $d_k=n$, and every value of $\varphi$ other than $\varphi(d_{k-1})=n$ is at most $m$.

Step 2: $d_{k-1}\in\{1,m\}$, and the other one is sent to $p$. If $\varphi(1),\varphi(m)$ were both $\le m$, they would differ by a non-zero multiple of $m-1$, so $\{\varphi(1),\varphi(m)\}=\{1,m\}$, a return as before. So $n=\varphi(1)$ or $n=\varphi(m)$. Let $y$ be the other one of $1,m$. Then $m-1\mid n-\varphi(y)$ and $n=pm\equiv p\pmod{m-1}$, so $\varphi(y)\equiv p\pmod{m-1}$ with $1\le\varphi(y)\le m$. Since $1<p<m$, the only such number is $p$: $\varphi(y)=p$.

Case A: $\varphi(1)=n$ and $\varphi(m)=p$. The divisor $p$ is not last and $\varphi(p)\notin\{p,n\}$, so $1\le\varphi(p)\le m$, and $m-p\mid\varphi(m)-\varphi(p)=p-\varphi(p)$. If $m\ge2p$, the only numbers in $[1,m]$ congruent to $p$ modulo $m-p$ are $p$ and $m$ (as $2p-m\le0$ and $2m-p>m$); $\varphi(p)=p$ is excluded and $\varphi(p)=m$ is a return. So $m<2p$. Every prime factor of a composite divisor of $n$ is at least $p$, so such a divisor is at least $p^2\ge2p$; hence $m$ is a prime $q>p$, $n=pq$, and the list is $q,p,1,pq$. By (a), $q-p\mid p-1$ (from $q,p$ with images $p,1$) and $p-1\mid pq-1$ (from $p,1$ with images $1,pq$). As $pq-1=q(p-1)+(q-1)$, we get $p-1\mid q-1$, hence $p-1\mid(q-1)-(p-1)=q-p$. Two positive integers dividing each other are equal: $q-p=p-1$, so $q=2p-1$ and $n=p(2p-1)$.

Case B: $\varphi(m)=n$ and $\varphi(1)=p$.

  • $d_1=1$. If $\varphi(z)=1$ for a divisor $z\ne1$, then $z-1\mid\varphi(z)-\varphi(1)=1-p$, so $2\le z\le p$, hence $z=p$; then $\varphi(1)=p$, $\varphi(p)=1$ is a return.

  • Multiples of a divisor of $m$ form a final block. Let $d\mid m$ and let $x\ne n$ be a divisor of $n$ that is a multiple of $d$. By (a), $x-m\mid P(x)-P(m)$, and $d\mid x-m$, so $d\mid\varphi(x)-n$; as $d\mid n$, also $d\mid\varphi(x)$. Thus after the first multiple of $d$ in the list, every later entry is a multiple of $d$: the multiples of $d$ fill the last positions of the list.

  • $p\nmid m$. Otherwise apply the previous point with $d=p$: the list starts $1,p$, so every entry after $1$ is a multiple of $p$. But a prime factor $r\ne p$ of $n$ is a divisor that is neither $1$ nor a multiple of $p$.

  • $m$ is a power of a prime. Let $r$ be a prime factor of $m$ and $d=m/r$. Since $r\ne p$, the number $n/d=pr$ has exactly the four divisors $1,p,r,pr$, so the multiples of $d$ among the divisors of $n$ are exactly $d,\ pd,\ rd=m,\ prd=n$. By the previous points they are the last four entries, and the last two are $m,n$; so $d_{k-2}\in\{m/r,\ pm/r\}$. If $r'\ne r$ were another prime factor of $m$, then also $d_{k-2}\in\{m/r',\ pm/r'\}$. But $m/r\ne m/r'$, $pm/r\ne pm/r'$, and $m/r=pm/r'$ or $pm/r=m/r'$ would mean $r'=pr$ or $r=pr'$, impossible for primes. So $m=q^e$ for a prime $q>p$ and some $e\ge1$, and with $d=q^{e-1}$ the list ends with $d,pd,qd,pqd$ or with $pd,d,qd,pqd$.

  • The order $pd,d,qd,pqd$ is impossible. Here $\varphi(d)=qd$ and $\varphi(qd)=pqd$, so by (a) $(q-1)d\mid pqd-qd=q(p-1)d$, i.e. $q-1\mid q(p-1)$. As $q-1$ and $q$ are coprime, $q-1\mid p-1$, but $0<p-1<q-1$.

  • The order $d,pd,qd,pqd$. Apply (b) to $u=d$, $v=pd$, $w=qd$, whose images are $pd,qd,pqd$: \[ L_1=\frac{qd-pd}{pd-d}=\frac{q-p}{p-1},\qquad L_2=\frac{pqd-qd}{qd-pd}=\frac{q(p-1)}{q-p} \] are integers with $L_1L_2=q$. So $L_1$ is a positive divisor of the prime $q$, and $L_1\le q-p<q$; hence $L_1=1$, $q=2p-1$ and $L_2=q$. By (b), $w-u=(q-1)d$ divides $L_2-L_1=q-1$, so $d=1$, i.e. $e=1$ and $n=pq=p(2p-1)$.

In both cases $n=p(2p-1)$ with $p$ and $q=2p-1$ prime. Together with the constructions, the answer is \[ \boxed{\,n=p^e\ (p\text{ prime},\ e\ge1)\quad\text{or}\quad n=p(2p-1)\ (p,\ 2p-1\text{ prime})\,} \]
