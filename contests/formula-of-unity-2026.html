Grade R5

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

Arthur is playing “Battleship” with a friend. In this game, one player places $10$ ships (four $1\times1$, three $1\times2$, two $1\times3$ and one $1\times4$) on a $10\times10$ board so that the ships do not touch each other, not even at corners, and the other player names cells one at a time in order to “sink” these ships. Arthur has already made several moves (see the figure; $\bullet$ – hit, $\otimes$ – miss). How many valid ship placements are there?

Show the answer

$2$

Show the solution – Forced ships, seven groups, counting sizes

Cells are named by column letter and row number. On this board the hits are A1, B1, J1, E2, E3, J3, J4, A5, F5, J5, C6, D6, A7, J7, B9, and the misses are C1, I1, A3, G5, A6, F6, A9, C9, B10. Two simple facts are used all the time.

  • Two occupied cells with a common side belong to the same ship, because different ships do not touch.

  • A cell that touches a hit cell only at a corner is empty: it cannot lie in the same (straight) ship as that hit cell, and it cannot lie in another ship, because ships do not touch even at corners.

Step 1: three ships are forced. If J2 were occupied, then J1, J2, J3, J4, J5 would be five occupied cells in one column, hence one ship of length at least $5$, which does not exist. In the same way J6 is empty (otherwise J3–J7 would be one ship). So J3, J4, J5 form a $1\times3$ ship. The cells B6 and E6 touch the hits A5 and F5 at corners, so they are empty, and C6, D6 form a $1\times2$ ship. The cells B7 and A8 touch C6 and B9 at corners, and A6 is a miss, so A7 is a $1\times1$ ship.

Step 2: the other seven ships. One $1\times4$, one $1\times3$, two $1\times2$ and three $1\times1$ ships remain: seven ships. By the corner rule the following cells are also empty: A2 (touches B1), D1 and F1 (touch E2), F4 (touches E3), E4 (touches F5), E5 (touches D6), B5 (touches C6), B8 (touches A7), I5 (touches J4). Ignoring the misses for a moment, each remaining hit lies in a ship of one of these forms:

  • (a) through A1, B1: A1B1 or A1–C1;

  • (b) through E2, E3: E2E3 or E1–E3;

  • (c) through A5: A5, A4A5 or A3–A5;

  • (d) through F5: F5, F5G5, F5–H5, F5F6, F5–F7 or F5–F8;

  • (e) through J1: J1, I1J1, H1–J1 or G1–J1;

  • (f) through B9: B9, B9B10, or a horizontal ship in row 9 containing B9 (A9B9, B9C9, A9–C9, B9–D9, A9–D9, B9–E9);

  • (g) through J7: J7, J7J8, J7–J9, J7–J10, I7J7, H7–J7 or G7–J7.

These seven areas are far apart, so no ship contains hits of two different groups. Seven groups need seven different ships, and exactly seven ships are left; so each remaining ship is the ship of one group and there are no other ships. Conversely, one placement chosen for each group gives a valid arrangement exactly when the sizes are $4,3,2,2,1,1,1$ in some order and no two chosen ships touch. Comparing the areas (and the three forced ships), ships of different groups can touch only in two ways: G7 (in G7–J7) touches F6, F7, F8 (in a vertical ship of group (d)), and E9 (in B9–E9) touches F8 (in F5–F8). The second case would need two $1\times4$ ships, so only the first one can matter.

Step 3: the misses of this grade. The miss C1 leaves only A1B1 for (a); the miss A3 leaves A5 or A4A5 for (c); the misses G5 and F6 leave only F5 for (d); the miss I1 leaves only J1 for (e); the misses A9, C9, B10 leave only B9 for (f); groups (b) and (g) keep all their forms.

Step 4: counting. Groups (d), (e), (f) are $1\times1$ ships, so they use up the three $1\times1$ ships, and (a), (b), (c), (g) get the sizes $4,3,2,2$. Group (a) is A1B1 of size $2$, and (c) has size at most $2$, so (c) is the other $1\times2$ ship A4A5. Then (b), whose sizes can only be $2$ or $3$, is the $1\times3$ ship E1–E3, and (g) is the $1\times4$ ship: J7–J10 or G7–J7 (here G7 touches nothing, because (d) is just F5). Hence there are exactly $\boxed{2}$ valid placements.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

A machine takes as input a natural number greater than $10$ and finds the sum of the squares of all its digits. Then, if the resulting number has at least $2$ digits, the machine finds the sum of the squares of its digits, and repeats these operations until it gets a one-digit number, which it returns as the result (for example, $24$ turns into $2^2+4^2=20$, which turns into $2^2+0^2=4$, and the machine returns $4$). Is there a number consisting only of even digits for which the machine returns an odd one-digit number?

Show the answer

Yes (for example $222\to12\to5$)

Show the solution – An explicit example

Yes. Take the number $222$, all of whose digits are even. The machine computes \[2^2+2^2+2^2=12,\qquad 1^2+2^2=5,\] and returns the odd one-digit number $5$. (Another example: $28\to 4+64=68\to 36+64=100\to 1+0+0=1$.)

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Truth and Lies

In a country inhabited only by knights, who always tell the truth, and liars, who always lie, people are preparing to celebrate Independence Day. Two residents saw a poster showing the two-digit age of the country, and told you the following.

  • First resident: 1) “The age is divisible by $5$.” 2) “The age is not divisible by $2$.”

  • Second resident: 1) “The age is divisible by $10$.” 2) “The sum of the digits of the age is not $7$.”

How old can the country be? Find all possibilities.

Show the answer

$16$, $25$, $34$ or $52$

Show the solution – Both statements of a resident are true or both false

Let $N$ be the age. A knight's two statements are both true and a liar's two statements are both false, so for each resident the two statements are both true or both false.

First resident. Either $N$ is divisible by $5$ and odd, that is, $N$ ends in $5$; or $N$ is not divisible by $5$ and is even.

Second resident. Either $N$ is divisible by $10$ and its digit sum is not $7$; or $N$ is not divisible by $10$ and its digit sum is $7$.

In both cases for the first resident, $N$ is not divisible by $10$ (a number ending in $5$ is odd; and a number not divisible by $5$ is not divisible by $10$). So the second resident is a liar and the digit sum of $N$ is $7$. The two-digit numbers with digit sum $7$ are \[16,\ 25,\ 34,\ 43,\ 52,\ 61,\ 70 .\] Those ending in $5$: only $25$. Those that are even and not divisible by $5$: $16,34,52$.

Check: for $25$ the first resident is a knight (divisible by $5$, odd) and the second a liar (not divisible by $10$, digit sum $7$); for $16$, $34$, $52$ both residents are liars. So the country can be $\boxed{16,\ 25,\ 34\text{ or }52}$ years old.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

The number $2026$ has been cut out of grid paper along the grid lines (see the figure). Cut one or more of the digits along the grid lines and assemble a $5\times8$ rectangle from all the resulting pieces. The total length of all cuts must not exceed $4$ (the side of a cell is $1$). Draw how the digits are cut into pieces, and then how the rectangle is assembled from these pieces.

Show the answer

construction (cut the second $2$ into four pieces, total cut length $3$)

Show the solution – Cut only the second 2

Number the rows $1$–$5$ from the top and the columns $1$–$8$ from the left in the $5\times8$ rectangle. In the figure, the second digit $2$ is cut into four pieces:

  • its right column of the top three rows (a straight $1\times3$ piece),

  • its left column of the bottom three rows (another straight $1\times3$ piece),

  • its top-left cell and its bottom-right cell (two single cells).

Only three unit segments are cut (between the two cells of row $1$, of row $3$ and of row $5$ of that digit), so the total length of the cuts is $3\le 4$. The other three digits are not cut.

Assembly. Put the first $2$ as it is in columns $1$–$2$; it covers these columns except the cells (row $2$, column $1$) and (row $4$, column $2$). Turn the $6$ upside down (rotate it by $180^\circ$) and put it in columns $3$–$5$: its notch is now in row $4$, columns $3$–$4$, open to the left, and its hole is the cell (row $2$, column $4$). Put the $0$ in columns $6$–$8$; its hole is column $7$, rows $2$–$4$. The uncovered cells are:

  • row $4$, columns $2,3,4$ (the gap of the $2$ next to the notch of the $6$): a horizontal $1\times3$ strip;

  • column $7$, rows $2,3,4$: a vertical $1\times3$ strip;

  • the single cells (row $2$, column $1$) and (row $2$, column $4$).

These are filled exactly by the four pieces of the second $2$ (one straight piece is turned to lie horizontally). The cell counts agree: $8+12+12+8=40=5\cdot8$. The construction is shown in the figure.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

The digits $1$, $2$ and $3$ are written on a board. Remarkably, their sum is divisible by each of them. How can three more digits be written on the board so that all six digits are different and their sum is divisible by each of them? Find all ways and prove that there are no others.

Show the answer

the digits $4,6,8$ (sum $24$)

Show the solution – The sum is a multiple of 6

The digit $0$ cannot be used, since a positive sum is not divisible by $0$. Let the new digits be $x,y,z$, distinct and chosen from $4,5,\dots,9$, and let $S=6+x+y+z$. Since $S$ is divisible by $1,2,3$, it is divisible by $6$.

The smallest possible value of $x+y+z$ is $4+5+6=15$ and the largest is $7+8+9=24$, so $21\le S\le30$ and $S=24$ or $S=30$.

  • $S=30$: then $x+y+z=24$, so $\{x,y,z\}=\{7,8,9\}$; but $30$ is not divisible by $7$.

  • $S=24$: then $x+y+z=18$, so $\{x,y,z\}$ is $\{4,5,9\}$, $\{4,6,8\}$ or $\{5,6,7\}$. The sum $24$ is not divisible by $5$, so only $\{4,6,8\}$ remains, and indeed $24$ is divisible by $1,2,3,4,6,8$.

So the only way is to write the digits $\boxed{4,\ 6,\ 8}$ (the sum is then $24$).

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 52: Solid Geometry and Spatial Visualization · Nets and Folding

A regular octahedron is a polyhedron made up of $8$ triangles with equal sides. An octahedron with edge $1$ was cut along several edges and unfolded onto the plane in one piece so that the faces do not overlap and no two faces touch along a segment unless they remained glued along it. What can the perimeter of the net be? Find all possibilities and prove that there are no others.

Show the answer

$10$

Show the solution – Cut edges form a spanning tree

Call the edges along which the polyhedron was cut cut edges. We first prove three facts.

(1) The perimeter of the net is twice the total length of the cut edges. A glued edge lies inside the net. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

(2) Every vertex of the polyhedron is an end of some cut edge. At each vertex of the octahedron four equilateral triangles meet, with angle sum $4\cdot60^\circ=240^\circ<360^\circ$. If no edge at a vertex were cut, all faces at that vertex would stay glued around it in the net, so their angles would have to fill the full angle $360^\circ$ around a point of the plane. This is impossible.

(3) The cut edges form a single tree containing all 6 vertices, so there are exactly 5 cut edges. The cut edges contain no closed path: a closed path of cuts would separate the surface into two parts and the net would not be in one piece. So the cut edges form a forest; let it have $k$ components $T_1,\dots,T_k$, with $v_1,\dots,v_k$ vertices ($v_1+\dots+v_k=6$ by (2), and each $T_i$ has $v_i-1\ge1$ edges). Walking once around the tree $T_i$ on the surface, we pass along each of its edges twice; in the net this walk is a closed broken line $\gamma_i$ formed by $2(v_i-1)$ boundary segments, with the net on its left. At a vertex $V$ of $T_i$ the cut edges at $V$ split the faces at $V$ into wedges; at the corner of $\gamma_i$ belonging to a wedge of angle $\omega$ the walk turns left by $180^\circ-\omega$, and the wedges at $V$ together contain all face angles at $V$. Hence the total turning of $\gamma_i$ is \[\tau_i=180^\circ\cdot2(v_i-1)-\sum_{V\in T_i}\alpha_V ,\] where $\alpha_V$ is the sum of the face angles at $V$. Since faces neither overlap nor touch along segments, $\gamma_i$ never crosses itself; it can only touch itself at isolated points. Push it slightly into the net: near a point where it touches itself, each pass through that point is pushed into its own wedge of faces (or into its own face, if the pass runs along a side there), and these do not overlap, so the pushed curve is a simple closed curve; it has the same total turning (a corner where the net angle exceeds $180^\circ$ becomes a small arc that turns by the same angle). The total turning of a simple closed curve made of segments and arcs is $+360^\circ$ or $-360^\circ$. As every $\alpha_V<360^\circ$, we get $\tau_i>360^\circ(v_i-1)-360^\circ v_i=-360^\circ$, so $\tau_i=+360^\circ$ for every $i$. Adding these $k$ equalities, \[360^\circ k=360^\circ\,(6-k)-\sum_{V}\alpha_V=360^\circ\,(6-k)-1440^\circ,\] because the sum of all face angles of the polyhedron is $8\cdot180^\circ=1440^\circ$. This gives $720^\circ k=720^\circ$, so $k=1$: the cut edges form one tree with all 6 vertices and 5 edges.

Conclusion. All edges have length $1$, so by (1) and (3) the perimeter of every such net is $2\cdot5=10$. Such a net exists: the figure shows one (a strip of eight triangles), in which faces meet only along glued edges or at single points. So the perimeter can only be $\boxed{10}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

What is the smallest number of cells of a $3\times2026$ board that must be colored so that every rectangle of $4$ cells contains at least one colored cell? Do not forget to prove that this number is indeed the smallest.

Show the answer

$1519$

Show the solution – Blocks of four columns

Call the rectangles of $4$ cells forbidden: in a $3\times2026$ board they are the $2\times2$ squares and the horizontal $1\times4$ strips.

At least $1519$ cells are needed. Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$, the board splits into $506$ blocks of size $3\times4$ and one block of size $3\times2$. In a $3\times4$ block each of the three rows is a $1\times4$ strip, so the block contains at least $3$ colored cells. The $3\times2$ block contains a $2\times2$ square, so it contains at least one colored cell. Hence at least $3\cdot506+1=1519$ cells are colored.

$1519$ cells suffice. Number the columns $1,\dots,2026$ and the rows top, middle, bottom. Color the middle cell of every column $1,5,9,\dots,2025$ (these are the columns $\equiv1\pmod 4$; there are $507$ of them) and the top and bottom cells of every column $3,7,11,\dots,2023$ (the columns $\equiv3\pmod4$; $506$ columns, $1012$ cells). In total $507+1012=1519$ cells are colored (see the figure).

  • Each row has a colored cell in every fourth column, so every horizontal $1\times4$ strip contains one. (In the middle row the colored columns are $1,5,\dots,2025$; in the other rows $3,7,\dots,2023$; any four consecutive columns between $1$ and $2026$ contain one of each kind.)

  • A $2\times2$ square occupies two consecutive columns, one of which is odd. If that column is $\equiv1\pmod4$, its middle cell is colored and lies in the square (every $2\times2$ square contains the middle row). If it is $\equiv3\pmod4$, its top and bottom cells are colored, and the square contains one of them.

So the smallest number is $\boxed{1519}$.

Grade R6

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

Arthur is playing “Battleship” with a friend. In this game, one player places $10$ ships (four $1\times1$, three $1\times2$, two $1\times3$ and one $1\times4$) on a $10\times10$ board so that the ships do not touch each other, not even at corners, and the other player names cells one at a time in order to “sink” these ships. Arthur has already made several moves (see the figure; $\bullet$ – hit, $\otimes$ – miss). How many valid ship placements are there?

Show the answer

$6$

Show the solution – Forced ships, seven groups, counting sizes

Cells are named by column letter and row number. On this board the hits are A1, B1, J1, E2, E3, J3, J4, A5, F5, J5, C6, D6, A7, J7, B9, and the misses are I1, G5, A6, F6, A9, C9, B10. Two simple facts are used all the time.

  • Two occupied cells with a common side belong to the same ship, because different ships do not touch.

  • A cell that touches a hit cell only at a corner is empty: it cannot lie in the same (straight) ship as that hit cell, and it cannot lie in another ship, because ships do not touch even at corners.

Step 1: three ships are forced. If J2 were occupied, then J1, J2, J3, J4, J5 would be five occupied cells in one column, hence one ship of length at least $5$, which does not exist. In the same way J6 is empty (otherwise J3–J7 would be one ship). So J3, J4, J5 form a $1\times3$ ship. The cells B6 and E6 touch the hits A5 and F5 at corners, so they are empty, and C6, D6 form a $1\times2$ ship. The cells B7 and A8 touch C6 and B9 at corners, and A6 is a miss, so A7 is a $1\times1$ ship.

Step 2: the other seven ships. One $1\times4$, one $1\times3$, two $1\times2$ and three $1\times1$ ships remain: seven ships. By the corner rule the following cells are also empty: A2 (touches B1), D1 and F1 (touch E2), F4 (touches E3), E4 (touches F5), E5 (touches D6), B5 (touches C6), B8 (touches A7), I5 (touches J4). Ignoring the misses for a moment, each remaining hit lies in a ship of one of these forms:

  • (a) through A1, B1: A1B1 or A1–C1;

  • (b) through E2, E3: E2E3 or E1–E3;

  • (c) through A5: A5, A4A5 or A3–A5;

  • (d) through F5: F5, F5G5, F5–H5, F5F6, F5–F7 or F5–F8;

  • (e) through J1: J1, I1J1, H1–J1 or G1–J1;

  • (f) through B9: B9, B9B10, or a horizontal ship in row 9 containing B9 (A9B9, B9C9, A9–C9, B9–D9, A9–D9, B9–E9);

  • (g) through J7: J7, J7J8, J7–J9, J7–J10, I7J7, H7–J7 or G7–J7.

These seven areas are far apart, so no ship contains hits of two different groups. Seven groups need seven different ships, and exactly seven ships are left; so each remaining ship is the ship of one group and there are no other ships. Conversely, one placement chosen for each group gives a valid arrangement exactly when the sizes are $4,3,2,2,1,1,1$ in some order and no two chosen ships touch. Comparing the areas (and the three forced ships), ships of different groups can touch only in two ways: G7 (in G7–J7) touches F6, F7, F8 (in a vertical ship of group (d)), and E9 (in B9–E9) touches F8 (in F5–F8). The second case would need two $1\times4$ ships, so only the first one can matter.

Step 3: the misses of this grade. The misses G5 and F6 leave only F5 for (d); the miss I1 leaves only J1 for (e); the misses A9, C9, B10 leave only B9 for (f). Groups (a), (b), (c), (g) keep all their forms.

Counting method. Groups (a) and (b) can only be $1\times2$ or $1\times3$ ships, with exactly one placement for each size; every group (c)–(g) can be a $1\times1$ ship in exactly one way (the hit cell alone). Hence a placement is obtained as follows: (a) and (b) take two of the sizes $3,2,2$; one more group $X$ among (c)–(g) takes the remaining one of these sizes; another group $Y$ among (c)–(g) is the $1\times4$ ship; the other three groups are $1\times1$ ships. Write $t_s(Z)$ for the number of placements of group $Z$ as a $1\times s$ ship. For a fixed $X$, the groups (a), (b), $X$ can be placed in \[w(X)=t_3(X)+2\,t_2(X)\] ways: if $X$ is the $1\times3$ ship, then (a) and (b) are both $1\times2$ ships; if $X$ is a $1\times2$ ship, then one of (a), (b) (two choices) is the $1\times3$ ship. Then $Y$ can be placed in $t_4(Y)$ ways.

Step 4: counting. Here $t_2=t_3=1$ for (c), so $w(\mathrm{c})=3$, and $t_4(\mathrm{c})=0$; for (g), $t_2=t_3=t_4=2$ (down or to the left), so $w(\mathrm{g})=6$; (d), (e), (f) are $1\times1$ only. The $1\times4$ ship can only be $Y=$ (g), and then $X\ne Y$ must be (c). The ship G7–J7 touches nothing, because (d) is just F5. The number of placements is $w(\mathrm{c})\,t_4(\mathrm{g})=3\cdot2=\boxed{6}$.

Problem 2

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

Is it possible to cut an $8\times8$ chessboard into several congruent pieces, so that in each piece the number of black cells is different from the number of white cells, and one $2\times2$ square?

Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – Straight trominoes

Yes. Any piece with an odd number of cells automatically has different numbers of black and white cells. Cut off the $2\times2$ square in a corner of the board. The rest consists of a $6\times8$ part (six full rows) and a $2\times6$ part (the remaining cells of the two rows next to the square). The $6\times8$ part is cut into $16$ vertical $1\times3$ strips (two in each column), and the $2\times6$ part into $4$ horizontal $1\times3$ strips. We get $20$ congruent straight trominoes, each with $2$ cells of one color and $1$ of the other, and one $2\times2$ square (see the figure).

Show solution 2 – L-trominoes

Yes. Cut off a $2\times2$ square in the corner of the board, say in rows $1$–$2$ and columns $1$–$2$. The remaining $60$ cells are rows $3$–$8$ (a $6\times8$ rectangle, which splits into eight $3\times2$ rectangles: two blocks of three rows times four blocks of two columns) and rows $1$–$2$, columns $3$–$8$ (a $2\times6$ rectangle, which splits into two $2\times3$ rectangles). Every $2\times3$ rectangle is the union of two L-shaped trominoes. This gives $20$ congruent L-trominoes; each consists of three cells, so its numbers of black and white cells (two and one, in some order) are different. Together with the $2\times2$ square this is a required cutting.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

The digits $1$, $2$ and $3$ are written on a board. Remarkably, their sum is divisible by each of them. How can several more digits be written on the board so that all the digits are different and their sum is divisible by each of them? Find all ways and prove that there are no others.

Show the answer

add $6$ (sum $12$), or add $4,6,8$ (sum $24$)

Show solution 1 – Ruling out 5, 7 and 9

The digit $0$ cannot be used (a positive sum is not divisible by $0$). So the new digits form a nonempty set $D\subseteq\{4,5,6,7,8,9\}$, and the sum $S=6+\sum D$ must be divisible by $1,2,3$, hence by $6$, and by every digit of $D$. Note that $S\le 1+2+\dots+9=45$.

  • If $5\in D$, then $S$ is divisible by $\operatorname{lcm}(5,6)=30$, so $S=30$ and $\sum D=24$. Every other digit of $D$ divides $30$, so it can only be $6$; but $5\ne24$ and $5+6\ne24$. Impossible.

  • If $7\in D$ and $5\notin D$, then $S$ is divisible by $\operatorname{lcm}(6,7)=42$, so $S=42$; but without the digit $5$ the sum is at most $45-5=40$. Impossible.

  • If $9\in D$ and $5,7\notin D$, then $S$ is divisible by $18$, so $S\in\{18,36\}$. But $S=6+9+s=15+s$, where $s$ is a sum of distinct elements of $\{4,6,8\}$, so $s\in\{0,4,6,8,10,12,14,18\}$ and $S\in\{15,19,21,23,25,27,29,33\}$. Impossible.

So $D$ is a nonempty subset of $\{4,6,8\}$. The seven possibilities give the sums $\{4\}$: $10$; $\{6\}$: $12$; $\{8\}$: $14$; $\{4,6\}$: $16$; $\{4,8\}$: $18$; $\{6,8\}$: $20$; $\{4,6,8\}$: $24$. Only $12$, $18$, $24$ are divisible by $6$; the sum $18$ of $\{4,8\}$ is not divisible by $4$, while $12$ is divisible by $1,2,3,6$ and $24$ by $1,2,3,4,6,8$.

So there are exactly two ways: add the single digit $\boxed{6}$ (sum $12$), or add the three digits $\boxed{4,\ 6,\ 8}$ (sum $24$).

Show solution 2 – Going through the possible sums

The digit $0$ cannot be used (a positive sum is not divisible by $0$), so the new digits form a nonempty set $D$ of distinct digits from $4$ to $9$. The sum $S=6+\sum D$ is divisible by $1$, $2$ and $3$, hence by $6$, and $10\le S\le 6+(4+5+\dots+9)=45$. So $S$ is one of $12,18,24,30,36,42$. Every digit of $D$ divides $S$, and the digits of $D$ add up to $S-6$.

  • $S=12$: the digits from $4$ to $9$ that divide $12$ are $4$ and $6$; the only subset of them with sum $6$ is $\{6\}$.

  • $S=18$: the divisors are $6$ and $9$; their subsets have sums $6$, $9$, $15$, never $12$.

  • $S=24$: the divisors are $4$, $6$, $8$; the sum $18$ is reached only by $\{4,6,8\}$.

  • $S=30$: the divisors are $5$ and $6$; their sum is at most $11<24$.

  • $S=36$: the divisors are $4$, $6$, $9$; their sum is at most $19<30$.

  • $S=42$: the divisors are $6$ and $7$; their sum is at most $13<36$.

Both remaining sets work: $12$ is divisible by $1,2,3,6$, and $24$ by $1,2,3,4,6,8$. So there are exactly two ways: add the single digit $\boxed{6}$, or add the three digits $\boxed{4,\ 6,\ 8}$.

A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. For what smallest natural number $k$ is the following statement true: “If the product of $6$ arbitrary natural numbers is divisible by $960$, then the product of some $k$ of them is divisible by $240$”?

Show the answer

$k=4$

Show the solution – Exponents of 2, 3 and 5

We have $960=2^{6}\cdot3\cdot5$ and $240=2^4\cdot3\cdot5$. The answer is $k=4$.

$k=3$ is not enough. Take the $6$ numbers $3,5,8,8,1,1$; their product is $3\cdot5\cdot2^{6}=960$. A product of some of them that is divisible by $240$ contains the factors $3$ and $5$, so it uses the numbers $3$ and $5$, and it needs at least $2^4$, so it uses at least two of the $8$'s (one $8$ gives only $2^3$). That is at least four numbers. So no product of three or fewer of these numbers is divisible by $240$, and the statement is false for every $k\le3$.

$k=4$ works. Let the product of $a_1,\dots,a_{6}$ be divisible by $960$. Some $a_i$ is divisible by $3$ and some $a_j$ by $5$; if $i=j$, replace $j$ by any other index. Hence the four numbers $a_i$, $a_j$ and the two numbers with exponents $p,q$ have a product divisible by $2^4\cdot3\cdot5=240$.

So the smallest $k$ is $\boxed{4}$.

Show solution 1 – Chinese remainder computation

Let $N$ be the age, $1000\le N\le9999$. Each resident's two statements are both true (knight) or both false (liar).

If the first is a knight and the second a liar, then $N\equiv80$ and $N\equiv92\pmod{101}$: impossible. If the first is a liar and the second a knight, then $N\equiv40$ and $N\equiv18\pmod{99}$: impossible. Two cases remain.

Both knights: $N\equiv80\pmod{101}$, $N\equiv18\pmod{99}$ (the other two conditions then hold automatically). Write $N=101a+80$. Since $101\equiv2\pmod{99}$, we need $2a+80\equiv18$, i.e. $2a\equiv-62\equiv37\pmod{99}$. As $2\cdot50=100\equiv1$, $a\equiv37\cdot50=1850\equiv68\pmod{99}$. So $N\equiv101\cdot68+80=6948\pmod{9999}$, and the only four-digit value is $N=6948$.

Both liars: $N\equiv40\pmod{99}$, $N\equiv92\pmod{101}$. With $N=101a+92$: $2a+92\equiv40$, $2a\equiv-52\equiv47$, $a\equiv47\cdot50=2350\equiv73\pmod{99}$, so $N\equiv101\cdot73+92=7465\pmod{9999}$ and $N=7465$.

Show solution 1 – Distance in the triangular lattice

Distance. Let $d(a,b)=\max(|a|,|b|,|a+b|)$. Each jump changes each of $a$, $b$, $a+b$ by at most $1$, so reaching $au+bv$ needs at least $d(a,b)$ jumps. The vertices with $d(a,b)\le60$: for a fixed $a$ with $|a|\le60$, the conditions $|b|\le60$ and $|a+b|\le60$ allow exactly $121-|a|$ values of $b$ (for $a\ge0$, $b$ runs from $-60$ to $60-a$; for $a<0$, from $-60-a$ to $60$). Use coordinates: with two tile sides $u,v$ at $60^\circ$ from the starting vertex $O$, every vertex is $au+bv$ with integers $a,b$, and a jump changes $(a,b)$ by $\pm(1,0)$, $\pm(0,1)$ or $\pm(1,-1)$. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

Show solution 2 – Areas through sines

Since $P$ is inside the convex quadrilateral, the four triangles $PAB$, $PBC$, $PCD$, $PDA$ are non-degenerate and fill the quadrilateral around $P$, so each of the four angles lies strictly between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ and $\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=360^\circ$. Then $a+100b-130c-500f=a-130c+100m=n$.

The interval $[-4500,-270]$ contains $4231\ge2026$ consecutive integers, so it contains an integer $n\equiv-F\pmod{2026}$. Choosing $a,b,c,f$ for this $n$ gives $N\equiv F+n\equiv0\pmod{2026}$.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

A country has $2026$ airports, numbered from $1$ to $2026$. There is a flight between two airports if the sum of their numbers is the square of a natural number. Is it true that even if one of the airports closes, one can still get from any of the remaining airports to any other, possibly changing planes at intermediate airports (but not at the closed one)?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – A Hamiltonian cycle, then two links for every new airport

Let $G_n$ be the network on the airports $1,\dots,n$ (flights between $i\ne j$ with $i+j$ a perfect square). We prove: for every $n$ with $32\le n\le2026$, the network $G_n$ is connected, and it stays connected after closing any one airport. For $n=2026$ this gives the answer yes.

$n=32$. The airports $1,\dots,32$ can be arranged in a cycle with all adjacent sums squares: \[1,8,28,21,4,32,17,19,30,6,3,13,12,24,25,11,\] \[5,31,18,7,29,20,16,9,27,22,14,2,23,26,10,15\] (and back to $1$; the sums of neighbors are 9, 36, 49, 25, 36, 49, 36, 49, 36, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 36, 16, 36, 49, 25, 36, 49, 36, 25, 36, 49, 36, 16, 25, 49, 36, 25, 16). So $G_{32}$ is connected, and if one airport closes, the other $31$ still form a path along this cycle.

From $n$ to $n+1$ ($32\le n\le2025$). Let $s$ be the smallest integer with $s^2\ge n+2$; then $s\le\sqrt{n+1}+1$, so \[(s+1)^2\le\bigl(\sqrt{n+1}+2\bigr)^2=n+5+4\sqrt{n+1}\le2n+1,\] where the last step means $4\sqrt{n+1}\le n-4$, i.e. $16n+16\le n^2-8n+16$, i.e. $n\ge24$. So $s^2$ and $(s+1)^2$ both lie in $[n+2,2n+1]$, and airport $n+1$ has flights to the two different airports $s^2-(n+1)$ and $(s+1)^2-(n+1)$ of $G_n$.

Now assume the statement for $G_n$. The network $G_{n+1}$ is connected, since $G_n$ is connected and airport $n+1$ has a flight into $G_n$. Close an airport $z$ of $G_{n+1}$. If $z=n+1$, the open airports form $G_n$, which is connected. If $z\le n$, then $G_n$ without $z$ is connected by the induction hypothesis, and airport $n+1$ has a flight to one of its two partners, which is different from $z$. In both cases the open airports are connected.

By induction the statement holds for $n=2026$: closing any one airport, one can still travel between any two of the remaining airports.

Problem 4

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Geometric Sequences

A point with coordinates $(k,\ell,m)$ is marked on the unit sphere, and \[m=\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}}.\] Prove that the numbers $1,\ \dfrac{mk}{\ell},\ k^2+\ell^2,\ \dfrac{k\ell}{m}$ (in this order) form a geometric progression.

Show solution 1 – Eliminating m

Let $r=\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}$. Since $m=\ell/r$ is defined, $r>0$, and since $m$ and $\ell$ are denominators in the statement, $m\ne0$ and $\ell\ne0$. From $k^2+\ell^2+m^2=1$ we get $r^2+\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}=1$, i.e. $\ell^2=r^2(1-r^2)$, and therefore \[k^2=r^2-\ell^2=r^4 .\] In particular $k\ne0$. The four numbers are \[1,\qquad \frac{mk}{\ell}=\frac{\ell}{r}\cdot\frac{k}{\ell}=\frac kr,\qquad k^2+\ell^2=r^2,\qquad \frac{k\ell}{m}=k\ell\cdot\frac r\ell=kr .\] Put $q=\frac kr\ne0$. Then $q^2=\frac{k^2}{r^2}=r^2$ and $q^3=q\cdot q^2=\frac kr\cdot r^2=kr$. So the numbers are $1,q,q^2,q^3$: a geometric progression with ratio $q=k/r$.

Show solution 2 – Spherical coordinates

Since $m=\ell/\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}$ is defined, $(k,\ell)\ne(0,0)$; write $k=r\cos\varphi$, $\ell=r\sin\varphi$ with $r=\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}>0$. Then $m=\sin\varphi$, and $m\ne0$ (it is a denominator), so $\sin\varphi\ne0$. The point is on the unit sphere: $r^2+m^2=1$, so $r^2=1-\sin^2\varphi=\cos^2\varphi$ and $r=|\cos\varphi|=\varepsilon\cos\varphi$ with $\varepsilon=\pm1$; in particular $\cos\varphi\ne0$. Hence $k=\varepsilon\cos^2\varphi$ and $\ell=\varepsilon\cos\varphi\sin\varphi$, and \[\frac{mk}{\ell}=\frac{\sin\varphi\cdot\varepsilon\cos^2\varphi}{\varepsilon\cos\varphi\sin\varphi}=\cos\varphi,\qquad k^2+\ell^2=r^2=\cos^2\varphi,\] \[\frac{k\ell}{m}=\frac{\varepsilon\cos^2\varphi\cdot\varepsilon\cos\varphi\sin\varphi}{\sin\varphi}=\varepsilon^2\cos^3\varphi=\cos^3\varphi .\] So the four numbers are $1,\cos\varphi,\cos^2\varphi,\cos^3\varphi$, a geometric progression with ratio $\cos\varphi\ne0$.

Problem 5

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 43: Locus, Transformation, and Construction Geometry · Classical Loci

An ant found itself on a plaza paved with equilateral triangles (see the figure). It can run parallel to the sides of the tiles at a speed of $1$ cm/s. The ant wants to get to the point $30$ cm to the right of its current position, and it has $1$ minute to do it. What is the area of the set of points it can visit along the way?

Show the answer

$1125\sqrt3$ cm$^2$

Show the solution – An ellipse for the hexagonal distance

Let $O$ be the start and $X$ the target, $OX=30$ cm along a tile side. Take coordinates with $O=(0,0)$, $X=(30,0)$; the ant runs along the directions $0^\circ,60^\circ,\dots,300^\circ$ (unit vectors $e_1,\dots,e_6$). For a vector $w$ let $\|w\|$ be the shortest length of a route with displacement $w$ made of such runs.

A formula for $\|w\|$. For the six unit vectors $n$ at angles $30^\circ,90^\circ,\dots,330^\circ$ put $\ell_n(w)=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\,n\cdot w$. The angle between any $e_i$ and any such $n$ is $30^\circ$, $90^\circ$ or $150^\circ$, so a run of length $t$ changes $\ell_n$ by at most $\frac2{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2t=t$; hence $\|w\|\ge\ell_n(w)$ for all six $n$. On the other hand, $w$ lies in the $60^\circ$ angle between two consecutive $e_i,e_{i+1}$, so $w=se_i+te_{i+1}$ with $s,t\ge0$, a route of length $s+t$, and for the $n$ bisecting that angle $\ell_n(w)=\frac2{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2(s+t)=s+t$. Therefore $\|w\|=\max_n\ell_n(w)$. In coordinates, with $w=(x,y)$: \[\ell_{30}=x+\tfrac{y}{\sqrt3},\qquad \ell_{90}=\tfrac{2y}{\sqrt3},\qquad \ell_{150}=-x+\tfrac{y}{\sqrt3},\] \[\ell_{210}=-\ell_{30},\qquad \ell_{270}=-\ell_{90},\qquad \ell_{330}=-\ell_{150}.\]

The set of visited points. A route from $O$ to $X$ through $Q$ has length at least $f(Q)=\|\overrightarrow{OQ}\|+\|\overrightarrow{QX}\|$, and the ant can go from $O$ to $Q$ and on to $X$ along shortest routes in time $f(Q)$ (arriving early does no harm). So the required set is $K=\{Q:\ f(Q)\le60\}$. We show that $K$ is the octagon with vertices \[A_1=(-15,0),\quad A_2=(-7.5,\,7.5\sqrt3),\quad A_3=(15,\,15\sqrt3),\] \[A_4=(37.5,\,7.5\sqrt3),\quad A_5=(45,0)\] and $A_6,A_7,A_8$, the mirror images of $A_4,A_3,A_2$ in the $x$-axis (see the figure).

The octagon lies in $K$. Each $\|\cdot\|$ is a maximum of linear functions, so $f$ is convex and its maximum on a convex polygon is attained at a vertex. At the vertices: $A_1=15e_4$ and $\overrightarrow{A_1X}=45e_1$, sum $60$; $A_2=15e_3$ and $\overrightarrow{A_2X}=30e_1+15e_6$, sum $15+45=60$; $A_3=30e_2$ and $\overrightarrow{A_3X}=30e_6$, sum $60$; $A_4=30e_1+15e_2$ and $\overrightarrow{A_4X}=15e_5$, sum $60$; $A_5=45e_1$ and $\overrightarrow{A_5X}=15e_4$, sum $60$. The lower vertices are symmetric, because the reflection in the $x$-axis maps the six directions to themselves and fixes $O$ and $X$. So $f\le60$ on the octagon.

Points outside the octagon are not in $K$. For any $n,n'$ we have $f(Q)\ge\ell_n(\overrightarrow{OQ})+\ell_{n'}(\overrightarrow{QX})$, a linear function of $Q$. For the four upper sides choose:

  • $A_1A_2$: $n=150^\circ$, $n'=330^\circ$, giving $g=2\bigl(-x+\frac y{\sqrt3}\bigr)+30$;

  • $A_2A_3$: $n=90^\circ$, $n'=330^\circ$, giving $g=-x+\sqrt3\,y+30$;

  • $A_3A_4$: $n=30^\circ$, $n'=270^\circ$, giving $g=x+\sqrt3\,y$;

  • $A_4A_5$: $n=30^\circ$, $n'=210^\circ$, giving $g=2\bigl(x+\frac y{\sqrt3}\bigr)-30$.

Each $g$ equals $60$ at both ends of its side (for example $g(A_1)=2\cdot15+30=60$, $g(A_2)=2\cdot15+30=60$ in the first line), so it equals $60$ on the whole line of that side, and it is less than $60$ at the point $(15,0)$ inside the octagon. A point beyond that side has $g>60$, hence $f>60$. The lower sides are symmetric. Every point outside the octagon lies beyond one of its sides, so $K$ is exactly the octagon.

Area. The upper half $A_1A_2A_3A_4A_5$ splits by the vertical lines $x=-7.5$, $x=15$, $x=37.5$ into two triangles of area $\frac12\cdot7.5\cdot7.5\sqrt3$ and two trapezoids of area $\frac12\cdot22.5\cdot(7.5\sqrt3+15\sqrt3)$, in total $2\cdot28.125\sqrt3+2\cdot253.125\sqrt3=562.5\sqrt3$. Hence the area of the set is \[2\cdot562.5\sqrt3=\boxed{1125\sqrt3\ \text{cm}^2}\approx1949\ \text{cm}^2.\]

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 29: Exponents, Powers, and Growth · Comparing Large Powers · also Grade R9, Problem 7; Grade R10, Problem 6

Prove that among the numbers from $1$ to $10^{1000}$, more than two million numbers contain only twos and fives in their prime factorization.

Show solution 1 – Powers of ten times a power of 2 or 5

Every number whose prime factors are only $2$ and $5$ is $2^a5^b$, and it can be written uniquely as $10^c\cdot2^d$ with $d\ge0$ (when $a\ge b$: $c=b$, $d=a-b$) or $10^c\cdot5^e$ with $e\ge1$ (when $a<b$: $c=a$, $e=b-a$).

Fix $c$ with $0\le c\le1000$ and put $r=1000-c$.

  • Since $2^3=8<10$, we have $2^d\le10^r$ for $d=0,1,\dots,3r$; so $10^c2^d\le10^{1000}$ for these $3r+1$ values of $d$.

  • Since $5<10$, we have $5^e\le10^r$ for $e=1,\dots,r$; so $10^c5^e\le10^{1000}$ for these $r$ values of $e$.

All these numbers are different, so the count of numbers from $1$ to $10^{1000}$ with prime factors only $2$ and $5$ is at least \[\sum_{r=0}^{1000}(4r+1)=4\cdot\frac{1000\cdot1001}2+1001=2002000+1001=2003001 .\] Even without the number $1$, this is more than two million.

Show solution 2 – Lattice points under a line

We count pairs of non-negative integers $(a,b)$ with $2^a5^b\le10^{1000}$; different pairs give different numbers.

Two estimates: since $5^3=125<128=2^7$, we have $5<2^{7/3}$ and $5^b\le2^{7b/3}$ (with equality only for $b=0$); and since $(5/4)^{10}>8$ (indeed $(5/4)^2=\frac{25}{16}>1.56$, $(5/4)^4>2.43$, $(5/4)^8>5.9$, $(5/4)^{10}>5.9\cdot1.56>9.2$), we get \[10^{1000}=8^{1000}\Bigl(\frac54\Bigr)^{1000}>2^{3000}\cdot8^{100}=2^{3300}.\] Hence $2^a5^b\le2^{a+7b/3}\le2^{3300}<10^{1000}$ whenever $3a+7b\le9900$. For each $b=0,1,\dots,1414$ (note $7\cdot1414=9898$) the number of admissible $a$ is $\bigl\lfloor\frac{9900-7b}3\bigr\rfloor+1>\frac{9900-7b}3$. So the number of pairs is greater than \[\sum_{b=0}^{1414}\frac{9900-7b}3=\frac{1415\cdot9900-7\cdot\frac{1414\cdot1415}2}{3},\] which equals \[\frac{14008500-7002835}3=\frac{7005665}3>2335000 .\] So there are more than two million such numbers (even after removing the number $1$).

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement · Area Ratios in Triangles and Polygons

A point $P$ is marked inside a convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ so that the triangles $PAB$, $PBC$, $PCD$, and $PDA$ have equal areas. It is also known that $PB=PC$. Prove that the quadrilateral $ABCD$ has a right angle.

Show solution 1 – Equal areas force a midpoint of a diagonal

Lemma: $P$ is the midpoint of a diagonal. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$. Since $P$ is inside the convex quadrilateral, the line $BP$ separates $A$ from $C$: the ray from $B$ through $P$ leaves the quadrilateral through the side $CD$, the side $DA$ or the vertex $D$, and in each case $A$ and $C$ lie on different sides of the line $BP$. The triangles $PAB$ and $PBC$ have the common side $PB$ and equal areas, so $A$ and $C$ are at equal distances from line $BP$. As they are on different sides of it, the segment $AC$ meets line $BP$ at a point $K$, and the right triangles formed by $A$, $C$, their feet of perpendiculars on line $BP$ and $K$ are congruent (equal legs, equal vertical angles at $K$), so $AK=KC$ and $K=M$. So the line $BP$ passes through $M$. In the same way $[PCD]=[PDA]$ shows that line $DP$ passes through $M$; $[PBC]=[PCD]$ shows that line $CP$ passes through $N$; and $[PDA]=[PAB]$ shows that line $AP$ passes through $N$.

Suppose $P\ne M$ and $P\ne N$. The lines $BP$ and $DP$ both contain $P$ and $M$, so they coincide: $B,P,D,M$ are collinear. Likewise $A,P,C,N$ are collinear. Then $P$ lies on both diagonals, so $P$ is their intersection point; but $M$ also lies on both diagonals, so $M=P$, a contradiction. Hence $P=M$ or $P=N$.

Conclusion. If $P=M$, then $PA=PC=PB$, so $B$ lies on the circle with diameter $AC$ (centre $P$, radius $PA$), and $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. If $P=N$, then $PB=PD=PC$, so $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $BD$, and $\angle BCD=90^\circ$. In both cases $ABCD$ has a right angle.

Show solution 2 – Cross products

Label the quadrilateral counterclockwise, put the origin at $P$ and write $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c,\vec d$ for the vectors $\overrightarrow{PA},\overrightarrow{PB},\overrightarrow{PC},\overrightarrow{PD}$. For $u=(u_1,u_2)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2)$ let $u\times v=u_1v_2-u_2v_1$. This product is linear in each factor, $v\times u=-u\times v$, $u\times v=0$ for nonzero $u,v$ only when they are parallel, and $u\times v$ is twice the area of the triangle with sides $u,v$ when $v$ is obtained from $u$ by a counterclockwise turn of less than $180^\circ$. Since $P$ is inside the quadrilateral, each of the triangles $PAB$, $PBC$, $PCD$, $PDA$ is traversed counterclockwise, so with $S$ their common area \[\vec a\times\vec b=\vec b\times\vec c=\vec c\times\vec d=\vec d\times\vec a=2S>0 .\]

$P$ is the midpoint of a diagonal. From $\vec b\times\vec c=\vec c\times\vec d$ we get $\vec c\times\vec b+\vec c\times\vec d=0$, i.e. $\vec c\times(\vec b+\vec d)=0$; from $\vec d\times\vec a=\vec a\times\vec b$ we get $\vec a\times(\vec b+\vec d)=0$. Suppose $\vec b+\vec d\ne\vec0$. Then the nonzero vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec c$ are both parallel to $\vec b+\vec d$, so $\vec c=\mu\vec a$ for some number $\mu$. Now $\vec b\times\vec c=\mu\,(\vec b\times\vec a)=-\mu\,(\vec a\times\vec b)$, and $\vec a\times\vec b=\vec b\times\vec c\ne0$ gives $\mu=-1$, i.e. $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$. So $\vec b+\vec d=\vec0$ or $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$: $P$ is the midpoint of $BD$ or of $AC$.

The right angle. If $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$, then $\vec c=-\vec a$, so $PA=PC=PB$ and $\vec a\cdot\vec a=\vec b\cdot\vec b$; therefore \[\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=(\vec a-\vec b)\cdot(-\vec a-\vec b)=\vec b\cdot\vec b-\vec a\cdot\vec a=0,\] and $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. If $\vec b+\vec d=\vec0$, then $\vec d=-\vec b$, so $PB=PD$ and, with $PB=PC$, $\vec b\cdot\vec b=\vec c\cdot\vec c$; therefore \[\overrightarrow{CB}\cdot\overrightarrow{CD}=(\vec b-\vec c)\cdot(-\vec b-\vec c)=\vec c\cdot\vec c-\vec b\cdot\vec b=0,\] and $\angle BCD=90^\circ$. In both cases $ABCD$ has a right angle.
