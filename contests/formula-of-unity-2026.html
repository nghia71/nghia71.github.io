Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

Arthur is playing “Battleship” with a friend. In this game, one player places $10$ ships (four $1\times1$, three $1\times2$, two $1\times3$ and one $1\times4$) on a $10\times10$ board so that the ships do not touch each other, not even at corners, and the other player names cells one at a time in order to “sink” these ships. Arthur has already made several moves (see the figure; $\bullet$ – hit, $\otimes$ – miss). How many valid ship placements are there?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Record each hit and miss, and connect hits that share a side. Which neighboring cells must be empty because two different ships cannot touch even at a corner?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the maximum ship length to test J2 and J6, and use diagonal contact to block extensions of the hits C6,D6 and A7. After identifying forced ships, group the other hits by which straight ship could contain them. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
List the allowed extensions in each group and remove those that cross a recorded miss. Compare the number of groups with the number of remaining ships, then assign the remaining ship lengths beginning with groups that can only be single cells. Count the surviving extensions of the longest ship and check their contact with the other ships.
Common trap
A ship with no hit still counts toward the fleet, so rule out such ships before assuming each remaining ship belongs to a hit group.
Show the answer

$2$

Show the solution – Forced ships, seven groups, counting sizes

Cells are named by column letter and row number. On this board the hits are A1, B1, J1, E2, E3, J3, J4, A5, F5, J5, C6, D6, A7, J7, B9, and the misses are C1, I1, A3, G5, A6, F6, A9, C9, B10. Two simple facts are used all the time.

  • Two occupied cells with a common side belong to the same ship, because different ships do not touch.

  • A cell that touches a hit cell only at a corner is empty: it cannot lie in the same (straight) ship as that hit cell, and it cannot lie in another ship, because ships do not touch even at corners.

Step 1: three ships are forced. If J2 were occupied, then J1, J2, J3, J4, J5 would be five occupied cells in one column, hence one ship of length at least $5$, which does not exist. In the same way J6 is empty (otherwise J3–J7 would be one ship). So J3, J4, J5 form a $1\times3$ ship. The cells B6 and E6 touch the hits A5 and F5 at corners, so they are empty, and C6, D6 form a $1\times2$ ship. The cells B7 and A8 touch C6 and B9 at corners, and A6 is a miss, so A7 is a $1\times1$ ship.

Step 2: the other seven ships. One $1\times4$, one $1\times3$, two $1\times2$ and three $1\times1$ ships remain: seven ships. By the corner rule the following cells are also empty: A2 (touches B1), D1 and F1 (touch E2), F4 (touches E3), E4 (touches F5), E5 (touches D6), B5 (touches C6), B8 (touches A7), I5 (touches J4). Ignoring the misses for a moment, each remaining hit lies in a ship of one of these forms:

  • (a) through A1, B1: A1B1 or A1–C1;

  • (b) through E2, E3: E2E3 or E1–E3;

  • (c) through A5: A5, A4A5 or A3–A5;

  • (d) through F5: F5, F5G5, F5–H5, F5F6, F5–F7 or F5–F8;

  • (e) through J1: J1, I1J1, H1–J1 or G1–J1;

  • (f) through B9: B9, B9B10, or a horizontal ship in row 9 containing B9 (A9B9, B9C9, A9–C9, B9–D9, A9–D9, B9–E9);

  • (g) through J7: J7, J7J8, J7–J9, J7–J10, I7J7, H7–J7 or G7–J7.

These seven areas are far apart, so no ship contains hits of two different groups. Seven groups need seven different ships, and exactly seven ships are left; so each remaining ship is the ship of one group and there are no other ships. Conversely, one placement chosen for each group gives a valid arrangement exactly when the sizes are $4,3,2,2,1,1,1$ in some order and no two chosen ships touch. Comparing the areas (and the three forced ships), ships of different groups can touch only in two ways: G7 (in G7–J7) touches F6, F7, F8 (in a vertical ship of group (d)), and E9 (in B9–E9) touches F8 (in F5–F8). The second case would need two $1\times4$ ships, so only the first one can matter.

Step 3: the misses of this grade. The miss C1 leaves only A1B1 for (a); the miss A3 leaves A5 or A4A5 for (c); the misses G5 and F6 leave only F5 for (d); the miss I1 leaves only J1 for (e); the misses A9, C9, B10 leave only B9 for (f); groups (b) and (g) keep all their forms.

Step 4: counting. Groups (d), (e), (f) are $1\times1$ ships, so they use up the three $1\times1$ ships, and (a), (b), (c), (g) get the sizes $4,3,2,2$. Group (a) is A1B1 of size $2$, and (c) has size at most $2$, so (c) is the other $1\times2$ ship A4A5. Then (b), whose sizes can only be $2$ or $3$, is the $1\times3$ ship E1–E3, and (g) is the $1\times4$ ship: J7–J10 or G7–J7 (here G7 touches nothing, because (d) is just F5). Hence there are exactly $\boxed{2}$ valid placements.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

A machine takes as input a natural number greater than $10$ and finds the sum of the squares of all its digits. Then, if the resulting number has at least $2$ digits, the machine finds the sum of the squares of its digits, and repeats these operations until it gets a one-digit number, which it returns as the result (for example, $24$ turns into $2^2+4^2=20$, which turns into $2^2+0^2=4$, and the machine returns $4$). Is there a number consisting only of even digits for which the machine returns an odd one-digit number?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Apply the machine once to several numbers with even digits. Even if that first result is even, what can its individual digits look like?
Hint 2: key idea
Try a number formed by repeating one small nonzero even digit. Its first image is the number of repetitions times the square of that digit, so you can search through these images without testing every input. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Vary the repetition count until the first image has at least two digits and includes both an odd and an even digit. Apply the digit-square rule to that image and check whether it stops with the required parity. Verify that the original input exceeds $10$.
Common trap
The machine stops at the first one-digit result, so do not apply an extra operation after it stops.
Show the answer

Yes (for example $222\to12\to5$)

Show the solution – An explicit example

Yes. Take the number $222$, all of whose digits are even. The machine computes \[2^2+2^2+2^2=12,\qquad 1^2+2^2=5,\] and returns the odd one-digit number $5$. (Another example: $28\to 4+64=68\to 36+64=100\to 1+0+0=1$.)

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Truth and Lies

In a country inhabited only by knights, who always tell the truth, and liars, who always lie, people are preparing to celebrate Independence Day. Two residents saw a poster showing the two-digit age of the country, and told you the following.

  • First resident: 1) “The age is divisible by $5$.” 2) “The age is not divisible by $2$.”

  • Second resident: 1) “The age is divisible by $10$.” 2) “The sum of the digits of the age is not $7$.”

How old can the country be? Find all possibilities.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a truth value beside each of the four statements for a trial age. For either resident, which pairs of truth values are allowed?
Hint 2: key idea
Split the first resident into the cases where both statements are true or both are false, and do the same for the second. Translate the divisibility statements into restrictions on the last digit and parity. (LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles)
Hint 3: approach
Use the first resident's two cases to test whether the age can be divisible by $10$. Apply the resulting truth status of the second resident to restrict the digit sum, then list the two-digit numbers with that sum. Check each against the first resident's two cases.
Common trap
A liar makes both statements false, including the statements containing the word 'not'.
Show the answer

$16$, $25$, $34$ or $52$

Show the solution – Both statements of a resident are true or both false

Let $N$ be the age. A knight's two statements are both true and a liar's two statements are both false, so for each resident the two statements are both true or both false.

First resident. Either $N$ is divisible by $5$ and odd, that is, $N$ ends in $5$; or $N$ is not divisible by $5$ and is even.

Second resident. Either $N$ is divisible by $10$ and its digit sum is not $7$; or $N$ is not divisible by $10$ and its digit sum is $7$.

In both cases for the first resident, $N$ is not divisible by $10$ (a number ending in $5$ is odd; and a number not divisible by $5$ is not divisible by $10$). So the second resident is a liar and the digit sum of $N$ is $7$. The two-digit numbers with digit sum $7$ are \[16,\ 25,\ 34,\ 43,\ 52,\ 61,\ 70 .\] Those ending in $5$: only $25$. Those that are even and not divisible by $5$: $16,34,52$.

Check: for $25$ the first resident is a knight (divisible by $5$, odd) and the second a liar (not divisible by $10$, digit sum $7$); for $16$, $34$, $52$ both residents are liars. So the country can be $\boxed{16,\ 25,\ 34\text{ or }52}$ years old.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

The number $2026$ has been cut out of grid paper along the grid lines (see the figure). Cut one or more of the digits along the grid lines and assemble a $5\times8$ rectangle from all the resulting pieces. The total length of all cuts must not exceed $4$ (the side of a cell is $1$). Draw how the digits are cut into pieces, and then how the rectangle is assembled from these pieces.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Count the cells in each digit and identify the holes and notches that would need to be filled in a rectangle. Try rotating a digit before deciding that it must be cut.
Hint 2: key idea
Try leaving the first $2$, the $0$, and a rotated $6$ intact. Arrange their narrow widths across the target rectangle so a gap in one piece can join a notch in the next. (LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Put the first $2$ on the left, turn the $6$ upside down beside it, and place the $0$ at the right. List the empty cells by connected strips and isolated cells, then look for these shapes inside the second $2$. Mark the separating unit edges and add their lengths before finalizing the cuts.
Common trap
The cut budget measures the total length of separating edges, so count each cut edge once.
Show the answer

construction (cut the second $2$ into four pieces, total cut length $3$)

Show the solution – Cut only the second 2

Number the rows $1$–$5$ from the top and the columns $1$–$8$ from the left in the $5\times8$ rectangle. In the figure, the second digit $2$ is cut into four pieces:

  • its right column of the top three rows (a straight $1\times3$ piece),

  • its left column of the bottom three rows (another straight $1\times3$ piece),

  • its top-left cell and its bottom-right cell (two single cells).

Only three unit segments are cut (between the two cells of row $1$, of row $3$ and of row $5$ of that digit), so the total length of the cuts is $3\le 4$. The other three digits are not cut.

Assembly. Put the first $2$ as it is in columns $1$–$2$; it covers these columns except the cells (row $2$, column $1$) and (row $4$, column $2$). Turn the $6$ upside down (rotate it by $180^\circ$) and put it in columns $3$–$5$: its notch is now in row $4$, columns $3$–$4$, open to the left, and its hole is the cell (row $2$, column $4$). Put the $0$ in columns $6$–$8$; its hole is column $7$, rows $2$–$4$. The uncovered cells are:

  • row $4$, columns $2,3,4$ (the gap of the $2$ next to the notch of the $6$): a horizontal $1\times3$ strip;

  • column $7$, rows $2,3,4$: a vertical $1\times3$ strip;

  • the single cells (row $2$, column $1$) and (row $2$, column $4$).

These are filled exactly by the four pieces of the second $2$ (one straight piece is turned to lie horizontally). The cell counts agree: $8+12+12+8=40=5\cdot8$. The construction is shown in the figure.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · LCM Logic

The digits $1$, $2$ and $3$ are written on a board. Remarkably, their sum is divisible by each of them. How can three more digits be written on the board so that all six digits are different and their sum is divisible by each of them? Find all ways and prove that there are no others.

Hint 1: understand the problem
What digits are still available, and can zero be one of them? Bound the total sum by choosing the three smallest or three largest possible additions.
Hint 2: key idea
The sum must be a multiple of the least common multiple of the three digits already present. Use the bounds on the sum to make a short list of possible totals before choosing the new digits. (LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction)
Hint 3: approach
For each possible total, subtract the sum of the original digits and list the triples of distinct available digits with that remaining sum. Test whether every digit in a triple divides the total. Check every surviving triple against all six divisibility conditions.
Common trap
Having the correct sum is only one condition, since each added digit must also divide it.
Show the answer

the digits $4,6,8$ (sum $24$)

Show the solution – The sum is a multiple of 6

The digit $0$ cannot be used, since a positive sum is not divisible by $0$. Let the new digits be $x,y,z$, distinct and chosen from $4,5,\dots,9$, and let $S=6+x+y+z$. Since $S$ is divisible by $1,2,3$, it is divisible by $6$.

The smallest possible value of $x+y+z$ is $4+5+6=15$ and the largest is $7+8+9=24$, so $21\le S\le30$ and $S=24$ or $S=30$.

  • $S=30$: then $x+y+z=24$, so $\{x,y,z\}=\{7,8,9\}$; but $30$ is not divisible by $7$.

  • $S=24$: then $x+y+z=18$, so $\{x,y,z\}$ is $\{4,5,9\}$, $\{4,6,8\}$ or $\{5,6,7\}$. The sum $24$ is not divisible by $5$, so only $\{4,6,8\}$ remains, and indeed $24$ is divisible by $1,2,3,4,6,8$.

So the only way is to write the digits $\boxed{4,\ 6,\ 8}$ (the sum is then $24$).

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 52: Solid Geometry and Spatial Visualization · Nets and Folding

A regular octahedron is a polyhedron made up of $8$ triangles with equal sides. An octahedron with edge $1$ was cut along several edges and unfolded onto the plane in one piece so that the faces do not overlap and no two faces touch along a segment unless they remained glued along it. What can the perimeter of the net be? Find all possibilities and prove that there are no others.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Label each edge of the octahedron as cut or still glued. How does cutting a unit edge affect the boundary of a net under the stated rule about faces touching?
Hint 2: key idea
Study the graph formed by the cut edges on the original octahedron. The angle sum at a vertex forces some incident cut, while a cycle of cuts would separate the surface into pieces. (LPS VI, Chapter 52: Solid Geometry and Spatial Visualization)
Hint 3: approach
Treat the cut graph as a forest and consider the boundary walk around each of its components. Use the total turning of each such boundary and the sum of all face angles to constrain the number of components. Convert the resulting edge count into boundary length and verify existence with a strip of faces.
Common trap
The prohibition on unglued segment contacts prevents two copies of a cut edge from disappearing into a shared boundary segment.
Show the answer

$10$

Show solution 1 – Cut edges form a spanning tree

Call the edges along which the polyhedron was cut cut edges. We first prove three facts.

(1) The perimeter of the net is twice the total length of the cut edges. A glued edge lies inside the net. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

(2) Every vertex of the polyhedron is an end of some cut edge. At each vertex of the octahedron four equilateral triangles meet, with angle sum $4\cdot60^\circ=240^\circ<360^\circ$. If no edge at a vertex were cut, all faces at that vertex would stay glued around it in the net, so their angles would have to fill the full angle $360^\circ$ around a point of the plane. This is impossible.

(3) The cut edges form a single tree containing all 6 vertices, so there are exactly 5 cut edges. The cut edges contain no closed path: a closed path of cuts would separate the surface into two parts and the net would not be in one piece. So the cut edges form a forest; let it have $k$ components $T_1,\dots,T_k$, with $v_1,\dots,v_k$ vertices ($v_1+\dots+v_k=6$ by (2), and each $T_i$ has $v_i-1\ge1$ edges). Walking once around the tree $T_i$ on the surface, we pass along each of its edges twice; in the net this walk is a closed broken line $\gamma_i$ formed by $2(v_i-1)$ boundary segments, with the net on its left. At a vertex $V$ of $T_i$ the cut edges at $V$ split the faces at $V$ into wedges; at the corner of $\gamma_i$ belonging to a wedge of angle $\omega$ the walk turns left by $180^\circ-\omega$, and the wedges at $V$ together contain all face angles at $V$. Hence the total turning of $\gamma_i$ is \[\tau_i=180^\circ\cdot2(v_i-1)-\sum_{V\in T_i}\alpha_V ,\] where $\alpha_V$ is the sum of the face angles at $V$. Since faces neither overlap nor touch along segments, $\gamma_i$ never crosses itself; it can only touch itself at isolated points. Push it slightly into the net: near a point where it touches itself, each pass through that point is pushed into its own wedge of faces (or into its own face, if the pass runs along a side there), and these do not overlap, so the pushed curve is a simple closed curve; it has the same total turning (a corner where the net angle exceeds $180^\circ$ becomes a small arc that turns by the same angle). The total turning of a simple closed curve made of segments and arcs is $+360^\circ$ or $-360^\circ$. As every $\alpha_V<360^\circ$, we get $\tau_i>360^\circ(v_i-1)-360^\circ v_i=-360^\circ$, so $\tau_i=+360^\circ$ for every $i$. Adding these $k$ equalities, \[360^\circ k=360^\circ\,(6-k)-\sum_{V}\alpha_V=360^\circ\,(6-k)-1440^\circ,\] because the sum of all face angles of the polyhedron is $8\cdot180^\circ=1440^\circ$. This gives $720^\circ k=720^\circ$, so $k=1$: the cut edges form one tree with all 6 vertices and 5 edges.

Conclusion. All edges have length $1$, so by (1) and (3) the perimeter of every such net is $2\cdot5=10$. Such a net exists: the figure shows one (a strip of eight triangles), in which faces meet only along glued edges or at single points. So the perimeter can only be $\boxed{10}$.

Show solution 2 – Euler's formula for the net

Label the vertices of the octahedron $A,A',B,B',C,C'$ so that $A$ and $A'$, $B$ and $B'$, $C$ and $C'$ are opposite; the faces are the eight triangles with one vertex from each pair. In the net the eight faces are unit equilateral triangles in the plane with disjoint interiors. Call an edge of the octahedron glued if it was not cut, and let $g$ be the number of glued edges.

The perimeter is $p=24-2g$. The faces have $8\cdot3=24$ sides. A glued edge gives two sides that lie on one segment inside the net. Every other side is a side of only one face of the net: a second face lying along it would touch the first along a segment without being glued there. So the boundary of the net consists of the $24-2g$ unglued sides, and $p=24-2g$.

At least $7$ edges are glued. The net is in one piece, so the eight faces are connected to each other through glued edges, and connecting $8$ objects needs at least $7$ links. Hence $g\ge7$ and $p\le10$. It remains to prove $g\le7$.

The net lies in a triangular lattice. Fix one face $F_0$ of the net and the tiling of the plane by unit triangles that contains $F_0$. A face glued to a face of the tiling along a side is the mirror image of that face in the side, so it is again a triangle of the tiling. As every face is reached from $F_0$ through glued sides, all eight faces are triangles of this tiling. Consequently all corners of faces are lattice points, all sides are lattice segments of length $1$, and a corner never lies inside a side of another face.

Each corner point has at least two boundary sides. Call a point of the plane that is a corner of a face a node, and let $V$ be the number of nodes. When two faces are glued along a side $QR$, the point $Q$ is the same vertex of the octahedron in both faces. Fix a node $Q$ and group the faces with a corner at $Q$ into fans: maximal chains of faces, consecutive around $Q$, each glued to the next along a side through $Q$. All faces of one fan have the same octahedron vertex at $Q$. A fan cannot close up all the way around $Q$: that would need $360^\circ/60^\circ=6$ different faces at one vertex of the octahedron, which has only $4$. So every fan has two end sides through $Q$. An end side is not glued (a face glued along it would also have a corner at $Q$ and would continue the fan), so it is a boundary side, and different fans have different end sides. Hence at least two boundary sides end at every node. Each of the $p$ boundary sides has two ends, so $2V\le2p$, that is, \[V\le p .\]

Euler's formula. The sides of the faces form a connected plane graph with $V$ vertices and $E=g+p$ edges (a glued segment counts once). Its faces are the $8$ triangles, the unbounded region outside the net, and $h\ge0$ bounded regions not covered by the net (holes). Euler's formula $V-E+(8+1+h)=2$ gives \[g=V-p+7+h\le7+h .\]

There are no holes, so $g=7$. Suppose $g\ge8$. Then $h\ge g-7\ge1$ and $p=24-2g$. Every boundary side has a face on one side and the unbounded region or a hole on the other. A hole is a bounded region whose boundary is made of lattice sides of length $1$, so it uses at least $3$ boundary sides. Hence the boundary of the unbounded region consists of at most \[p-3h\le(24-2g)-3(g-7)=45-5g\le5\] sides; let $L\le5$ be their total length. Let $K$ be the net together with its holes (everything except the unbounded region), and let $w$ and $t$ be the width and the height of $K$ (the lengths of its projections on the $x$- and $y$-axes). For all but finitely many $x$ strictly inside the projection of $K$, the vertical line through $x$ meets $K$, and walking from a point of $K$ up and down along this line we leave $K$, crossing an outer side above and another one below. So the projections of the outer sides on the $x$-axis cover this interval at least twice: if the outer sides have horizontal and vertical extents $a_i,b_i\ge0$, then $\sum a_i\ge2w$, and in the same way $\sum b_i\ge2t$. By the triangle inequality for the vectors $(a_i,b_i)$, \[L=\sum_i\sqrt{a_i^2+b_i^2}\ \ge\ \sqrt{\Bigl(\sum_i a_i\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(\sum_i b_i\Bigr)^2}\ \ge\ 2\sqrt{w^2+t^2}\ \ge\ 2\sqrt{2wt}.\] So $K$, which lies in a $w\times t$ rectangle, has area at most $wt\le L^2/8\le25/8$. But $K$ contains the eight faces, of total area $8\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}4=2\sqrt3>\frac{25}8$ (because $(2\sqrt3)^2=12>\frac{625}{64}$). This contradiction shows $g\le7$. Together with $g\ge7$ we get $g=7$ and $p=24-14=10$.

A net exists. The figure shows one: the faces $ABC$, $ABC'$, $AB'C$, $AB'C'$, $A'BC$, $A'BC'$, $A'B'C$, $A'B'C'$ are glued along the seven edges $AB$, $AC$, $BC$, $AB'$, $A'B$, $A'C$, $A'B'$ (dashed). Each dashed segment carries the same two octahedron vertices in both of its faces, so folding along them rebuilds the octahedron; the eight triangles are different triangles of the lattice, so they do not overlap, and any two faces that are not glued meet at most at a point. Its perimeter is $24-2\cdot7=10$.

So the perimeter of the net can only be $\boxed{10}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

What is the smallest number of cells of a $3\times2026$ board that must be colored so that every rectangle of $4$ cells contains at least one colored cell? Do not forget to prove that this number is indeed the smallest.

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the shapes that a four-cell rectangle can have in a board only three cells high. Check where such a rectangle can cross a boundary between column blocks.
Hint 2: key idea
Split the board into full blocks of four columns and a final shorter block. The three horizontal strips in each full block give a local lower bound, while a $2\times2$ square constrains the remainder. (LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
For a construction, put a colored middle cell in one column of each four-column period and colored outer cells two columns later. Test horizontal strips in each row and $2\times2$ squares using the parity of their columns. Include the final partial period when comparing the construction with the lower bound.
Common trap
Rectangles crossing between two chosen blocks still have to contain a colored cell.
Show the answer

$1519$

Show the solution – Blocks of four columns

Call the rectangles of $4$ cells forbidden: in a $3\times2026$ board they are the $2\times2$ squares and the horizontal $1\times4$ strips.

At least $1519$ cells are needed. Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$, the board splits into $506$ blocks of size $3\times4$ and one block of size $3\times2$. In a $3\times4$ block each of the three rows is a $1\times4$ strip, so the block contains at least $3$ colored cells. The $3\times2$ block contains a $2\times2$ square, so it contains at least one colored cell. Hence at least $3\cdot506+1=1519$ cells are colored.

Show the solution – Cut edges form a spanning tree

Call the edges along which the polyhedron was cut cut edges. We first prove three facts.

(1) The perimeter of the net is twice the total length of the cut edges. A glued edge lies inside the net. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

This is impossible.

The cut edges contain no closed path: a closed path of cuts would separate the surface into two parts and the net would not be in one piece. Walking once around the tree $T_i$ on the surface, we pass along each of its edges twice; in the net this walk is a closed broken line $\gamma_i$ formed by $2(v_i-1)$ boundary segments, with the net on its left. At a vertex $V$ of $T_i$ the cut edges at $V$ split the faces at $V$ into wedges; at the corner of $\gamma_i$ belonging to a wedge of angle $\omega$ the walk turns left by $180^\circ-\omega$, and the wedges at $V$ together contain all face angles at $V$. Since faces neither overlap nor touch along segments, $\gamma_i$ never crosses itself; it can only touch itself at isolated points. Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 52: Solid Geometry and Spatial Visualization · Nets and Folding

A $1\times1\times2$ cuboid was cut along several edges and unfolded onto the plane in one piece so that the faces do not overlap and no two faces touch along a segment unless they remained glued along it. What can the perimeter of the net be? Find all possibilities and prove that there are no others.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a candidate net, identify which original edges have been cut and how many boundary copies each produces. Keep track of edge lengths as well as their number.
Hint 2: key idea
Study the cut edges as a graph spanning the cuboid's vertices, using the angle deficit at each vertex and boundary turning to show that this graph is a single tree. Its total edge length determines the net's perimeter. (LPS VI, Chapter 52: Solid Geometry and Spatial Visualization)
Hint 3: approach
Exclude uncut vertices and cut cycles, then use the boundary walks and total face angles to establish connectedness. Parameterize the cut length by the number of long edges in the tree, find its possible range, and draw valid non-overlapping nets to test which cases occur.
Common trap
An abstract cut tree is not enough to prove attainability; the unfolding must also avoid overlap and unintended segment contacts.
Show the answer

$16$, $18$, $20$ or $22$

Show the solution – Cut edges form a spanning tree

Call the edges along which the polyhedron was cut cut edges. We first prove three facts.

(1) The perimeter of the net is twice the total length of the cut edges. A glued edge lies inside the net. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

(2) Every vertex of the polyhedron is an end of some cut edge. At each vertex of a cuboid three right angles meet, with sum $270^\circ<360^\circ$. If no edge at a vertex were cut, all faces at that vertex would stay glued around it in the net, so their angles would have to fill the full angle $360^\circ$ around a point of the plane. This is impossible.

(3) The cut edges form a single tree containing all 8 vertices, so there are exactly 7 cut edges. The cut edges contain no closed path: a closed path of cuts would separate the surface into two parts and the net would not be in one piece. So the cut edges form a forest; let it have $k$ components $T_1,\dots,T_k$, with $v_1,\dots,v_k$ vertices ($v_1+\dots+v_k=8$ by (2), and each $T_i$ has $v_i-1\ge1$ edges). Walking once around the tree $T_i$ on the surface, we pass along each of its edges twice; in the net this walk is a closed broken line $\gamma_i$ formed by $2(v_i-1)$ boundary segments, with the net on its left. At a vertex $V$ of $T_i$ the cut edges at $V$ split the faces at $V$ into wedges; at the corner of $\gamma_i$ belonging to a wedge of angle $\omega$ the walk turns left by $180^\circ-\omega$, and the wedges at $V$ together contain all face angles at $V$. Hence the total turning of $\gamma_i$ is \[\tau_i=180^\circ\cdot2(v_i-1)-\sum_{V\in T_i}\alpha_V ,\] where $\alpha_V$ is the sum of the face angles at $V$. Since faces neither overlap nor touch along segments, $\gamma_i$ never crosses itself; it can only touch itself at isolated points. Push it slightly into the net: near a point where it touches itself, each pass through that point is pushed into its own wedge of faces (or into its own face, if the pass runs along a side there), and these do not overlap, so the pushed curve is a simple closed curve; it has the same total turning (a corner where the net angle exceeds $180^\circ$ becomes a small arc that turns by the same angle). The total turning of a simple closed curve made of segments and arcs is $+360^\circ$ or $-360^\circ$. As every $\alpha_V<360^\circ$, we get $\tau_i>360^\circ(v_i-1)-360^\circ v_i=-360^\circ$, so $\tau_i=+360^\circ$ for every $i$. Adding these $k$ equalities, \[360^\circ k=360^\circ\,(8-k)-\sum_{V}\alpha_V=360^\circ\,(8-k)-2160^\circ,\] because the sum of all face angles of the polyhedron is $6\cdot360^\circ=2160^\circ$. This gives $720^\circ k=720^\circ$, so $k=1$: the cut edges form one tree with all 8 vertices and 7 edges.

The possible lengths. The $1\times1\times2$ cuboid has $8$ edges of length $1$ (the sides of the two square faces) and $4$ edges of length $2$. Let $n$ of the $7$ cut edges have length $2$. The edges of length $1$ form two separate squares, so a tree containing all vertices must use at least one edge of length $2$; hence $1\le n\le4$. The total length of the cuts is $(7-n)+2n=7+n$, and by (1) the perimeter is $2(7+n)\in\{16,18,20,22\}$.

All four values occur. The figure shows a net for each value: in the nets with perimeters $16,18,20,22$ the cut tree contains $1,2,3,4$ edges of length $2$, respectively. In each of them the six faces do not overlap and non-glued faces meet at most at a point. So the perimeter can be $\boxed{16,\ 18,\ 20\text{ or }22}$ and nothing else.

Show solution 1 – Chinese remainder computation

Let $N$ be the age, $1000\le N\le9999$. Each resident's two statements are both true (knight) or both false (liar).

Both knights: $N\equiv80\pmod{101}$, $N\equiv18\pmod{99}$ (the other two conditions then hold automatically). Write $N=101a+80$. So $N\equiv101\cdot68+80=6948\pmod{9999}$, and the only four-digit value is $N=6948$.

Both liars: $N\equiv40\pmod{99}$, $N\equiv92\pmod{101}$. With $N=101a+92$: $2a+92\equiv40$, $2a\equiv-52\equiv47$, $a\equiv47\cdot50=2350\equiv73\pmod{99}$, so $N\equiv101\cdot73+92=7465\pmod{9999}$ and $N=7465$.

Check: $6948=101\cdot68+80=99\cdot70+18$, and $7465=101\cdot73+92=99\cdot75+40$. So the country is $\boxed{6948\text{ or }7465}$ years old.

Both knights: $y-x\equiv80\pmod{101}$ and $x+y\equiv18\pmod{99}$, so $y-x\in\{80,-21\}$ and $x+y\in\{18,117\}$. The numbers $x+y$ and $y-x$ have the same parity, which leaves $(y-x,x+y)=(80,18)$ (giving $x=-31$, impossible) and $(-21,117)$, giving $x=69$, $y=48$, $N=6948$.

Both liars: $y-x\equiv92\pmod{101}$ and $x+y\equiv40\pmod{99}$. Since $y-x\le89$, $y-x=-9$, which is odd, so $x+y$ is odd: $x+y=139$. Then $x=74$, $y=65$, $N=7465$.

Show solution 1 – Distance in the triangular lattice

Place the grasshopper at the origin and let $u,v$ be two tile sides from the origin at an angle of $60^\circ$. Every vertex of the tiling is $au+bv$ with integers $a,b$, and a jump changes $(a,b)$ by one of $\pm(1,0)$, $\pm(0,1)$, $\pm(1,-1)$.

Distance. Let $d(a,b)=\max(|a|,|b|,|a+b|)$. Each jump changes each of $a$, $b$, $a+b$ by at most $1$, so reaching $au+bv$ needs at least $d(a,b)$ jumps. Conversely, $d(a,b)$ jumps suffice: if $a,b\ge0$, use $a$ jumps $(1,0)$ and $b$ jumps $(0,1)$; if $a\ge0\ge b$ and $a\ge|b|$, use $|b|$ jumps $(1,-1)$ and $a-|b|$ jumps $(1,0)$; the other sign patterns are symmetric.

If $60-d$ is odd, replace the first jump $P\to Q$ by the two jumps $P\to R\to Q$ around a tile $PQR$; this adds one jump, and then add $(60-d-1)/2$ jumps there and back.

Counting. The vertices with $d(a,b)\le60$: for a fixed $a$ with $|a|\le60$, the conditions $|b|\le60$ and $|a+b|\le60$ allow exactly $121-|a|$ values of $b$ (for $a\ge0$, $b$ runs from $-60$ to $60-a$; for $a<0$, from $-60-a$ to $60$). Show the solution – Cut edges form a spanning tree

Call the edges along which the polyhedron was cut cut edges. We first prove three facts.

(1) The perimeter of the net is twice the total length of the cut edges. A glued edge lies inside the net. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. So the boundary of the net consists exactly of two copies of every cut edge, and no two copies overlap.

(2) Every vertex of the polyhedron is an end of some cut edge. At each vertex of a cuboid three right angles meet, with sum $270^\circ<360^\circ$. If no edge at a vertex were cut, all faces at that vertex would stay glued around it in the net, so their angles would have to fill the full angle $360^\circ$ around a point of the plane. This is impossible.

(3) The cut edges form a single tree containing all 8 vertices, so there are exactly 7 cut edges. The cut edges contain no closed path: a closed path of cuts would separate the surface into two parts and the net would not be in one piece. So the cut edges form a forest; let it have $k$ components $T_1,\dots,T_k$, with $v_1,\dots,v_k$ vertices ($v_1+\dots+v_k=8$ by (2), and each $T_i$ has $v_i-1\ge1$ edges). Walking once around the tree $T_i$ on the surface, we pass along each of its edges twice; in the net this walk is a closed broken line $\gamma_i$ formed by $2(v_i-1)$ boundary segments, with the net on its left. At a vertex $V$ of $T_i$ the cut edges at $V$ split the faces at $V$ into wedges; at the corner of $\gamma_i$ belonging to a wedge of angle $\omega$ the walk turns left by $180^\circ-\omega$, and the wedges at $V$ together contain all face angles at $V$. Hence the total turning of $\gamma_i$ is \[\tau_i=180^\circ\cdot2(v_i-1)-\sum_{V\in T_i}\alpha_V ,\] where $\alpha_V$ is the sum of the face angles at $V$. Since faces neither overlap nor touch along segments, $\gamma_i$ never crosses itself; it can only touch itself at isolated points. Push it slightly into the net: near a point where it touches itself, each pass through that point is pushed into its own wedge of faces (or into its own face, if the pass runs along a side there), and these do not overlap, so the pushed curve is a simple closed curve; it has the same total turning (a corner where the net angle exceeds $180^\circ$ becomes a small arc that turns by the same angle). The total turning of a simple closed curve made of segments and arcs is $+360^\circ$ or $-360^\circ$. As every $\alpha_V<360^\circ$, we get $\tau_i>360^\circ(v_i-1)-360^\circ v_i=-360^\circ$, so $\tau_i=+360^\circ$ for every $i$. Adding these $k$ equalities, \[360^\circ k=360^\circ\,(8-k)-\sum_{V}\alpha_V=360^\circ\,(8-k)-2160^\circ,\] because the sum of all face angles of the polyhedron is $6\cdot360^\circ=2160^\circ$. A cut edge separates two faces, and in the net each of these faces has a copy of the edge on its boundary; on the other side of such a copy there is no face, because faces may touch along a segment only where they are glued. (3) The cut edges form a single tree containing all 60 vertices, so there are exactly 59 cut edges. Lemma: $P$ is the midpoint of a diagonal. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$. Since $P$ is inside the convex quadrilateral, the line $BP$ separates $A$ from $C$: the ray from $B$ through $P$ leaves the quadrilateral through the side $CD$, the side $DA$ or the vertex $D$, and in each case $A$ and $C$ lie on different sides of the line $BP$. Yes

Show solution 1 – Four free digits cover all residues

Yes, for every phone number. Number the digit positions of the $23$-digit number $N$ from the right, starting with $0$. The new digits stand at the even positions $0,2,\dots,22$ (the last digit at position $0$, the first digit at position $22$), and the digits of the phone number at the odd positions $1,3,\dots,21$.

From $n-9\le n-a+130c\le n+1170$ we get $-4509\le100m\le900$, so $-45\le m\le9$. Every integer $m$ in this range is $b-5f$ with digits $b,f$: if $m\ge0$ take $f=0$, $b=m$; if $m<0$ take $f=\lceil -m/5\rceil\le9$ and $b=m+5f\in\{0,\dots,4\}$. Then $a+100b-130c-500f=a-130c+100m=n$.

The interval $[-4500,-270]$ contains $4231\ge2026$ consecutive integers, so it contains an integer $n\equiv-F\pmod{2026}$. Choosing $a,b,c,f$ for this $n$ gives $N\equiv F+n\equiv0\pmod{2026}$.

Show solution 2 – Two blocks of three digits modulo 1013

Yes, for every phone number. Number the digit positions of the $23$-digit number $N$ from the right, starting with $0$. Many residues from three digits. Let $S$ be the set of residues of the $600$ numbers $a+100b+10^4c$ with digits $a,b\in\{0,\dots,9\}$ and $c\in\{0,\dots,5\}$. We show $|S|=570$. Note $10^4=10\cdot1013-130\equiv-130$. If two different triples give the same residue, their differences $\Delta a,\Delta b,\Delta c$ satisfy \[\Delta a+100\,\Delta b-130\,\Delta c=1013\,u\] for an integer $u$, with $|\Delta a|,|\Delta b|\le9$ and $|\Delta c|\le5$. The left side has absolute value at most $9+900+650<2026$, so $u\in\{-1,0,1\}$; swapping the two triples we may assume $u\in\{0,1\}$.

  • $u=0$: modulo $10$, $\Delta a\equiv0$, so $\Delta a=0$; then $10\Delta b=13\Delta c$, so $10\mid\Delta c$, hence $\Delta c=0$ and $\Delta b=0$, and the triples are equal.

  • $u=1$: modulo $10$, $\Delta a\equiv3$, so $\Delta a=3$ or $\Delta a=-7$. If $\Delta a=3$, then $10\Delta b-13\Delta c=101$, so $3\Delta c\equiv9\pmod{10}$, $\Delta c=3$ and $\Delta b=14$, impossible. If $\Delta a=-7$, then $10\Delta b-13\Delta c=102$, so $3\Delta c\equiv8\pmod{10}$, $\Delta c\equiv6\pmod{10}$, $\Delta c=-4$ and $\Delta b=5$.

So two different triples with equal residues always differ by $\pm D$, where $D=(-7,5,-4)$. Three triples $x,y,z$ with one residue are impossible: $x-y$ and $y-z$ would be $\pm D$, so $x-z\in\{0,\pm2D\}$, but $x-z$ must be $\pm D$. Hence every residue of $S$ comes from one or two triples, and the pairs of triples differing by $D$ are counted by choosing the triple with the larger $a$: it has $a\in\{7,8,9\}$, $b\in\{0,\dots,4\}$, $c\in\{4,5\}$, and the other triple is then $(a-7,b+5,c-4)$; this gives $3\cdot5\cdot2=30$ pairs. Pigeonhole. Since $1013$ does not divide $100$, there is a residue $r$ with $100r\equiv-C$. Put $q=100^3$; multiplication by $q$ permutes the residues modulo the prime $1013$, so the set $T=\{r-qt:\ t\in S\}$ also has $570$ elements. As $570+570=1140>1013$, the sets $S$ and $T$ have a common residue: $s\equiv r-qt$ for some $s,t\in S$. Choose $(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ as a triple $(a,b,c)$ giving $s$, and $(e_4,e_5,e_6)$ as a triple giving $t$; these are digits. Then \[N=C+100(s+qt)\equiv C+100r\equiv0\pmod{1013}.\] So $N$ is divisible by $1013$ and by $2$, hence by $2026$, and it is a $23$-digit number of the required form.

A country has $2026$ airports, numbered from $1$ to $2026$. There is a flight between two airports if the sum of their numbers is the square of a natural number. Is it true that even if one of the airports closes, one can still get from any of the remaining airports to any other, possibly changing planes at intermediate airports (but not at the closed one)?

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a small number of airports, draw the links whose endpoint sums are squares. If some airports can be arranged in one cycle using every airport, inspect what remains when a vertex closes.
Hint 2: key idea
Find a cycle through the first $32$ airports, then add the later airports one at a time. It is enough for each new airport to have two distinct neighbors already in the network. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Construct or search for the starting cycle and verify every adjacent sum, including the closing one. For airport $n+1$, choose the first square at least $n+2$ and the next square, and check both are at most $2n+1$. Consider deletion of the new airport and deletion of an old airport separately in the induction.
Common trap
One earlier neighbor proves connectivity when nothing closes, but that neighbor might be the one airport removed.
Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution – A Hamiltonian cycle, then two links for every new airport

Let $G_n$ be the network on the airports $1,\dots,n$ (flights between $i\ne j$ with $i+j$ a perfect square). We prove: for every $n$ with $32\le n\le2026$, the network $G_n$ is connected, and it stays connected after closing any one airport. For $n=2026$ this gives the answer yes.

$n=32$. The airports $1,\dots,32$ can be arranged in a cycle with all adjacent sums squares: \[1,8,28,21,4,32,17,19,30,6,3,13,12,24,25,11,\] \[5,31,18,7,29,20,16,9,27,22,14,2,23,26,10,15\] (and back to $1$; the sums of neighbors are 9, 36, 49, 25, 36, 49, 36, 49, 36, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 36, 16, 36, 49, 25, 36, 49, 36, 25, 36, 49, 36, 16, 25, 49, 36, 25, 16). So $G_{32}$ is connected, and if one airport closes, the other $31$ still form a path along this cycle.

From $n$ to $n+1$ ($32\le n\le2025$). Let $s$ be the smallest integer with $s^2\ge n+2$; then $s\le\sqrt{n+1}+1$, so \[(s+1)^2\le\bigl(\sqrt{n+1}+2\bigr)^2=n+5+4\sqrt{n+1}\le2n+1,\] where the last step means $4\sqrt{n+1}\le n-4$, i.e. $16n+16\le n^2-8n+16$, i.e. $n\ge24$. So $s^2$ and $(s+1)^2$ both lie in $[n+2,2n+1]$, and airport $n+1$ has flights to the two different airports $s^2-(n+1)$ and $(s+1)^2-(n+1)$ of $G_n$.

Now assume the statement for $G_n$. The network $G_{n+1}$ is connected, since $G_n$ is connected and airport $n+1$ has a flight into $G_n$. Close an airport $z$ of $G_{n+1}$. If $z=n+1$, the open airports form $G_n$, which is connected. If $z\le n$, then $G_n$ without $z$ is connected by the induction hypothesis, and airport $n+1$ has a flight to one of its two partners, which is different from $z$. In both cases the open airports are connected.

By induction the statement holds for $n=2026$: closing any one airport, one can still travel between any two of the remaining airports.

Hint 3: approach
Use the second listed term as a candidate ratio. Compare its square and cube with the third and fourth listed terms using the relation just obtained.

The displayed fractions require nonzero denominators, and $k$ may be negative, so avoid taking an unsigned square root for it.
Show solution 1 – Eliminating m

Let $r=\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}$. Since $m=\ell/r$ is defined, $r>0$, and since $m$ and $\ell$ are denominators in the statement, $m\ne0$ and $\ell\ne0$. From $k^2+\ell^2+m^2=1$ we get $r^2+\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}=1$, i.e. $\ell^2=r^2(1-r^2)$, and therefore \[k^2=r^2-\ell^2=r^4 .\] In particular $k\ne0$. The four numbers are \[1,\qquad \frac{mk}{\ell}=\frac{\ell}{r}\cdot\frac{k}{\ell}=\frac kr,\qquad k^2+\ell^2=r^2,\qquad \frac{k\ell}{m}=k\ell\cdot\frac r\ell=kr .\] Put $q=\frac kr\ne0$. Show solution 2 – Spherical coordinates

Since $m=\ell/\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}$ is defined, $(k,\ell)\ne(0,0)$; write $k=r\cos\varphi$, $\ell=r\sin\varphi$ with $r=\sqrt{k^2+\ell^2}>0$. The point is on the unit sphere: $r^2+m^2=1$, so $r^2=1-\sin^2\varphi=\cos^2\varphi$ and $r=|\cos\varphi|=\varepsilon\cos\varphi$ with $\varepsilon=\pm1$; in particular $\cos\varphi\ne0$.   • Since $2^3=8<10$, we have $2^d\le10^r$ for $d=0,1,\dots,3r$; so $10^c2^d\le10^{1000}$ for these $3r+1$ values of $d$.

  • Since $5<10$, we have $5^e\le10^r$ for $e=1,\dots,r$; so $10^c5^e\le10^{1000}$ for these $r$ values of $e$.

All these numbers are different, so the count of numbers from $1$ to $10^{1000}$ with prime factors only $2$ and $5$ is at least \[\sum_{r=0}^{1000}(4r+1)=4\cdot\frac{1000\cdot1001}2+1001=2002000+1001=2003001 .\] Even without the number $1$, this is more than two million.

Show solution 2 – Lattice points under a line

We count pairs of non-negative integers $(a,b)$ with $2^a5^b\le10^{1000}$; different pairs give different numbers.

Two estimates: since $5^3=125<128=2^7$, we have $5<2^{7/3}$ and $5^b\le2^{7b/3}$ (with equality only for $b=0$); and since $(5/4)^{10}>8$ (indeed $(5/4)^2=\frac{25}{16}>1.56$, $(5/4)^4>2.43$, $(5/4)^8>5.9$, $(5/4)^{10}>5.9\cdot1.56>9.2$), we get \[10^{1000}=8^{1000}\Bigl(\frac54\Bigr)^{1000}>2^{3000}\cdot8^{100}=2^{3300}.\] Hence $2^a5^b\le2^{a+7b/3}\le2^{3300}<10^{1000}$ whenever $3a+7b\le9900$. For each $b=0,1,\dots,1414$ (note $7\cdot1414=9898$) the number of admissible $a$ is $\bigl\lfloor\frac{9900-7b}3\bigr\rfloor+1>\frac{9900-7b}3$. So the number of pairs is greater than \[\sum_{b=0}^{1414}\frac{9900-7b}3=\frac{1415\cdot9900-7\cdot\frac{1414\cdot1415}2}{3},\] which equals \[\frac{14008500-7002835}3=\frac{7005665}3>2335000 .\] So there are more than two million such numbers (even after removing the number $1$).

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement · Area Ratios in Triangles and Polygons

A point $P$ is marked inside a convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ so that the triangles $PAB$, $PBC$, $PCD$, and $PDA$ have equal areas. It is also known that $PB=PC$. Prove that the quadrilateral $ABCD$ has a right angle.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the four triangles around $P$. Triangles $PAB$ and $PBC$ share the side $PB$: what does the equality of their areas say about the distances from $A$ and from $C$ to the line $BP$?
Hint 2: key idea
The equal-area comparisons put $BP,DP$ through the midpoint of $AC$ and $AP,CP$ through the midpoint of $BD$. Test whether $P$ can differ from both midpoints without forcing those lines to coincide with the diagonals. (LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement)
Hint 3: approach
In each possible midpoint case, combine the equal half-diagonal lengths with $PB=PC$. Draw the circle centered at that midpoint through the diagonal endpoints and locate the third relevant vertex.
Common trap
The argument may identify different vertices in the two cases, so do not assume a particular angle is right at the outset.
Show solution 1 – Equal areas force a midpoint of a diagonal

Lemma: $P$ is the midpoint of a diagonal. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$. Since $P$ is inside the convex quadrilateral, the line $BP$ separates $A$ from $C$: the ray from $B$ through $P$ leaves the quadrilateral through the side $CD$, the side $DA$ or the vertex $D$, and in each case $A$ and $C$ lie on different sides of the line $BP$. The triangles $PAB$ and $PBC$ have the common side $PB$ and equal areas, so $A$ and $C$ are at equal distances from line $BP$. As they are on different sides of it, the segment $AC$ meets line $BP$ at a point $K$, and the right triangles formed by $A$, $C$, their feet of perpendiculars on line $BP$ and $K$ are congruent (equal legs, equal vertical angles at $K$), so $AK=KC$ and $K=M$. So the line $BP$ passes through $M$. In the same way $[PCD]=[PDA]$ shows that line $DP$ passes through $M$; $[PBC]=[PCD]$ shows that line $CP$ passes through $N$; and $[PDA]=[PAB]$ shows that line $AP$ passes through $N$.

Suppose $P\ne M$ and $P\ne N$. The lines $BP$ and $DP$ both contain $P$ and $M$, so they coincide: $B,P,D,M$ are collinear. Likewise $A,P,C,N$ are collinear. Then $P$ lies on both diagonals, so $P$ is their intersection point; but $M$ also lies on both diagonals, so $M=P$, a contradiction. Hence $P=M$ or $P=N$.

Conclusion. If $P=M$, then $PA=PC=PB$, so $B$ lies on the circle with diameter $AC$ (centre $P$, radius $PA$), and $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. If $P=N$, then $PB=PD=PC$, so $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $BD$, and $\angle BCD=90^\circ$. In both cases $ABCD$ has a right angle.

Show solution 2 – Cross products

Label the quadrilateral counterclockwise, put the origin at $P$ and write $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c,\vec d$ for the vectors $\overrightarrow{PA},\overrightarrow{PB},\overrightarrow{PC},\overrightarrow{PD}$. For $u=(u_1,u_2)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2)$ let $u\times v=u_1v_2-u_2v_1$. This product is linear in each factor, $v\times u=-u\times v$, $u\times v=0$ for nonzero $u,v$ only when they are parallel, and $u\times v$ is twice the area of the triangle with sides $u,v$ when $v$ is obtained from $u$ by a counterclockwise turn of less than $180^\circ$. Since $P$ is inside the quadrilateral, each of the triangles $PAB$, $PBC$, $PCD$, $PDA$ is traversed counterclockwise, so with $S$ their common area \[\vec a\times\vec b=\vec b\times\vec c=\vec c\times\vec d=\vec d\times\vec a=2S>0 .\]

$P$ is the midpoint of a diagonal. From $\vec b\times\vec c=\vec c\times\vec d$ we get $\vec c\times\vec b+\vec c\times\vec d=0$, i.e. $\vec c\times(\vec b+\vec d)=0$; from $\vec d\times\vec a=\vec a\times\vec b$ we get $\vec a\times(\vec b+\vec d)=0$. Suppose $\vec b+\vec d\ne\vec0$. Then the nonzero vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec c$ are both parallel to $\vec b+\vec d$, so $\vec c=\mu\vec a$ for some number $\mu$. Now $\vec b\times\vec c=\mu\,(\vec b\times\vec a)=-\mu\,(\vec a\times\vec b)$, and $\vec a\times\vec b=\vec b\times\vec c\ne0$ gives $\mu=-1$, i.e. $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$. So $\vec b+\vec d=\vec0$ or $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$: $P$ is the midpoint of $BD$ or of $AC$.

The right angle. If $\vec a+\vec c=\vec0$, then $\vec c=-\vec a$, so $PA=PC=PB$ and $\vec a\cdot\vec a=\vec b\cdot\vec b$; therefore \[\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=(\vec a-\vec b)\cdot(-\vec a-\vec b)=\vec b\cdot\vec b-\vec a\cdot\vec a=0,\] and $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. If $\vec b+\vec d=\vec0$, then $\vec d=-\vec b$, so $PB=PD$ and, with $PB=PC$, $\vec b\cdot\vec b=\vec c\cdot\vec c$; therefore \[\overrightarrow{CB}\cdot\overrightarrow{CD}=(\vec b-\vec c)\cdot(-\vec b-\vec c)=\vec c\cdot\vec c-\vec b\cdot\vec b=0,\] and $\angle BCD=90^\circ$. In both cases $ABCD$ has a right angle.
