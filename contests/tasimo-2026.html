Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Winning and Losing Positions

On an infinite square grid, a car occupies one unit square and is oriented North, East, South, or West. Two players play a game taking turns using these moves:

  • Player 1 moves the car forward in its current orientation by at least $1$ and at most $10$ squares.

  • Player 2 rotates the car in place by $90^\circ$ left, or $90^\circ$ right.

Player 1 starts. Player 2 wins if within the first $1000$ Player 1's moves the car visits some square twice. Otherwise, Player 1 wins. Determine which player has a winning strategy.

Note: Neither rotating the car by Player 2 nor driving through a square counts as a new visit. In particular, each move of Player 1 makes exactly one square visited by one more time than before.

Show the answer

Player 2 has a winning strategy.

Show the solution – Turning towards the axes

Player 2 wins. Put the starting square at the origin of a coordinate grid. Player 1's moves alternate between horizontal and vertical ones, because every rotation by Player 2 turns the car by $90^\circ$, and after each rotation Player 2 chooses between the two opposite directions along the new axis.

Strategy of Player 2: after each move of Player 1, turn the car so that it points towards the axis it is going to approach: if the next move will be horizontal, point it in the direction of decreasing $|x|$ (either direction if $x=0$); if vertical, in the direction of decreasing $|y|$.

Claim: the car always stays in the square $-10\le x,y\le10$. At the start $x=y=0$. A move of Player 1 changes only one coordinate, by $1$ to $10$ steps. The first move goes from $0$ to a value in $[-10,10]$. Every later horizontal move starts from some $x$ with $|x|\le10$ and goes towards $0$ (or away from $0$ if $x=0$): if $1\le x\le 10$, the new value is between $x-10\ge-9$ and $x-1$, so it lies in $[-10,10]$; the case $x<0$ is symmetric, and from $x=0$ the new value is in $[-10,10]$. The same holds for $y$.

The square contains $21^2=441$ cells. The car stops on a cell after each of the first $1000$ moves of Player 1, so among these $1000>441$ stopping cells two coincide. Hence some square is visited twice within the first $1000$ moves (indeed within the first $442$), and Player 2 wins.

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Coordinate Optimization of Synthetic Figures

We are given an acute triangle $ABC$ in which $AB<AC$. Let $I$ be its incenter and $D$ be the projection of $I$ on $BC$. Line $AI$ meets $BC$ at $E$. Let $S$ be the midpoint of the arc $BAC$. Let $F\neq D$ be the point on line segment $CE$ such that $A,D,S,F$ are concyclic. Let $P$ be the intersection point of lines $AF$ and $SE$. Show that $\angle PIA=\angle FDA$.

Show solution 1 – Coordinates from the half-angle tangents

Scale the figure so that the inradius is $1$, and choose coordinates with $D=(0,0)$, $I=(0,1)$ and $BC$ on the $x$-axis, $C$ to the right. Let $p=\tan\frac{\angle B}{2}$ and $q=\tan\frac{\angle C}{2}$. Since $AB<AC$ we have $\angle C<\angle B$, so $0<q<p$; since $\frac{\angle B}{2}+\frac{\angle C}{2}<90^\circ$ we have $pq<1$; and since the triangle is acute, $p,q<1$.

The points. $DB=\frac1p$ and $DC=\frac1q$, so $B=(-\frac1p,0)$, $C=(\frac1q,0)$. Line $BA$ has slope $\tan B=\frac{2p}{1-p^2}$ and line $CA$ has slope $-\frac{2q}{1-q^2}$; one checks that both pass through \[A=\Big(\frac{q-p}{1-pq},\ \frac{2}{1-pq}\Big).\] Line $AI$ meets the $x$-axis at $E=\big(\frac{p-q}{1+pq},0\big)$. The point $S$ is the intersection of the perpendicular bisector $x=\frac{p-q}{2pq}$ of $BC$ with the external bisector at $A$ (the line through $A$ perpendicular to $AI$), and indeed \[S=\Big(\frac{p-q}{2pq},\ \frac{(p+q)^2}{2pq(1-pq)}\Big),\qquad S-A=\frac{p-q}{2pq(1-pq)}\,(1+pq,\ p-q)\perp(q-p,\ 1+pq)\parallel A-I .\] The circle through $D$, $A$, $S$ has an equation $x^2+y^2+Ux+Wy=0$; it meets the $x$-axis again at $F=(-U,0)$. Substituting $A$ and $S$ and eliminating $W$ gives \[F=\Big(\frac{(p-q)(p^2+pq+q^2+1)}{pq\,(p^2+q^2+2)},\ 0\Big),\] whose abscissa is positive. With $N=pq(p-q)^2+(p+q)^2+4pq>0$, the point \[P=\Big(\frac{(p-q)(p^2+q^2+2)}{N},\ \frac{2(p+q)^2}{N}\Big)\] satisfies $P=A+\lambda(F-A)$ with $\lambda=\frac{2pq(p^2+q^2+2)}{N}$ and $P=E+\mu(S-E)$ with $\mu=\frac{4pq(1-pq)}{N}$, so it is the intersection of $AF$ and $SE$.

The two angles. We have $2(p+q)^2-N=(p-q)^2(1-pq)$, so \[A-I=\tfrac{1}{1-pq}\,u_1,\quad P-I=\tfrac{p-q}{N}\,u_2,\qquad u_1=(q-p,\ 1+pq),\quad u_2=\big(p^2+q^2+2,\ (p-q)(1-pq)\big),\] \[A-D=\tfrac{1}{1-pq}\,v_1,\quad F-D=\text{(positive)}\cdot v_2,\qquad v_1=(q-p,\ 2),\quad v_2=(1,0),\] all with positive factors. For two vectors $u,w$ the cross product $u\times w=u_xw_y-u_yw_x$ and the dot product $u\cdot w$ determine the oriented angle from $u$ to $w$ up to a common positive factor. Here \[u_1\times u_2=-(p-q)^2(1-pq)-(1+pq)(p^2+q^2+2)=-2(1+p^2)(1+q^2),\] \[u_1\cdot u_2=-(p-q)\big(p^2+q^2+2-(1-p^2q^2)\big)=-(p-q)(1+p^2)(1+q^2),\] while $v_1\times v_2=-2$ and $v_1\cdot v_2=-(p-q)$. So $(u_1\times u_2,\ u_1\cdot u_2)=(1+p^2)(1+q^2)\,(v_1\times v_2,\ v_1\cdot v_2)$: the oriented angle from ray $IA$ to ray $IP$ equals the oriented angle from ray $DA$ to ray $DF$. In particular $\angle PIA=\angle FDA$.

Show solution 2 – An excenter and a harmonic quadrangle

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$ (so $c<b$), let $I_A$ be the excenter opposite $A$, let $M$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, and let $Z$ be the intersection of line $AS$ with line $BC$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$, and the following standard facts.

  • $A,I,E,M,I_A$ lie on the bisector of angle $A$ in this order, and $MB=MI=MI_A$ (incenter–excenter lemma).

  • $S$ and $M$ both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, so $SM$ is a diameter of the circumcircle and $SM\perp BC$. Hence $\angle SAM=\angle SBM=90^\circ$; in particular $AS$ is the external bisector of angle $A$ (so $Z$ exists, as $AB\ne AC$).

Step 1: positions on $BC$ and on the circle. By the external bisector theorem $\frac{ZB}{ZC}=\frac cb<1$, so $Z$ lies on ray $CB$ beyond $B$. Next, $BD=\frac{a+c-b}{2}$ and $BE=\frac{ac}{b+c}$, and \[BE-BD=\frac{2ac-(a+c-b)(b+c)}{2(b+c)}=\frac{(b-c)(b+c-a)}{2(b+c)}>0 .\] So the order on line $BC$ is $Z,B,D,E,C$, and $F$ (on segment $CE$) lies on the other side of $D$ from $Z$. Finally, $AB<AC$ gives $\angle C<\angle B$, so the arc $AB$ not containing $C$ is shorter than the arc $AC$ not containing $B$; hence the midpoint $S$ of the arc $BAC$ lies on the arc $AC$, and the order on the circle is $B,A,S,C,M$. Thus $S$ and $C$ lie on the same side of the line $AM$ (the line $AI$), while $B$ and $Z$ lie on the other side.

Step 2: $F$ lies on line $SI_A$. Let $K$ be the point where the incircle touches $AB$. The right triangles $AKI$ (right angle at $K$) and $SBM$ (right angle at $B$) have $\angle KAI=\frac{\angle A}{2}=\angle BAM=\angle BSM$, so they are similar and $\frac{IK}{IA}=\frac{MB}{MS}$, that is, $\frac{ID}{IA}=\frac{MI_A}{MS}$. Moreover $\angle DIA=\angle I_AMS$: the rays $ID$ and $MS$ are perpendicular to $BC$ and point in opposite directions ($I$ is above $BC$, $M$ below it), and the rays $IA$ and $MI_A$ lie on the bisector and point in opposite directions. By SAS, the triangles $IDA$ and $MI_AS$ are similar, with $I\mapsto M$, $D\mapsto I_A$, $A\mapsto S$. This similarity reverses orientation: the half-turn sends the rays $ID$, $IA$ to rays in the directions of $MS$, $MI_A$, but our correspondence sends $D$ to the ray $MI_A$ and $A$ to the ray $MS$, i.e. it swaps the two rays. An orientation-reversing similarity changes the sign of directed angles, so \[\measuredangle(AI,AD)=-\measuredangle(SM,SI_A)=\measuredangle(SI_A,SM).\] Therefore, using $SM\perp BC$ and $AS\perp AI$, \[\measuredangle(SI_A,BC)=\measuredangle(SI_A,SM)+90^\circ=\measuredangle(AI,AD)+90^\circ=\measuredangle(AI,AD)+\measuredangle(AS,AI)=\measuredangle(AS,AD).\] On the other hand, $A,D,S,F$ are concyclic, so $\measuredangle(FS,BC)=\measuredangle(FS,FD)=\measuredangle(AS,AD)$. The lines $FS$ and $SI_A$ make the same angle with $BC$ and both pass through $S$, so they coincide: $F\in SI_A$.

Step 3: $P$, $I$, $Z$ are collinear. The lines $BI$ and $BI_A$ are the internal and external bisectors of angle $ABE$ in triangle $ABE$, so $\frac{IA}{IE}=\frac{BA}{BE}=\frac{I_AA}{I_AE}$: the points $I$ and $I_A$ divide $AE$ harmonically. Now consider the four points $A,S,E,F$. No three are collinear: $S\notin AI$; $E,F\in BC$ and $A,S\notin BC$; $F\ne Z$ by Step 1; and $F\ne E$, for otherwise $E$ would lie on both lines $AI_A$ and $SI_A$, which would then coincide and contain $S$. The three diagonal points of this quadrangle are \[AF\cap SE=P,\qquad AE\cap SF=I_A\ \text{(by Step 2)},\qquad AS\cap EF=Z .\] By the harmonic property of the complete quadrangle, the line $ZP$ meets the line $AE$ at the harmonic conjugate of $I_A$ with respect to $A$ and $E$, which is $I$. So $P$, $I$, $Z$ are collinear.

Step 4: the angles. Since $\angle IAZ=\angle IDZ=90^\circ$, the points $A$, $I$, $D$, $Z$ lie on the circle with diameter $IZ$. The points $I$ and $D$ lie on the same side of the line $AZ$ (the whole triangle lies on one side of the external bisector at $A$), so the inscribed angles on the chord $AZ$ are equal: $\angle ZIA=\angle ZDA$.

$P$ is not on line $AI$ (line $AF$ meets it only at $A$, line $SE$ only at $E$). The points of line $AF$ on the $B$-side of $AI$ lie beyond $A$, farther from $BC$ than $A$; the points of line $SE$ on the $B$-side of $AI$ lie beyond $E$, on the other side of $BC$ from $S$ (here we use that $S$ and $F$ are on the $C$-side: $S$ by Step 1, and $F$ because it lies on segment $EC$ and $F\ne E$). These sets do not meet, so $P$ is on the $C$-side of $AI$, while $Z$ is on the $B$-side. Hence the line $ZP$ crosses $AI$ between $Z$ and $P$, i.e. $I$ lies between $Z$ and $P$, and $\angle PIA=180^\circ-\angle ZIA$. Also $Z$ and $F$ lie on opposite sides of $D$, so $\angle FDA=180^\circ-\angle ZDA$. Therefore \[\angle PIA=180^\circ-\angle ZIA=180^\circ-\angle ZDA=\angle FDA .\]

Problem 3

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

Let $p,q$ be two distinct odd prime numbers. Prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that \[\gcd\left(\binom{2n}{n},\,pq\right)=1.\]

Show the solution – Nested digit intervals in two bases

Reduction to digits. Call $n$ $p$-good if every base-$p$ digit of $n$ is at most $\frac{p-1}{2}$. By Legendre's formula, \[v_p\binom{2n}{n}=\sum_{k\ge1}\Big(\Big\lfloor\frac{2n}{p^k}\Big\rfloor-2\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{p^k}\Big\rfloor\Big),\] and the $k$-th term is $1$ if the remainder $n\bmod p^k$ is at least $\frac{p^k}{2}$ and $0$ otherwise. For a $p$-good $n$ the remainder $n\bmod p^k$ has $k$ digits, all at most $\frac{p-1}2$, so it is at most $\frac{p-1}{2}(1+p+\cdots+p^{k-1})=\frac{p^k-1}{2}$. Hence $p\nmid\binom{2n}{n}$ for $p$-good $n$. So it suffices to find infinitely many $n$ that are both $p$-good and $q$-good.

Digit intervals. For a $p$-good integer $c\ge0$ and $i\ge0$ let \[H_p(c,i)=\Big\{c\,p^i,\ c\,p^i+1,\ \dots,\ c\,p^i+\tfrac{p^i-1}{2}\Big\},\] a block of $\frac{p^i+1}{2}$ consecutive integers. It contains every $p$-good number whose base-$p$ expansion starts with the digits of $c$ followed by $i$ more digits. For $i\ge1$ its children are $H_p(cp+e,\,i-1)$, $e=0,1,\dots,\frac{p-1}{2}$. The first child starts at $cp^i$, the last one ends at $cp^i+\frac{p^i-1}{2}$, and between two consecutive children there are exactly $\frac{p^{i-1}-1}{2}$ integers of $H_p(c,i)$ that lie in no child.

Lemma. Let $H=H_p(c,i)$ with $i\ge1$ and let $K$ be a block of consecutive integers that meets $H$ and has more than $\frac{p^{i-1}-1}{2}$ elements. Then $K$ meets a child of $H$. Indeed, take $z\in K\cap H$ outside all children. Then $z$ lies in a gap between two consecutive children; $K$ has more elements than the gap, so it reaches past one end of the gap, where it meets a child.

The descent. We keep a pair $I=H_p(c,i)$, $J=H_q(d,j)$ ($c$ $p$-good, $d$ $q$-good) with $I\cap J\neq\varnothing$ and \[(\ast)\qquad p^{i-1}\le q^j+1\quad\text{and}\quad q^{j-1}\le p^i+1 .\] While $i+j>0$, do the following.

  • If $i\ge1$ and ($j=0$ or $q^{j-1}\le p^{i-1}+1$): by $(\ast)$, $|J|=\frac{q^j+1}{2}>\frac{p^{i-1}-1}{2}$, so by the Lemma $J$ meets a child $I'=H_p(c',i-1)$ of $I$. Replace $I$ by $I'$. Condition $(\ast)$ still holds: $p^{i-2}<p^{i-1}\le q^j+1$, and $q^{j-1}\le p^{i-1}+1$ by the choice of this case.

  • Otherwise $j\ge1$ and ($i=0$ or $q^{j-1}>p^{i-1}+1$). By $(\ast)$, $|I|=\frac{p^i+1}{2}>\frac{q^{j-1}-1}{2}$, so $I$ meets a child $J'=H_q(d',j-1)$ of $J$; replace $J$ by $J'$. Condition $(\ast)$ still holds: $q^{j-2}<q^{j-1}\le p^i+1$, and if $i\ge1$ then $p^{i-1}<q^{j-1}$.

Each step lowers $i+j$ by $1$. When $i=j=0$, the blocks are the single numbers $c$ and $d$, and they meet, so $c=d=:n$. This $n$ is $p$-good and $q$-good (each step appends an allowed digit), and $n$ lies in the starting block $I$.

Starting pairs. Let $\alpha=\log_q p$, which is irrational ($p^a=q^b$ is impossible for $a,b\ge1$). For any $\varepsilon>0$ some $a\ge1$ has $\|a\alpha\|<\varepsilon$ (distance to the nearest integer): among the fractional parts of $\alpha,2\alpha,\dots,(K+1)\alpha$ with $K>\frac1\varepsilon$ two lie within $\frac1K$ of each other, and the difference of their indices works. Since $\|a\alpha\|>0$, repeating with a smaller $\varepsilon$ gives new values of $a$; so there are infinitely many $a$ with $\|a\alpha\|<\log_q\frac43$. For such $a$ let $b$ be the integer nearest to $a\alpha$; then $\frac34<\frac{p^a}{q^b}<\frac43$ (so $b\ge1$, as $p^a\ge3$). Start with $I=H_p(1,a)$ and $J=H_q(1,b)$ (the digit $1$ is allowed since $p,q\ge3$). They meet, because $p^a<\frac43q^b\le q^b+\frac{q^b-1}{2}$ and symmetrically (using $q^b\ge3$). Condition $(\ast)$ holds: $p^{a-1}=\frac{p^a}{p}<\frac{4q^b}{9}<q^b$ and likewise $q^{b-1}<p^a$. The descent produces $n\ge p^a$ that is $p$-good and $q$-good. As $a$ can be taken arbitrarily large, there are infinitely many such $n$, and for each of them $\gcd\big(\binom{2n}{n},pq\big)=1$.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 16: Diophantine Equations: Linear and Structural Foundations · Bounded Integer Solutions

A bank issues coins with denominations $a$ and $b$, where $a>b>1$ are coprime integers. The bank is interested in situations where a person has a set of coins with a total value of $n$, but cannot pay an exact amount of $k$. For given natural numbers $k<n$, we say that $k$ is $n$-bad if there exists a set of coins with a total value of $n$ such that none of its subsets has a total value equal to $k$. In particular, if no set of coins with a total value of $n$ exists, then no $k<n$ is $n$-bad. For each $n$, let $f(n)$ be the number of natural numbers less than $n$ that are $n$-bad. Prove that there exists exactly one positive integer that occurs infinitely many times in the sequence $f(1),f(2),f(3),\ldots$.

Show the answer

proof (the value is $(a-1)(b-1)$)

Show the solution – The residue class of the Frobenius number

Call $k\ge0$ representable if $k=ia+jb$ with integers $i,j\ge0$, and let $g=ab-a-b$. Every integer $k>g$ is representable (classical), so the set $\mathcal N$ of non-representable positive integers is finite and contained in $[1,g]$; it is nonempty, since $1\in\mathcal N$. A set of coins of total $n$ is a pair $(x,y)$ with $xa+yb=n$; its subset sums are the numbers $ia+jb$ with $0\le i\le x$, $0\le j\le y$. Note that this set of sums is symmetric: $k$ is a sum iff $n-k$ is.

Step 1: $f(n)=2|\mathcal N|$ for every $n=Mab-a-b$ with $M\ge2$. Here $xa+yb=n$ means $(x+1)a+(y+1)b=Mab$; as $\gcd(a,b)=1$ this gives $x=bs-1$, $y=a(M-s)-1$ with $1\le s\le M-1$. So sets of coins exist, and each has $x\ge b-1$ and $y\ge a-1$.

  • Every $k\in\mathcal N$ is $n$-bad (it is never a subset sum), and so is $n-k$ (by the symmetry). These are $2|\mathcal N|$ different numbers in $[1,n-1]$: $k\le g$ while $n-k\ge n-g=(M-1)ab\ge ab>g$.

  • Every other $k\in[1,n-1]$ has $k$ and $n-k$ both representable, and we show that it is a subset sum of every set $(x,y)=(bs-1,\ a(M-s)-1)$. Write $k=ia+jb$ and $n-k=i'a+j'b$ with $0\le i,i'\le b-1$ (reduce $i$ modulo $b$ using $ba=ab$). Adding, $(i+i')a\equiv n\equiv-a\pmod b$, so $i+i'=b-1$, and then $j+j'=a(M-1)-1$; hence $j\le a(M-1)-1$. Write $j=aw+r$ with $0\le r\le a-1$, so $0\le w\le M-2$. We look for $t$ with $0\le t\le s-1$ and $0\le j-at\le a(M-s)-1$; then $k=(i+bt)a+(j-at)b$ with $0\le i+bt\le b-1+b(s-1)=x$ and $0\le j-at\le y$ is a subset sum. The second condition reads $w-(M-s)+1\le t\le w$, and the interval $[\max(0,w-M+s+1),\ \min(w,s-1)]$ is nonempty because $w\le M-2$ and $1\le s\le M-1$.

So $f(n)=2|\mathcal N|$, a positive constant, for all $n\equiv-a-b\pmod{ab}$, $n>ab$: this value occurs infinitely often.

Step 2: every other value occurs finitely often. Let $n>g$ with $n\not\equiv-a-b\pmod{ab}$. Then $n\not\equiv-a\pmod b$ or $n\not\equiv-b\pmod a$ (by the Chinese remainder theorem).

  • If $n\not\equiv-a\pmod b$: take the set of coins $(x,y)$ with $0\le x\le b-1$ (it exists because $n>g$). From $xa\equiv n\not\equiv(b-1)a\pmod b$ we get $x\le b-2$. A subset sum $ia+jb$ has $i\le b-2$, so it is never $\equiv(b-1)a\pmod b$. Thus all $k\in[1,n-1]$ with $k\equiv(b-1)a\pmod b$ are $n$-bad, and $f(n)\ge\lfloor\frac{n-1}{b}\rfloor$.

  • If $n\not\equiv-b\pmod a$: in the same way, with the set having $0\le y\le a-2$, we get $f(n)\ge\lfloor\frac{n-1}{a}\rfloor$.

So $f(n)\ge\lfloor\frac{n-1}{a}\rfloor$ for all such $n$. Given any value $v\ne2|\mathcal N|$, the equation $f(n)=v$ can hold only for $n\le g$ or for $n\not\equiv-a-b\pmod{ab}$ with $\lfloor\frac{n-1}{a}\rfloor\le v$, i.e. for finitely many $n$.

Hence exactly one positive integer, namely $2|\mathcal N|$, occurs infinitely many times in $f(1),f(2),\dots$. (By Sylvester's theorem $|\mathcal N|=\frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{2}$, so this value is $(a-1)(b-1)$.)

Problem 5

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Partial Fractions and Decomposition in Sums

Find all non-zero polynomials $P(x)$ with real coefficients such that there exists a natural number $N$ with the following property: for every natural number $n\ge N$, there exists a polynomial $Q_n(x)$ with real coefficients such that \[\frac{1}{P(x)}+\frac{1}{P(x+1)}+\cdots+\frac{1}{P(x+n-1)}=\frac{1}{Q_n(x)}.\]

Show the answer

$P(x)=c$ and $P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)$ with real $c\ne0$, $d$

Show solution 1 – Passing to the infinite sum

These polynomials work. For $P=c$: $\sum_{i<n}\frac1c=\frac{1}{c/n}$. For $P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)$: $\frac{1}{P(x+i)}=\frac1c\Big(\frac{1}{x+d+i}-\frac{1}{x+d+i+1}\Big)$, so the sum telescopes to $\frac1c\Big(\frac1{x+d}-\frac{1}{x+d+n}\Big)=\frac{n}{c(x+d)(x+d+n)}$, the reciprocal of the polynomial $Q_n=\frac{c}{n}(x+d)(x+d+n)$.

No others. Let $m=\deg P\ge1$ with leading coefficient $c$, and write $S_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac1{P(x+i)}=\frac1{Q_n(x)}$ for $n\ge N$. As $x\to+\infty$, $S_n(x)\sim\frac{n}{cx^m}$, so $Q_n$ has degree $m$ and leading coefficient $\frac cn$.

$m=1$ is impossible. If $P=c(x-r)$, then $S_n$ has simple poles at the $n$ distinct points $r,r-1,\dots,r-n+1$ (each comes from one term only), while $\frac1{Q_n}$ with $\deg Q_n=1$ has one pole. Contradiction for $n\ge2$.

$m\ge2$. Choose $x_0$ larger than all real roots of $P$. For real $x>x_0$ all terms $\frac1{P(x+i)}$ have the sign of $c$, and since $|P(y)|\ge\frac{|c|}2y^m$ for large $y$, the series $S_\infty(x)=\sum_{i\ge0}\frac1{P(x+i)}$ converges, with $S_\infty(x)\ne0$. Pick $m+1$ distinct points $x_0<t_0<\dots<t_m$. Then $Q_n(t_k)=\frac1{S_n(t_k)}\to\frac1{S_\infty(t_k)}$ as $n\to\infty$. A polynomial of degree at most $m$ is determined by its values at $t_0,\dots,t_m$, and by Lagrange interpolation its coefficients are fixed linear combinations of these values; so the coefficients of $Q_n$ converge, and $Q_n\to R$ coefficientwise for a polynomial $R$. The leading coefficient $\frac cn$ tends to $0$, so $\deg R\le m-1$. For every $x>x_0$, $Q_n(x)\to R(x)$ and $Q_n(x)=\frac1{S_n(x)}\to\frac1{S_\infty(x)}$, hence $S_\infty(x)=\frac1{R(x)}$ there. Consequently, for $x>x_0$ and every $n\ge1$, \[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac1{P(x+i)}=S_\infty(x)-S_\infty(x+n)=\frac1{R(x)}-\frac1{R(x+n)},\] and since both sides are rational functions, this is an identity. $R$ is not constant (otherwise the sum would vanish). Let $\deg R=e\ge1$. Then $R(x+n)-R(x)$ has degree exactly $e-1$, and for $n\ge N$ \[Q_n(x)\big(R(x+n)-R(x)\big)=R(x)R(x+n).\] If $e\ge2$, the polynomial $R(x+n)-R(x)$ has a complex root $z_n$; then $R(z_n)R(z_n+n)=0$ and $R(z_n+n)=R(z_n)$, so both $z_n$ and $z_n+n$ are roots of $R$. Then the difference of two roots of $R$ equals $n$ for every $n\ge N$, impossible for a polynomial with finitely many roots. So $e=1$, $R(x)=\alpha x+\beta$ with $\alpha\ne0$, and the identity with $n=1$ gives \[\frac1{P(x)}=\frac1{R(x)}-\frac1{R(x+1)}=\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha x+\beta)(\alpha x+\alpha+\beta)},\qquad P(x)=\alpha\Big(x+\frac\beta\alpha\Big)\Big(x+\frac\beta\alpha+1\Big).\] So the answer is $\boxed{P(x)=c\ \text{ or }\ P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)}$ with $c\ne0$ and $d$ real.

Show solution 2 – Counting poles

These polynomials work. For $P=c$ the sum is $\frac nc=\frac1{c/n}$. For $P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)$ we have $\frac1{P(x+i)}=\frac1c\bigl(\frac1{x+d+i}-\frac1{x+d+i+1}\bigr)$, so the sum telescopes to $\frac1c\bigl(\frac1{x+d}-\frac1{x+d+n}\bigr)=\frac{n}{c(x+d)(x+d+n)}$, the reciprocal of the polynomial $Q_n=\frac cn(x+d)(x+d+n)$.

No others. Let $P$ have degree $m\ge1$ and leading coefficient $c$, let $F=\frac1P$, and for $n\ge N$ let $S_n(x)=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}F(x+s)=\frac1{Q_n(x)}$. We work with rational functions over $\mathbb C$. For a rational function $R=\frac UW$ in lowest terms, call $\deg W$ the pole count of $R$; it is the sum of the orders of all poles of $R$.

Step 1: $S_n$ has pole count $m$. As $x\to+\infty$, each $F(x+s)$ is asymptotic to $\frac1{cx^m}$, so $S_n(x)\sim\frac n{cx^m}$ and $Q_n=\frac1{S_n}$ has degree $m$. Since $S_n=\frac1{Q_n}$ is in lowest terms, its pole count is $m$.

Step 2: partial fractions. Over $\mathbb C$, $F=\sum_{\rho}\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{c_{\rho,r}}{(x-\rho)^r}$, a finite sum over the roots $\rho$ of $P$, and $F$ has a pole of order at least $r$ at $\rho$ whenever $c_{\rho,r}\ne0$. Split the roots into classes, two roots being in the same class when their difference is an integer. Fix a class, write its points as $\alpha+i$ with $i$ in a finite set of integers contained in $[i_0,i_1]$, and put $c_i=c_{\alpha+i,r}$ for a fixed order $r$ (with $c_i=0$ when $\alpha+i$ is not a root). The term $F(x+s)$ contributes $\frac{c_i}{(x-(\alpha+i-s))^r}$, so the coefficient of $(x-\alpha-k)^{-r}$ in $S_n$ is \[\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}c_{k+s}=c_k+c_{k+1}+\dots+c_{k+n-1}.\] For $n>i_1-i_0$ and each of the $n-(i_1-i_0)$ integers $k$ with $i_1-n+1\le k\le i_0$, this window contains all of $[i_0,i_1]$, and the coefficient equals $C=\sum_ic_i$. If $C\ne0$, then $S_n$ has a pole at each of these $n-(i_1-i_0)$ points, so its pole count is at least $n-(i_1-i_0)$, which exceeds $m$ for large $n$, contradicting Step 1. Hence \[\sum_ic_{\alpha+i,r}=0\qquad\text{for every class and every order }r. \tag{1}\]

Step 3: $F(x)=G(x+1)-G(x)$. In the class above define $g_i=c_{i_0}+c_{i_0+1}+\dots+c_{i-1}$ for $i_0\le i\le i_1+1$ (so $g_{i_0}=0$, and $g_{i_1+1}=0$ by (1)), so that $g_{i+1}-g_i=c_i$. Let $G$ be the sum, over all classes and orders, of the terms $\frac{g_i}{(x-\alpha-i)^r}$. Then \[G(x+1)=\sum\frac{g_i}{(x-\alpha-(i-1))^r}=\sum\frac{g_{i+1}}{(x-\alpha-i)^r},\] so $G(x+1)-G(x)=\sum\frac{g_{i+1}-g_i}{(x-\alpha-i)^r}=F(x)$. Consequently, by telescoping, \[S_n(x)=G(x+n)-G(x). \tag{2}\] $G$ is not $0$ (as $F\ne0$), it is proper (it tends to $0$ at infinity), and its poles lie in finitely many points. Let $B\ge1$ be the pole count of $G$.

Step 4: $m=2B$. For large $n$ the poles of $G(x+n)$ (the poles of $G$ shifted by $-n$) are disjoint from those of $G$, so by (2) every pole of $G$ and every pole of $G(x+n)$ is a pole of $S_n$ of the same order. Hence the pole count of $S_n$ is $2B$, and by Step 1, $m=2B$.

Step 5: $m\le B+1$. Write $G=\frac UW$ in lowest terms, with $\deg W=B$ and $e=\deg W-\deg U$; then $1\le e\le B$. Near infinity $G$ has an expansion $G(x)=\sum_{k\ge e}u_kx^{-k}$ with $u_e\ne0$. Since $(x+1)^{-k}-x^{-k}=-kx^{-k-1}+O(x^{-k-2})$, we get $F(x)=G(x+1)-G(x)=-eu_ex^{-e-1}+O(x^{-e-2})$. But $F=\frac1P$ behaves like $\frac1{cx^m}$, so $m=e+1\le B+1$.

Step 6: conclusion. From $2B=m\le B+1$ we get $B=1$, $m=2$ and $e=1$, so $G(x)=\frac u{x-\beta}$ with $u\ne0$, and \[\frac1{P(x)}=\frac u{x+1-\beta}-\frac u{x-\beta}=\frac{-u}{(x-\beta)(x-\beta+1)},\qquad P(x)=-\frac1u(x-\beta)(x-\beta+1).\] The roots of $P$ are $\beta$ and $\beta-1$. As $P$ has real coefficients, $\bar\beta$ is also a root; $\bar\beta=\beta-1$ is impossible (the imaginary parts would give $\operatorname{Im}\beta=0$, and then $\beta=\beta-1$), so $\bar\beta=\beta$ and $\beta$ is real. With $c=-\frac1u$ and $d=-\beta$ this is $P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)$.

So the answer is $\boxed{P(x)=c\ \text{ or }\ P(x)=c(x+d)(x+d+1)}$ with $c\ne0$ and $d$ real.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Extremal Graph Structure

Each of $10$ children has their own favorite type of candy, and these types are distinct for all children. Their grandmother orders a certain number of candies from a store, choosing any non-negative number of candies for each of the $10$ types. Then, the shopkeeper places all ordered candies into $10$ transparent bags, distributing individual candies among the bags in any way he wishes. Upon seeing the contents of the bags, the grandmother gives each child exactly one bag. After that, each child gives the grandmother one happiness point for each candy of their favorite type found in their received bag (and gives zero points for candies of other types).

For a natural number $m$, determine the minimum natural number $g=g(m)$ with the following property: the grandmother can order a total of $g$ candies such that, no matter how the shopkeeper distributes them into the bags, she will always be able to distribute the bags to the children in a way that secures at least $m$ happiness points.

Show the answer

$g(m)=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}$ for $m\le10$ and $g(m)=10m-45$ for $m\ge10$

Show the solution – Covers of the assignment problem

Reformulation. Let the grandmother order $n_t\ge0$ candies of type $t$ ($t=1,\dots,10$), $g=\sum n_t$. A distribution by the shopkeeper is a $10\times10$ matrix of non-negative integers $M_{tb}$ (candies of type $t$ in bag $b$) with row sums $n_t$. Giving bag $\pi(t)$ to the child who likes type $t$, for a permutation $\pi$, earns $\sum_tM_{t\pi(t)}$; let $\operatorname{val}(M)$ be the maximum over $\pi$. A cover of $M$ is a pair of non-negative integer vectors $(u_t)$, $(v_b)$ with $u_t+v_b\ge M_{tb}$ for all $t,b$. Clearly $\sum_tM_{t\pi(t)}\le\sum_tu_t+\sum_bv_b$ for every $\pi$ and every cover. Egerváry's theorem (1931) states that for every square matrix of non-negative integers, $\operatorname{val}(M)$ equals the minimum of $\sum u_t+\sum v_b$ over all covers by non-negative integer vectors. We use only the non-trivial direction (some cover has value $\operatorname{val}(M)$), and we prove it for completeness.

Proof of the direction used. Here we allow integer covers with entries of any sign; the inequality above still holds for them. Start with the cover $u_t=\max_bM_{tb}$, $v_b=0$. Given an integer cover, call $(t,b)$ tight if $u_t+v_b=M_{tb}$. If some permutation $\pi$ has all pairs $(t,\pi(t))$ tight, the cover has value $\sum_tM_{t\pi(t)}\le\operatorname{val}(M)$, hence exactly $\operatorname{val}(M)$. Otherwise, by Hall's theorem applied to the bipartite graph of tight pairs, there is a set $X$ of rows whose set $Y$ of tight partners has $|Y|<|X|$. Lower $u_t$ by $1$ for $t\in X$ and raise $v_b$ by $1$ for $b\in Y$. A pair $(t,b)$ with $t\in X$, $b\notin Y$ was not tight, so $u_t+v_b\ge M_{tb}+1$ before and $\ge M_{tb}$ after; for all other pairs $u_t+v_b$ did not decrease. This is again a cover, of value smaller by $|X|-|Y|\ge1$. The values are integers bounded below by $\operatorname{val}(M)$, so after finitely many steps we reach a cover of value $\operatorname{val}(M)$. Finally, since $M\ge0$, every $u_t+v_b\ge0$, so $\min_tu_t+\min_bv_b\ge0$ and at most one of these minima is negative. If, say, $\min_tu_t=-\delta<0$, add $\delta$ to every $u_t$ and subtract it from every $v_b$; this keeps all sums $u_t+v_b$ and the value, and now all entries are $\ge0$ (symmetrically if $\min_bv_b<0$). This proves the direction used. Put \[\Phi(n)=\min_{V\ge0}\Big(V+\sum_t\Big\lceil\frac{(n_t-V)^+}{10}\Big\rceil\Big),\qquad y^+=\max(y,0).\]

Claim: with the order $n=(n_t)$, the grandmother can guarantee exactly $\Phi(n)$ points.

  • The shopkeeper can hold her to $\Phi(n)$. Take $V$ attaining the minimum and $u_t=\lceil(n_t-V)^+/10\rceil$. Put at most $u_t+V$ candies of type $t$ into bag $1$ and at most $u_t$ into each other bag; this room, $10u_t+V\ge n_t$, suffices. Then $u$ together with $v=(V,0,\dots,0)$ is a cover, so every assignment earns at most $\sum u_t+V=\Phi(n)$.

  • She always gets $\Phi(n)$. For any distribution $M$, take a cover with $\sum u_t+\sum v_b=\operatorname{val}(M)$ and let $V=\sum_bv_b$. Then $n_t=\sum_bM_{tb}\le10u_t+V$, so $u_t\ge\lceil(n_t-V)^+/10\rceil$ and $\operatorname{val}(M)=\sum u_t+V\ge\Phi(n)$.

So $g(m)$ is the least $\sum n_t$ with $\Phi(n)\ge m$.

Lower bound. Let $\Phi(n)\ge m$ and $w=\min(m,10)$. For $V=0,1,\dots,w-1$ we have $\sum_t\lceil(n_t-V)^+/10\rceil\ge m-V$. We claim that for every integer $x\ge0$, \[\sum_{V=0}^{w-1}\Big\lceil\frac{(x-V)^+}{10}\Big\rceil\le x .\] If $x\le w$, the terms with $V<x$ equal $1$ (as $1\le x-V\le10$) and the others vanish, so the sum is $x$. If $x>w$, write $x=10k+r$ with $1\le r\le10$: the terms with $V\le r-1$ equal $k+1$ and the remaining ones equal $k$ (for $k\ge1$, $10k\ge x-V\ge x-9>10(k-1)$; for $k=0$ they vanish), so the sum is $kw+\min(r,w)\le10k+r=x$. Summing the claim over $t$: \[g=\sum_tn_t\ \ge\ \sum_{V=0}^{w-1}(m-V)=\begin{cases}\frac{m(m+1)}{2},&m\le10,\\[2pt] 10m-45,&m\ge10.\end{cases}\]

Constructions. For $m\le10$ order $m,m-1,\dots,1$ candies of $m$ types. For $V\ge m$ the expression in $\Phi$ is at least $V\ge m$; for $V\le m-1$ exactly $m-V$ types have more than $V$ candies, each contributing at least $1$, so the expression is at least $V+(m-V)=m$. For $m\ge10$ order $10(m-9)$ candies of one type and $9,8,\dots,1$ of the other nine (total $10m-45$). For $0\le V\le9$ the big type contributes $\lceil(10(m-9)-V)/10\rceil=m-9$ and the types with more than $V$ candies among $1,\dots,9$ contribute $9-V$, total $V+(m-9)+(9-V)=m$. For $10\le V\le m-1$ the big type alone gives at least $V+\frac{10(m-9)-V}{10}=m-9+\frac{9V}{10}\ge m$ (note $10(m-9)>V$ here), and for $V\ge m$ the expression is at least $V\ge m$. Hence $\Phi\ge m$ in both cases, and \[g(m)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{m(m+1)}{2},&m\le10,\\[4pt]10m-45,&m\ge10.\end{cases}\]
