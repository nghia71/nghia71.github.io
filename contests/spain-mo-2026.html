Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

Find the value of a positive integer $N$ such that \[2026<\frac{1}{\sqrt1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}+\dots+\frac{1}{\sqrt N}<2027.\]

Show the answer

for example $N=1014^2=1028196$ (exactly the $N$ with $1027649\le N\le 1028662$ work)

Show the solution

Solution (Telescoping bounds)

Write $S(N)=\frac1{\sqrt1}+\frac1{\sqrt2}+\dots+\frac1{\sqrt N}$. The idea is to squeeze each term between two differences of square roots, which telescope.

For every $k\ge1$, \[2\bigl(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k\bigr)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt k}<\frac{2}{2\sqrt k}=\frac1{\sqrt k},\] and for every $k\ge2$, \[\frac1{\sqrt k}=\frac{2}{2\sqrt k}<\frac{2}{\sqrt k+\sqrt{k-1}}=2\bigl(\sqrt k-\sqrt{k-1}\bigr).\] Adding the first inequality for $k=1,\dots,N$, and the second for $k=2,\dots,N$ (keeping the term $1$ for $k=1$), the right-hand sides telescope: \[2\bigl(\sqrt{N+1}-1\bigr)<S(N)<1+2\bigl(\sqrt N-1\bigr)=2\sqrt N-1 .\] The upper bound $2\sqrt N-1$ equals $2027$ exactly when $\sqrt N=1014$, which suggests the choice below.

Take $N=1014^2$. The upper bound gives $S(N)<2\cdot1014-1=2027$, and the lower bound gives \[S(N)>2\sqrt{1014^2+1}-2>2\cdot1014-2=2026 .\] Hence $N=1014^2=\boxed{1028196}$ works.

Remark. The problem asks for one such $N$. Since consecutive values of $S$ differ by about $\frac1{1014}$, roughly $1014$ consecutive values of $N$ work; a computer summation shows that they are exactly $1027649\le N\le1028662$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB<BC$. The perpendicular bisector of $AC$ intersects $BC$ in $D$. The circle passing through $A,C,D$ contains a point $E\ne D$ such that $DE$ is parallel to $AB$. Prove that $AE^2+BC^2=BE^2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Similar triangles and an isosceles triangle)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles of triangle $ABC$ at $A,B,C$. Since $AB<BC$, we have $\gamma<\alpha$.

Where the points are. Since $\gamma<\alpha$, the ray from $A$ inside the angle $BAC$ that makes the angle $\gamma$ with $AC$ meets segment $BC$ at an interior point $D_0$ with $\angle D_0AC=\gamma=\angle D_0CA$, so $D_0A=D_0C$. The perpendicular bisector of $AC$ meets line $BC$ only once, so $D=D_0$: the point $D$ is inside segment $BC$, $\angle DAC=\gamma$ and $\angle BAD=\alpha-\gamma$. Let $\omega$ be the circle $ACD$.

The line through $D$ parallel to $AB$ meets segment $AC$ at an interior point $P$ (Thales, $CP:CA=CD:CB$). The point $P$ lies on the chord $AC$, so it is inside $\omega$; hence the second intersection $E$ of line $DP$ with $\omega$ lies on ray $DP$ beyond $P$, on the other side of $AC$ from $D$ and $B$. So $A,D,C,E$ lie on $\omega$ in this order. Also, the whole line $DE$ is parallel to $AB$, so $E$ is on the same side of line $AB$ as $D$ and $C$.

Two angles. The angles $\angle EAC$ and $\angle EDC$ stand on the chord $EC$ from the same arc, so $\angle EAC=\angle EDC=\angle ABC=\beta$ (corresponding angles, $DE\parallel BA$). The angles $\angle ECA$ and $\angle EDA$ stand on the chord $EA$ from the same arc, so $\angle ECA=\angle EDA=\angle DAB=\alpha-\gamma$ (alternate angles: $P$ and $B$ lie on opposite sides of $AD$). As $E$ and $B$ are on opposite sides of $AC$, \[\angle BAE=\alpha+\beta,\qquad \angle BCE=\gamma+(\alpha-\gamma)=\alpha .\]

The point $Q$. Since $\beta+\alpha<180^\circ$, the ray $CE$ meets the ray $BA$ at a point $Q$, and triangle $BQC$ has angles $\beta$ at $B$ and $\alpha$ at $C$, hence $\gamma$ at $Q$. So triangles $BQC$ and $BCA$ are similar ($B\to B$, $Q\to C$, $C\to A$), which gives \[\frac{BQ}{BC}=\frac{BC}{BA},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad BA\cdot BQ=BC^2 .\] In particular $BQ=BC^2/BA>BA$, so $A$ lies between $B$ and $Q$. Moreover $E$ lies between $C$ and $Q$, because $C$ and $E$ are on the same side of line $AB$ while $Q$ is on it.

An isosceles triangle. Now $\angle EAQ=180^\circ-\angle BAE=\gamma$ and $\angle AQE=\angle BQC=\gamma$. Hence $EA=EQ$. Let $H$ be the midpoint of $AQ$; then $EH\perp AQ$, and $H$ lies between $A$ and $Q$, so $BH-AH=BA$ and $BH+AH=BH+HQ=BQ$. By Pythagoras in the right triangles $EHB$ and $EHA$, \[BE^2-AE^2=BH^2-AH^2=(BH-AH)(BH+AH)=BA\cdot BQ=BC^2 .\] Therefore $AE^2+BC^2=BE^2$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(a,0)$ with $a=BC$, and $A=(p,q)$ with $q\neq0$; write $c^2=AB^2=p^2+q^2$, so $c<a$.

The point $D$. $D=(d,0)$ with $DA=DC$ means $(d-p)^2+q^2=(a-d)^2$, i.e. $2d(a-p)=a^2-c^2$. (Here $a\ne p$: if $a=p$ then $c^2=a^2+q^2>a^2$.)

The circle. A circle through $C=(a,0)$ and $D=(d,0)$ meets the $x$-axis at $x=a$ and $x=d$ ($d\ne a$, because $DA=DC>0$), so its equation is $x^2+y^2-(a+d)x+vy+ad=0$ for some $v$. It passes through $A$, so \[vq=(a+d)p-c^2-ad. \tag{1}\]

The point $E$. The line through $D$ parallel to $AB$ consists of the points $(d+tp,\ tq)$. Substituting into the circle, \[(d+tp)^2+t^2q^2-(a+d)(d+tp)+vtq+ad=t\bigl(tc^2+2dp-(a+d)p+vq\bigr).\] The root $t=0$ gives $D$; the other root, using (1), is \[t=\frac{(a+d)p-2dp-vq}{c^2}=\frac{c^2+ad-2dp}{c^2},\qquad E=(d+tp,\ tq).\]

The identity. For any point $E$ we have $BE^2-AE^2=|E|^2-|E-A|^2=2\,E\cdot A-|A|^2$. Here $E\cdot A=dp+tc^2$, so \[BE^2-AE^2=2dp+2(c^2+ad-2dp)-c^2=c^2+2d(a-p)=c^2+(a^2-c^2)=a^2=BC^2 .\] Hence $AE^2+BC^2=BE^2$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

We say that a sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ of positive integers is roceña if, for all $n\ge4$, \[a_n=a_{n-1}+\gcd(a_{n-2},a_{n-3})-1.\] Does there exist any roceña sequence such that $2\le a_n\le100\cdot n^{100}$ for all $n\ge1$?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (The gcds force a doubling every four steps)

The answer is no. Suppose such a sequence exists. For $n\ge2$ put $d_n=\gcd(a_n,a_{n-1})$. The rule says \[a_{m+2}=a_{m+1}+d_m-1\qquad(m\ge2). \tag{1}\] In particular $a_{m+2}\ge a_{m+1}$, so the sequence is non-decreasing from $a_3$ on.

Step 1: $d_{m+2}$ divides $d_m-1$. Indeed $d_{m+2}$ divides $a_{m+2}$ and $a_{m+1}$, hence their difference $d_m-1$.

Step 2: some $d_m$ equals $1$. Otherwise $d_m\ge2$ for all $m\ge2$, so $d_m-1$ is a positive multiple of $d_{m+2}$ and $d_{m+2}\le d_m-1<d_m$. Then $d_2>d_4>d_6>\cdots$ would be an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, which is impossible.

Step 3: if $d_m=1$ (with $m\ge2$), then $d_{m+4}=1$. By (1), $a_{m+2}=a_{m+1}$; call this number $x$. Then $d_{m+2}=\gcd(x,x)=x$. Let $y=d_{m+1}=\gcd(a_{m+1},a_m)$; it divides $a_{m+1}=x$. By (1) again, \[a_{m+3}=x+y-1,\qquad a_{m+4}=a_{m+3}+x-1 .\] Now $d_{m+4}$ divides $a_{m+4}-a_{m+3}=x-1$ and $a_{m+3}=x+y-1$, hence it divides their difference $y$. Since $y\mid x$, any common divisor of $y$ and $x-1$ divides $x-(x-1)=1$. So $d_{m+4}=1$.

Step 4: growth. By Steps 2 and 3 there is $m_0\ge2$ with $d_{m_0+4k}=1$ for all $k\ge0$. Put $b_k=a_{m_0+4k+2}$, and apply Step 3 with $m=m_0+4k$, $x=b_k$: since the sequence is non-decreasing, \[b_{k+1}=a_{m+6}\ge a_{m+4}=a_{m+3}+x-1\ge a_{m+2}+x-1=2b_k-1 ,\] that is, $b_{k+1}-1\ge2(b_k-1)$. As $b_0\ge2$, we get $b_k-1\ge2^k$, so \[a_{m_0+4k+2}>2^k\qquad\text{for all }k\ge0 .\] Step 5: contradiction. Take $k=2^t$ with $t\ge12$ and $2^t\ge m_0+2$. Then $m_0+4k+2\le5k=5\cdot2^t$, and \[100\,(m_0+4k+2)^{100}\le100\cdot5^{100}\cdot2^{100t}<2^{7}\cdot2^{233}\cdot2^{100t}=2^{100t+240}<2^{2^t}=2^k,\] because $2^t>100t+240$ for $t\ge12$. So $a_{m_0+4k+2}>100(m_0+4k+2)^{100}$, against the assumption.

Hence no roceña sequence satisfies $2\le a_n\le100\cdot n^{100}$ for all $n$: the answer is no.

Remark. The hypothesis $a_n\ge2$ is needed: the constant sequence $1,1,1,\dots$ is roceña.

Day 2

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

For each positive integer $n$, let $a(n)$ be the largest value of $k$ such that $n$ is a multiple of all of $1,2,\dots,k$. Prove that if $\sqrt[3]{a(n)}$ is an integer, then $\sqrt[3]{a(n+2520)}$ is an integer.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Which values a(n) can take)

Write $L_k=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,k)$. By definition $a(n)=k$ means $L_k\mid n$ but $L_{k+1}\nmid n$.

Lemma. For every $n$, the number $a(n)+1$ is a prime power. Let $k=a(n)$ and suppose $k+1=uv$ with coprime $u,v\ge2$. Then $2\le u,v\le k$, so $u\mid L_k$ and $v\mid L_k$, and since they are coprime, $k+1=uv\mid L_k$. Then $L_{k+1}=\operatorname{lcm}(L_k,k+1)=L_k$ divides $n$, contradicting $a(n)=k$.

Which cubes occur. Suppose $a(n)=m^3$. Then $m^3+1$ is a prime power: $m=1$ gives $2$, $m=2$ gives $9$. For $m\ge3$, $m^3+1=(m+1)(m^2-m+1)$ with both factors larger than $1$, so both are powers of the same prime; since $m^2-m+1>m+1$ (as $m^2>2m$), the factor $m+1$ divides $m^2-m+1=(m+1)(m-2)+3$, hence $m+1\mid3$, impossible for $m\ge3$. So $a(n)\in\{1,8\}$.

Case $a(n)=1$. This means $2\nmid n$. Then $n+2520$ is odd too, so $a(n+2520)=1=1^3$.

Case $a(n)=8$. This means $L_8=840\mid n$ and $L_9=2520\nmid n$. As $840\mid2520$, also $840\mid n+2520$ and $2520\nmid n+2520$. So $a(n+2520)=8=2^3$.

In both cases $\sqrt[3]{a(n+2520)}$ is an integer.

Solution 2 (Periodicity of small values)

Write $L_k=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,k)$, so that $a(n)=k$ means $L_k\mid n$ and $L_{k+1}\nmid n$. Note that $L_9=2520$, so $L_k\mid2520$ for every $k\le9$; therefore, for $k\le9$, \[L_k\mid n\iff L_k\mid n+2520. \tag{1}\] Small values are preserved. If $a(n)=k\le8$, then $L_k\mid n$ and $L_{k+1}\nmid n$ with $k+1\le9$; by (1) the same holds for $n+2520$, so $a(n+2520)=a(n)$. This covers the cubes $1$ and $8$.

No larger cube occurs. It remains to show that $a(n)=m^3$ with $m\ge3$ is impossible, i.e. that $m^3+1$ always divides $L_{m^3}$ (then $L_{m^3+1}=L_{m^3}$, so $L_{m^3}\mid n$ forces $L_{m^3+1}\mid n$). We have $m^3+1=(m+1)(m^2-m+1)$ and $m^2-m+1=(m+1)(m-2)+3$.

  • If $3\nmid m+1$: then $\gcd(m+1,m^2-m+1)=\gcd(m+1,3)=1$, and both factors are at most $m^3$, so their product divides $L_{m^3}$.

  • If $3\mid m+1$: write $m=3j+2$ with $j\ge1$. Then $m+1=3(j+1)$ and $m^2-m+1=3(3j^2+3j+1)$, so \[m^3+1=9(j+1)\cdot(3j^2+3j+1).\] The second factor $3j(j+1)+1$ is $\equiv1$ modulo $3$ and modulo $j+1$, so it is coprime to $9(j+1)$. Both factors are at most $m^3$ (e.g. $9(j+1)\le(3j+2)^3$ for $j\ge1$), so again their product divides $L_{m^3}$.

Hence $a(n)$ is never a cube larger than $8$, and whenever $a(n)$ is a cube, $a(n+2520)=a(n)$ is the same cube.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction

The restaurant of the Hotel Las Rozas has three signature dishes: artichokes, beef and cod. Every evening the restaurant cooks one instance of each dish, so two guests cannot eat the same dish on the same evening. Let $n\ge3$. $n$ guests $H_1,H_2,\dots,H_n$ will stay at the hotel, where for each $1\le k\le n$ the guest $H_k$ stays from the day $k$ at noon until the day $k+3$ at noon. Every guest wishes to try all three dishes, one on each evening of their stay.

Determine, as a function of $n$, the number of ways to arrange the dish that each guest will eat on each dinner, from the evening of the first day until the evening of the $(n+2)$-th day, fulfilling the wishes of every guest.

Show the answer

$3\cdot2^n+6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Choosing the dishes guest by guest)

Call the dishes $\mathrm A,\mathrm B,\mathrm C$. Guest $H_k$ is at dinner on evenings $k,k+1,k+2$; let him eat $x_k,y_k,z_k$ on these evenings, so $\{x_k,y_k,z_k\}=\{\mathrm A,\mathrm B,\mathrm C\}$. On evening $j$ the guests present are those $H_k$ with $k\le j\le k+2$. So the conditions are: each triple $(x_k,y_k,z_k)$ is an ordering of the three dishes, and \[x_2\ne y_1;\qquad x_j,\ y_{j-1},\ z_{j-2}\ \text{distinct}\ (3\le j\le n);\qquad y_n\ne z_{n-1}.\] Choosing step by step. Choose $x_1$ ($3$ ways), $y_1\ne x_1$ ($2$ ways); then $z_1$ is the remaining dish. Choose $x_2\ne y_1$: $2$ ways, namely $x_2=z_1$ or $x_2=x_1$. Now for $k=2,3,\dots,n$ in turn, when $x_k$ and $z_{k-1}$ are known: the dish $y_k$ must differ from $x_k$ (guest $H_k$) and from $z_{k-1}$ (evening $k+1$); then $z_k$ is the remaining dish for $H_k$, and, if $k<n$, $x_{k+1}$ is forced to be the dish different from $y_k$ and $z_{k-1}$ (evening $k+1$; these two differ). Every condition in the list above is used exactly once in this process, so the arrangements correspond exactly to the choices made. The number of choices for $y_k$ is \[2\ \text{ if } x_k=z_{k-1},\qquad 1\ \text{ if } x_k\ne z_{k-1}.\] Key lemma. For $3\le k\le n$: $x_k=z_{k-1}\iff x_{k-1}=z_{k-2}$. By evening $k$, $x_k$ is the dish different from $y_{k-1}$ and $z_{k-2}$; by guest $H_{k-1}$, $z_{k-1}$ is the dish different from $y_{k-1}$ and $x_{k-1}$. Two “third dishes” of the pairs $\{y_{k-1},z_{k-2}\}$ and $\{y_{k-1},x_{k-1}\}$ coincide exactly when $z_{k-2}=x_{k-1}$.

So all the conditions $x_k=z_{k-1}$ ($2\le k\le n$) hold or all fail, according to the choice of $x_2$:

  • if $x_2=z_1$: each of $y_2,\dots,y_n$ has $2$ choices, giving $3\cdot2\cdot2^{n-1}=3\cdot2^n$ arrangements;

  • if $x_2=x_1$: each $y_k$ is forced, giving $3\cdot2=6$ arrangements.

The total is $\boxed{3\cdot2^n+6}$.

Solution 2 (Two families of guests)

Call the dishes $\mathrm A,\mathrm B,\mathrm C$, and let guest $H_k$ eat $x_k,y_k,z_k$ on evenings $k,k+1,k+2$; call the triple $\rho_k=(x_k,y_k,z_k)$ (an ordering of the three dishes) his menu. On evening $j$ the guests present are the $H_k$ with $k\le j\le k+2$, and they must eat different dishes.

Claim 1. For $2\le k\le n$, either $\rho_k=\rho_{k-1}$, or $x_k=z_{k-1}$, and not both. On evening $k$ both $H_{k-1}$ and $H_k$ dine, so $x_k\ne y_{k-1}$. On evening $k+1$ both dine again, so $y_k\ne z_{k-1}$. If $x_k\ne z_{k-1}$, the only dish left for $x_k$ is $x_{k-1}$; then $y_k\notin\{x_k,z_{k-1}\}=\{x_{k-1},z_{k-1}\}$ forces $y_k=y_{k-1}$, and so $z_k=z_{k-1}$: the menus agree. If the menus agree, then $x_k=x_{k-1}\ne z_{k-1}$.

Claim 2. The same alternative holds for all $k$. Let $2\le k\le n-1$; on evening $k+1$ the guests $H_{k-1},H_k,H_{k+1}$ eat $z_{k-1},y_k,x_{k+1}$. If $\rho_k=\rho_{k-1}$, then $z_{k-1}=z_k$, so $x_{k+1}\ne z_k$ and by Claim 1, $\rho_{k+1}=\rho_k$. If $x_k=z_{k-1}$, then $x_{k+1}\ne z_{k-1}=x_k$, so $\rho_{k+1}\ne\rho_k$ and by Claim 1, $x_{k+1}=z_k$.

So every valid arrangement is of one of two types:

  • (I) all guests have the same menu;

  • (II) every guest starts with the dish his predecessor ends with: $x_k=z_{k-1}$ for $2\le k\le n$.

Both types are valid. In type (I) evening $j$ serves the first, second and third dish of the common menu to $H_j,H_{j-1},H_{j-2}$ (those present), all different. In type (II), on evening $j$ ($3\le j\le n$) the dishes are $x_j=z_{j-1}$, $y_{j-1}$ and $z_{j-2}=x_{j-1}$, which are the three dishes of $\rho_{j-1}$; on evening $2$: $x_2=z_1\ne y_1$; on evening $n+1$: $y_n\ne x_n=z_{n-1}$.

Counting. Type (I): $6$ arrangements (the common menu). Type (II): $\rho_1$ in $6$ ways; then each next menu starts with a forced dish and the other two dishes can be ordered in $2$ ways, giving $6\cdot2^{n-1}=3\cdot2^n$. The types are disjoint (in type (I), $x_2=x_1\ne z_1$). The answer is $\boxed{3\cdot2^n+6}$.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

In the scalene triangle $ABC$, the incircle $\omega$ touches $BC,CA,AB$ at $D,E,F$ respectively. Let $G$ be the point on the line $EF$ such that $AG$ is parallel to $BC$. Prove that $\omega$ contains the orthocenter of the triangle $ADG$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The antipode of $D$ and a dot product)

Let $I$ be the incenter, $r$ the inradius, and $D'$ the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite to $D$. We show that $D'$ is the orthocenter of triangle $ADG$; since $D'\in\omega$, this proves the claim.

Existence and non-degeneracy. $EF\perp AI$ and $BC$ is perpendicular to $AI$ only if $AB=AC$; the triangle is scalene, so $EF$ is not parallel to $BC$ and $G$ exists. $A\notin EF$, so $G\ne A$, and $D$ is not on the line $AG$ (which is parallel to $BC$ and different from it), so $ADG$ is a genuine triangle.

One altitude. $ID\perp BC$ and $AG\parallel BC$, so the line $DD'$ (through $I$) is perpendicular to $AG$: it is the altitude from $D$.

Second altitude. Put the origin at $I$ and use vectors; so $D'=-D$ and $|D|=|E|=|F|=r$. The tangent $AE$ is perpendicular to the radius $IE$, so $(A-E)\cdot E=0$, i.e. $A\cdot E=r^2$; likewise $A\cdot F=r^2$. The set $\{X: X\cdot A=r^2\}$ is a line (as $A\neq0$) containing $E$ and $F$, hence it is the line $EF$. Therefore \[G\cdot A=r^2 .\] Also $AG\parallel BC\perp ID$ gives $(A-G)\cdot D=0$. Now \[(G-D')\cdot(A-D)=(G+D)\cdot(A-D)=G\cdot A-G\cdot D+D\cdot A-D\cdot D=r^2-r^2+(A-G)\cdot D=0 .\] So $GD'\perp AD$: the line $GD'$ is the altitude from $G$.

The altitudes from $D$ and from $G$ meet at $D'$, so $D'$ is the orthocenter of $ADG$, and it lies on $\omega$.

Solution 2 (A harmonic pencil)

Let $I$ be the incenter, $D'$ the point of $\omega$ opposite to $D$, $M$ the midpoint of $BC$, $U=DD'\cap EF$, and let $AD$ meet $\omega$ again at $X$. ($G$ exists because the triangle is scalene, so $EF\nparallel BC$.)

Step 1: $U$ lies on the median $AM$. Draw through $U$ the parallel to $BC$; let it meet $AB,AC$ at $P,R$. Since $ID\perp BC$, $IU\perp PR$. The points $P,U,F,I$ lie on the circle with diameter $IP$ (right angles at $U$ and $F$), and $R,U,E,I$ on the circle with diameter $IR$. Hence $\angle IPU=\angle IFU=\angle IFE=\angle IEF=\angle IEU=\angle IRU$, using $IE=IF$ (in other positions of $U$ the same chain holds with directed angles). So triangle $IPR$ is isosceles and its altitude $IU$ bisects $PR$. The homothety at $A$ taking $PR$ to $BC$ takes the midpoint $U$ of $PR$ to $M$; so $A,U,M$ are collinear.

Step 2: a harmonic range on $EF$. The lines $AB,AC,AM,AG$ cut the line $BC$ at $B,C,M$ and the point at infinity (as $AG\parallel BC$), and $(B,C;M,\infty)=-1$ because $M$ is the midpoint. So the four lines form a harmonic pencil, and cutting it by the line $EF$ gives \[(F,E;U,G)=-1 .\] Step 3: a harmonic quadrilateral. The tangents to $\omega$ at $E$ and $F$ meet at $A$, and $A,D,X$ are collinear; hence $(D,X;E,F)=-1$ on $\omega$. Project $\omega$ from its point $D'$ onto the line $EF$ ($D'\ne E,F$, since otherwise the tangents at $D$ and at $E$ or $F$ would be parallel): $D\mapsto D'D\cap EF=U$, $E\mapsto E$, $F\mapsto F$, and $X\mapsto V:=D'X\cap EF$. Projection preserves cross-ratios, so $(U,V;E,F)=-1$. By Step 2 also $(U,G;E,F)=-1$, and the harmonic conjugate of $U$ with respect to $E,F$ is unique, so $V=G$: the point $G$ lies on the line $D'X$.

(Here $X\ne D'$: otherwise $A$ would lie on the line $DD'$; then $A,I,D$ are collinear, so the bisector $AI$ is perpendicular to $BC$, which forces $AB=AC$.)

Step 4. Since $DD'$ is a diameter, $\angle DXD'=90^\circ$, so $GD'=XD'\perp AD$. Also $DD'\perp BC\parallel AG$. Thus $D'$ lies on the altitudes of triangle $ADG$ from $G$ and from $D$, so $D'$ is its orthocenter, and $D'\in\omega$.
