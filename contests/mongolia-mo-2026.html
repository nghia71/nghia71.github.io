Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Let $a_1, a_2$ be fixed positive integers. For $n \ge 2$, define the sequence $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ by $a_{n+1} = \frac{a_2}{a_1}a_n - a_{n-1}$. Suppose that $a_n$ is a positive integer for all positive integers $n$. Prove that the ratio $\frac{a_2}{a_1}$ is an integer.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A closed form with integer polynomials)

Write $r=\frac{a_2}{a_1}=\frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms, with $p,q$ positive integers and $\gcd(p,q)=1$. We must show $q=1$.

Define polynomials $c_1(x)=1$, $c_2(x)=x$ and $c_{n+1}(x)=x\,c_n(x)-c_{n-1}(x)$ for $n\ge 2$. By induction on $n$, each $c_n$ has integer coefficients, degree $n-1$ and leading coefficient $1$ (the step multiplies a monic polynomial of degree $n-1$ by $x$ and subtracts one of smaller degree). Also $a_n=a_1c_n(r)$ for all $n\ge1$: this holds for $n=1,2$, and both sides satisfy the same recurrence $u_{n+1}=ru_n-u_{n-1}$.

Write $c_n(x)=x^{n-1}+e_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\dots+e_0$ with integers $e_j$. Then \[ N_n:=q^{n-1}c_n\!\left(\tfrac pq\right)=p^{n-1}+e_{n-2}p^{n-2}q+\dots+e_0q^{n-1} \] is an integer with $N_n\equiv p^{n-1}\pmod q$, and $q^{n-1}a_n=a_1N_n$.

Suppose $q>1$ and let $\ell$ be a prime factor of $q$. Since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $\ell\nmid p^{n-1}$, so $\ell\nmid N_n$. As $a_n$ is an integer, $\ell^{n-1}$ divides $q^{n-1}a_n=a_1N_n$, and therefore $\ell^{n-1}\mid a_1$. This holds for every $n$, which is impossible for the positive integer $a_1$. Hence $q=1$, that is, $\frac{a_2}{a_1}$ is an integer.

Solution 2 (Powers of a prime drop at every step)

Write $r=\frac{a_2}{a_1}$ and suppose $r$ is not an integer. Then there is a prime $\ell$ that divides the denominator of $r$ in lowest terms. For a nonzero rational number $x$, let $v(x)$ be the exponent of $\ell$ in $x$ (so $x=\ell^{v(x)}\frac{u}{w}$ with $u,w$ integers not divisible by $\ell$). Then $v(xy)=v(x)+v(y)$, and if $v(x)\ne v(y)$ then $v(x-y)=\min(v(x),v(y))$. Also $v(x)\ge 0$ for every nonzero integer $x$. Put $e=-v(r)\ge 1$.

Claim. $v(a_{n+1})=v(a_n)-e$ for every $n\ge1$.

For $n=1$, $a_2=ra_1$ gives $v(a_2)=v(a_1)-e$. Suppose $v(a_n)=v(a_{n-1})-e$ for some $n\ge2$. Then \[ v(ra_n)=v(a_n)-e<v(a_n)+e=v(a_{n-1}), \] so in $a_{n+1}=ra_n-a_{n-1}$ the two terms have different exponents, and $v(a_{n+1})=v(ra_n)=v(a_n)-e$. (All $a_n$ are positive, so the exponents are defined.) This proves the claim.

Consequently $v(a_n)=v(a_1)-(n-1)e$, which is negative once $n>1+v(a_1)$. But $a_n$ is a positive integer, so $v(a_n)\ge0$, a contradiction. Therefore $\frac{a_2}{a_1}$ is an integer.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\omega$. Let $I$ be its incenter, and the points $E$ and $F$ lie on sides $AC$ and $AB$, respectively, such that $E, I, F$ are collinear and $EF \perp AI$. The lines $BI$ and $CI$ intersect $\omega$ again at $M_B$ and $M_C$, respectively, and the circumcircles of triangles $BFM_B$ and $CEM_C$ intersect the line $BC$ again at points $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

Prove that $BX = CY$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Chords from a vertex along the bisector)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$. Since $AI$ bisects $\angle EAF$ and $AI\perp EF$, the right triangles $AIE$ and $AIF$ are congruent (common side $AI$, equal angles at $A$), so $AE=AF$.

Lemma. Let a circle $\Gamma$ pass through $B$ and meet the lines $BA$, $BC$ and the bisector of $\angle ABC$ again at $U$, $V$, $W$. Measure $BU$, $BV$, $BW$ as signed lengths, positive along the rays $BA$, $BC$ and the internal bisector. Then \[ BU+BV=2\cos\tfrac{B}{2}\cdot BW . \] Proof. Put $B$ at the origin, let $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ be the unit vectors along rays $BA,BC$, and let $\mathbf o$ be the centre of $\Gamma$. Since $\Gamma$ passes through the origin, a point $\mathbf z$ lies on $\Gamma$ iff $|\mathbf z|^2=2\,\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf o$. For $\mathbf z=t\mathbf u$ this reads $t^2=2t\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf o$, whose nonzero root is $t=2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf o$; hence $BU=2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf o$ (this is $0$ if $\Gamma$ is tangent to $BA$). Likewise $BV=2\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf o$ and $BW=2\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf o$, where $\mathbf w=(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)/|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|$ and $|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|=2\cos\frac B2$. So $BU+BV=2(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf o=2\cos\frac B2\cdot BW$. $\square$

The line $BI$ is the bisector of $\angle ABC$; as $I$ lies inside $\omega$, the point $M_B$ lies on the ray $BI$, so $BM_B>0$. Apply the lemma to $\omega$ (here $U=A$, $V=C$, $W=M_B$): $c+a=2\cos\frac B2\cdot BM_B$. Apply it to the circle $BFM_B$ (here $U=F$, $V=X$, $W=M_B$): $BF+BX=2\cos\frac B2\cdot BM_B$ with $BX$ signed. Subtracting, \[ BX=a+c-BF=a+AF>a , \] so $X$ lies on ray $BC$ beyond $C$ and $BX=a+AF$. In the same way, at vertex $C$ with the circles $\omega$ and $CEM_C$, the point $Y$ lies on ray $CB$ beyond $B$ and $CY=a+b-CE=a+AE$. Since $AE=AF$, we conclude $BX=CY$.

Solution 2 (Congruent triangles at the arc midpoint)

Let $a=BC$. Since $AI$ bisects $\angle EAF$ and is perpendicular to $EF$, the right triangles $AIE$ and $AIF$ are congruent, so $AE=AF$. Note $F\ne A$ (otherwise $A$ would lie on $EF$, but $AI\perp EF$ at $I\ne A$).

Because $BI$ bisects $\angle ABC$, the point $M_B$ is the midpoint of the arc $AC$ of $\omega$ not containing $B$; hence $M_BA=M_BC$, and $A,B,C,M_B$ lie on $\omega$ in this cyclic order, so $\angle BAM_B+\angle BCM_B=180^\circ$.

Let $X'$ be the point on ray $BC$ beyond $C$ with $CX'=AF$. Since $F$ lies on segment $AB$, \[ \angle FAM_B=\angle BAM_B=180^\circ-\angle BCM_B=\angle X'CM_B . \] Together with $M_BA=M_BC$ and $AF=CX'$, this shows that triangles $M_BAF$ and $M_BCX'$ are congruent (SAS). Hence $\angle M_BFA=\angle M_BX'C=\angle M_BX'B$, and so \[ \angle BFM_B+\angle BX'M_B=(180^\circ-\angle AFM_B)+\angle AFM_B=180^\circ . \] The points $F$ (on ray $BA$) and $X'$ (on ray $BC$) lie on opposite sides of the internal bisector $BM_B$; therefore $B,F,M_B,X'$ are concyclic. So $X'$ is a point of line $BC$, different from $B$, on the circle $BFM_B$; that circle meets the line in only two points, hence $X'=X$ and $BX=BC+CX'=a+AF$.

The same argument at vertex $C$ (with $M_C$, the midpoint of arc $AB$, and the point $Y'$ on ray $CB$ beyond $B$ with $BY'=AE$) gives $CY=a+AE$. As $AE=AF$, we get $BX=CY$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

A country consists of $62$ islands. Some pairs of islands are connected by bridges; we say two islands are adjacent if they are joined by a bridge. Suppose that:

  • Each island is adjacent to at least $3$ other islands.

  • For any two adjacent islands, there exists an island adjacent to both of them.

  • There do not exist $4$ islands such that every pair among them is adjacent.

Determine the minimum possible number of bridges.

Show the answer

$100$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A neighbour of large degree, then double counting)

Think of islands as vertices and bridges as edges of a graph $G$ with $62$ vertices and $E$ edges. The conditions say: every degree is at least $3$; every edge lies in a triangle; there is no $K_4$ (four pairwise adjacent vertices).

Lemma. Every vertex of degree $3$ has a neighbour of degree at least $4$.

Proof. Let $v$ have neighbours $x,y,z$. For each edge $vx$ there is a vertex adjacent to both $v$ and $x$, that is, $x$ is adjacent to $y$ or $z$; the same holds for $y$ and $z$. So among $x,y,z$ there are at least two edges, and not three (that would give the $K_4$ $vxyz$). Say $xy$ and $yz$ are edges and $xz$ is not. If $\deg y\ge4$ we are done; otherwise $N(y)=\{v,x,z\}$. Suppose also $\deg x=3$, so $N(x)=\{v,y,w\}$ with $w\notin\{v,y,z\}$. The edge $xw$ lies in a triangle, so $w$ is adjacent to $v$ or to $y$. But $N(v)=\{x,y,z\}$ and $N(y)=\{v,x,z\}$ do not contain $w$. This contradiction shows $\deg x\ge4$. $\square$

Lower bound. Let $T$ be the set of vertices of degree $3$, $|T|=t$, let $U$ be the other $u=62-t$ vertices (each of degree at least $4$), and let $s$ be the sum of the degrees of the vertices in $U$. Then $2E=3t+s$ and $s\ge 4u$. By the lemma, at least $t$ edges join $T$ to $U$, and each of them is counted in $s$; so $s\ge t$. Hence \[ 2E=3t+\tfrac15 s+\tfrac45 s\ \ge\ 3t+\tfrac15 t+\tfrac45\cdot 4u=\tfrac{16}{5}(t+u)=\tfrac{16\cdot 62}{5}=198.4, \] so $E\ge 99.2$, i.e. $E\ge 100$.

Construction. The wheel $W_k$ is a cycle of $k$ vertices together with a hub joined to all of them; it has $k+1$ vertices and $2k$ edges. Take $11$ copies of $W_4$ and one copy of $W_6$: $11\cdot5+7=62$ islands and $11\cdot8+12=100$ bridges. Every rim vertex has degree $3$ and the hubs have degrees $4$ and $6$. Each rim edge forms a triangle with the hub, and each spoke forms a triangle with a rim edge at its end. Four pairwise adjacent islands would lie in one wheel and contain at least three rim vertices, which would form a triangle in the rim cycle of length $4$ or $6$; this is impossible. So all conditions hold.

The minimum number of bridges is $\boxed{100}$. (The statement does not require the bridges to connect all islands, and the example above is not connected; a connected network needs at least $101$ bridges, see the remark after Solution 2.)

Solution 2 (Discharging along triangles)

Use the graph language: $62$ vertices, minimum degree $3$, every edge in a triangle, no $K_4$. Give every vertex $w$ the charge $\deg w-\frac{16}{5}$; the total charge is $2E-\frac{16\cdot 62}{5}$. We move charge without changing the total and show that all final charges are nonnegative.

Local structure. Let $v$ have degree $3$ and neighbours $x,y,z$. Each of $x,y,z$ is adjacent to another of them (the edges at $v$ lie in triangles), and not all three pairs are adjacent (no $K_4$). So, after renaming, $xy,yz$ are edges and $xz$ is not: we call $y$ the middle neighbour of $v$ and $x,z$ its end neighbours. If $\deg y=3$, then $N(y)=\{v,x,z\}$, and $\deg x\ge 4$: otherwise $N(x)=\{v,y,w\}$ with $w\notin\{v,y,z\}$, and the edge $xw$ would need a common neighbour in $\{v,y\}$, while neither $v$ nor $y$ is adjacent to $w$. By symmetry $\deg z\ge4$ as well.

Rule. Each vertex $v$ of degree $3$ receives $\frac15$: from its middle neighbour if that has degree at least $4$, and otherwise $\frac1{10}$ from each of its two end neighbours (which then have degree at least $4$). Vertices of degree $3$ never give.

A vertex of degree $3$ ends with $3-\frac{16}5+\frac15=0$. A vertex $w$ of degree $d\ge4$ gives at most $\frac15$ to each neighbour (for a fixed receiver $v$, $w$ is either its middle or one of its ends), so it ends with at least $d-\frac{16}{5}-\frac d5=\frac{4(d-4)}{5}\ge 0$.

Hence $2E-\frac{16\cdot62}{5}\ge 0$, so $E\ge 99.2$ and $E\ge100$.

Equality-type examples show $100$ is attained: in the wheel $W_4$ (a $4$-cycle plus a hub joined to its four vertices) the hub has degree $4$ and gives $\frac15$ to each rim vertex, and every charge ends at exactly $0$ (here $E=8=\frac{16}{5}\cdot 5/2$). Taking $11$ copies of $W_4$ and one wheel $W_6$ (a $6$-cycle plus a hub) gives $55+7=62$ islands and $88+12=100$ bridges. Every condition holds: rim vertices have degree $3$, every edge is a rim edge or a spoke and lies in a triangle with the hub or with a rim edge, and four pairwise adjacent vertices would have to include three rim vertices of one wheel forming a triangle, which a $4$-cycle or $6$-cycle does not contain.

So the minimum is $\boxed{100}$.

Remark (connected networks). The statement does not require the network to be connected. If it had to be, at least $101$ bridges would be needed. In the rule above, a vertex $w$ of degree $4$ gives at most $\frac35$ unless its component is a $W_4$ with hub $w$. Indeed, the graph induced on $N(w)$ is triangle-free (a triangle together with $w$ would be a $K_4$). If $w$ is the middle neighbour of three neighbours $v_1,v_2,v_3$, each $v_i$ has degree $3$ and two neighbours in $N(w)$; a triangle-free graph on four vertices in which three vertices have degree $2$ is a $4$-cycle, so the fourth neighbour $v_4$ also has two neighbours in $N(w)$; if $\deg v_4=3$ the component is a $W_4$, and otherwise $v_4$ receives nothing from $w$, so $w$ gives $\frac35$. If $w$ is the middle neighbour of at most two of them, it gives at most $\frac25+2\cdot\frac1{10}=\frac35$. Now let a $62$-vertex graph with our properties have no $W_4$ component, let $t$ be the number of vertices of degree $3$, $u_4$ the number of degree $4$, and $\sigma=\sum(\deg w-3)$ over the vertices of degree at least $4$. Each degree-$3$ vertex receives $\frac15$, so $\frac t5\le\frac35u_4+\sum_{\deg w\ge5}\frac{\deg w}5$. With $t=62-u_4-\#\{\deg w\ge5\}$ this gives $62\le4u_4+\sum_{\deg w\ge5}(\deg w+1)\le4\sigma$ (as $d+1\le4(d-3)$ for $d\ge5$). So $\sigma\ge16$ and $2E=3\cdot62+\sigma\ge202$, i.e. $E\ge101$. A connected network on $62$ islands has no $W_4$ component, so it needs at least $101$ bridges.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation

Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, b_1, b_2, b_3$ be real numbers. Define the sequences $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ and $(y_n)_{n\ge 1}$ by \[x_1 = 0,\ y_1 = 1,\] and for all positive integers $n \ge 1$, \[\begin{cases} x_{n+1} = a_1x_n + a_2y_n + a_3\\ y_{n+1} = b_1x_n + b_2y_n + b_3\end{cases}\] Suppose that $y_n = x_n^3 + 1$ for all $n \ge 1$. Prove that the sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ takes only finitely many distinct values.

Show the solution

Solution (All terms are roots of one polynomial)

Since $y_n=x_n^3+1$, the recurrence for $x$ becomes \[ x_{n+1}=a_1x_n+a_2(x_n^3+1)+a_3=g(x_n),\qquad g(t)=a_2t^3+a_1t+(a_2+a_3). \] On the other hand $y_{n+1}=b_1x_n+b_2y_n+b_3=b_2x_n^3+b_1x_n+b_2+b_3$ and also $y_{n+1}=x_{n+1}^3+1=g(x_n)^3+1$. Hence every $x_n$ is a root of \[ h(t)=g(t)^3+1-\bigl(b_2t^3+b_1t+b_2+b_3\bigr). \]

Case 1: $h$ is not the zero polynomial. Then $h$ has degree at most $9$ and at most $9$ real roots, so the sequence $(x_n)$ takes at most $9$ distinct values.

Case 2: $h$ is the zero polynomial. If $a_2\ne0$, then $g$ has degree $3$, $g^3$ has degree $9$ with leading coefficient $a_2^3$, and $h$ has degree $9$, a contradiction. So $a_2=0$ and $g(t)=a_1t+a_3$. The coefficient of $t^2$ in $h(t)=(a_1t+a_3)^3+1-b_2t^3-b_1t-b_2-b_3$ is $3a_1^2a_3$, so $a_1=0$ or $a_3=0$.

  • If $a_1=0$, then $x_{n+1}=g(x_n)=a_3$ for all $n$, so the sequence takes at most the two values $0$ and $a_3$.

  • If $a_3=0$, then $x_{n+1}=a_1x_n$, and from $x_1=0$ we get $x_n=0$ for all $n$.

In every case the sequence $(x_n)$ takes only finitely many distinct values.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\omega$. The points $E$ and $F$ lie on sides $AC$ and $AB$, respectively, such that the quadrilateral $BCEF$ is cyclic. Let $D$ be a point on segment $EF$. The lines $BD$ and $CD$ meet $\omega$ again at $K$ and $L$, respectively.

Suppose that the circumcircles of $\triangle BFK$ and $\triangle CEL$ intersect at two distinct points $X$ and $Y$. Prove that the midpoint of the segment $XY$ lies on $\omega$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Radical axis and signed powers on one line)

Let $\Gamma_1$ be the circle $BFK$ and $\Gamma_2$ the circle $CEL$. They are different circles (they meet in exactly two points), and their radical axis is the line $XY$. For a point $Z$ and a circle $\Gamma$ we write $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(Z)$ for the power of $Z$.

Step 1: $A$ and $D$ lie on line $XY$. Since $BCEF$ is cyclic, the power of $A$ with respect to that circle gives $AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC$. Line $AB$ meets $\Gamma_1$ at $F$ and $B$, and line $AC$ meets $\Gamma_2$ at $E$ and $C$, so $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(A)=AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}(A)$. Line $BK$ passes through $D$ and meets both $\Gamma_1$ and $\omega$ at $B$ and $K$, so $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(D)=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(D)$; similarly, using line $CL$, $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}(D)=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(D)$. Hence $A$ and $D$ have equal powers with respect to $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ and lie on their radical axis. As $A\notin EF$, $A\ne D$, so line $XY$ is the line $AD$; call it $\ell$.

Step 2: a cyclic quadrilateral. Let $P$ be the second intersection of $\ell$ with $\omega$. Using directed angles between lines (modulo $180^\circ$), \[ \measuredangle BPD=\measuredangle BPA=\measuredangle BCA=\measuredangle BCE=\measuredangle BFE=\measuredangle BFD, \] using that $A,B,C,P$ lie on $\omega$ and $B,C,E,F$ are concyclic. Hence $B,F,D,P$ are concyclic, and the power of $A$ with respect to this circle gives $AD\cdot AP=AF\cdot AB$.

Step 3: computing on $\ell$. Put a coordinate on $\ell$ with $A$ at $0$ and $D$ at $d>0$; let $P$, $X$, $Y$ have coordinates $p,x,y$. For a point $Z$ of $\ell$ with coordinate $z$, $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(Z)=(z-x)(z-y)$ and $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)=z(z-p)$, because $\ell$ meets $\Gamma_1$ at $X,Y$ and $\omega$ at $A,P$. Let $k=AF\cdot AB>0$. Then \[ xy=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(A)=k,\qquad dp=k\ \ \text{(Step 2, $D$ and $P$ are on the same side of $A$)}, \] and from $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(D)=\operatorname{pow}_\omega(D)$, \[ (d-x)(d-y)=d(d-p)\ \Longrightarrow\ xy-d(x+y)=-dp\ \Longrightarrow\ k-d(x+y)=-k\ \Longrightarrow\ x+y=\frac{2k}{d}=2p . \] So the midpoint of $XY$ has coordinate $\frac{x+y}{2}=p$: it is the point $P$, which lies on $\omega$.

Solution 2 (Power functions written with vectors)

Place the origin at $A$ and treat points as vectors; $Z\cdot W$ is the dot product and $|Z|^2=Z\cdot Z$. Let $\Gamma_1$ be the circle $BFK$ and $\Gamma_2$ the circle $CEL$. For a circle $\Gamma$ with centre $Q$ and radius $r$, the power of a point $Z$ is $\operatorname{pow}_\Gamma(Z)=|Z-Q|^2-r^2=|Z|^2-2\,Z\cdot Q+|Q|^2-r^2$. Two facts follow at once.

  • For two circles, $\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma}(Z)-\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma'}(Z)$ is an affine function of $Z$, that is, of the form $\mathbf n\cdot Z+\text{const}$ (the terms $|Z|^2$ cancel).

  • An affine function $\varphi$ that vanishes at two distinct points $P_1,P_2$ vanishes on the whole line $P_1P_2$: on the points $P_1+t(P_2-P_1)$ it is a polynomial of degree at most $1$ in $t$ with the two roots $t=0,1$, hence zero. More generally, if $\varphi(P_1)=\alpha$ and $\varphi(P_2)=\beta$, then $\varphi(P_1+t(P_2-P_1))=(1-t)\alpha+t\beta$.

Setup. The points $E,F$ are different from $A$ (if, say, $F=A$, the circle through $B,C,F$ would be $\omega$ and $BCEF$ would degenerate), so the line $EF$ does not pass through $A$ and $D\ne A$. Let $b=AB$, $c=AC$ and $k=AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC>0$ (power of $A$ with respect to the circle $BCEF$; $F$ and $B$ lie on the same ray from $A$, as do $E$ and $C$). Since $AF=k/b$ and $AE=k/c$, \[ F=\frac{k}{b^2}\,B,\qquad E=\frac{k}{c^2}\,C,\qquad D=(1-t)F+tE\ \ \text{for some } t\in[0,1]. \]

Step 1: the power of $\omega$ on line $AD$. Let $O$ be the centre of $\omega$. As $\omega$ passes through the origin $A$, $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)=|Z|^2-2\,Z\cdot O$. As $B,C\in\omega$, $2\,B\cdot O=|B|^2=b^2$ and $2\,C\cdot O=c^2$. Therefore \[ 2\,D\cdot O=(1-t)\frac{k}{b^2}\,b^2+t\,\frac{k}{c^2}\,c^2=k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \operatorname{pow}_\omega(sD)=s^2|D|^2-sk\quad\text{for every real } s . \]

Step 2: the powers of $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ on line $AD$. Let $\varphi_1=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}-\operatorname{pow}_\omega$, an affine function. Both circles pass through $B$ and $K$, so $\varphi_1(B)=\varphi_1(K)=0$. Here $K\ne B$: the point $D$ lies inside $\omega$ (it is a point of the closed triangle $ABC$ other than a vertex), so the line $BD$ meets $\omega$ in two distinct points. Hence $\varphi_1$ vanishes on line $BK$, which contains $D$: $\varphi_1(D)=0$. At $A$, $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(A)=0$, and line $AB$ meets $\Gamma_1$ at $F$ and $B$, both on the same side of $A$, so $\varphi_1(A)=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(A)=AF\cdot AB=k$. By the second fact, $\varphi_1(sD)=(1-s)k$. In the same way $\varphi_2=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}-\operatorname{pow}_\omega$ vanishes at $C$ and $L$, hence at $D$, and $\varphi_2(A)=AE\cdot AC=k$, so $\varphi_2(sD)=(1-s)k$. Adding Step 1, \[ \operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(sD)=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}(sD)=s^2|D|^2-2ks+k\qquad\text{for every real } s. \tag{$*$} \]

Step 3: $X$ and $Y$ lie on line $AD$. The circles $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ meet in two points, so they are distinct and not concentric, and the points with equal powers form a line, their radical axis, which contains $X$ and $Y$. By $(*)$ the points $A$ ($s=0$) and $D$ ($s=1$) have equal powers, and $A\ne D$; so the radical axis is the line $AD$. Thus $X=s_1D$ and $Y=s_2D$ for two distinct reals $s_1,s_2$, and since $X,Y$ lie on $\Gamma_1$, $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the two roots of $s^2|D|^2-2ks+k=0$ by $(*)$.

Step 4: the midpoint. By Vieta's formulas $s_1+s_2=\dfrac{2k}{|D|^2}$, so the midpoint of $XY$ is $M=s_0D$ with $s_0=\dfrac{k}{|D|^2}$. By Step 1, \[ \operatorname{pow}_\omega(M)=s_0^2|D|^2-s_0k=s_0\Bigl(\frac{k}{|D|^2}\,|D|^2-k\Bigr)=0 . \] A point of power $0$ lies on the circle, so the midpoint of $XY$ lies on $\omega$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

A function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ is called khongor if $f(1) = 1$ and for all positive integers $m, n$, \[f(f(n)f(n+m)) = f(f(n)f(m)) + f(f(n))^2.\] Let $f$ be a khongor function. Determine the maximum possible number of primes $p$ among the first $2026$ primes for which $f(p) < p$.

Note: The set $\mathbb{N}$ denotes the set of positive integers.

Show the answer

$1013$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The error term depends only on the gcd)

Step 1: $f$ is an involution. With $n=1$ and $f(1)=1$ the equation gives $f(f(m+1))=f(f(m))+1$. Since $f(f(1))=f(1)=1$, induction gives $f(f(m))=m$ for all $m$. So $f$ is a bijection of $\mathbb N$ and $f(f(n))^2=n^2$.

Step 2. Define $G(n,m)=f(f(n)f(m))-nm$, an integer. The equation reads $G(n,n+m)+n(n+m)=G(n,m)+nm+n^2$, that is, \[ G(n,n+m)=G(n,m)\qquad\text{for all } n,m\ge1 . \] $G$ is symmetric, so $G$ does not change when the larger argument is replaced by the difference of the two. Running the subtractive Euclidean algorithm, $G(n,m)=G(d,d)$ with $d=\gcd(n,m)$. Put $e(d)=G(d,d)$; note $e(1)=f(f(1))-1=0$. Writing $n=f(a)$, $m=f(b)$ (possible since $f$ is onto) and using $f(f(a))=a$, we get for all $a,b$: \[ f(ab)=f(a)f(b)+e\bigl(\gcd(f(a),f(b))\bigr). \tag{$\star$} \] For fixed $a$ the error $E_a(b)=f(ab)-f(a)f(b)$ takes only the values $e(d)$ with $d\mid f(a)$, so $|E_a(b)|\le C_a$ for a constant $C_a$.

Step 3: $e\equiv0$. Compute $f(a^2b)$ twice with $(\star)$. First, with the pair $(a,ab)$ and then $(a,b)$: $f(a^2b)=f(a)f(ab)+E_a(ab)=f(a)^2f(b)+f(a)E_a(b)+E_a(ab)$. Second, with the pair $(a^2,b)$, and $f(a^2)=f(a)^2+e(f(a))$ (from $(\star)$ with $b=a$): $f(a^2b)=f(a)^2f(b)+e(f(a))f(b)+E_{a^2}(b)$. Therefore \[ e(f(a))\,f(b)=f(a)E_a(b)+E_a(ab)-E_{a^2}(b). \] The right side is bounded by $f(a)C_a+C_a+C_{a^2}$ for all $b$, while $f(b)$ takes every positive integer value. Hence $e(f(a))=0$; as $f$ is onto, $e\equiv 0$. Now $(\star)$ says $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ for all $a,b$: $f$ is completely multiplicative.

Step 4: primes go to primes. If $p$ is prime, $f(p)\ne1=f(1)$. If $f(p)=uv$ with $u,v>1$, then $p=f(f(p))=f(u)f(v)$ with $f(u),f(v)>1$, impossible. So $f(p)$ is prime.

Step 5: the bound. Let $\mathcal P$ be the set of the first $2026$ primes and $S=\{p\in\mathcal P: f(p)<p\}$. For $p\in S$, $q=f(p)$ is a prime smaller than $p$, hence $q\in\mathcal P$, and $f(q)=p>q$, so $q\notin S$. Since $f$ is injective, $f$ maps $S$ injectively into $\mathcal P\setminus S$, so $|S|\le 2026-|S|$, i.e. $|S|\le 1013$.

Step 6: construction. Let $p_1<p_2<\dots$ be the primes. Let $\sigma$ swap $p_{2i-1}\leftrightarrow p_{2i}$ for $1\le i\le1013$ and fix all other primes, and set $f(\prod p^{\alpha_p})=\prod\sigma(p)^{\alpha_p}$. Then $f(1)=1$, $f$ is completely multiplicative and $f(f(n))=n$, so \[ f(f(n)f(n+m))=n(n+m)=nm+n^2=f(f(n)f(m))+f(f(n))^2 . \] Among the first $2026$ primes exactly $p_2,p_4,\dots,p_{2026}$ satisfy $f(p)<p$. The maximum is $\boxed{1013}$.

Solution 2 (Bounded periodic errors and a squaring trick)

Involution. Putting $n=1$ and using $f(1)=1$ gives $f(f(m+1))=f(f(m))+1$, and with $f(f(1))=1$ we get $f(f(m))=m$ for all $m$. So $f$ is a bijection and $f(f(n))^2=n^2$.

Periodic errors. Let $c_n(m)=f(f(n)f(m))-nm$. The equation says exactly that $c_n(m+n)=c_n(m)$. So for fixed $n$ the function $c_n$ is periodic with period $n$, takes finitely many values, and $|c_n(m)|\le K_n$ for some constant $K_n$. Substituting $n=f(a)$, $m=f(b)$: \[ \bigl|f(ab)-f(a)f(b)\bigr|=\bigl|c_{f(a)}(f(b))\bigr|\le K_{f(a)}\qquad\text{for all } a,b . \]

Squares. Fix $a$ and write $L=K_{f(a)}$, $L'=K_{f(a^2)}$. On one hand $f(a^2b)=f(a)f(ab)+\varepsilon_1=f(a)^2f(b)+f(a)\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_1$ with $|\varepsilon_1|,|\varepsilon_2|\le L$; on the other hand $f(a^2b)=f(a^2)f(b)+\varepsilon_3$ with $|\varepsilon_3|\le L'$. So $\bigl(f(a^2)-f(a)^2\bigr)f(b)$ is bounded as $b$ varies; since $f(b)$ takes all positive integer values, $f(a^2)=f(a)^2$ for every $a$.

All errors vanish. For any $n,m$, using $f(u^2)=f(u)^2$ twice, \[ \bigl(nm+c_n(m)\bigr)^2=f(f(n)f(m))^2=f\bigl(f(n)^2f(m)^2\bigr)=f\bigl(f(n^2)f(m^2)\bigr)=n^2m^2+c_{n^2}(m^2), \] so $2nm\,c_n(m)+c_n(m)^2=c_{n^2}(m^2)$. Fix $n$ and a residue $r$ modulo $n$, and let $m$ run through $m\equiv r\pmod n$. Then $c_n(m)=c_n(r)=:\gamma$ is constant and the right side stays bounded by $K_{n^2}$, while the left side is $2nm\gamma+\gamma^2$. Letting $m\to\infty$ forces $\gamma=0$. Thus $c_n\equiv0$, i.e. $f(f(n)f(m))=nm$; with $n=f(a)$, $m=f(b)$ this is $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$.

Conclusion. If $p$ is prime and $f(p)=uv$ with $u,v>1$, then $p=f(u)f(v)$ with both factors larger than $1$; so $f$ maps primes to primes. If $p$ is among the first $2026$ primes and $f(p)<p$, then $q=f(p)$ is also among them and $f(q)=p>q$; pairing $p$ with $q$ shows that at most half of the $2026$ primes, i.e. at most $1013$, satisfy $f(p)<p$. Conversely the completely multiplicative $f$ that swaps the $(2i-1)$-th and $(2i)$-th primes for $i\le 1013$ and fixes all other primes satisfies $f(1)=1$, $f(f(n))=n$ and $f(f(n)f(n+m))=n(n+m)=f(f(n)f(m))+f(f(n))^2$, and exactly $1013$ of the first $2026$ primes have $f(p)<p$. The answer is $\boxed{1013}$.
