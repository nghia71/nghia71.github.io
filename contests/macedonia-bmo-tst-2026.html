Problem 1

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Transformation Chains in Geometry

Let $\triangle ABC$ be a scalene triangle with circumcircle $k$. Isosceles triangles $\triangle BAK$, $\triangle CBL$, and $\triangle ACM$ with bases $BA$, $CB$, and $AC$, respectively, are constructed outward from $\triangle ABC$ such that \[\angle ABK+\angle BCL+\angle CAM=90^\circ,\] and none of the points $K,L,M$ lie on $k$. Let $S$ be the second point of intersection of $k$ and the circumcircle of $\triangle AKM$, and let $A',B',$ and $C'$ be the second points of intersection of $k$ with $LS$, $MS$, and $KS$, respectively. Show that the lines $AA'$, $BB'$, and $CC'$ are concurrent.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark which two of $A,B,C$ are equidistant from each of $K,L,M$. Express the three apex angles in terms of the base angles in the hypothesis.
Hint 2: key idea
Rotate about $K,L,M$ to send $B$ to $A$, $C$ to $B$, and $A$ to $C$, respectively. Use their total angle and the point fixed by their composition, then factor two rotations as reflections with the line $KL$ shared between them. (LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Identify the remaining reflection axes from the center of the composition, and track the images of $A,B,C$ under the corresponding reflections to find a common point $X$. Use the circles centered at $K,L,M$ and tangent-chord angles to help show $S$ lies on $(BKL)$ and $(CLM)$. Use these circles and $(AKM)$ to compare the directions of the chords through $A,B,C$ with the lines joining them to $X$.
Common trap
Use the outward orientation when choosing the signs of the three rotations.
Show solution 1 – Three rotations and a common reflection point

Directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines are taken modulo $180^\circ$; $\sigma_\ell$ denotes the reflection in a line $\ell$. Label $ABC$ counterclockwise.

Step 1: the point $X$. Let $\theta_K=180^\circ-2\angle ABK$, $\theta_L=180^\circ-2\angle BCL$, $\theta_M=180^\circ-2\angle CAM$ be the apex angles; then $\theta_K+\theta_L+\theta_M=540^\circ-180^\circ=360^\circ$. As the triangles are erected outwards, the counterclockwise rotations $\rho_K,\rho_L,\rho_M$ about $K,L,M$ through $\theta_K,\theta_L,\theta_M$ map $B\mapsto A$, $C\mapsto B$, $A\mapsto C$. The composition $\rho_M\circ\rho_K\circ\rho_L$ has total angle $360^\circ$, so it is a translation; it fixes $C$, so it is the identity, and $\rho_K\circ\rho_L=\rho_M^{-1}$ is the rotation about $M$ through $-\theta_M$.

Write $\rho_L=\sigma_{LK}\circ\sigma_b$ and $\rho_K=\sigma_c\circ\sigma_{LK}$, where $b$ is the line through $L$ and $c$ the line through $K$ making the angles $\frac{\theta_L}2$ and $\frac{\theta_K}{2}$ with $LK$ (a rotation is the product of two reflections in lines through its centre at half its angle). Then $\rho_K\circ\rho_L=\sigma_c\circ\sigma_b$ is the rotation about $b\cap c$; hence $b\cap c=M$, i.e. $b=LM$ and $c=KM$. Now \[B=\rho_L(C)=\sigma_{LK}\sigma_{LM}(C),\qquad A=\rho_K(B)=\sigma_{KM}\sigma_{KL}(B),\] so there is a point \[X=\sigma_{LM}(C)=\sigma_{KL}(B)=\sigma_{KM}(A).\] In particular $KA=KB=KX$, $LB=LC=LX$, $MC=MA=MX$, and $KL\perp BX$, $LM\perp CX$, $MK\perp AX$.

Step 2: three inscribed-angle identities. In the circle with centre $L$ through $B,C,X$, the tangent-chord angle and $t_B\perp LB$, $BX\perp LK$ give \[\measuredangle(CB,CX)=\measuredangle(t_B,BX)=\measuredangle(LB,LK),\quad\text{and similarly}\quad \measuredangle(BC,BX)=\measuredangle(LC,LM). \tag{1}\] In the same way, in the circles with centres $M$ (through $C,X,A$) and $K$ (through $A,B,X$): \[\measuredangle(CX,CA)=\measuredangle(MX,MK)=\measuredangle(MK,MA),\quad \measuredangle(CA,CX)=\measuredangle(MA,MK),\quad \measuredangle(BA,BX)=\measuredangle(KA,KM). \tag{2}\] (the middle equality in the first identity holds because $MK$ is the mirror line of $MA$ and $MX$).

Step 3: $S$ lies on the circles $BKL$ and $CLM$. Using $S\in k$ and $S\in(AKM)$, then (2) and (1): \[\measuredangle(SB,SK)=\measuredangle(SB,SA)+\measuredangle(SA,SK)=\measuredangle(CB,CA)+\measuredangle(MA,MK)=\measuredangle(CB,CA)+\measuredangle(CA,CX)=\measuredangle(CB,CX)=\measuredangle(LB,LK),\] so $S,B,K,L$ are concyclic. Likewise \[\measuredangle(SC,SM)=\measuredangle(SC,SA)+\measuredangle(SA,SM)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(KA,KM)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(BA,BX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)=\measuredangle(LC,LM),\] so $S,C,L,M$ are concyclic.

Step 4: the lines pass through $X$. Since $A,B,S,A'$ lie on $k$ and $A'\in SL$, and $S,B,K,L$ are concyclic, \[\measuredangle(AB,AA')=\measuredangle(SB,SA')=\measuredangle(SB,SL)=\measuredangle(KB,KL).\] In the circle with centre $K$ through $A,B,X$ (with $KL\perp BX$), $\measuredangle(AB,AX)=\measuredangle(t_B,BX)=\measuredangle(KB,KL)$. Hence $AA'$ and $AX$ make the same angle with $AB$: $A'\in AX$. In the same way, using the circle $SCLM$ and (1), $\measuredangle(BC,BB')=\measuredangle(SC,SM)=\measuredangle(LC,LM)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)$, so $B'\in BX$; and using the circle $SAKM$ and (2), $\measuredangle(CA,CC')=\measuredangle(SA,SK)=\measuredangle(MA,MK)=\measuredangle(CA,CX)$, so $C'\in CX$.

Therefore the lines $AA'$, $BB'$, $CC'$ all pass through $X$, the reflection of $A$ in $KM$ (equally of $B$ in $KL$ and of $C$ in $LM$).

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers and Ceva on the circle

Label $ABC$ counterclockwise, take $k$ as the unit circle, and denote the complex coordinate of a point by the lower-case letter ($a,b,c,s,\dots$); for points of $k$ we have $\bar a=1/a$, and so on. Let $\theta_K=\angle ABK$, $\theta_L=\angle BCL$, $\theta_M=\angle CAM$ be the base angles, so $\theta_K+\theta_L+\theta_M=90^\circ$, and put \[j=e^{-2i\theta_K},\qquad l=e^{-2i\theta_L},\qquad m=e^{-2i\theta_M},\qquad jlm=e^{-i\pi}=-1 .\] None of $1+j$, $1+l$, $1+m$ is $0$, since $0<\theta<90^\circ$.

The apexes. The outer side of the directed side $A\to B$ is on its right. The apex of the isosceles triangle with base $AB$ and base angle $\theta_K$ is the midpoint of $AB$ moved by $\frac12\tan\theta_K\cdot|AB|$ to the right, that is $a+\beta(b-a)$ with $\beta=\frac{1-i\tan\theta_K}{2}$. Since $\beta+\bar\beta=1$ and $\beta/\bar\beta=j$, we get $\beta=\frac{j}{1+j}$, and similarly for the other sides ($B\to C$ and $C\to A$); writing $K,L,M$ also for the coordinates of these points, \[K=\frac{a+jb}{1+j},\qquad L=\frac{b+lc}{1+l},\qquad M=\frac{c+ma}{1+m}.\]

Lines through two points of $k$. For $u,v$ on $k$ the line $uv$ (the tangent if $u=v$) is \[z+uv\,\bar z=u+v .\tag{1}\] Hence the second intersection of $k$ with the line through $s\in k$ and a point $z\notin k$ is $\frac{s-z}{s\bar z-1}$. In particular \[a'=\frac{s-L}{s\bar L-1},\qquad b'=\frac{s-M}{s\bar M-1},\qquad c'=\frac{s-K}{s\bar K-1},\] where $s\bar L\ne1$ etc. because $K,L,M$ are not on $k$.

Ceva on the circle. We claim: the lines $AA'$, $BB'$, $CC'$ have a common point of the projective plane (they are concurrent or parallel) if and only if \[\Delta:=(b-a')(c-b')(a-c')-(c-a')(a-b')(b-c')=0.\] Indeed, write a line as $\alpha z+\bar\alpha\bar z+\gamma=0$ with $\gamma$ real; in Cartesian coordinates it is $2\,\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)X-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)Y+\gamma=0$, and since the rows $(\alpha,\bar\alpha,\gamma)$ and $(\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha,\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha,\gamma)$ are related by a fixed invertible linear map, three lines have a common projective point if and only if $\det[\alpha_r,\bar\alpha_r,\gamma_r]_{r=1}^3=0$. Multiplying (1) by $\mu$ with $\mu^2=\frac{1}{uv}$ brings it to this form ($\bar\mu=\frac1\mu=\mu uv$, and $-\mu(u+v)$ is real). So the condition is \[\det\begin{pmatrix}1&aa'&a+a'\\1&bb'&b+b'\\1&cc'&c+c'\end{pmatrix}=0,\] and expanding, this determinant equals $\Delta$.

Computing $\Delta$. Using $\bar L=\frac{l/b+1/c}{1+l}$, a short expansion gives \[b(s\bar L-1)-s+L=\frac{(b-c)(s/c-l)}{1+l},\qquad c(s\bar L-1)-s+L=\frac{(b-c)(1-ls/b)}{1+l},\] so $b-a'$ and $c-a'$ are these numbers divided by $s\bar L-1$. The cyclic shift $a\to b\to c\to a$, $j\to l\to m\to j$ (which sends $K\to L\to M\to K$, $a'\to b'\to c'\to a'$ and fixes $s$) gives the analogous formulas for $b'$ and $c'$. Therefore \[\Delta=-\frac{(b-c)(c-a)(a-b)}{(1+j)(1+l)(1+m)(s\bar K-1)(s\bar L-1)(s\bar M-1)}\;E,\] where \[E:=\Bigl(l-\frac sc\Bigr)\Bigl(m-\frac sa\Bigr)\Bigl(j-\frac sb\Bigr)+\Bigl(1-\frac{ls}{b}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{ms}{c}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{js}{a}\Bigr)\] (because $(\frac sc-l)(\frac sa-m)(\frac sb-j)=-(l-\frac sc)(m-\frac sa)(j-\frac sb)$). So it suffices to show that $E=0$. (In $E$ the terms free of $s$ give $jlm+1=0$ and the terms in $s^3$ give $-\frac{s^3}{abc}(1+jlm)=0$.)

Using the circle $AKMS$. Subtracting the equation $z\bar z-1=0$ of $k$ from the equation of the circle $(AKMS)$ (normalised with $z\bar z$ coefficient $1$) leaves a linear equation satisfied by $A$ and $S$, i.e. an equation of the line $AS$; two equations of the same line are proportional, so by (1) there is a complex $\lambda$ with \[(AKMS):\quad z\bar z-1=\lambda\,(z+as\bar z-a-s).\] Write $p(z)=z\bar z-1$ and $q(z)=z+as\bar z-a-s$. Since $K$ and $M$ lie on this circle, $p(K)q(M)=p(M)q(K)$. Direct expansion with $K=a+\frac{j}{1+j}(b-a)$, $M=a+\frac{1}{1+m}(c-a)$ gives \begin{align*}p(K)&=\frac{(b-a)(j^2/a-1/b)}{(1+j)^2},& q(K)&=\frac{(b-a)(j-s/b)}{1+j},\\ p(M)&=\frac{(c-a)(1/a-m^2/c)}{(1+m)^2},& q(M)&=\frac{(c-a)(1-ms/c)}{1+m}.\end{align*} Since $a\ne b$ and $a\ne c$, the condition $p(K)q(M)=p(M)q(K)$ becomes \begin{align*}F:=(1+m)\Bigl(\frac{j^2}{a}-\frac1b\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{ms}{c}\Bigr)-(1+j)\Bigl(\frac1a-\frac{m^2}{c}\Bigr)\Bigl(j-\frac sb\Bigr)=0.\end{align*} Finally, substituting $l=-\frac{1}{jm}$ into $E$ and expanding, one finds the identity \[E=-\frac{s}{jm}\,F .\] Hence $E=0$ and $\Delta=0$: the three lines have a common projective point.

They are not parallel. Suppose $BB'\parallel CC'$. The reflection in the diameter of $k$ perpendicular to these chords swaps $B\leftrightarrow B'$, $C\leftrightarrow C'$ and reverses directed angles; with directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and the inscribed angle theorem this gives $\measuredangle(SB',SC')=-\measuredangle(AB,AC)=\measuredangle(AC,AB)$. The lines $SB'$, $SC'$ are $SM$, $SK$, and $A,K,M,S$ are concyclic, so $\measuredangle(SB',SC')=\measuredangle(SM,SK)=\measuredangle(AM,AK)$. Thus \[\measuredangle(AM,AC)+\measuredangle(AC,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,AK)=\measuredangle(AC,AB),\] that is, $\measuredangle(AM,AC)+\measuredangle(AB,AK)=0$. But $K$ lies on the other side of $AB$ from $C$ with $\angle KAB=\theta_K$, and $M$ on the other side of $AC$ from $B$ with $\angle MAC=\theta_M$; as $ABC$ is counterclockwise, $\measuredangle(AB,AK)=-\theta_K$ and $\measuredangle(AM,AC)=-\theta_M$. Their sum $-(\theta_K+\theta_M)$ is not a multiple of $180^\circ$, since $0<\theta_K+\theta_M<90^\circ$. This contradiction shows $BB'\nparallel CC'$, so the common point is a point of the plane: $AA'$, $BB'$, $CC'$ are concurrent.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes · Minimal Solution Strategy

Determine all positive integers $a,b$ such that \[a\mid b^2+1\quad\text{and}\quad b\mid a^2+1.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Check what a common divisor of $a$ and $b$ would have to divide besides $a$ and $b$. Find a single expression that both $a$ and $b$ divide.
Hint 2: key idea
Use coprimality to write $a^2+b^2+1=kab$ for an integer $k$. For fixed $k$ and $b$, treat this as a quadratic in $a$ and compare its two roots. (LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes)
Hint 3: approach
For each feasible $k$, take a positive pair of smallest sum and use the other positive root to constrain that pair. With $k$ determined, swap the variables as needed and repeatedly replace the larger entry by the other root, checking the case where the smaller entry is $1$ separately. Reverse these replacements to generate candidates and check that the quadratic equation is preserved.
Common trap
Check positivity of the other root as well as integrality before using it for descent.
Show the answer

$(a,b)=(x_m,x_{m+1})$ or $(x_{m+1},x_m)$ with $x_0=x_1=1$, $x_{m+1}=3x_m-x_{m-1}$: $(1,1),(1,2),(2,5),(5,13),(13,34),\dots$ and the swapped pairs

Show solution 1 – Vieta jumping

Reduction. A common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $b^2+1$ and $b^2$, hence $1$: $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Since $a$ and $b$ both divide $a^2+b^2+1$, so does $ab$: $a^2+b^2+1=k\,ab$ for a positive integer $k$.

$k=3$. Fix $k$ and, among the positive solutions of $x^2+y^2+1=kxy$, take one $(a,b)$ with $a+b$ smallest; as the equation is symmetric, we may assume $a\ge b$. The quadratic $x^2-kb\,x+(b^2+1)=0$ has the roots $a$ and $a'=kb-a=\frac{b^2+1}{a}$, a positive integer; $(a',b)$ is again a solution, so $a'+b\ge a+b$, i.e. $\frac{b^2+1}a\ge a$, i.e. $a^2\le b^2+1$. With $a\ge b\ge1$ this forces $a=b$. Then $2a^2+1=ka^2$, so $a^2\mid1$, $a=1$ and $k=3$. Hence every solution satisfies \[a^2+b^2+1=3ab. \tag{1}\]

All solutions of (1). Let $(a,b)$ solve (1) with $a>b$. Then $(b,\,3b-a)$ also solves (1) (the other root in $a$), and $3b-a=\frac{b^2+1}a$ is a positive integer. If $b\ge2$ it is less than $b$, because $ab\ge(b+1)b>b^2+1$; if $b=1$, then (1) gives $a=2$ and the new pair is $(1,1)$. The only solution with $a=b$ is $(1,1)$ (as $a^2\mid1$). So repeated steps $(a,b)\mapsto(b,3b-a)$ lead from every solution with $a\ge b$ down to $(1,1)$. Reversing the steps, the solutions with $a\ge b$ are exactly $(x_{m+1},x_m)$, $m\ge0$, where \[x_0=x_1=1,\qquad x_{m+1}=3x_m-x_{m-1}\qquad(1,1,2,5,13,34,89,\dots),\] and conversely each such pair solves (1) (by induction, using that $(b,3b-a)$ solves (1) when $(a,b)$ does).

Back to the problem. Every solution of (1) satisfies the conditions: $b^2+1=a(3b-a)$ and $a^2+1=b(3a-b)$. Therefore the answer is all pairs $(x_{m+1},x_m)$ and $(x_m,x_{m+1})$, i.e. \[\boxed{(1,1),\ (1,2),\ (2,1),\ (2,5),\ (5,2),\ (5,13),\ (13,5),\ \dots}\] (the $x_m$ are the Fibonacci numbers of odd index).

Show solution 2 – Descent on the divisibility conditions

Call a pair $(u,v)$ of positive integers good if $u\mid v^2+1$ and $v\mid u^2+1$; the condition is symmetric in $u$ and $v$.

Lemma. If $(u,v)$ is good, then $w=\frac{v^2+1}{u}$ is a positive integer and $(v,w)$ is good.

Proof. $w$ is a positive integer because $u\mid v^2+1$, and $w\mid v^2+1$ because $uw=v^2+1$. Modulo $v$ we have $u^2w^2=(v^2+1)^2\equiv1$ and $u^2\equiv-1$ (as $v\mid u^2+1$), so $-w^2\equiv1$, i.e. $v\mid w^2+1$. $\square$

Small cases. If $a=b$, then $a\mid a^2+1$ gives $a\mid1$, so $(a,b)=(1,1)$. If $a=1<b$, then $b\mid2$, so $(a,b)=(1,2)$.

Descent. Let $(a,b)$ be good with $2\le a<b$. By the lemma applied to $(b,a)$, the pair $(a,c)$ with $c=\frac{a^2+1}{b}$ is good, and \[c\le\frac{a^2+1}{a+1}<a\qquad(\text{as }a^2+1<a^2+a\text{ for }a\ge2).\] So $(c,a)$ is a good pair with smaller entries and $c<a$. Repeating, the smaller entry decreases until it becomes $1$; at that moment the pair is $(1,2)$ by the small cases. Conversely, by the lemma, from $(c,a)$ we return to $(a,b)$ with $b=\frac{a^2+1}{c}$. Hence the good pairs $(a,b)$ with $a<b$ are exactly $(y_m,y_{m+1})$, $m\ge0$, where \[y_0=1,\quad y_1=2,\quad y_{m+1}=\frac{y_m^2+1}{y_{m-1}}\qquad(1,2,5,13,34,\dots),\] all of them good by the lemma (starting from the good pair $(1,2)$).

The recurrence. For $m\ge2$, subtracting $y_{m-1}^2+1=y_{m-2}y_m$ from $y_m^2+1=y_{m-1}y_{m+1}$ gives $y_m^2-y_{m-1}^2=y_{m-1}y_{m+1}-y_{m-2}y_m$, i.e. \[y_m\,(y_m+y_{m-2})=y_{m-1}\,(y_{m+1}+y_{m-1}),\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{y_{m+1}+y_{m-1}}{y_m}=\frac{y_m+y_{m-2}}{y_{m-1}} .\] This ratio is therefore the same for all $m\ge1$, and for $m=1$ it is $\frac{5+1}{2}=3$. So $y_{m+1}=3y_m-y_{m-1}$.

With $x_0=1$ and $x_{m+1}=y_m$ we get the sequence $x_0=x_1=1$, $x_{m+1}=3x_m-x_{m-1}$, and the answer is $(1,1)$ together with all pairs of consecutive terms of $1,2,5,13,34,\dots$ in either order: \[\boxed{(1,1),\ (1,2),\ (2,1),\ (2,5),\ (5,2),\ (5,13),\ (13,5),\ \dots}\]

Problem 3

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

We call a sequence $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ average if for every nonnegative integer $i$, $a_i\le a_{i+1}$ and $a_{i+3}\le a_i+a_{i+2}$ hold. Determine the smallest positive integer $p$ for which there exists an average sequence $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ such that \[\frac{a_{i+p}}{a_i}>\frac{a_{i+1}}{a_i}+\frac{a_{i+2}}{a_{i+1}}+\dots+\frac{a_{i+p}}{a_{i+p-1}}\] holds for every nonnegative integer $i$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the ratios on the right for a sequence whose successive terms have a constant ratio. How are those ratios related to the one on the left?
Hint 2: key idea
On a positive tail, use AM-GM on the successive ratios, whose product is the left-hand side. Find a competing upper bound on growth from the recurrence inequality by comparing the terms to powers of the positive root of $\rho^3=\rho^2+1$. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Check $p=1$ directly, and for larger $p$ iterate the AM-GM lower bound along indices spaced $p$ places apart. Compare it with the upper bound obtained by induction from the recurrence, and estimate powers of $\rho$ to rule out small $p$. For a construction, look for a sequence that makes your growth estimates as sharp as possible, then translate the strict inequality into a condition on $p$.
Common trap
Justify passing to a positive tail before using AM-GM, and handle the case where every term is negative separately.
Show the answer

$p=6$

Show the solution – Growth rate versus AM-GM

Positive terms. The terms are non-zero (they are divided by). If all terms are negative, then, since the sequence is non-decreasing, each ratio $\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}$ lies in $(0,1]$; the left side $\frac{a_{i+p}}{a_i}$ is the product of the $p$ ratios on the right, hence at most the first of them, and for $p\ge2$ smaller than their sum. So the inequality fails (for $p=1$ it fails anyway, see below). Otherwise some $a_N>0$, and then $a_k>0$ for all $k\ge N$; the sequence $a_N,a_{N+1},\dots$ is again average and satisfies the inequality for every $i$. Re-indexing, we may therefore assume that all terms are positive. Let $\rho$ be the real root of $x^3=x^2+1$. Since $1.46^3<1.46^2+1$ and $1.47^3>1.47^2+1$, we have $1.46<\rho<1.47$.

Average sequences grow at most like $\rho^n$. Let $C=\max\bigl(a_0,\frac{a_1}{\rho},\frac{a_2}{\rho^2}\bigr)$. If $a_j\le C\rho^j$ for $j\le n+2$, then $a_{n+3}\le a_{n+2}+a_n\le C\rho^n(\rho^2+1)=C\rho^{n+3}$. So $a_n\le C\rho^n$ for all $n$.

$p\le5$ is impossible. For $p=1$ the inequality reads $\frac{a_{i+1}}{a_i}>\frac{a_{i+1}}{a_i}$. Let $2\le p\le5$ and suppose an average sequence satisfies the inequality for all $i$. The $p$ ratios on the right have product $\frac{a_{i+p}}{a_i}=:Q_i$, so by AM-GM the right side is at least $pQ_i^{1/p}$, and $Q_i>pQ_i^{1/p}$ gives $Q_i>\lambda:=p^{p/(p-1)}$. Hence $a_{mp}>\lambda^m a_0$ for all $m$, while $a_{mp}\le C\rho^{mp}$; letting $m\to\infty$ we get $\lambda\le\rho^p$, i.e. $p\le\rho^{p-1}$. But \[\rho<2,\qquad\rho^2<1.47^2<3,\qquad\rho^3=\rho^2+1<4,\qquad\rho^4=\rho^3+\rho=\rho^2+\rho+1<1.47^2+1.47+1<5,\] a contradiction for each $p=2,3,4,5$.

$p=6$ works. Take $a_i=\rho^i$. It is increasing, and $a_{i+3}=a_i+a_{i+2}$, so it is average. The inequality becomes $\rho^6>6\rho$, i.e. $\rho^5>6$, and indeed \[\rho^5=\rho^4\cdot\rho=\rho^3+\rho^2+\rho=2\rho^2+\rho+1>2\cdot1.46^2+1.46+1>6.7 .\] So the smallest $p$ is $\boxed{6}$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Process Termination and Reachability

Let $G$ be a connected graph with at least two vertices. The vertices of $G$ represent cities, whereas the edges represent two-way roads. A total of $k$ diplomats are distributed among the vertices of $G$. Each move consists of picking a vertex $v$ having at least as many diplomats as its degree $\deg_G(v)$, and sending one diplomat along every edge incident to $v$ (to the corresponding neighbors of $v$). Determine the largest possible value of $k$ such that, for every initial distribution of the $k$ diplomats among the vertices of $G$, every possible sequence of moves is finite.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a graph that is a short path. Record how many diplomats a city receives if each of its neighbours makes one move.
Hint 2: key idea
If a city stops making moves but its neighbour keeps moving, the city keeps gaining diplomats. In a hypothetical infinite sequence, use connectedness to see which cities must keep moving. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
At a time by which all cities have moved, order the cities by their last move and count the diplomats received from neighbours with later last moves. For a construction, look for a distribution in which every city can move exactly once in some order and the configuration then returns to its start. Count how many diplomats such a distribution needs.
Common trap
The last move times must all be measured before the same snapshot of the process.
Show the answer

$k=|E(G)|-1$

Show the solution – Last firings and an acyclic order

Let $G$ have $n$ vertices and $m\ge1$ edges. We show that the answer is $\boxed{m-1}$. A move at $v$ is called firing $v$.

With $k\ge m$ an infinite sequence can occur. List the vertices as $v_1,\dots,v_n$ and put on $v_i$ as many diplomats as $v_i$ has neighbours $v_j$ with $j>i$ (in total $m$), and the remaining $k-m$ diplomats anywhere. Fire $v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n$ in this order. When $v_i$ is fired it holds at least its initial number plus one diplomat from each earlier neighbour, i.e. at least $\deg v_i$, so the move is allowed. After the $n$ moves every vertex has sent $\deg v$ diplomats and received one from each neighbour, so the distribution is the initial one again; repeating gives an infinite sequence.

With $k\le m-1$ every sequence is finite. Suppose an infinite sequence of moves exists.

First, every vertex fires infinitely often. Otherwise some vertex $v$ fires only finitely often; after its last firing $v$ never loses diplomats, and since the total number is finite, it can receive only finitely many more; so each neighbour of $v$ fires only finitely often. By connectedness, every vertex fires finitely often, and the sequence is finite, a contradiction.

Now take a moment $T$ after which every vertex has fired at least once. For each vertex $v$, consider its last firing before $T$; every neighbour that fired after that moment sent a diplomat to $v$, and $v$ has not fired since, so at time $T$ vertex $v$ holds at least as many diplomats as it has neighbours that fired later than $v$ did. For an edge $uv$, the last firings of $u$ and $v$ happened at different moments; the vertex whose last firing was earlier counts this edge. So every edge is counted at least once, and the total number of diplomats is at least $m$, contradicting $k\le m-1$.

Hence the largest $k$ is $m-1=|E(G)|-1$.
