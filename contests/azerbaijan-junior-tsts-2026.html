Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Worst-Case Thresholds

Find the largest $k$ (in terms of $n$) satisfying the following condition: for any placement of a permutation of $\{1,2,\ldots,n^2\}$ in an $n\times n$ square, there exist $2$ neighbours with difference at least $k$.

Note: neighbours are two cells which share at least one vertex.

Show the answer

$k=n+1$

Show the solution

Solution (A short king path from $1$ to $n^2$)

Let $n\ge2$.

Every arrangement has two neighbours differing by at least $n+1$. Neighbours are king moves. A king can go from any cell to any other cell of the $n\times n$ board in at most $n-1$ moves (each move changes the row and the column by at most $1$). Take such a path $c_0,c_1,\dots,c_j$ ($j\le n-1$) from the cell of $1$ to the cell of $n^2$. The differences of consecutive numbers along it add up to $n^2-1$, so one of them is at least \[\frac{n^2-1}{j}\ge\frac{n^2-1}{n-1}=n+1 .\]

An arrangement where all neighbour differences are at most $n+1$. Write the numbers row by row: the cell in row $i$ and column $j$ ($1\le i,j\le n$) gets $(i-1)n+j$. Horizontal neighbours differ by $1$, vertical ones by $n$, diagonal ones by $n-1$ or $n+1$.

Hence the largest $k$ is $\boxed{n+1}$.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $abc\geq 1$. Show that \[\frac{1}{a^4+b^3+c^2}+\frac{1}{b^4+c^3+a^2}+\frac{1}{c^4+a^3+b^2}\leq 1.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Cauchy-Schwarz with weights $1,b,c^2$)

Let $S=a^2+b^2+c^2$. By Cauchy-Schwarz, \[(a^4+b^3+c^2)(1+b+c^2)\ge\bigl(a^2\cdot1+b^{3/2}b^{1/2}+c\cdot c\bigr)^2=S^2,\] so $\dfrac1{a^4+b^3+c^2}\le\dfrac{1+b+c^2}{S^2}$, and similarly for the other two fractions. Adding, \[\sum\frac1{a^4+b^3+c^2}\le\frac{3+(a+b+c)+S}{S^2}.\] By AM-GM and $abc\ge1$, $S\ge3(abc)^{2/3}\ge3$; also $a+b+c\le\sqrt{3S}\le S$ (as $S\ge3$) and $3\le S$. Hence the numerator is at most $3S\le S^2$, and the sum is at most $1$. Equality holds for $a=b=c=1$.

Solution 2 (Weighted AM-GM and the sum $\sum\frac1{1+a+ab}=1$)

Reduction to $abc=1$. Let $k=(abc)^{-1/3}\le1$ and $a'=ka$, $b'=kb$, $c'=kc$, so $a'b'c'=1$. Then $a'^4+b'^3+c'^2\le a^4+b^3+c^2$, and similarly for the other denominators, so the left side for $a,b,c$ is at most the left side for $a',b',c'$. Hence it suffices to prove the inequality when $abc=1$, which we assume from now on.

One denominator. We show $a^4+b^3+c^2\ge1+a+ab$. Put $X=a^4$, $Y=b^3$, $Z=c^2$. By the weighted AM-GM inequality, \begin{align*} \tfrac{6}{26}X+\tfrac{8}{26}Y+\tfrac{12}{26}Z&\ge X^{6/26}Y^{8/26}Z^{12/26}=(abc)^{12/13}=1,\\ \tfrac{11}{26}X+\tfrac{6}{26}Y+\tfrac{9}{26}Z&\ge a^{44/26}b^{18/26}c^{18/26}=a\,(abc)^{9/13}=a,\\ \tfrac{9}{26}X+\tfrac{12}{26}Y+\tfrac{5}{26}Z&\ge a^{36/26}b^{36/26}c^{10/26}=ab\,(abc)^{5/13}=ab . \end{align*} (In each line the weights add up to $1$.) The coefficients of $X$, of $Y$ and of $Z$ in the three lines add up to $\frac{26}{26}=1$, so adding the lines gives $X+Y+Z\ge1+a+ab$. In the same way (cyclically) $b^4+c^3+a^2\ge1+b+bc$ and $c^4+a^3+b^2\ge1+c+ca$.

The sum. Hence the left side is at most \[\frac1{1+a+ab}+\frac1{1+b+bc}+\frac1{1+c+ca}.\] Using $abc=1$, $\frac1{1+b+bc}=\frac{a}{a+ab+abc}=\frac{a}{1+a+ab}$ and $\frac1{1+c+ca}=\frac{ab}{ab+abc+a^2bc}=\frac{ab}{1+a+ab}$, so the three fractions add up to $\frac{1+a+ab}{1+a+ab}=1$. This proves the inequality; equality holds for $a=b=c=1$.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Tangent and External Angle Relations

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral. The line through $A$ parallel to $BD$ intersects the line through $B$ parallel to $AC$ at $E$. Let $M$ and $N$ be the second intersections of the circumcircle of triangle $EAB$ with the lines $EC$ and $ED$, respectively. Prove that the quadrilateral $MNDC$ is cyclic.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The tangent at $E$ is parallel to $CD$)

Directed angles between lines are taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circle $EAB$ and $t$ its tangent at $E$.

$t\parallel CD$. By the tangent-chord angle, $\angle(t,EA)=\angle(BE,BA)$. As $EA\parallel BD$ and $EB\parallel AC$, this says $\angle(t,BD)=\angle(AC,AB)$. In the circle $ABCD$, $\angle(AC,AB)=\angle(DC,DB)$ (both subtend the chord $BC$). So $\angle(t,BD)=\angle(DC,BD)$, i.e. $t\parallel DC$.

Conclusion. $M,N$ lie on $\omega$, so by the tangent-chord angle $\angle(ME,MN)=\angle(t,EN)$. Since $t\parallel DC$ and $EN$ is the line $ED$, $\angle(t,EN)=\angle(DC,DE)$. As $M\in EC$ and $N\in ED$, \[\angle(MC,MN)=\angle(ME,MN)=\angle(DC,DE)=\angle(DC,DN),\] so $M,N,D,C$ are concyclic.

Solution 2 (Power of a point as a linear function)

Let $\omega$ be the circle $EAB$ with centre $O$. For a point $Z$ put $\Phi(Z)=EZ^2-\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)$. Since $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(Z)=OZ^2-OE^2$, we get $\Phi(Z)=(Z-E)^2-(Z-O)^2+(E-O)^2=2(Z-E)\cdot(O-E)$, a linear function of $Z$.

For $Z=C$: the line $EC$ meets $\omega$ at $E$ and $M$, so $\operatorname{pow}_\omega(C)=CM\cdot CE$ (signed) and $\Phi(C)=CE^2-CM\cdot CE=EM\cdot EC$ (signed). Similarly $\Phi(D)=EN\cdot ED$. So \[EM\cdot EC=EN\cdot ED\iff\Phi(C)=\Phi(D)\iff (C-D)\cdot(O-E)=0\iff OE\perp CD\iff \text{the tangent at }E\parallel CD .\] The last statement holds: with directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and $t$ the tangent at $E$, the tangent-chord angle and the parallels $EA\parallel BD$, $EB\parallel AC$ give $\angle(t,BD)=\angle(t,EA)=\angle(BE,BA)=\angle(AC,AB)$, and $\angle(AC,AB)=\angle(DC,DB)$ in the circle $ABCD$; so $t\parallel DC$. Hence $EM\cdot EC=EN\cdot ED$, and by the converse of the power of a point, $M,N,D,C$ are concyclic.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 39: Perfect Powers and Prime Exponents · Squares, Cubes, and Higher Powers

Find all positive integers $a,b,c$ and prime numbers $p$ such that both conditions hold at the same time: \[a^2+4ab+b=b^2\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2^cp^2+1=b.\]

Show the answer

$(a,b,c,p)=(68,289,5,3)$

Show the solution

Solution ($b$ is a perfect square)

Step 1: $b$ is a square. The first equation gives $a^2=b(b-4a-1)$, so $b-4a-1>0$. Let $g=\gcd(b,b-4a-1)$. The two numbers $b/g$ and $(b-4a-1)/g$ are coprime with square product $a^2/g^2$, so $b=gs^2$, $b-4a-1=gt^2$ and $a=gst$. Subtracting, $g(s^2-t^2)=4gst+1$, so $g\mid1$: $g=1$, $b=s^2$, $a=st$ and \[s^2-4st-t^2=1.\qquad(1)\]

Step 2: the second equation. Now $(s-1)(s+1)=2^cp^2$ with $c\ge1$; so $s$ is odd and $s\pm1$ are even with $\gcd(s-1,s+1)=2$.

  • $p=2$: $(s-1)(s+1)=2^{c+2}$ forces $s-1=2$, $s=3$ (two even numbers with gcd $2$ and product a power of $2$ are $2$ and $4$).

  • $p$ odd: $c=1$ is impossible ($4\mid(s-1)(s+1)$), so $\frac{s-1}2\cdot\frac{s+1}2=2^{c-2}p^2$ with coprime consecutive factors. One of them is $p^2$ and the other $2^{c-2}$ (they cannot be $1$ and $2^{c-2}p^2$, since $2^{c-2}p^2\ge9>2$). So $p^2-2^{c-2}=\pm1$. Then $p^2+1=2^{c-2}$ is impossible ($p^2+1\equiv2\pmod 8$ would force $p^2=1$), and $(p-1)(p+1)=2^{c-2}$ gives $p=3$, $c=5$, and $s=17$.

Step 3: equation (1). It reads $t^2+4st+1=s^2$, so $5s^2-1=(t+2s)^2$ must be a square. For $s=3$: $44$, not a square. For $s=17$: $1444=38^2$, so $t=4$ and $a=st=68$, $b=289$.

Check: $68^2+4\cdot68\cdot289+289=83521=289^2$ and $2^5\cdot3^2+1=289$. The only solution is $\boxed{(a,b,c,p)=(68,289,5,3)}$.

Junior TSTST

Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 37: Polynomials and Algebraic Identities · Special Identities

If $\sqrt t=3+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt t}$, find the value of $\dfrac{t^6+2t^3+1}{t^3}$.

Show the answer

$1300$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Through $t+\frac1t$)

The condition says $\sqrt t-\frac1{\sqrt t}=3$. Squaring, $t-2+\frac1t=9$, so $t+\frac1t=11$. Then \[\frac{t^6+2t^3+1}{t^3}=t^3+\frac1{t^3}+2=\Bigl(t+\frac1t\Bigr)^3-3\Bigl(t+\frac1t\Bigr)+2=1331-33+2=\boxed{1300}.\]

Solution 2 (A perfect square)

Let $w=\sqrt t-\frac1{\sqrt t}=3$. Note $\frac{t^6+2t^3+1}{t^3}=\Bigl(t^{3/2}+t^{-3/2}\Bigr)^2=\Bigl(t^{3/2}-t^{-3/2}\Bigr)^2+4$. Moreover $t^{3/2}-t^{-3/2}=w^3+3w=27+9=36$ (expand $\bigl(\sqrt t-\frac1{\sqrt t}\bigr)^3$). Hence the value is $36^2+4=\boxed{1300}$.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 37: Polynomials and Algebraic Identities · Polynomial Structure

Find all positive integers $a$ such that \[\frac{5a^5-2a^4-7a^3+6a^2-a+4}{a^2-2}\] is an integer.

Show the answer

$a\in\{1,2,4\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Polynomial division)

Dividing, \[5a^5-2a^4-7a^3+6a^2-a+4=(a^2-2)(5a^3-2a^2+3a+2)+5a+8 .\] So the fraction is an integer if and only if $a^2-2\mid 5a+8$. For $a\ge7$ we have $a^2-2>5a+8>0$ (as $a^2-5a-10>0$), so this is impossible. Checking $a=1,\dots,6$: $a^2-2=-1,2,7,14,23,34$ and $5a+8=13,18,23,28,33,38$; divisibility holds for $a=1,2,4$ only. The answer is $\boxed{a\in\{1,2,4\}}$.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Substitution and Elimination

Solve the following system of equations in real numbers $x,y$: \[\begin{cases} x^2+2y=2xy,\\ x^3+x^2y=y^2.\end{cases}\]

Show the answer

$(x,y)\in\{(0,0),\ (\tfrac12,-\tfrac14),\ (\tfrac43,\tfrac83)\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Solve the first equation for $y$)

The first equation is $x^2=2y(x-1)$. If $x=1$ it gives $1=0$; so $x\ne1$ and $y=\frac{x^2}{2(x-1)}$. If $x=0$ then $y=0$, and $(0,0)$ satisfies both equations. Let $x\ne0$. The second equation $x^3+x^2y=y^2$ becomes, after dividing by $x^3$, \[1+\frac{x}{2(x-1)}=\frac{x}{4(x-1)^2}\iff 4(x-1)^2+2x(x-1)=x\iff 6x^2-11x+4=0,\] so $x=\frac43$ or $x=\frac12$, with $y=\frac83$ or $y=-\frac14$. Both pairs check in the second equation. The solutions are $\boxed{(0,0),\ (\tfrac12,-\tfrac14),\ (\tfrac43,\tfrac83)}$.

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking · Strategic Residues

Solve in positive integers $(a,b)$ the equation $3^a=b^2+2025$.

Show the answer

no solutions

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Divide out $81$, then work modulo $3$)

Since $b^2+2025>2025>3^6$, we have $a\ge7$. Then $81\mid 3^a$ and $81\mid2025=81\cdot25$, so $81\mid b^2$ and $9\mid b$. Writing $b=9c$, the equation becomes $3^{a-4}=c^2+25$ with $a-4\ge3$. Modulo $3$: $c^2\equiv-25\equiv2\pmod3$, but squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$. So there are no solutions in positive integers.

Solution 2 (Modulo $4$, then a difference of squares)

Modulo $4$, $b^2+2025\equiv b^2+1\in\{1,2\}$, while $3^a\equiv3$ for odd $a$. So $a$ is even, $a=2m$, and \[(3^m-b)(3^m+b)=2025=3^4\cdot5^2 .\] Put $d=3^m-b$; it is a positive divisor of $2025$ with $d<3^m+b=\frac{2025}{d}$, so $d<45$, and $2\cdot3^m=d+\frac{2025}d$. For $d=1,3,5,9,15,25,27$ the sum $d+\frac{2025}d$ equals $2026,678,410,234,150,106,102$, so $3^m$ would be $1013,339,205,117,75,53,51$, none of which is a power of $3$. So there are no solutions in positive integers.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 54: Prime Patterns and Number-Theoretic Search · Prime Restrictions by Congruence

  • (i) Prove that if both $p$ and $p^2+2$ are primes, then $p^3+2$ is also prime.

  • (ii) Prove that if both $p$ and $8p-1$ are primes, then $8p+1$ is composite.

Show the solution

Solution (Work modulo $3$)

(i) If $p\ne3$, then $p^2\equiv1\pmod3$ and $p^2+2$ is a multiple of $3$ larger than $3$, not a prime. So $p=3$, and then $p^3+2=29$ is prime.

(ii) Among the three consecutive integers $8p-1$, $8p$, $8p+1$ one is divisible by $3$. If $p=3$, then $8p+1=25$ is composite. If $p\ne3$, then $3\nmid8p$, and $3\nmid 8p-1$ because $8p-1$ is a prime larger than $3$ (as $8p-1\ge15$). So $3\mid8p+1>3$, and $8p+1$ is composite.

Problem 6

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Higher-Degree Factoring

Find all real solutions of \[x^5-2x^2-9x-6=0.\]

Show the answer

$x=2$ and $x=-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Rational roots and factoring)

Trying divisors of $6$: $x=-1$ gives $-1-2+9-6=0$ and $x=2$ gives $32-8-18-6=0$. Dividing by $(x-2)(x+1)=x^2-x-2$ and then once more by $x+1$, \[x^5-2x^2-9x-6=(x^2-x-2)(x^3+x^2+3x+3)=(x-2)(x+1)^2(x^2+3).\] Since $x^2+3>0$, the real solutions are $\boxed{x=2}$ and $\boxed{x=-1}$ (a double root).

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Angle Bisectors and Incenter

In triangle $ABC$ we have $AB=6$ and $AC=9$. Let $AD$ be the angle bisector of $\angle BAC$ with $D$ lying on $BC$. Let $DE$ be the angle bisector of $\angle ADC$ with $E$ lying on $AC$, such that $AE:EC=2:1$. Find the ratio $\dfrac{AD}{DE}$.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{AD}{DE}=3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Bisector theorem and Stewart)

By the bisector theorem in triangle $ABC$, $BD:DC=AB:AC=2:3$. By the bisector theorem in triangle $ADC$, $AE:EC=AD:DC$, so $AD=2DC$. With $AC=9$: $AE=6$, $EC=3$.

Let $BC=5x$, so $BD=2x$, $DC=3x$ and $AD=6x$. The length of the bisector $AD$ satisfies (Stewart) $AD^2=AB\cdot AC-BD\cdot DC$, i.e. $36x^2=54-6x^2$, so $x^2=\frac97$, $DC^2=9x^2=\frac{81}7$, $AD^2=\frac{324}7$.

For the bisector $DE$ of triangle $ADC$: $DE^2=DA\cdot DC-AE\cdot EC=\frac{18}{\sqrt7}\cdot\frac9{\sqrt7}-18=\frac{162}7-18=\frac{36}7$. Hence $AD:DE=\frac{18}{\sqrt7}:\frac6{\sqrt7}=\boxed{3}$.

Solution 2 (The angle $ADC$ is $120^\circ$)

By the bisector theorem, $BD:DC=AB:AC=2:3$ and $AE:EC=AD:DC$; with $AE:EC=2:1$ this gives $AD=2DC$, $AE=6$, $EC=3$. Let $DC=d$, so $AD=2d$. The law of cosines in triangle $ADC$: $81=4d^2+d^2-4d^2\cos\angle ADC$. Using $BD=\frac23d$ and $AB=6$ in triangle $ABD$ with $\cos\angle ADB=-\cos\angle ADC$: $36=4d^2+\frac49d^2+\frac83d^2\cos\angle ADC$. Eliminating $\cos\angle ADC$ (multiply the second equation by $\frac32$ and add): $81+54=5d^2+6d^2+\frac23d^2$, so $d^2=\frac{81}{7}$ and then $\cos\angle ADC=\frac{5d^2-81}{4d^2}=-\frac12$: $\angle ADC=120^\circ$.

So $DE$ bisects a $120^\circ$ angle: $\angle ADE=\angle EDC=60^\circ$. The areas give $\frac12\,AD\cdot DC\sin120^\circ=\frac12\,AD\cdot DE\sin60^\circ+\frac12\,DE\cdot DC\sin60^\circ$, i.e. $AD\cdot DC=DE(AD+DC)$, so $DE=\frac{2d\cdot d}{3d}=\frac23d$ and $\frac{AD}{DE}=\frac{2d}{\frac23d}=\boxed{3}$.

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 8: Triangle Congruence and Rigid Geometry · Isosceles and Equilateral Structure

In quadrilateral $ABCD$ we have $AD=12$, $\angle BAC=30^\circ$, $\angle CAD=50^\circ$, $\angle CDB=60^\circ$ and $\angle BDA=20^\circ$. Find the area of triangle $BCD$.

Show the answer

$36\sqrt3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two isosceles triangles)

The quadrilateral is convex, so $C$ lies inside $\angle BAD$ and $B$ inside $\angle ADC$. In triangle $ABD$: $\angle BAD=30^\circ+50^\circ=80^\circ$ and $\angle BDA=20^\circ$, so $\angle ABD=80^\circ=\angle BAD$ and $DB=DA=12$. In triangle $ACD$: $\angle CAD=50^\circ$ and $\angle ADC=20^\circ+60^\circ=80^\circ$, so $\angle ACD=50^\circ=\angle CAD$ and $DC=DA=12$. So triangle $BCD$ has $DB=DC=12$ and $\angle BDC=60^\circ$: it is equilateral with side $12$, and its area is $\frac{\sqrt3}4\cdot144=\boxed{36\sqrt3}$.

Solution 2 (The circle centred at $D$)

The quadrilateral is convex, so $C$ lies inside $\angle BAD$ and $B$ inside $\angle ADC$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle with centre $D$ and radius $DA=12$. In triangle $ABD$, $\angle BAD=80^\circ$ and $\angle ADB=20^\circ$ give $\angle ABD=80^\circ$, so $DB=DA$ and $B\in\Gamma$.

Let $C'$ be the point of $\Gamma$ on the ray $DC$. The central angle $\angle BDC'=60^\circ$ and $\angle ADC'=20^\circ+60^\circ=80^\circ<180^\circ$, so $A$ is not on the minor arc $BC'$ and the inscribed angle is $\angle BAC'=\frac12\angle BDC'=30^\circ$. Also $C'$ is not on the minor arc $AB$ (of $20^\circ$), so $C'$ lies on the same side of line $AB$ as the centre $D$, as $C$ does. Hence $C'$ lies on the ray from $A$ making $30^\circ$ with $AB$ towards $D$, which is the ray $AC$. The rays $AC$ and $DC$ meet only at $C$, so $C'=C$ and $DC=12$. The area of $BCD$ is $\frac12\cdot12\cdot12\sin60^\circ=\boxed{36\sqrt3}$.
