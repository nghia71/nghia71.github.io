Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Divisibility Chains and Factor Relations

Find all pairs of primes $p$ and $q$ for which there exists a positive integer $n$ such that \[\frac{1}{p+q+2}-\frac{1}{pq+p+q+2}=\frac{1}{n}.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Give the first denominator a name and express the second denominator in terms of it. Look at what cancels when you subtract the two fractions.
Hint 2: key idea
Take the reciprocal of the combined fraction and isolate the part that must be an integer. Use primality to turn the resulting condition into divisibility relations between $p$ and $q$. (LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Treat equal primes separately and keep the original divisibility by $pq$ for checking candidates. For unequal primes, put the smaller one first and use the bound on the number divisible by the larger prime to identify its only possible multiple. Substitute that relation back into the other prime's divisibility condition.
Common trap
The two primes may be equal, so do not assume $p<q$ (or $p\ne q$) from the start.
Show the answer

$p=q=2$

Show solution 1 – Reduce to two divisibilities

Put $A=p+q+2$. Then $pq+p+q+2=A+pq$ and \[\frac1A-\frac1{A+pq}=\frac{pq}{A(A+pq)},\qquad\text{so}\qquad n=\frac{A(A+pq)}{pq}=\frac{A^2}{pq}+A .\] Hence a suitable $n$ exists exactly when $pq\mid A^2$.

In particular $p\mid A^2$, so the prime $p$ divides $A=p+q+2$, that is, $p\mid q+2$. In the same way $q\mid p+2$.

If $p=q$, then $p\mid p+2$ gives $p\mid 2$, so $p=q=2$. Then $A=6$, $pq=4$ divides $A^2=36$, and $n=9+6=15$; indeed $\frac16-\frac1{10}=\frac1{15}$.

If $p\ne q$, say $p<q$, then $q\mid p+2$ and $0<p+2\le q+1<2q$ force $p+2=q$. Now $p\mid q+2=p+4$ gives $p\mid 4$, so $p=2$ and $q=4$, which is not prime. No pair arises.

So the only pair is $\boxed{p=q=2}$ (with $n=15$).

Show solution 2 – A size bound from the product

Subtracting the fractions, \[\frac1{p+q+2}-\frac1{pq+p+q+2}=\frac{pq}{(p+q+2)(pq+p+q+2)},\] so $n=\frac{(p+q+2)(pq+p+q+2)}{pq}=(p+q+2)+\frac{(p+q+2)^2}{pq}$, and a positive integer $n$ exists exactly when $pq\mid(p+q+2)^2$.

Case $p=q$. The condition reads $p^2\mid(2p+2)^2=4(p+1)^2$. Since $p$ does not divide $p+1$, this forces $p^2\mid4$, so $p=q=2$.

Case $p\ne q$. The prime $p$ divides $(p+q+2)^2$, hence divides $p+q+2$, hence divides $q+2$; likewise $q\mid p+2$. As $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes, they are coprime, so \[pq\mid(p+2)(q+2)=pq+2(p+q)+4,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad pq\mid 2p+2q+4 .\] The right-hand number is positive, so $pq\le 2p+2q+4$, that is, $(p-2)(q-2)\le 8$. Say $p<q$.

  • $p=2$: then $q\mid p+2=4$, so $q=2=p$, excluded.

  • $p=3$: then $q-2\le8$, so $q\in\{5,7\}$; but $p=3$ must divide $q+2\in\{7,9\}$, so $q=7$, and then $q=7$ must divide $p+2=5$, false.

  • $p\ge5$: then $q\ge7$ and $(p-2)(q-2)\ge3\cdot5>8$, impossible.

So no pair with $p\ne q$ works. For $p=q=2$ we get $n=6+\frac{36}4=15$, and indeed $\frac16-\frac1{10}=\frac1{15}$. The only pair is $\boxed{p=q=2}$.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

A $2025\times 2027$ board is to be completely covered with $2\times 2$ square pieces and $1\times 3$ rectangular pieces (they can be placed horizontally or vertically). The pieces cannot overlap, and no empty squares can be left on the board. What is the minimum number of $1\times 3$ rectangular pieces needed to cover the whole board?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try tiling a small board with both dimensions odd using the same two types of pieces. Write an equation for the total area covered by the two types.
Hint 2: key idea
Mark the cells whose row and column numbers are both even. Every square tile covers exactly one marked cell, and the area equation also restricts the number of bars modulo $4$. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the number of square tiles by the number of marked cells and combine the area bound with the residue restriction. For a construction, look for a tiling in which the square tiles use as many marked cells as possible and the bars are confined to a few thin strips. Build it from a small odd board extended by strips whose widths suit both tile shapes.
Common trap
The two added strips must account for the new corner region exactly once.
Show the answer

$1353$

Show the solution – Counting even-even cells, then an L-shaped induction

Call the $2\times2$ pieces squares and the $1\times3$ pieces bars; let a tiling use $s$ squares and $t$ bars. Number the rows $1,\dots,2025$ and the columns $1,\dots,2027$.

Lower bound. Counting cells, $4s+3t=2025\cdot2027$.

(i) $t\equiv1\pmod 4$. Since $2025\equiv1$ and $2027\equiv3\pmod4$, we get $3t\equiv3\pmod 4$, so $t\equiv1\pmod4$.

(ii) $s\le 1012\cdot1013$. Call a cell even if its row number and its column number are both even. Two consecutive rows contain exactly one even row and two consecutive columns contain exactly one even column, so every square covers exactly one even cell. Different squares cover different cells, hence $s$ is at most the number of even cells, which is $1012\cdot1013$.

Therefore \[3t=2025\cdot2027-4s\ \ge\ (2\cdot1012+1)(2\cdot1013+1)-4\cdot1012\cdot1013=2\cdot1012+2\cdot1013+1=4051,\] so $t\ge 4051/3>1350$, i.e. $t\ge1351$. The numbers $1351$ and $1352$ are $3$ and $0$ modulo $4$, so by (i), $t\ge1353$.

Construction with $1353$ bars. Claim: if an $R\times C$ board with $R,C$ odd and $R\ge3$ can be tiled using $t$ bars, then the $(R+6)\times(C+6)$ board can be tiled using $t+4$ bars.

Indeed, put the $R\times C$ tiling in the top-left corner. The remaining region consists of an $R\times6$ strip on the right (rows $1,\dots,R$) and a $6\times(C+6)$ strip at the bottom.

  • Right strip: rows $1$–$2$ are covered by three squares, row $3$ by two horizontal bars, and the remaining $R-3$ rows (an even number) by squares.

  • Bottom strip: columns $1$–$2$ are covered by three squares, column $3$ by two vertical bars, and the remaining $C+3$ columns (an even number) by squares.

This adds exactly $4$ bars, proving the claim (the figure shows the first step).

Start with the $3\times5$ board covered by five vertical bars. Applying the claim $337$ times gives a tiling of the $(3+6\cdot337)\times(5+6\cdot337)=2025\times2027$ board with $5+4\cdot337=1353$ bars.

Hence the minimum number of $1\times3$ pieces is $\boxed{1353}$.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a triangle and $D$ the point of tangency of its incircle with segment $BC$. Let $P$ be a point on segment $AB$ and $Q$ a point on segment $AC$ such that $AP=AQ$. Let $\Gamma_1$ be the circle tangent to segments $AB$ and $BC$ at points $P$ and $R$, respectively. Similarly, let $\Gamma_2$ be the circle tangent to segments $AC$ and $BC$ at points $Q$ and $S$, respectively. The circumcircles of $APR$ and $AQS$ are distinct and intersect at a point $T$ distinct from $A$. Prove that $A$, $D$ and $T$ are collinear.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the tangency points on $BC$ and record the equal tangent segments from $B$ and $C$. Distinguish the tangent circles from the circumcircles through $A$.
Hint 2: key idea
Let $R'$ and $S'$ be the second intersections of $BC$ with the two circumcircles. Use power at $B$ and $C$ along the side lines and along $BC$ to find their signed positions. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Put $B$ at zero on the number line along $BC$ and express the four intersection coordinates using the side lengths and $AP=AQ$. Compare the midpoints of $RS$ and $R'S'$ with the coordinate of $D$. Set up the two signed products that give the powers of $D$.
Common trap
A second intersection with the line $BC$ can lie outside the segment, so retain signed distances.
Show the solution – D is the midpoint of two chords on BC

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ and $x=AP=AQ$, with $0<x<\min(b,c)$. Tangent segments from a point to a circle are equal, so \[BR=BP=c-x,\qquad CS=CQ=b-x,\qquad BD=s-b .\] Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $APR$ and $\omega_2$ the circle $AQS$. We use signed lengths on line $BC$, with $B$ at $0$ and $C$ at $a$, and the power of a point $Z$ with respect to a circle, which equals $\overline{ZU}\cdot\overline{ZV}$ for every line through $Z$ meeting the circle at $U$ and $V$.

Second intersections with $BC$. The power of $B$ with respect to $\omega_1$ is $BP\cdot BA=(c-x)c$, since $P$ and $A$ lie on the same ray from $B$. Line $BC$ meets $\omega_1$ at $R$ (coordinate $c-x$) and at a second point $R'$ (a tangency $R'=R$ would give $(c-x)^2=(c-x)c$, impossible). Hence $(c-x)\cdot\overline{BR'}=(c-x)c$, so $R'$ has coordinate $c$. In the same way the power of $C$ with respect to $\omega_2$ is $(b-x)b$, and $\omega_2$ meets $BC$ at $S$ (coordinate $a-b+x$) and at $S'$ with coordinate $a-b$.

$D$ is a common midpoint. The coordinate of $D$ is $s-b=\frac{a+c-b}{2}$, and \[\frac{(c-x)+(a-b+x)}{2}=\frac{a+c-b}2=\frac{c+(a-b)}2 .\] So $D$ is the midpoint of $RS$ and of $R'S'$: the reflection in $D$ swaps $R\leftrightarrow S$ and $R'\leftrightarrow S'$.

Equal powers. Hence \[\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_1}(D)=\overline{DR}\cdot\overline{DR'}=(-\overline{DS})(-\overline{DS'})=\overline{DS}\cdot\overline{DS'}=\operatorname{pow}_{\omega_2}(D).\] The circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are distinct (this is part of the hypothesis; see the note) and meet at $A$ and $T\ne A$, so the set of points with equal powers is the line $AT$, the radical axis. Therefore $D$ lies on line $AT$: the points $A$, $D$, $T$ are collinear.

Problem 4

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

There are $2026$ students sitting at a round table, and each one has a pencil. At a given moment, the teacher gives a signal, and each student gives their pencil to one of their two neighbors. Determine the maximum number of students who can be left without a pencil after the teacher's signal.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a small circle of students and mark each pass with an arrow. For a student to end up empty, which way must each of their neighbours pass?
Hint 2: key idea
Compare two students with exactly one seat between them. The student in that middle seat cannot send their pencil in both directions, which restricts which seats can be empty together. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Number the seats and follow the even and odd labels as separate cycles, pairing each empty seat with the next seat in its cycle to obtain a bound. To try to attain it, look for a periodic pass pattern that leaves seats empty as densely as the bound allows. Check how the join around the table affects the proposed empty seats.
Common trap
The first and last seats of each parity cycle are also two seats apart.
Show the answer

$1012$

Show solution 1 – Students two seats apart

Number the seats $0,1,\dots,2025$ around the table, indices modulo $2026$. Student $i$ ends up without a pencil exactly when neither neighbour passes to $i$: student $i-1$ passes to $i-2$ and student $i+1$ passes to $i+2$.

Bound. Students $i$ and $i+2$ cannot both end up empty: for $i$ the student $i+1$ must pass to $i+2$, while for $i+2$ the same student $i+1$ must pass to $i$.

The $1013$ students with even numbers, in the order $0,2,4,\dots,2024$, form a cycle in which consecutive students (including $2024$ and $0$) are two seats apart. No two consecutive students of this cycle are both empty. If $e$ of them are empty, each empty one is followed in the cycle by a non-empty one, and these followers are distinct, so $2e\le1013$ and $e\le506$. The same holds for the odd-numbered students. Hence at most $2\cdot506=1012$ students are left without a pencil.

Example. Let student $i$ pass to the left ($i-1$) if $i\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and to the right ($i+1$) if $i\equiv2,3\pmod4$, for $0\le i\le2023$; students $2024$ and $2025$ pass in any direction. For $i=4k+1$ with $0\le k\le505$, student $4k$ passes left and student $4k+2$ passes right, so $i$ is empty; for $i=4k+2$ with $0\le k\le505$, student $4k+1$ passes left and student $4k+3$ passes right, so $i$ is empty. These are $2\cdot506=1012$ empty students.

The maximum is $\boxed{1012}$.

Show solution 2 – Counting direction changes

Number the seats $0,1,\dots,2025$ around the table, indices modulo $2026$, and write $d_i=L$ if student $i$ passes to $i-1$ and $d_i=R$ if student $i$ passes to $i+1$. Student $j$ receives nothing exactly when student $j-1$ passes away from $j$ and student $j+1$ passes away from $j$, that is, when \[d_{j-1}=L\quad\text{and}\quad d_{j+1}=R .\]

Bound. Look at the odd-numbered students $j=2u+1$. Student $2u+1$ is empty exactly when the pair $(d_{2u},d_{2u+2})$ equals $(L,R)$. Write the $1013$ letters $d_0,d_2,d_4,\dots,d_{2024}$ around a circle (after $d_{2024}$ comes $d_{2026}=d_0$). Then the number of empty odd-numbered students is the number of places where this circular word changes from $L$ to $R$. Going once around the circle, changes $L\to R$ and changes $R\to L$ alternate, so there are equally many of each; and there are at most $1013$ changes in all, one per pair of neighbouring letters. Hence at most $\lfloor1013/2\rfloor=506$ changes are of type $L\to R$, and at most $506$ odd-numbered students are empty.

The even-numbered students $j=2u$ are handled in the same way with the circular word $d_1,d_3,\dots,d_{2025}$, again of $1013$ letters: at most $506$ of them are empty. In total at most $1012$ students are left without a pencil.

Example. For $0\le i\le2023$ let $d_i=L$ if $i\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and $d_i=R$ if $i\equiv2,3\pmod4$; let $d_{2024}$ and $d_{2025}$ be arbitrary. Then $d_0,d_2,\dots,d_{2022}$ reads $L,R,L,R,\dots,L,R$ ($1012$ letters), with $506$ changes $L\to R$, at the pairs $(d_{4k},d_{4k+2})$, $0\le k\le505$; so the students $4k+1$ are empty. Likewise $d_1,d_3,\dots,d_{2023}$ reads $L,R,\dots,L,R$, with changes $L\to R$ at $(d_{4k+1},d_{4k+3})$, so the students $4k+2$, $0\le k\le505$, are empty. That is $1012$ empty students.

The maximum is $\boxed{1012}$.

Show solution 3 – Where the pencils of each half go

Number the seats $0,1,\dots,2025$ around the table, indices modulo $2026$. The neighbours of an even seat are odd seats and the neighbours of an odd seat are even seats.

Bound. The $1013$ students in even seats all pass their pencils to students in odd seats, and only they pass pencils to odd seats. So the $1013$ odd-seated students receive exactly $1013$ pencils in total. Each student has only two neighbours, so receives at most $2$ pencils; hence at least $\lceil1013/2\rceil=507$ odd-seated students receive a pencil, and at most $1013-507=506$ of them are left without one. In the same way the odd-seated students send $1013$ pencils to the $1013$ even-seated students, and at most $506$ even-seated students are left without a pencil. In total at most $1012$ students are left without a pencil.

Example. Let student $i$ pass to the left neighbour $i-1$ if $i\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and to the right neighbour $i+1$ if $i\equiv2,3\pmod4$ $(0\le i\le2025)$. For $0\le k\le505$:

  • student $4k+1$ gets nothing, since student $4k$ passes left and student $4k+2$ passes right;

  • student $4k+2$ gets nothing, since student $4k+1$ passes left and student $4k+3$ passes right.

These are $2\cdot506=1012$ students without a pencil (all seats involved are at most $2023$).

The maximum is $\boxed{1012}$.

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

For each positive integer $n$, define $T_n=1+2+\dots+n$. Find all integers $c\ge 0$ for which there exists an integer $r\ge 0$ such that the following inequality holds for all integers $n\ge 1$: \[T_{n+r}\le cn+T_n<T_{n+r+1}.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a small fixed shift, write out which summands appear in $T_{n+r}$ beyond those already in $T_n$. Do the same for the upper bound.
Hint 2: key idea
Subtract $T_n$ and express both remaining triangular-number differences as linear functions of $n$. Compare the coefficients of $n$, since a fixed choice of $r$ must work for arbitrarily large $n$. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Rule out coefficients $c$ smaller than $r$ or larger than $r+1$. For the remaining coefficient cases, check what the constant terms require, using $n=1$ when the lower bound is tightest there. Verify that each surviving choice satisfies both inequalities for every positive $n$.
Common trap
The right-hand inequality is strict, even when the coefficients of $n$ on its two sides agree.
Show the answer

$c\in\{0,1,2\}$

Show the solution – Linear inequalities in n

Since $T_{n+j}-T_n=(n+1)+\dots+(n+j)=jn+\frac{j(j+1)}2$, the condition for a fixed $r$ reads, for all $n\ge1$, \[rn+\frac{r(r+1)}2\ \le\ cn\ <\ (r+1)n+\frac{(r+1)(r+2)}2,\] that is, \[(c-r)\,n\ \ge\ \frac{r(r+1)}2\qquad\text{and}\qquad (c-r-1)\,n\ <\ \frac{(r+1)(r+2)}2 .\] If $c<r$, the left side of the first inequality is negative while the right side is $\ge0$. If $c>r+1$, the left side of the second inequality grows without bound in $n$. So $c=r$ or $c=r+1$.

  • $c=r$: the first inequality becomes $0\ge \frac{r(r+1)}2$, so $r=0$ and $c=0$; the second, $-n<1$, holds.

  • $c=r+1$: the second inequality becomes $0<\frac{(r+1)(r+2)}2$, true; the first becomes $n\ge\frac{r(r+1)}2$ for all $n\ge1$, i.e. $\frac{r(r+1)}2\le1$, so $r\in\{0,1\}$ and $c\in\{1,2\}$.

Conversely $c=0,1,2$ work with $r=0,0,1$: $T_n\le T_n<T_{n+1}$; $T_n\le n+T_n<T_n+n+1=T_{n+1}$; $T_n+n+1\le 2n+T_n<T_n+2n+3$.

The answer is $\boxed{c\in\{0,1,2\}}$.

Problem 6

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments

There are $n$ cards ($n\ge 2$) numbered from $1$ to $n$, each with a different number. Find all $n$ such that, for every divisor $k$ of $n$ with $1\le k<n$, and for any distribution of the $n$ cards into $k$ piles, each containing $n/k$ cards, there exists a pile containing two cards whose sum is a multiple of $k$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Choose a divisor $k$ in a small example and replace the card labels by their remainders modulo $k$. Try filling a pile while avoiding pairs whose remainders add to zero modulo $k$.
Hint 2: key idea
Each residue occurs equally often, and a pile avoiding the desired pair cannot contain opposite residues or repeat a residue equal to its own negative. Decide when this restriction blocks every distribution and when you can build one distribution that avoids it. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Check the divisor $k=1$, then look for small composite cases in which the residue counts leave no room for a distribution avoiding the pairs. For every other composite, build a distribution with no qualifying pair, choosing the pile structure according to the parity of $n$ or its smallest prime factor. Deal with self-opposite residues and the zero residue before the others.
Common trap
To rule out a value of $n$, one allowed divisor and one distribution without any qualifying pair are enough.
Show the answer

$n$ prime, or $n=9$

Show the solution – Residues, and a cut-up list

Fix a divisor $k$ of $n$ and put $m=n/k$. Only residues modulo $k$ matter, and each residue class contains exactly $m$ of the cards $1,\dots,n$. Two cards with residues $u,v$ have sum divisible by $k$ iff $u+v\equiv0\pmod k$. Call a pile safe if it contains no such two cards: it has at most one card $\equiv0$, at most one card $\equiv k/2$ when $k$ is even, and never cards of both residues $r$ and $-r$. The number $n$ has the required property iff for every divisor $k<n$ there is no split into $k$ safe piles of $m$ cards.

$n$ prime. The only divisor $k<n$ is $k=1$: one pile of $n\ge2$ cards, and any two cards have sum divisible by $1$. So every prime works.

$n=9$. For $k=1$ as above. For $k=3$, $m=3$: suppose the three piles are safe. The three cards $3,6,9\equiv0$ lie in different piles, so each pile has exactly one of them and two further cards from $\{1,4,7\}$ (residue $1$) and $\{2,5,8\}$ (residue $2$). A pile may not mix residues $1$ and $2$, so its two further cards have equal residues. Then the residue-$1$ cards come in pairs, but there are three of them, a contradiction. So $n=9$ works.

$n$ even, $n\ge4$. Take $k=n/2\ge2$ and piles of $m=2$ cards; each residue occurs on two cards.

  • $k$ odd: put the two cards $\equiv0$ with the two cards $\equiv1$ into two piles $\{0,1\}$ (sum $\equiv1$), and for $2\le r\le k-1$ put the two cards $\equiv r$ together (sum $\equiv 2r\not\equiv0$, as $k$ is odd).

  • $k$ even: form two piles $\{0,k/2\}$ (sum $\equiv k/2$) and, for every other $r$, a pile of the two cards $\equiv r$ (sum $2r\not\equiv0$, since $2r\equiv0$ only for $r\equiv0,k/2$).

All piles are safe, so even $n\ge4$ fail.

$n$ odd, composite, $n\ne9$. Let $p$ be the smallest prime factor of $n$, and take $k=n/p$, $m=p$. All prime factors of $k$ are at least $p$, so $k\ge p=m$; $k$ is odd; and $k\ne3$, since $k=3$ would force $p=3$ and $n=9$. So $k\ge5$. Let $h=\frac{k-1}2\ge2$. Write the nonzero cards in a list: first the $m$ cards of residue $1$, then those of residue $2$, …, residue $h$, then residue $-1$, residue $-2$, …, residue $-h$; this lists $(k-1)m$ cards. Cut the list into consecutive blocks: first $m$ blocks of length $m-1$, then $k-m$ blocks of length $m$ (in total $m(m-1)+(k-m)m=(k-1)m$ cards), and add one card $\equiv0$ to each of the $m$ short blocks. This gives $k$ piles of $m$ cards. They are safe:

  • each pile has at most one card $\equiv0$, and $0+r\not\equiv0$ for $r\not\equiv0$;

  • each residue fills $m$ consecutive places of the list and a block has at most $m$ places, so a block meets at most two residues, adjacent in the list;

  • two cards of the same residue $r\not\equiv0$ have sum $2r\not\equiv0$ ($k$ odd);

  • adjacent residues $r,r+1$ ($1\le r<h$) and $-r,-(r+1)$ have sums $\pm(2r+1)$ with $3\le 2r+1\le k-2$; the adjacent pair $h,-1$ has sum $h-1$ with $1\le h-1\le k-1$. None is $\equiv0$.

So these $n$ fail.

Every $n\ge2$ is prime, $9$, even and $\ge4$, or odd composite $\ne9$. The required $n$ are exactly $\boxed{\text{the primes and } n=9}$.
