Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Let $n$ be a positive integer that is not a perfect cube, and let $a, b, c$ be nonzero integers such that $a+b+c=0$. Prove that \[n(a^3-n)(b^3-n)(c^3-n)\] is not the cube of an integer.

Show the solution

Solution (An identity and three valuations)

Step 1: an identity. Let $e=ab+bc+ca$ and $g=abc$. Since $a+b+c=0$, the numbers $a,b,c$ are the roots of \[P(x)=(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)=x^3+ex-g .\] Thus $a^3=g-ea$, so $a^3-n=(g-n)-ea$, and similarly for $b$ and $c$. Note that $e=-\frac12(a^2+b^2+c^2)\neq0$ (square $a+b+c=0$). Put $t=\frac{g-n}{e}$. Then \[(a^3-n)(b^3-n)(c^3-n)=e^3(t-a)(t-b)(t-c)=e^3P(t)=e^3\bigl(t^3+et-g\bigr)=(g-n)^3+e^3(g-n)-e^3g .\] Hence \[n(a^3-n)(b^3-n)(c^3-n)=n\,u^3+n^2v^3,\qquad u=abc-n,\quad v=-(ab+bc+ca).\tag{1}\] (For readers who know some algebraic number theory: with $\theta=\sqrt[3]{n}$, the left side is the norm of $\theta(a-\theta)(b-\theta)(c-\theta)=u\theta+v\theta^2$.)

Step 2: $nu^3+n^2v^3$ is a cube only if $u=v=0$. Suppose $nu^3+n^2v^3=w^3$ with integers $u,v,w$. As $n$ is not a perfect cube, $n\ne0,\pm1$ and some prime $p$ occurs in $n$ with an exponent $s$ not divisible by $3$ (for $n<0$ apply this to $|n|$). Let $\nu(x)$ be the exponent of $p$ in a non-zero integer $x$. If $u,v,w\ne0$, then \[\nu(nu^3)\equiv s,\qquad \nu(n^2v^3)\equiv 2s,\qquad \nu(w^3)\equiv0\pmod 3,\] and $0,s,2s$ are pairwise different modulo $3$. So among the non-zero ones of the three integers $nu^3$, $n^2v^3$, $-w^3$ the exponents of $p$ are pairwise different. But a sum of non-zero integers with pairwise different exponents of $p$ is not $0$: it is divisible by exactly the smallest of these powers of $p$. Since $nu^3+n^2v^3-w^3=0$, all three terms must vanish, i.e. $u=v=w=0$.

Step 3. In (1), $v=-(ab+bc+ca)=\frac12(a^2+b^2+c^2)>0$, because $a,b,c$ are non-zero. So $v\neq0$, and by Step 2 the number $n(a^3-n)(b^3-n)(c^3-n)=nu^3+n^2v^3$ is not the cube of an integer (not even $0=0^3$: Step 2 allows $w=0$).

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities

Each cell of a $2026 \times 2026$ grid has a different color. In one move, the colors in a single row or column may be shifted cyclically by any number of positions. Find the minimum number of moves needed to rotate the coloring by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise.

Show the answer

$6075$

Show the solution

Solution (Three rounds suffice; counting how often each cell moves)

Let $n=2026$; we show that the minimum is $3n-3$. Only the fact that $n$ is even will be used. Number the rows $1,\dots,n$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,n$ from left to right, and take all indices modulo $n$. The cell (colour) that starts in row $r$, column $c$ is called the cell $(r,c)$; after the rotation it must be in position $(n+1-c,\ r)$. A move on a row is called an $H$-move for the cells it shifts, a move on a column a $V$-move.

A sequence of $3n-3$ moves.

  • 1. For each $c=2,\dots,n$, shift column $c$ down by $1-c$ (that is, up by $c-1$).

  • 2. For each $r=2,\dots,n$, shift row $r$ to the right by $r-1$.

  • 3. For each $c=1,\dots,n-1$, shift column $c$ down by $-c$ (that is, up by $c$).

The cell $(r,c)$ goes in round 1 to $(r+1-c,\ c)$; it is then in row $r+1-c$, so round 2 moves it to column $c+(r+1-c)-1=r$, i.e. to $(r+1-c,\ r)$; round 3 moves it to row $r+1-c-r=1-c\equiv n+1-c$. So it ends in $(n+1-c,r)$, as required. (The omitted shifts are shifts by $0$.) The number of moves is $3(n-1)$.

The lower bound. Consider any sequence of $T$ moves that produces the rotation. We may assume that no move is a shift by a multiple of $n$: such a move changes nothing and can be deleted, which only makes $T$ smaller. So every move really moves each of its $n$ cells to another row or column. For a cell $P$ let $\mathrm{len}(P)$ be the number of moves that shift $P$. Each move shifts $n$ cells, so \[\sum_{P}\mathrm{len}(P)=nT.\tag{1}\] The cell $(r,c)$ must change its column unless $c=r$ (main diagonal) and must change its row unless $r+c=n+1$ (anti-diagonal). As $n$ is even, no cell is on both diagonals, and no cell keeps its position. A cell changes column only in $H$-moves and row only in $V$-moves. So the cells with $\mathrm{len}(P)\le2$ are of the following types (the letters give the moves of the cell in order). Note that a main-diagonal cell cannot have the pattern $HV$ or $VH$: its single $H$-move would take it out of its column for good. Likewise an anti-diagonal cell cannot have the pattern $HV$ or $VH$, as its single $V$-move would take it out of its row for good.

  • $V$ or $VV$: a main-diagonal cell $(j,j)$ that is never shifted horizontally. It stays in column $j$, and every move of column $j$ shifts it; so column $j$ is moved exactly once, resp. exactly twice, in the whole sequence.

  • $H$ or $HH$: an anti-diagonal cell $(i,n+1-i)$ that is never shifted vertically; row $i$ is moved exactly once, resp. twice.

  • $HV$: a cell $(r,c)$ off both diagonals, shifted first inside its row $r$ to its final column $r$ and later inside column $r$ to its final row. Its $H$-move is the first move of row $r$ (before it the cell sat in row $r$), and its $V$-move is the last move of column $r$ (after it the cell sits in column $r$).

  • $VH$: a cell $(r,c)$ off both diagonals, shifted first inside its column $c$ to its final row $n+1-c$ and later inside this row. Its $V$-move is the first move of column $c$ and its $H$-move is the last move of row $n+1-c$.

Define the deficit of a cell as $\max(0,\,3-\mathrm{len}(P))$: it is $2$ for types $V$, $H$, it is $1$ for types $VV,HH,HV,VH$, and $0$ for all other cells. By (1), \[3n^2-nT=\sum_P\bigl(3-\mathrm{len}(P)\bigr)\le\Delta,\qquad \Delta:=\text{the sum of all deficits}.\tag{2}\]

Charging the deficits to the $2n$ lines. The deficit of a main-diagonal cell $(j,j)$ is charged to column $j$; that of an anti-diagonal cell $(i,n+1-i)$ to row $i$. The deficit $1$ of an $HV$ cell $(r,c)$ is charged half to row $r$ and half to column $r$; the deficit of a $VH$ cell $(r,c)$ half to column $c$ and half to row $n+1-c$.

Each line receives at most $\frac12$ from $HV$ cells and at most $\frac12$ from $VH$ cells. Two $HV$ cells $(r,c)$, $(r,c')$ of the same row $r$ would both be shifted by the first move of row $r$, by the same amount, into the same column $r$: impossible. So at most one $HV$ cell charges row $r$, and at most one charges column $r$. Two $VH$ cells $(r,c),(r',c)$ of the same column $c$ would both be shifted by the first move of column $c$ into the same row $n+1-c$: impossible. So at most one $VH$ cell charges column $c$, and at most one charges row $n+1-c$.

Each line receives at most $2$ in total. Consider column $j$ (rows are treated in the same way, with the roles of $H$ and $V$, and of the two diagonals, exchanged).

  • If column $j$ is never moved, it receives nothing (the cell $(j,j)$ must leave its row, and the $HV$ and $VH$ cells charging column $j$ are shifted by a move of column $j$).

  • If column $j$ is moved exactly once and $(j,j)$ has type $V$ (charge $2$): this move shifts by $n+1-2j$. An $HV$ cell $(j,c)$ charging column $j$ would be carried by the same move from row $j$ to row $n+1-c$, a shift by $n+1-c-j$; this gives $c=j$, impossible. A $VH$ cell $(r,j)$ charging column $j$ would be carried from row $r$ to row $n+1-j$, a shift by $n+1-j-r$; this gives $r=j$, impossible. So the total charge is $2$. If $(j,j)$ is not of type $V$, the charge is at most $\frac12+\frac12$.

  • If column $j$ is moved exactly twice, it receives at most $1$ from $(j,j)$ (type $VV$) and at most $1$ from $HV$ and $VH$ cells.

  • If column $j$ is moved three or more times, $(j,j)$ has no deficit and the charge is at most $1$.

Therefore $\Delta\le2\cdot2n=4n$, and (2) gives $3n^2-nT\le4n$, i.e. $T\ge3n-4$.

$T=3n-4$ is impossible. If $T=3n-4$, then (2) forces $\Delta=4n$, so every line receives exactly $2$. By the case distinction, a column receives $2$ only if its main-diagonal cell has type $V$ or $VV$, and a row receives $2$ only if its anti-diagonal cell has type $H$ or $HH$. So no main-diagonal cell is ever shifted horizontally, and no anti-diagonal cell is ever shifted vertically. But look at the first move of the sequence, when all cells are still in place: if it is a move of row $i$, it shifts the main-diagonal cell $(i,i)$ horizontally; if it is a move of column $j$, it shifts the anti-diagonal cell $(n+1-j,j)$ vertically. Contradiction.

Hence $T\ge3n-3$, and with the construction the minimum number of moves is $3\cdot2026-3=\boxed{6075}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with incenter $I$ and circumcircle $\Gamma$. The incircle of triangle $ABC$ touches sides $AC$ and $AB$ at points $E$ and $F$, respectively. Let $S \ne A$ be the second intersection point of $\Gamma$ with the circumcircle of triangle $AEF$. Suppose the reflection of line $SI$ across line $EF$ meets lines $AC$ and $AB$ at points $P$ and $Q$, respectively. Let $X, Y$ be the points on line $EF$ such that $PX \perp AC$ and $QY \perp AB$.

Prove that the circles with diameters $CX$ and $BY$ have a common point on line $PQ$ and a common point on $\Gamma$.

Show the solution

Solution (Power of the foot on EF, then an angle chase)

We assume $AB\neq AC$ (otherwise the circles $AEF$ and $\Gamma$ are tangent at $A$ and $S$ is not defined), and that the reflected line is not parallel to $AC$ or $AB$ (this is implicit in the statement, since it meets these lines at $P$ and $Q$). By “the line $PQ$” we mean this reflected line; this matters only when $\angle A=90^\circ$, where the reflected line passes through $A$ and $P=Q=A$ (see the end of Step 2). Let the incircle touch $BC$ at $D$, let $D_0$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $EF$, and let $B_0,C_0$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $B,C$ to the line $EF$. Let $\omega_C,\omega_B$ be the circles with diameters $CX$ and $BY$.

Step 1: the line $SI$ passes through $D_0$. Invert in the incircle (center $I$). Since $IE\perp AE$ and $EF\perp AI$, the image of $A$ is the midpoint $T_0$ of $EF$; likewise $B,C$ go to the midpoints of $FD$, $DE$. So $\Gamma$ goes to the nine-point circle of triangle $DEF$. The circle $AEF$ passes through $I$ (it has diameter $AI$) and through the fixed points $E,F$, so it goes to the line $EF$. Hence the image of $S$ is a common point of the line $EF$ and the nine-point circle of $DEF$ other than $T_0$ (the image of $A$): it is the foot $D_0$ of the altitude from $D$. ($D_0\neq T_0$ because $DE\ne DF$.) Thus $I$, $S$, $D_0$ are collinear.

Consequently the reflection of the line $SI$ in $EF$ is the line through $D_0$ and the reflection $I'$ of $I$ in $EF$: the line $PQ$ passes through $D_0$.

Step 2: coordinates on $EF$. Take $EF$ as the $x$-axis with origin $T_0$ and unit $T_0E$, with $A$ above: $E=(1,0)$, $F=(-1,0)$, $I=(0,-\kappa)$, $A=(0,\frac1\kappa)$, $I'=(0,\kappa)$, where $\kappa=\tan\frac A2$ (then $IE\perp AE$). The lines $AC$, $AB$ are $x+\kappa y=1$ and $-x+\kappa y=1$. Let $L=AE=AF$, $u=BF=BD$, $v=CE=CD$ (tangent lengths). The $x$-coordinates of $B$, $C$ are $-(1+\frac uL)$ and $1+\frac vL$ (as $\overrightarrow{FB}=\frac uL\overrightarrow{AF}$, $\overrightarrow{EC}=\frac vL\overrightarrow{AE}$), and $D$ divides $BC$ in the ratio $BD:DC=u:v$. Hence \[B_0=\Bigl(-1-\frac uL,0\Bigr),\quad C_0=\Bigl(1+\frac vL,0\Bigr),\quad D_0=(t,0),\qquad t=\frac{-v(1+\frac uL)+u(1+\frac vL)}{u+v}=\frac{u-v}{u+v}.\] The line $PQ$ joins $D_0=(t,0)$ and $I'=(0,\kappa)$: $y=\kappa(1-\frac xt)$. Intersecting with $AC$ and $AB$: \[x_P=\frac{(1-\kappa^2)t}{t-\kappa^2},\qquad x_Q=-\frac{(1-\kappa^2)t}{t+\kappa^2}.\] The line $PX$ is perpendicular to $AC$, hence parallel to $IE$, i.e. to the vector $(1,\kappa)$; so $x_X=x_P-\frac{y_P}{\kappa}=x_P-(1-\frac{x_P}{t})$. Similarly $QY\parallel IF$, parallel to $(-1,\kappa)$, and $x_Y=x_Q+(1-\frac{x_Q}t)$. A short computation gives \[x_X=\frac{1-\kappa^2t}{t-\kappa^2},\qquad x_Y=\frac{1+\kappa^2t}{t+\kappa^2},\] and therefore \[x_X-t=\frac{1-t^2}{t-\kappa^2},\quad x_Y-t=\frac{1-t^2}{t+\kappa^2},\quad x_P-t=\frac{t(1-t)}{t-\kappa^2},\quad x_Q-t=-\frac{t(1+t)}{t+\kappa^2}.\tag{1}\] (If $\angle A=90^\circ$, then $\kappa=1$, so $I'=A$ and $x_P=x_Q=0$: indeed $P=Q=A$. The formulas (1) and everything below remain valid with $\kappa=1$; here $X=F$ and $Y=E$.) Also, from $1-t=\frac{2v}{u+v}$ and $1+t=\frac{2u}{u+v}$, \[x_{C_0}-t=v\Bigl(\frac{2}{u+v}+\frac1L\Bigr),\qquad x_{B_0}-t=-u\Bigl(\frac{2}{u+v}+\frac1L\Bigr).\tag{2}\]

Step 3: a common point on $PQ$. The circle $\omega_C$ passes through $P$ (because $\angle CPX=90^\circ$) and through $C_0$ (because $\angle CC_0X=90^\circ$). Let $V_1$ be its second intersection with the line $PQ$ ($V_1=P$ in case of tangency). The lines $EF$ and $PQ$ pass through $D_0$, so the power of $D_0$ with respect to $\omega_C$ gives, with signed lengths, \[\overline{D_0P}\cdot\overline{D_0V_1}=\overline{D_0X}\cdot\overline{D_0C_0}.\] In the same way $\omega_B$ passes through $Q$ and $B_0$, and its second intersection $V_2$ with $PQ$ satisfies $\overline{D_0Q}\cdot\overline{D_0V_2}=\overline{D_0Y}\cdot\overline{D_0B_0}$. Signed lengths on $EF$ are differences of $x$-coordinates, and the ratio $\overline{D_0Q}:\overline{D_0P}$ on the line $PQ$ equals $(x_Q-t):(x_P-t)$. By (1) and (2), \[\frac{\overline{D_0V_1}}{\overline{D_0V_2}}=\frac{x_X-t}{x_Y-t}\cdot\frac{x_Q-t}{x_P-t}\cdot\frac{x_{C_0}-t}{x_{B_0}-t} =\frac{t+\kappa^2}{t-\kappa^2}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{(1+t)(t-\kappa^2)}{(1-t)(t+\kappa^2)}\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(-\frac vu\Bigr)=\frac{1+t}{1-t}\cdot\frac vu=1,\] since $\frac{1+t}{1-t}=\frac uv$. Hence $V_1=V_2=:V$: the two circles have a common point $V$ on the line $PQ$.

Step 4: a common point on $\Gamma$. The circle $\omega_B$ passes through $B$ (it has diameter $BY$), through $Q$ (as $\angle BQY=90^\circ$) and through $V$; the circle $\omega_C$ passes through $C$, $P$ and $V$. Let $U$ be the second common point of $\Gamma$ and $\omega_B$, with $U=B$ if these two circles are tangent at $B$. We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,\ell')$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. In the circle $\omega_B$, the chord $VB$ gives $\angle(UV,UB)=\angle(QV,QB)=\angle(PQ,AB)$, because $Q,V$ lie on $PQ$ and $Q,B$ lie on $AB$. In the circle $\Gamma$, the chord $BC$ gives $\angle(UB,UC)=\angle(AB,AC)$. Adding, \[\angle(UV,UC)=\angle(PQ,AB)+\angle(AB,AC)=\angle(PQ,AC)=\angle(PV,PC),\] because $P,V$ lie on $PQ$ and $P,C$ lie on $AC$. Hence $U$ lies on the circle through $P$, $C$, $V$, which is $\omega_C$.

If two of the points used coincide ($V=P$, $V=Q$, $P=C$, $Q=B$ or $U=B$), the chord through the double point is replaced by the tangent to the circle at that point (tangent-chord angle), and the same computation applies. For instance, if $V=P$, the equality $\angle(UP,UC)=\angle(PQ,PC)$ says that the circle $UPC$ touches the line $PQ$ at $P$; and $\omega_C$ is the only circle through $P$ and $C$ that touches $PQ$ at $P$ (here $\omega_C$ meets $PQ$ only at $P$). So again $U\in\omega_C$.

Thus the circles with diameters $CX$ and $BY$ have the common point $U$ on $\Gamma$, and the common point $V$ on the line $PQ$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $B, C$ be fixed points in the interior of a circle $\omega$. The line $BC$ meets $\omega$ at points $A, D$ such that $A, B, C, D$ are collinear in that order. Let $P$ be a point on $\omega$, distinct from $A$ and $D$. The lines $PB$ and $PC$ meet $\omega$ again at points $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

Suppose the common external tangents to the circumcircles of triangles $ABX$ and $CDY$ meet at point $S$. Prove that, as $P$ varies, $S$ lies on one of two fixed lines.

Show the solution

Solution (Two fixed points on the perpendicular bisectors)

Let $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ be the circles $ABX$, $CDY$, with centers $O_1,O_2$ and radii $R_1,R_2$ (the tangents meet, so $R_1\ne R_2$). Put $p=AB$, $q=CD$, and let $\ell_1,\ell_2$ be the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $CD$; they are parallel and different, $O_1\in\ell_1$, $O_2\in\ell_2$.

Draw $AD$ horizontally and suppose first that $P$ lies above the line $AD$. Let $\alpha=\angle APD$; it is the same for all $P$ above $AD$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $AD$ above $AD$, so $NA=ND$ and $\angle AND=\alpha$. Define the fixed points \[K_1\in\ell_1,\ K_2\in\ell_2\ \text{ above }AD,\ \text{at heights }\ \tfrac p2\cot\tfrac\alpha2\ \text{ and }\ \tfrac q2\cot\tfrac\alpha2 ;\] equivalently, $K_1AB$ and $K_2CD$ are the isosceles triangles with apex angle $\alpha$ erected above $AB$ and $CD$ ($K_1$ lies on $AN$ with $K_1B\parallel ND$, and $K_2$ lies on $DN$ with $K_2C\parallel NA$). We claim that $S$ lies on the line $K_1K_2$.

Step 1: the angles at $X$ and $Y$. Since $B$ is inside $\omega$ on the chord $AD$, the point $X$ lies on the ray $PB$ beyond $B$: below $AD$, and on the same side of the line $AP$ as $B$ and $D$. Hence $X$ and $D$ see the chord $AP$ under the same angle: \[\xi:=\angle AXB=\angle AXP=\angle ADP .\] In the same way $Y$ lies below $AD$ and $\eta:=\angle CYD=\angle PYD=\angle PAD$. From triangle $APD$, \[\xi+\eta+\alpha=180^\circ.\tag{1}\]

Step 2: the centers. The chord $AB$ of $\Gamma_1$ is seen from $X$, a point below $AD$, under the angle $\xi$. So $R_1=\frac{p}{2\sin\xi}$, and $O_1$ is the point of $\ell_1$ at height $-\frac p2\cot\xi$ (below $AD$ if $\xi<90^\circ$, above if $\xi>90^\circ$). Similarly $R_2=\frac q{2\sin\eta}$ and $O_2$ is at height $-\frac q2\cot\eta$. Let $\mathbf e$ be the unit vector pointing upward. Then \[K_1-O_1=\frac p2\Bigl(\cot\frac\alpha2+\cot\xi\Bigr)\mathbf e=\frac{p}{2\sin\xi}\cdot\frac{\sin(\xi+\frac\alpha2)}{\sin\frac\alpha2}\,\mathbf e=\lambda_1R_1\,\mathbf e,\qquad \lambda_1=\frac{\sin(\xi+\frac\alpha2)}{\sin\frac\alpha2},\] and likewise $K_2-O_2=\lambda_2R_2\,\mathbf e$ with $\lambda_2=\frac{\sin(\eta+\frac\alpha2)}{\sin\frac\alpha2}$. By (1), $\bigl(\xi+\frac\alpha2\bigr)+\bigl(\eta+\frac\alpha2\bigr)=180^\circ$, so $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=:\lambda$.

Step 3: the homothety. The common external tangents meet at the center $S$ of the homothety with positive ratio $R_2/R_1$ that maps $\Gamma_1$ to $\Gamma_2$; it maps $O_1$ to $O_2$. It maps the point $K_1=O_1+\lambda R_1\mathbf e$ to $O_2+\frac{R_2}{R_1}\lambda R_1\mathbf e=O_2+\lambda R_2\mathbf e=K_2$. A homothety maps each point to a point collinear with it and the center, so $S$, $K_1$, $K_2$ are collinear; and $K_1\ne K_2$ since $\ell_1\neq\ell_2$. Thus $S$ lies on the fixed line $K_1K_2$.

Step 4. If $P$ lies below $AD$, the same argument (reflect the figure in the line $AD$; the angle $\alpha$ is replaced by $180^\circ-\alpha$, the angle under which the other arc sees $AD$) shows that $S$ lies on the line $K_1'K_2'$, where $K_1'AB$, $K_2'CD$ are the isosceles triangles with apex angle $180^\circ-\alpha$ below $AD$. So $S$ always lies on one of the two fixed lines $K_1K_2$ and $K_1'K_2'$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Let $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ denote the set of positive real numbers and $\mathbb{R}$ the set of all real numbers. Find all strictly increasing functions $f : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ such that, for every $x, z > 0$, the equation \[f(x^2-xy+y^2) + f(y^2-yz+z^2) = f(x^2+xz+z^2)\] has exactly one solution $y \in \mathbb{R}$.

Show the answer

$f(t)=c\sqrt t$ with a constant $c>0$

Show the solution

Solution (A triangle picture and the smallest value of g(d)/d)

A geometric picture. Put $g(d)=f(d^2)$ for $d>0$; $g$ is strictly increasing. Fix $x,z>0$. Take three lines through a point $O$ at angles $0^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $120^\circ$, the point $X$ on the first ray with $OX=x$, the point $Z$ on the third ray with $OZ=z$, and for real $y$ the point $Y$ of the second line with signed coordinate $y$. By the law of cosines ($\angle XOY=\angle YOZ=60^\circ$ for $y>0$, both $120^\circ$ for $y<0$, and $\angle XOZ=120^\circ$) \[XY^2=x^2-xy+y^2,\qquad YZ^2=y^2-yz+z^2,\qquad XZ^2=x^2+xz+z^2 .\] So the equation says $g(XY)+g(YZ)=g(XZ)$. The second line bisects the angle $XOZ$; let $Y_0$ be its intersection with the segment $XZ$ (so $y_0=\frac{xz}{x+z}$) and note $XY_0:Y_0Z=x:z$.

$f(t)=c\sqrt t$ is a solution. Then $g(d)=cd$, and the equation $XY+YZ=XZ$ holds exactly when $Y$ lies on the segment $XZ$, i.e. only for $Y=Y_0$: exactly one solution.

A first consequence (no continuity needed). Let $x=z$. Then the left side is $2f(x^2-xy+y^2)$, which is unchanged under $y\mapsto x-y$. So the unique solution is $y=\frac x2$, and $2f(\frac34x^2)=f(3x^2)$. Hence \[f(4t)=2f(t)\ \text{ for all }t>0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad g(2d)=2g(d).\]

The ratio $g(d)/d$. Put $\rho(d)=\frac{g(d)}d$ for $d>0$. From $g(2d)=2g(d)$ we get $\rho(2d)=\rho(d)$, so every value of $\rho$ is already taken on $[1,2]$; and for $1\le d\le2$ we have $\rho(d)\ge\frac{g(1)}2>0$, because $g$ is increasing. Hence \[m:=\inf_{d>0}\rho(d)>0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad g(d)\ge m\,d\ \text{ for all }d>0. \tag{1}\] Choose numbers $c_k\in[1,2]$ with $\rho(c_k)\to m$; passing to a subsequence, we may assume $c_k\to d_0$ for some $d_0\in[1,2]$.

The solutions are pushed towards the segment. Fix $\tau$ with $0<\tau<1$. For each $k$ take the triangle $OXZ$ of the picture with $x:z=\tau:(1-\tau)$, scaled so that $XZ=c_k$ (all these triangles are similar, and $XY_0:XZ=x:(x+z)=\tau$). By hypothesis the equation has a solution: there is a point $Y_k$ on the bisector line with $g(a_k)+g(b_k)=g(c_k)$, where $a_k=XY_k$ and $b_k=Y_kZ$. By (1) and the triangle inequality, \[m\,c_k\le m\,(a_k+b_k)\le g(a_k)+g(b_k)=g(c_k)=\rho(c_k)\,c_k .\tag{2}\] Therefore \[0\le\frac{a_k+b_k-c_k}{c_k}\le\frac{\rho(c_k)}m-1\to0 .\] Now look at the fixed triangle with $XZ=1$. On the bisector line, the function $\varphi(Y)=XY+YZ-XZ$ is continuous, tends to infinity at both ends of the line (as $XY+YZ\ge2|y|-x-z$), and vanishes only at $Y_0$, the only point of the line on the segment $XZ$. Hence for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\eta>0$ such that $\varphi(Y)<\eta$ implies $|XY-XY_0|<\varepsilon$ (outside a bounded interval $\varphi$ is large, and on the compact rest with $|XY-XY_0|\ge\varepsilon$ it has a positive minimum). Applying this to the rescaled points $Y_k$, we get $\frac{a_k}{c_k}\to XY_0=\tau$, and so \[a_k\to\tau d_0 .\] Moreover, (2) gives $\bigl(g(a_k)-m\,a_k\bigr)+\bigl(g(b_k)-m\,b_k\bigr)\le(\rho(c_k)-m)\,c_k\to0$, and both brackets are non-negative by (1). So $g(a_k)-m\,a_k\to0$, and hence \[g(a_k)\to m\,\tau d_0 .\]

$g$ is linear. Let $0<s<d_0$, and choose any $s'$ with $s<s'<d_0$. Apply the previous paragraph with $\tau=\frac{s'}{d_0}$: there are numbers $a_k\to s'$ with $g(a_k)\to m\,s'$. For large $k$ we have $a_k>s$, so $g(s)<g(a_k)$ because $g$ is increasing; letting $k\to\infty$ gives $g(s)\le m\,s'$. This holds for every $s'\in(s,d_0)$, so $g(s)\le m\,s$. Together with (1), $g(s)=m\,s$ for all $0<s<d_0$. Finally, every $d>0$ can be written as $d=2^is$ with an integer $i$ and $\frac{d_0}2\le s<d_0$, and then $g(d)=2^ig(s)=m\,d$ (the relation $g(2d)=2g(d)$ works in both directions).

So $g(d)=md$ for all $d>0$, that is, $f(t)=g(\sqrt t)=m\sqrt t$. Together with the verification above, the solutions are exactly \[\boxed{f(t)=c\sqrt t\quad(c>0)}.\]

Remark. The hypothesis “exactly one solution” is used only once, for $x=z$, to get $f(4t)=2f(t)$; after that only the existence of a solution is used, for well-chosen triangles. No continuity of $f$ is assumed: the extremal ratio $m$ replaces the intermediate value theorem.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Each positive integer is coloured with exactly one of the two colours azure and beige, and each colour is used infinitely often. Let \[a_1 < a_2 < \cdots \qquad\text{and}\qquad b_1 < b_2 < \cdots\] be the azure and beige integers, respectively. Form the real numbers \[\alpha = 0.a_{b_1}a_{b_2}a_{b_3}\cdots, \qquad \beta = 0.b_{a_1}b_{a_2}b_{a_3}\cdots,\] where each decimal is formed by concatenating the usual decimal representations of the indicated integers. Can both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be rational?

Show the answer

No

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Periodic decimals have few long blocks; then count residues)

No. Suppose both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are rational. Let $A$ and $B$ be the sets of azure and beige integers, and for $x\ge0$ let $A(x)$, $B(x)$ be the numbers of azure, resp. beige, integers in $[1,x]$; so $A(x)+B(x)=x$ for integers $x$. The decimal of $\alpha$ is the concatenation of the $\alpha$-terms $a_{b_1}<a_{b_2}<\cdots$ (the azure numbers with a beige index), and that of $\beta$ is the concatenation of the $\beta$-terms $b_{a_1}<b_{a_2}<\cdots$ (the beige numbers with an azure index).

Step 1: consequences of periodicity. A rational number has an eventually periodic decimal expansion. Choose $M$ and $p$ such that the digit sequences of both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ satisfy $\text{digit}_{t+p}=\text{digit}_t$ for all positions $t\ge M$.

  • (P1) Among the terms that begin at a position $\ge M$, at most $p$ have any given number of digits. If two terms with the same number $L$ of digits begin at positions $t<t'$, both $\ge M$, with $t\equiv t'\pmod p$, they are the same string of $L$ digits, hence equal; but the terms are distinct. At most $M$ terms begin before position $M$. Consequently, the number of $\alpha$-terms (and of $\beta$-terms) that are $\le x$ is at most \[\lambda(x):=M+p\,(\log_{10}x+1)\qquad(x\ge1).\]

  • (P2) Let $m\ge1$. The terms that have at least $m$ digits and begin at a position $\ge M$ leave at most $p$ different remainders modulo $10^m$. The last $m$ digits of such a term lie in the periodic part, so they are determined by the residue modulo $p$ of the position where the term ends.

Step 2: one colour is very rare. The $\alpha$-terms $\le x$ are the $a_i$ with $i$ beige and $i\le A(x)$; there are $B(A(x))$ of them. Similarly there are $A(B(x))$ $\beta$-terms $\le x$. By (P1), \[B\bigl(A(x)\bigr)\le\lambda(x),\qquad A\bigl(B(x)\bigr)\le\lambda(x)\qquad\text{for all integers }x\ge1.\] Let $z=\min(A(x),B(x))$. If $z=B(x)\le A(x)$, then $B(z)\le B(A(x))\le\lambda(x)$ and $A(z)=A(B(x))\le\lambda(x)$; if $z=A(x)\le B(x)$, then $A(z)\le A(B(x))\le\lambda(x)$ and $B(z)=B(A(x))\le\lambda(x)$. In both cases $z=A(z)+B(z)\le2\lambda(x)$: \[\min\bigl(A(x),B(x)\bigr)\le2\lambda(x)\quad\text{for all }x.\] For large $x$ we have $4\lambda(x+1)+1<x$, so $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ cannot both be $\le2\lambda(x)$, and the smaller one cannot change from $A$ to $B$ between $x$ and $x+1$ (that would give $x+1\le A(x)+1+B(x+1)\le4\lambda(x+1)+1$). Hence one colour is the rare one for all large $x$. The problem is symmetric in the two colours (exchanging them exchanges $\alpha$ and $\beta$), so we may assume \[B(x)\le2\lambda(x)\quad\text{for all }x\ge x_0.\tag{1}\] In particular beige numbers are rare: any interval of $N$ consecutive integers inside $[1,x]$, $x\ge x_0$, contains at least $N-2\lambda(x)$ azure numbers.

Step 3: the numbers $q_r$. For $r\ge1$ let $q_r=B(a_{b_r})$ be the number of beige integers below the $\alpha$-term $a_{b_r}$. Below $a_{b_r}$ there are exactly $b_r-1$ azure integers, so \[a_{b_r}=b_r+q_r .\tag{2}\] Since $a_i\ge i$ and $a_{b_r}\ne b_r$ (different colours), $a_{b_r}>b_r$, so $b_1,\dots,b_r$ are below $a_{b_r}$ and $q_r\ge r$. The beige numbers counted by $q_r$ other than $b_1,\dots,b_r$ are $b_{r+1},\dots,b_{q_r}$; they lie in the interval $(b_r,\,b_r+q_r)$. Put $j_r=q_r-r$.

Claim: there is a constant $c$ such that $j_r\le c\log_{10}(r+10)$ for all large $r$. First, $q_r\le B(b_r+q_r)\le2\lambda(b_r+q_r)$, so for large $r$ we have $q_r<b_r$ (otherwise $q_r\le 2\lambda(2q_r)$, which bounds $q_r$, while $q_r\ge r$). Thus $b_{r+1},\dots,b_{r+j_r}$ lie in $(b_r,2b_r)$, an interval in which the integers have at most two different numbers of digits. Those $b_{r+i}$ whose index $r+i$ is azure are $\beta$-terms; by (P1) there are at most $2p$ of them (for large $r$ all of them begin after position $M$). The other indices $r+i$, $1\le i\le j_r$, are beige integers in $[1,r+j_r]$; by (1) there are at most $2\lambda(r+j_r)$ of them. Hence \[j_r\le2p+2\lambda(r+j_r)=2p+2M+2p\bigl(\log_{10}(r+j_r)+1\bigr).\] If $j_r\ge r$ this bounds $j_r$ by a constant; otherwise $r+j_r<2r$. In both cases $j_r\le c\log_{10}(r+10)$ for a suitable constant $c$. So \[r\le q_r\le r+c\log_{10}(r+10)\qquad\text{for all large }r.\tag{3}\]

Step 4: the contradiction. Let $m$ be a large integer and consider the $10^m$ indices $r$ with $10^m\le r<2\cdot10^m$. For such $r$, $a_{b_r}>b_r\ge r\ge10^m$ has more than $m$ digits, and for large $m$ these terms begin after position $M$.

If $r$ is azure, then $b_r$ is a $\beta$-term and $a_{b_r}$ is an $\alpha$-term; by (P2) each of them leaves one of at most $p$ remainders modulo $10^m$, so by (2) the difference $q_r=a_{b_r}-b_r$ leaves one of at most $p^2$ remainders modulo $10^m$. By (3), $q_r$ lies in the interval $[10^m,\ 2\cdot10^m+c(m+1)]$, which (for large $m$) has length less than $2\cdot10^m$ and so contains at most $2$ integers in each residue class modulo $10^m$. Hence $q_r$ takes at most $2p^2$ values, and for each value $q$ the index $r$ lies in $[q-c(m+1),\,q]$ by (3). So the number of azure $r$ in $[10^m,2\cdot10^m)$ is at most $2p^2\,(c(m+1)+1)$.

On the other hand, by (1) this interval contains at least $10^m-2\lambda(2\cdot10^m)=10^m-2M-2p(m+2)$ azure numbers. For large $m$, \[10^m-2M-2p(m+2)>2p^2\bigl(c(m+1)+1\bigr),\] a contradiction. Therefore $\alpha$ and $\beta$ cannot both be rational.

Solution 2 (Late blocks are truncations of finitely many numbers)

No. Suppose both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are rational. For $x\ge0$ let $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ be the numbers of azure, resp. beige, integers in $[1,x]$; so $A(x)+B(x)=x$ for integers $x$. The decimal of $\alpha$ is the concatenation of the $\alpha$-terms $a_{b_1}<a_{b_2}<\cdots$, and that of $\beta$ is the concatenation of the $\beta$-terms $b_{a_1}<b_{a_2}<\cdots$.

Step 1: few terms of each length. A rational number has an eventually periodic decimal expansion. Choose $M$ and $p$ such that the digit sequences of both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ satisfy $\text{digit}_{t+p}=\text{digit}_t$ for all positions $t\ge M$. Call a term late if it begins at a position $\ge M$. If two late terms of the same decimal with the same number $L$ of digits begin at positions $t<t'$ with $t\equiv t'\pmod p$, they are the same string of $L$ digits, hence equal; but the terms are distinct. So at most $p$ late terms of each decimal have $L$ digits, and at most $M$ terms begin before position $M$. Consequently the number of $\alpha$-terms (and of $\beta$-terms) that are $\le x$ is at most \[\lambda(x):=M+p\,(\log_{10}x+1)\qquad(x\ge1).\]

Step 2: one colour is very rare. The $\alpha$-terms $\le x$ are the $a_i\le x$ with $i$ beige, that is, the $a_i$ with $i$ beige and $i\le A(x)$; there are $B(A(x))$ of them. Similarly there are $A(B(x))$ $\beta$-terms $\le x$. So \[B\bigl(A(x)\bigr)\le\lambda(x),\qquad A\bigl(B(x)\bigr)\le\lambda(x).\] Let $z=\min(A(x),B(x))$. If $z=B(x)$, then $B(z)\le B(A(x))\le\lambda(x)$ and $A(z)=A(B(x))\le\lambda(x)$; if $z=A(x)$, then $A(z)\le A(B(x))\le\lambda(x)$ and $B(z)=B(A(x))\le\lambda(x)$. In both cases $z=A(z)+B(z)\le2\lambda(x)$. For large $x$ we have $4\lambda(x+1)+1<x$; then $A(x)$, $B(x)$ are not both $\le2\lambda(x)$, and the smaller of the two cannot switch from $A$ to $B$ between $x$ and $x+1$ (that would give $x+1=A(x+1)+B(x+1)\le A(x)+1+B(x+1)\le4\lambda(x+1)+1$), nor from $B$ to $A$. Exchanging the colours exchanges $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so we may assume that \[B(x)\le2\lambda(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\ge x_0 .\tag{1}\] In particular there are infinitely many azure numbers.

Step 3: an $\alpha$-term close to a $\beta$-term. Let $j$ be azure. Then $y=b_j$ is a $\beta$-term (its index $j$ is azure), and $x=a_{b_j}$ is an $\alpha$-term (its index $b_j$ is beige). Since $x$ is the $b_j$-th azure number, $A(x)=b_j=y$, hence \[x-y=x-A(x)=B(x).\] As $x$ is azure and $y$ is beige, $x\ne y$, so $x>y$. As $j\to\infty$ through azure values, $y=b_j\to\infty$; and only finitely many terms are not late, so for all large azure $j$ both $x$ and $y$ are late terms, $x\ge x_0$, and by (1) \[0<x-y\le2\lambda(x).\tag{2}\]

Step 4: late terms are truncations. For each position $t\ge M$ of $\alpha$ or of $\beta$ whose digit $d_t$ is nonzero, consider the number \[D_t=d_t+\frac{d_{t+1}}{10}+\frac{d_{t+2}}{10^2}+\cdots\in[1,10].\] The tail $d_td_{t+1}\ldots$ depends only on $t$ modulo $p$ and on the decimal, so these numbers form a finite set $\mathcal D$. If a late term $N$ with $L$ digits begins at position $t$, then $d_t\ne0$ and $N$ is the integer with digits $d_t\ldots d_{t+L-1}$, so \[10^{L-1}D_t-N=0.d_{t+L}d_{t+L+1}\ldots\in[0,1].\tag{3}\] Each tail $d_td_{t+1}\ldots$ ($t\ge M$, $d_t\neq0$) contains the nonzero digit $d_t$ again every $p$ places, so it does not end in zeros. Two different digit strings with the same value end one in zeros and the other in nines; hence different tails give different numbers in $\mathcal D$. Also every $D_t>1$: $D_t=1$ would force $d_t=1$ and all later digits $0$. Let $\delta>0$ be the smallest of the numbers $D-1$ ($D\in\mathcal D$) and $|D-D'|$ ($D\ne D'$ in $\mathcal D$).

Claim: if $x>y$ are late terms (of $\alpha$ or of $\beta$) and $x$ has $L$ digits, then $x-y\ge\delta\,10^{L-1}-1$.

  • If $y$ also has $L$ digits, let $D_x,D_y\in\mathcal D$ belong to the positions where $x$ and $y$ begin. If $D_x=D_y$, the two tails are equal, so their first $L$ digits agree and $x=y$, which is false. So $|D_x-D_y|\ge\delta$, and by (3), $x-y=|x-y|\ge10^{L-1}|D_x-D_y|-1\ge\delta\,10^{L-1}-1$.

  • If $y$ has fewer digits, then $y\le10^{L-1}-1$, while $x\ge10^{L-1}D_x-1$ by (3); so $x-y\ge10^{L-1}(D_x-1)\ge\delta\,10^{L-1}$.

Step 5: the contradiction. Take a large azure $j$ and the pair $x>y$ of Step 3; let $x$ have $L$ digits, so $x<10^L$ and $L\to\infty$ as $j\to\infty$. By the Claim and (2), \[\delta\,10^{L-1}-1\le x-y\le2\lambda(x)<2M+2p\,(L+1).\] The left side grows exponentially in $L$ and the right side only linearly, so this fails for large $L$. Therefore $\alpha$ and $\beta$ cannot both be rational.

Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities

At the International Mouse Olympiad, $2026$ teams of $2026$ mice each are to occupy the cells of a $2026 \times 2026$ grid, with one mouse per cell. To discourage collaboration within teams, the organizers wish to maximize the team distance: the sum of the taxicab distances over all unordered pairs of mice on the same team. What is the maximum possible team distance?

Note. If two mice occupy cells $(r_1, c_1)$ and $(r_2, c_2)$, their taxicab distance is $|r_1-r_2|+|c_1-c_2|$.

In this sample $4 \times 4$ arrangement, each colour represents a team of four. The red mice $A$ and $B$ contribute $|1-4|+|1-4| = 6$ to the team distance. Summing over all same-colour pairs gives $22+17+20+17 = 76$.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{2026^2(2026^2-1)}{3}=5\,616\,120\,320\,100$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting pairs across each grid line)

Let $n=2026$. The taxicab distance is the sum of the row distance $|r_1-r_2|$ and the column distance $|c_1-c_2|$, so the team distance is $R+K$, where $R$ is the sum of the row distances and $K$ the sum of the column distances over all same-team pairs. We bound $R$; the bound for $K$ is the same.

Bound for $R$. For $k=1,\dots,n-1$ consider the horizontal grid line between rows $k$ and $k+1$. The row distance of two mice is the number of these lines that separate them. So $R=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}R_k$, where $R_k$ is the number of same-team pairs separated by the $k$th line. If team $t$ has $u_t$ mice in rows $1,\dots,k$ (hence $n-u_t$ below), then \[R_k=\sum_{t=1}^{n}u_t(n-u_t),\qquad \sum_{t=1}^{n}u_t=kn\] (the first $k$ rows contain $kn$ mice). Since $u(n-u)=k(n-k)+(n-2k)(u-k)-(u-k)^2$, \[R_k=n\,k(n-k)+(n-2k)\sum_t(u_t-k)-\sum_t(u_t-k)^2=n\,k(n-k)-\sum_t(u_t-k)^2\le n\,k(n-k),\] with equality iff every team has exactly $k$ mice in the first $k$ rows. Therefore \[R\le n\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)=n\cdot\frac{(n-1)n(n+1)}{6}=\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}{6},\] using $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)=n\cdot\frac{n(n-1)}2-\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}=\frac{(n-1)n(n+1)}6$. Equality holds iff every team has exactly $k$ mice in the first $k$ rows for every $k$, i.e. exactly one mouse in each row. Likewise $K\le\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}6$, with equality iff every team has one mouse in each column.

Both bounds are attained together. Put the mouse in row $r$, column $c$ into team $(c-r)\bmod n$ (a Latin square). Each team has exactly one mouse in every row and in every column, so $R=K=\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}6$.

The maximum team distance is $\dfrac{n^2(n^2-1)}{3}$, which for $n=2026$ is \[\frac{2026^2\,(2026^2-1)}{3}=4\,104\,676\cdot1\,368\,225=\boxed{5\,616\,120\,320\,100}\] (For $n=4$ the maximum is $80$; the sample arrangement gives $76$.)

Solution 2 (Sorting each team and the rearrangement inequality)

Let $n=2026$. The taxicab distance is the row distance plus the column distance, so the team distance is $R+K$, where $R$ (resp. $K$) is the sum of the row (resp. column) distances $|r_1-r_2|$ (resp. $|c_1-c_2|$) over all pairs of mice of the same team. We bound $R$; the bound for $K$ is the same with rows and columns exchanged. Then we show that both bounds are attained by one arrangement.

One team. Let the rows of the mice of one team be $r_1\le r_2\le\dots\le r_n$. In $\sum_{i<j}(r_j-r_i)$ the number $r_i$ appears with $+$ sign $i-1$ times and with $-$ sign $n-i$ times, so \[\sum_{i<j}(r_j-r_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2i-n-1)\,r_i .\]

All teams. Summing over the $n$ teams, $R$ is a sum of $n^2$ products: each mouse's row number is multiplied by a coefficient $2i-n-1$, where $i$ is its rank inside its team. The multiset of row numbers consists of each of $1,\dots,n$ exactly $n$ times (each row holds $n$ mice), and the multiset of coefficients consists of each of the numbers $2i-n-1$ ($i=1,\dots,n$) exactly $n$ times (once per team). By the rearrangement inequality, a sum of products of two fixed multisets is largest when both are sorted in the same order, i.e. when the row number $k$ is always paired with the coefficient $2k-n-1$: \[R\le n\sum_{k=1}^{n}k(2k-n-1)=n\Bigl(\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{3}-\frac{n(n+1)^2}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{n^2(n+1)(n-1)}{6}.\] The same bound holds for the columns, so the team distance is at most $\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}3$.

Equality. If every team has exactly one mouse in each row and one in each column, then in each team $r_i=i$, so equality holds for rows, and similarly for columns. Such an arrangement exists: give the cell in row $r$ and column $c$ to team $(r+c)\bmod n$.

Hence the maximum is $\dfrac{2026^2(2026^2-1)}{3}=\boxed{5\,616\,120\,320\,100}$

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Let $\mathbb{N}$ be the set of positive integers. Find all functions $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ such that \[f^{f(n)+m}(n) = n + f(n+m)\] holds for all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$. Here, $f^k(n)$ denotes $\underbrace{f(f(\cdots f(n)\cdots))}_{k \text{ times}}$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=n+1$ for all $n$

Show the solution

Solution (First far out, then everywhere)

Answer: $f(n)=n+1$. It works: $f^k(n)=n+k$, so the left side is $n+f(n)+m=2n+m+1$ and the right side is $n+(n+m+1)$.

Conversely, let $f$ satisfy the equation, written $E(n,m)$.

Step 1: a simpler relation. Apply $f$ to both sides of $E(n,m)$ and compare with $E(n,m+1)$: $f\bigl(n+f(n+m)\bigr)=f^{f(n)+m+1}(n)=n+f(n+m+1)$. With $k=n+m$ this reads \[f\bigl(n+f(k)\bigr)=n+f(k+1)\qquad\text{for all }k\ge2,\ 1\le n\le k-1.\tag{1}\]

Step 2: $f(t)=t+1$ for all large $t$.

(a) If $j\ge2$ and $f(j)=j+1$, $f(j+1)=j+2$, then $f(t)=t+1$ for $j+2\le t\le 2j$. This is (1) with $k=j$: $f(n+j+1)=n+j+2$ for $1\le n\le j-1$.

(b) There are two consecutive integers $j,j+1$ with $j\ge3$, $f(j)=j+1$, $f(j+1)=j+2$. First, $f(k)\ge2$ for some $k\ge3$: otherwise $f(3)=f(4)=1$ and (1) with $k=3$, $n=2$ gives $f(3)=2+f(4)=3$. Fix such $k$ and put $u=f(k)\ge2$, $v=f(k+1)$. By (1), $f(u+n)=v+n$ for $1\le n\le k-1$; in particular $f(u+1)=v+1$ and $f(u+2)=v+2$. Now apply (1) with $k$ replaced by $u+1\ (\ge3)$ and $n=1,2$: \[f\bigl(n+f(u+1)\bigr)=n+f(u+2),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad f(v+2)=v+3,\ \ f(v+3)=v+4 .\] So $j=v+2\ge3$ works.

(c) Let $[L,U]$ be an interval of integers with $U\ge L+1$, $L\ge3$, on which $f(t)=t+1$ (by (b), $[j,j+1]$ is one). Applying (a) to $j=L,L+1,\dots,U-1$ gives $f(t)=t+1$ on the intervals $[j+2,2j]$; consecutive ones overlap or touch ($j+3\le 2j+1$), so $f(t)=t+1$ on $[L+2,2U-2]$, hence on $[L,2U-2]$. Since $U\ge4$, $2U-2>U$: the interval can be enlarged again and again. Therefore \[f(t)=t+1\qquad\text{for all }t\ge L.\tag{2}\]

Step 3: every orbit reaches $[L,\infty)$. Fix $n$. If $f^i(n)<L$ for all $i\ge0$, the left side of $E(n,m)$ is less than $L$ for all $m$, while the right side is $n+f(n+m)=2n+m+1$ for $n+m\ge L$, which is unbounded. So there is a least $s=s(n)\ge0$ with $f^s(n)\ge L$, and by (2) $f^{i}(n)=f^s(n)+(i-s)$ for all $i\ge s$. Define \[e(n)=f^i(n)-i\quad(\text{any }i\ge s(n));\] this does not depend on $i$. For large $m$, $E(n,m)$ becomes $e(n)+f(n)+m=2n+m+1$, so \[f(n)=2n+1-e(n)\qquad\text{for all }n.\tag{3}\] Moreover $e(f(n))=f^{i}(f(n))-i=f^{i+1}(n)-(i+1)+1=e(n)+1$.

Step 4: conclusion. Fix $n$ and let $u_i=f^i(n)$, so $e(u_i)=e(n)+i$. By (3), $u_{i+1}=2u_i+1-e(n)-i$. For the differences $d_i=u_{i+1}-u_i=u_i+1-e(n)-i$ this gives $d_{i+1}-d_i=d_i-1$, i.e. \[d_{i+1}-1=2\,(d_i-1),\qquad\text{so}\qquad d_i-1=2^i(d_0-1).\] For $i\ge s(n)$ we have $u_i\ge L$ and $d_i=1$ by (2). Hence $2^i(d_0-1)=0$, $d_0=1$, that is $f(n)=n+1$.

So $f(n)=n+1$ for every $n$; the answer is $\boxed{f(n)=n+1}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $A_1A_2A_3A_4A_5A_6$ be a convex hexagon such that the distance from $A_i$ to the line $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$ is the same for all $1 \le i \le 6$, where indices are taken modulo $6$. For each $k \in \{1, 2\}$, let $I_k$ and $G_k$ denote the incenter and centroid of triangle $A_kA_{k+2}A_{k+4}$, respectively. Suppose that $I_1 \ne I_2$ and $G_1 \ne G_2$.

Prove that the lines $I_1I_2$ and $G_1G_2$ are either parallel or coincident.

Show the solution

Solution (The two triangles have the same perimeter and the same perimeter centroid)

Indices are taken modulo $6$, the hexagon is labelled counterclockwise, and $h$ is the common distance. For vectors $u,v$ write $[u,v]=u_xv_y-u_yv_x$; thus $[u,v]>0$ when $v$ is to the left of $u$. Let $w_i$ be the unit vector in the direction $\overrightarrow{A_{i-1}A_{i+1}}$ and $E_i=\overrightarrow{A_iA_{i+1}}$. We shall prove \[I_1-I_2=3\,(G_1-G_2),\] which gives the statement, since both vectors are non-zero.

Step 1: each side bisects the angle between two diagonals. The hexagon is convex, so $A_i$ lies on the right of the directed line $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$, at distance $h$; hence $[w_i,\overrightarrow{A_{i-1}A_i}]=-h$ and $[w_i,\overrightarrow{A_{i}A_{i+1}}]=h$. Applying the first relation to the index $i+1$, \[[w_i,E_i]=h,\qquad[w_{i+1},E_i]=-h,\qquad\text{so}\qquad[w_i+w_{i+1},E_i]=0 .\] In the convex quadrilateral $A_{i-1}A_iA_{i+1}A_{i+2}$ the diagonals satisfy $[w_i,w_{i+1}]>0$. So $E_i=t\,(w_i+w_{i+1})$ with $t\,[w_i,w_{i+1}]=h$, hence $t>0$: the side $A_iA_{i+1}$ points along the bisector of the angle between $w_i$ and $w_{i+1}$. Let $2\theta_i$ be this angle ($0<\theta_i<90^\circ$) and $u_i$ the unit vector along $E_i$. Then \[\angle A_{i-1}A_{i+1}A_i=\angle A_{i+2}A_iA_{i+1}=\theta_i,\qquad w_i+w_{i+1}=2\cos\theta_i\,u_i .\tag{1}\]

Step 2: feet of the perpendiculars. Let $F_i$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A_i$ to $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$; $A_iF_i=h$. In triangle $A_{i-1}A_iA_{i+1}$ the angles at $A_{i-1}$ and $A_{i+1}$ are $\theta_{i-1}$ and $\theta_i$ by (1), both acute, so $F_i$ lies inside the segment $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$ and \[A_{i-1}F_i=h\cot\theta_{i-1},\qquad F_iA_{i+1}=h\cot\theta_i,\qquad A_iA_{i+1}=\frac{h}{\sin\theta_i}.\tag{2}\] Thus each side $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$ of one of the two triangles is cut by $F_i$ into the pieces $[A_{i-1},F_i]$ and $[F_i,A_{i+1}]$.

Equal perimeters. By (2), $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}=h(\cot\theta_{i-1}+\cot\theta_i)$. The triangle $A_1A_3A_5$ has the sides with $i=2,4,6$ and $A_2A_4A_6$ those with $i=1,3,5$; in both cases the perimeter is \[p=h\,(\cot\theta_1+\cot\theta_2+\dots+\cot\theta_6).\]

Step 3: a formula for $3G-I$. For a triangle $ABC$ with sides $a,b,c$, perimeter $p$, centroid $G$ and incenter $I=\frac{aA+bB+cC}{p}$ (position vectors), \[a\cdot\frac{B+C}2+b\cdot\frac{C+A}2+c\cdot\frac{A+B}2=\frac{(p-a)A+(p-b)B+(p-c)C}{2}=\frac p2\,(3G-I).\] The left side is the sum over the sides of (length)$\times$(midpoint). For a segment cut into two pieces, (length)$\times$(midpoint) is the sum of the same quantities for the pieces (for $XZ$ cut at $Y$: $|XY|\frac{X+Y}2+|YZ|\frac{Y+Z}2=|XZ|\frac{X+Z}{2}$, as $Y=\frac{|YZ|X+|XY|Z}{|XZ|}$). Let $\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2$ be these sums for the triangles $A_1A_3A_5$ and $A_2A_4A_6$, computed with the twelve pieces of Step 2. Since the perimeters are equal, it suffices to prove $\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2$: then $3G_1-I_1=3G_2-I_2$.

Step 4: $\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2$. Group the twelve pieces in pairs: for each $i$, the piece $\sigma_i=[F_i,A_{i+1}]$ (on the side $A_{i-1}A_{i+1}$) and the piece $\sigma_i'=[A_i,F_{i+1}]$ (on the side $A_iA_{i+2}$). Both have length $h\cot\theta_i$ by (2). For even $i$, $\sigma_i$ belongs to the triangle $A_1A_3A_5$ and $\sigma_i'$ to $A_2A_4A_6$; for odd $i$ it is the other way round. Hence \[\Sigma_1-\Sigma_2=\sum_{i=1}^{6}(-1)^i\,h\cot\theta_i\cdot\frac{(F_i+A_{i+1})-(A_i+F_{i+1})}{2}.\] The right triangles $A_iF_iA_{i+1}$ and $A_{i+1}F_{i+1}A_i$ have the common hypotenuse $A_iA_{i+1}$, the legs $A_iF_i=A_{i+1}F_{i+1}=h$, and the angle $\theta_i$ at $A_{i+1}$, resp. $A_i$; both lie on the inner side of $A_iA_{i+1}$. So $A_iF_iF_{i+1}A_{i+1}$ is an isosceles trapezoid (or a rectangle) with $F_iF_{i+1}\parallel A_iA_{i+1}$, and the projections of its legs on $A_iA_{i+1}$ have length $h\sin\theta_i$ each. Therefore \[(F_i+A_{i+1})-(A_i+F_{i+1})=\overrightarrow{A_iA_{i+1}}-\overrightarrow{F_iF_{i+1}}=2h\sin\theta_i\,u_i ,\] and, using (1), \[\Sigma_1-\Sigma_2=h^2\sum_{i=1}^{6}(-1)^i\cos\theta_i\,u_i=\frac{h^2}{2}\sum_{i=1}^6(-1)^i\,(w_i+w_{i+1})=0,\] because each $w_j$ occurs twice, with opposite signs.

Conclusion. $3G_1-I_1=3G_2-I_2$, i.e. $I_1-I_2=3(G_1-G_2)$. As $I_1\neq I_2$ and $G_1\ne G_2$, the lines $I_1I_2$ and $G_1G_2$ have the same direction: they are parallel or coincide.

Remark. The point $\frac{3G-I}{2}$ is the Spieker center (the centroid of the boundary of the triangle). So the two triangles have equal perimeters and the same Spieker center; from $2\cdot\text{area(hexagon)}=(r_1+h)p=(r_2+h)p$ they also have equal inradii and equal areas.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure

Does there exist a geometric progression of positive integers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2026}$ such that the sequence of their digit sums $S(a_1), S(a_2), \ldots, S(a_{2026})$ forms an arithmetic progression with a non-zero common difference?

Here, $S(x)$ denotes the sum of digits of $x$ in its decimal representation.

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Odd powers of a number made of nines)

Yes. Let $k=2026$, $B=10^k$, and \[a_m=(B-1)^{2m-1}\qquad(m=1,2,\dots,2026).\] This is a geometric progression of positive integers with ratio $(B-1)^2$. We show that $S(a_m)=9k\,m$, an arithmetic progression with difference $9k\ne0$.

Two facts about digit sums. (i) If $0\le c_j<B$, then $S\bigl(\sum_jc_jB^j\bigr)=\sum_jS(c_j)$: the decimal representation is obtained by writing the $c_j$ one after another, each with $k$ digits (padded by zeros). (ii) If $1\le c\le B$, then $S(B-c)=9k-S(c-1)$: indeed $B-c=(B-1)-(c-1)$, and subtracting $c-1$ (which has at most $k$ digits) from the number $B-1$ consisting of $k$ nines needs no borrowing, so each digit $d$ of $c-1$ is replaced by $9-d$.

The computation. Let $i=2m-1\le4051$. All binomial coefficients satisfy $\binom ij\le2^i<2^{4052}=16^{1013}<100^{1013}=B$. By the binomial theorem, as $i$ is odd, \[(B-1)^i=\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-j}\binom ijB^j=\sum_{\substack{1\le j\le i\\ j\ \text{odd}}}\Bigl(\binom ijB^j-\binom i{j-1}B^{j-1}\Bigr).\] Each bracket equals \[\Bigl(\binom ij-1\Bigr)B^{j}+\Bigl(B-\binom i{j-1}\Bigr)B^{j-1},\] and both coefficients lie in $[0,B-1]$. So this is the representation of $(B-1)^i$ in base $B$, and by (i) and (ii) \[S\bigl((B-1)^i\bigr)=\sum_{j\ \text{odd}}\Bigl[S\Bigl(\binom ij-1\Bigr)+9k-S\Bigl(\binom i{j-1}-1\Bigr)\Bigr].\] When $j$ runs through the odd numbers in $[1,i]$, so does $j'=i-j+1$ (as $i$ is odd), and $\binom i{j-1}=\binom i{i-j+1}=\binom i{j'}$. Hence the two sums $\sum_{j\text{ odd}}S\bigl(\binom ij-1\bigr)$ and $\sum_{j\text{ odd}}S\bigl(\binom i{j-1}-1\bigr)$ are equal and cancel. There are $\frac{i+1}2=m$ odd values of $j$, so \[S(a_m)=S\bigl((B-1)^{2m-1}\bigr)=9k\cdot m .\] Thus $S(a_1),\dots,S(a_{2026})$ is the arithmetic progression $9k,18k,\dots,2026\cdot9k$ with common difference $9k=18234$.

Example with $k=2$: $99$, $99^3=970299$, $99^5=9509900499$ have digit sums $18,36,54$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $\mathcal{P} = P_1P_2\cdots P_{2026}$ and $\mathcal{Q} = Q_1Q_2\cdots Q_{2026}$ be convex polygons such that every vertex of $\mathcal{Q}$ lies strictly in the interior of $\mathcal{P}$. Suppose that $Q_iQ_{i+1} \parallel P_iP_{i+1}$ for every $1 \le i \le 2026$, with subscripts taken modulo $2026$.

Find the maximum number of indices $1 \le i \le 2026$ with $Q_iQ_{i+1} > P_iP_{i+1}$.

Show the answer

$2023$

Show the solution

Solution (Widths for the bound, a truncated triangle for the example)

Let $n=2026$. We prove that the maximum is $n-3$. Polygons are convex in the strict sense (all interior angles less than $180^\circ$). Write $p_i=\overrightarrow{P_iP_{i+1}}$ and $q_i=\overrightarrow{Q_iQ_{i+1}}$, so $\sum p_i=\sum q_i=0$ and $q_i=\mu_ip_i$ with real $\mu_i\neq0$; put $\lambda_i=|\mu_i|=\frac{Q_iQ_{i+1}}{P_iP_{i+1}}$.

Step 1: all $\mu_i$ have the same sign. Going round $\mathcal P$, the direction of $p_i$ turns at each vertex by the exterior angle; say these turns are counterclockwise, by angles $\theta_i\in(0,\pi)$ with $\sum\theta_i=2\pi$. For $\mathcal Q$ the turns $\varphi_i$ from $q_i$ to $q_{i+1}$ are either all in $(0,\pi)$ or all in $(-\pi,0)$, with sum $\pm2\pi$. Since $q_i\parallel p_i$, $\varphi_i\equiv\theta_i\pmod\pi$. If all $\varphi_i<0$, then $\varphi_i=\theta_i-\pi$ and summing gives $-2\pi=2\pi-n\pi$, i.e. $n=4$, which is false. So $\varphi_i=\theta_i$ for all $i$: the turn from $q_i$ to $q_{i+1}$ equals the turn from $p_i$ to $p_{i+1}$, hence $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{i+1}$ have the same sign. Consequently (multiplying $\sum\mu_ip_i=0$ by $\pm1$) \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_ip_i=0,\qquad\text{and so}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^n(\lambda_i-1)\,p_i=0.\tag{1}\]

Step 2: widths. For a unit vector $u$, the width $w_u(\mathcal P)$ of $\mathcal P$ in direction $u$ is the length of its projection to a line parallel to $u$. Going once round the polygon, the projection of the moving point covers this segment exactly twice, so \[2\,w_u(\mathcal P)=\sum_i|p_i\cdot u|,\qquad 2\,w_u(\mathcal Q)=\sum_i\lambda_i|p_i\cdot u| .\] As $\mathcal Q$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal P$, $w_u(\mathcal Q)<w_u(\mathcal P)$ for every $u$: \[\sum_{i=1}^n(\lambda_i-1)\,|p_i\cdot u|<0\qquad\text{for every unit vector }u.\tag{2}\]

Step 3: at least three indices have $\lambda_i\le1$. Suppose $\lambda_i>1$ for all $i$ except at most two indices $a,b$. Choose a unit vector $u\perp p_a$. Taking the scalar product of (1) with $u$, the term $i=a$ vanishes: \[(1-\lambda_b)\,(p_b\cdot u)=\sum_{i\ne a,b}(\lambda_i-1)\,(p_i\cdot u),\qquad\text{so}\qquad(1-\lambda_b)\,|p_b\cdot u|\le\sum_{i\ne a,b}(\lambda_i-1)\,|p_i\cdot u| ,\] because $\lambda_i-1>0$ for $i\neq a,b$. Hence \[\sum_{i=1}^n(\lambda_i-1)\,|p_i\cdot u|=\sum_{i\ne a,b}(\lambda_i-1)|p_i\cdot u|-(1-\lambda_b)|p_b\cdot u|\ \ge0,\] contradicting (2). (If fewer than two indices are exceptional, take any other indices for $a$, $b$.) Therefore $Q_iQ_{i+1}\le P_iP_{i+1}$ for at least three indices, and at most $n-3$ indices have $Q_iQ_{i+1}>P_iP_{i+1}$.

Step 4: an example with $n-3$. Let $\mathcal Q$ be a regular $n$-gon with center $O$ (the origin), side $s$ and inradius $\rho$, vertices in counterclockwise order, and let $\nu_i$ be the outward unit normal of the side $Q_iQ_{i+1}$. Thus \[Q_i\cdot\nu_i=Q_{i+1}\cdot\nu_i=\rho,\qquad Q_k\cdot\nu_i\le\rho\ \text{ for all }k.\] Choose three indices $a<b<c$ whose normals do not lie in a half-plane (for instance $a=1$, $b=676$, $c=1351$: consecutive normals differ by $\frac{360^\circ}{2026}$, so these three are about $120^\circ$ apart). Let $\Delta$ be the triangle bounded by the lines $z\cdot\nu_a=1$, $z\cdot\nu_b=1$, $z\cdot\nu_c=1$, and let $V_{ab},V_{bc},V_{ca}$ be its vertices ($V_{ab}$ on the first two lines, and so on). Define \[W_i=V_{ab}\ (a<i\le b),\qquad W_i=V_{bc}\ (b<i\le c),\qquad W_i=V_{ca}\ (\text{otherwise}),\] and, with $T=\rho$, \[P_i=\tfrac12Q_i+T\,W_i\qquad(i=1,\dots,n).\] Sides. $p_i=\frac12q_i+T(W_{i+1}-W_i)$. For $i\notin\{a,b,c\}$, $W_{i+1}=W_i$ and $p_i=\frac12q_i$. For $i=a$, $W_{a+1}-W_a=V_{ab}-V_{ca}$ is the side of $\Delta$ on the line $z\cdot\nu_a=1$, a positive multiple of $q_a$ (both are obtained from $\nu_a$ by a counterclockwise quarter turn); similarly for $b,c$. So every $p_i$ is a positive multiple of $q_i$: $\mathcal P=P_1\cdots P_n$ is a convex polygon with $P_iP_{i+1}\parallel Q_iQ_{i+1}$, and $P_iP_{i+1}=\frac12Q_iQ_{i+1}<Q_iQ_{i+1}$ for the $n-3$ indices $i\notin\{a,b,c\}$.

$\mathcal Q$ is strictly inside $\mathcal P$. The convex polygon $\mathcal P$ is the intersection of the half-planes $z\cdot\nu_i\le P_i\cdot\nu_i$. We claim $W_i\cdot\nu_i\ge1$. For $a<i\le b$, the direction of $\nu_i$ lies in the angle (less than $180^\circ$) between $\nu_a$ and $\nu_b$, so $\nu_i=x\nu_a+y\nu_b$ with $x,y\ge0$, and $x+y\ge|\nu_i|=1$; hence $W_i\cdot\nu_i=V_{ab}\cdot\nu_i=x+y\ge1$. The other ranges are analogous. Therefore, for every vertex $Q_k$ and every $i$, \[P_i\cdot\nu_i=\tfrac12\rho+T\,W_i\cdot\nu_i\ \ge\ \tfrac12\rho+\rho>\rho\ \ge Q_k\cdot\nu_i .\] So each vertex of $\mathcal Q$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal P$.

Hence the maximum number of indices is $n-3=\boxed{2023}$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Fix an odd integer $k > 2026$. Yugi has a Yu-Gi-Oh! deck containing an odd number $n$ of cards, each of which is one of two types: a Monster Card or a Trap Card.

On each move, Yugi chooses $k$ distinct cards and uses the Millennium Puzzle to determine whether there are more Monster Cards or Trap Cards among those $k$ cards. Cards may be chosen again on later moves, and his choices may depend on the answers he obtained on the previous moves.

Prove that, for all sufficiently large $n$, Yugi can determine which type is more numerous in the entire deck using fewer than $\frac{n}{100}$ moves.

Note. Results with $\frac{1}{100}$ replaced by a larger constant $c_k < 1$, which may depend on $k$, may receive partial credit depending on the value of $c_k$.

Show the solution

Solution (Cancel balanced sets found by binary search)

Write $M$ for Monster and $T$ for Trap, and let $\ell=\lceil\log_2k\rceil$. We describe a strategy that uses at most \[\frac{(2+\ell)\,n}{k}+k^2+k\] moves. Since $k\ge2027$, we have $\frac{2+\ell}{k}\le\frac{3+\log_2k}{k}<\frac1{100}$ (the function $k-100(3+\log_2k)$ is increasing for $k\ge145$ and is positive at $k=2027$, where $100(3+\log_2 2027)<1400$). So the number of moves is less than $\frac n{100}$ for all sufficiently large $n$.

The basic tool. Suppose two disjoint sets of $k$ cards are known, $X=\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$ with answer $M$ and $Z=\{z_1,\dots,z_k\}$ with answer $T$. For $0\le i\le k$ let \[A_i=\{z_1,\dots,z_i\}\cup\{x_{i+1},\dots,x_k\},\] so $A_0=X$ and $A_k=Z$. By binary search (keep indices $lo<hi$ with answer $M$ for $A_{lo}$ and $T$ for $A_{hi}$, and ask about the middle one) at most $\ell$ moves give an index $i$ such that $A_i$ has answer $M$ and $A_{i+1}$ has answer $T$. These sets share the $k-1$ cards of $C=\{z_1,\dots,z_i\}\cup\{x_{i+2},\dots,x_k\}$; $A_i=C\cup\{x_{i+1}\}$ has at least $\frac{k+1}2$ Monsters and $A_{i+1}=C\cup\{z_{i+1}\}$ has at most $\frac{k-1}2$. The two numbers differ by at most $1$, hence: \[x_{i+1}\text{ is }M,\quad z_{i+1}\text{ is }T,\quad C\text{ contains exactly }\tfrac{k-1}2\text{ cards of each type}.\] So the $k+1$ cards of $C\cup\{x_{i+1},z_{i+1}\}$ contain equally many $M$ and $T$. We call $C$ a balanced set; note that for any card $y\notin C$ the answer for $C\cup\{y\}$ is the type of $y$.

The strategy. Yugi keeps a set of live cards (at the start all $n$ cards), split into a pool $U$ of cards not in a live block, and two lists $\mathcal M$, $\mathcal T$ of pairwise disjoint blocks of $k$ live cards whose answers are known to be $M$, resp. $T$. He repeats:

  • (a) If $\mathcal M$ and $\mathcal T$ are both non-empty, take a block $X$ from $\mathcal M$ and a block $Z$ from $\mathcal T$ and apply the basic tool. The $k+1$ cards of $C\cup\{x_{i+1},z_{i+1}\}$ are removed from the live cards; the remaining $k-1$ cards of $X\cup Z$ go back to the pool $U$.

  • (b) Otherwise, if $|U|\ge k$, take $k$ cards of $U$, ask about them, and put this block into $\mathcal M$ or $\mathcal T$ according to the answer.

  • (c) Otherwise stop.

Each step (a) removes equally many $M$ and $T$ cards, so the difference (number of live $M$) $-$ (number of live $T$) never changes: the more numerous type among the live cards is always the more numerous type of the deck. The number of live cards stays odd.

The end. When Yugi stops, one of the lists is empty, say $\mathcal T$ (the other case is symmetric), and $|U|\le k-1$. The live cards are the blocks of $\mathcal M$ and the cards of $U$.

  • If $|\mathcal M|\ge k$: each block of $\mathcal M$ has at least $\frac{k+1}2$ Monsters, so the live cards contain at least $|\mathcal M|\frac{k+1}{2}>\frac{|\mathcal M|k+|U|}{2}$ Monsters (because $|\mathcal M|\ge k>|U|$). Monsters are in the majority.

  • If $|\mathcal M|<k$: fewer than $k^2+k$ cards are live. If $n\ge k^2+k$, some step (a) has been carried out, so Yugi knows a balanced set $C$ (from the first step (a); its cards are no longer live). For each live card $y$ he asks about $C\cup\{y\}$ and learns the type of $y$. After fewer than $k^2+k$ further moves he knows all live cards and hence the majority.

Counting the moves. Let $\alpha$ be the number of steps (a) and $\gamma$ the number of blocks alive at the end. Each step (a) costs at most $\ell$ moves and uses up two blocks; each block was created by one move in a step (b). So before the final phase Yugi makes at most $\ell\alpha+(2\alpha+\gamma)$ moves. The $\alpha$ removed sets (of $k+1$ cards) and the $\gamma$ final blocks are pairwise disjoint, so $(k+1)\alpha+k\gamma\le n$ and $\alpha+\gamma\le\frac nk$. Hence the total number of moves is at most \[(\ell+2)\alpha+\gamma+k^2+k\le(\ell+2)(\alpha+\gamma)+k^2+k\le\frac{(\ell+2)\,n}{k}+k^2+k,\] as claimed. For $k=2027$ this is about $0.0064\,n$, and it is less than $\frac{n}{100}$ as soon as $n$ is large.
