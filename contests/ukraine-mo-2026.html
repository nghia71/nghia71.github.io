Problem 8.1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $a, b$ be arbitrary positive numbers. The following four numbers are written on the board: $ab$, $a+b$, $\frac{a}{b}$, and $\frac{b}{a}$. Prove that it is always possible to choose two of them whose ratio is greater than or equal to $2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two cases on the larger number)

The four numbers do not change if $a$ and $b$ are swapped (only $\frac ab$ and $\frac ba$ trade places), so we may assume $a\ge b$.

  • If $a\le 1$, then also $b\le1$, so $\frac1a\ge1$ and $\frac1b\ge1$, and \[\frac{a+b}{ab}=\frac1a+\frac1b\ge 2 .\]

  • If $a>1$, then, using $\frac ab\ge 1$, \[\frac{a+b}{\;b/a\;}=\frac{a(a+b)}{b}=a\Bigl(\frac ab+1\Bigr)\ge 2a>2 .\]

In both cases two of the numbers have ratio at least $2$.

Solution 2 (A product of two ratios)

We may assume $a\ge b$ (swapping $a$ and $b$ only exchanges $\frac ab$ and $\frac ba$). Consider the two ratios \[R_1=\frac{a+b}{ab},\qquad R_2=\frac{a+b}{\;b/a\;}=\frac{a(a+b)}{b}.\] Their product is \[R_1R_2=\frac{(a+b)^2}{b^2}=\Bigl(\frac ab+1\Bigr)^2\ge (1+1)^2=4,\] because $\frac ab\ge1$. Two positive numbers with product at least $4$ cannot both be smaller than $2$; hence $R_1\ge2$ or $R_2\ge2$, which is what we had to prove.

Problem 8.2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Inside triangle $ABC$, where $\angle ABC = 3\angle ACB$, a point $D$ is chosen such that $\angle DAB = \angle DAC$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. On the perpendicular from $M$ to line $BC$, choose a point $E$ lying in the same half-plane with respect to line $BC$ as point $A$, outside triangle $ABC$, such that $\angle DEM = \frac{\angle BAC}{2}$. Prove that $\angle AED = \angle ABD$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A hidden isosceles triangle and a cyclic quadrilateral)

Let $\gamma=\angle ACB$, so $\angle ABC=3\gamma$ and $\angle BAC=180^\circ-4\gamma$; let $\ell$ be the perpendicular to $BC$ at $M$ (the perpendicular bisector of $BC$) and let $L$ be the point where line $AD$ (the bisector of $\angle A$) meets $BC$.

Where things are. Since $\angle C<\angle B$, we have $AB<AC$, so $A$ lies on the same side of $\ell$ as $B$; also $BL:LC=AB:AC<1$, so $L$ lies between $B$ and $M$. Hence $\ell$ meets side $AC$ at an interior point $K$, the part of $\ell$ inside the triangle is the segment $MK$, and $E$ (on $\ell$, on $A$'s side of $BC$, outside the triangle) lies on the ray from $K$ away from $M$.

Step 1: $AB=AK$ and $\angle AKD=\angle ABD$. As $K$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, $\angle KBC=\angle KCB=\gamma$. Hence $\angle ABK=3\gamma-\gamma=2\gamma$, and $\angle AKB=2\gamma$ as an exterior angle of triangle $KBC$. So $AB=AK$, and the bisector $AD$ of the apex angle of the isosceles triangle $ABK$ is its axis of symmetry: the reflection in line $AD$ swaps $B$ and $K$ and fixes $A$ and $D$. Therefore $\angle AKD=\angle ABD$.

Step 2: $A,D,K,E$ are concyclic. Since $K$ lies between $M$ and $E$, \[\angle DEK=\angle DEM=\tfrac12\angle BAC=\angle DAC=\angle DAK .\] So $A$ and $E$ see the segment $DK$ at the same angle, and it remains to see that they are on the same side of line $DK$. Look at the rays from $K$ into the half-plane of line $AC$ that contains $B$. As a point runs along $BC$ from $C$ through $M$ to $L$, the ray from $K$ to it turns steadily away from $KC$ and never reaches the opposite ray $KA$; so these rays come in the order $KC,\ KM,\ KL,\ KA$. The ray $KD$ lies strictly between $KL$ and $KA$, because $D$ is an inner point of segment $AL$. Hence line $DK$ has $A$ on one side and $M$ on the other, and $E$, which lies on the ray from $K$ opposite to $KM$, is on $A$'s side. Therefore $A,D,K,E$ lie on one circle.

Step 3. Line $AD$ meets $\ell$ below $BC$ (both $A$ and $L$ are on $B$'s side of $\ell$), so $K$ and $E$, which are above $BC$, lie on the same side of line $AD$. In the circle through $A,D,K,E$ the angles $\angle AED$ and $\angle AKD$ therefore subtend the chord $AD$ from the same side, and by Step 1 \[\angle AED=\angle AKD=\angle ABD.\]

Solution 2 (Coordinates and the directions of two rays)

Put $\theta=\angle ACB$ and $t=\tan\theta$. Then $\angle ABC=3\theta$ and $4\theta<180^\circ$, so $0<\theta<45^\circ$ and $0<t<1$. Also $\angle BAC=180^\circ-4\theta$, so the condition on $E$ reads $\angle DEM=90^\circ-2\theta$.

Coordinates of $A$. Take $B=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and \[A=(a,h),\qquad a=\frac{1-3t^2}{4(1-t^2)},\qquad h=\frac{t(3-t^2)}{4(1-t^2)} .\] This is the right point. Indeed, $\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta=\cos^3\theta\,(1-3t^2)$ and $\sin3\theta=3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta=\cos^3\theta\,(3t-t^3)$, so $(a,h)$ is a positive multiple of $(\cos3\theta,\sin3\theta)$ and lies on the ray from $B$ at angle $3\theta$ to $BC$. Moreover $1-a=\frac{3-t^2}{4(1-t^2)}$, so $h=(1-a)t$ and $h>0$: the point lies on the ray from $C$ at angle $\theta$ to $CB$.

The bisector. Seen from $A$, the direction towards $B$ makes the angle $3\theta-180^\circ$ with the positive $x$-axis, and the direction towards $C$ makes the angle $-\theta$, because $C-A=(1-a)(1,-t)$. The internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ has the mean direction $\theta-90^\circ$, that is, the direction of the vector $(t,-1)$. So every point $(x,y)$ of the bisector satisfies \[y=h-\frac{x-a}{t},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad x+ty=a+th=\frac{1+t^2}{4}.\tag{1}\] The bisector meets $BC$ at $x_0=\frac{1+t^2}4$. Let $D=(x,y)$. It lies strictly between $A$ and $(x_0,0)$, so $x$ lies between $a$ and $x_0$. Both are less than $\frac12$: $x_0<\frac12$ as $t<1$, and $a<\frac12$ because $1-3t^2<2-2t^2$. Hence $x<\frac12$.

The point $E$. Here $M=(\frac12,0)$ and $E=(\frac12,e)$ with $e>0$. The ray $EM$ points straight down. Since $x<\frac12$, the point $D$ lies to the left of line $EM$. The angle between rays $ED$ and $EM$ is $90^\circ-2\theta<90^\circ$, so $D$ is lower than $E$, and the ray $ED$ points to the left and $2\theta$ below the horizontal: \[e-y=\Bigl(\tfrac12-x\Bigr)\tan2\theta=\Bigl(\tfrac12-x\Bigr)\frac{2t}{1-t^2}.\]

The ray $EA$. Since $a<\frac12$, the ray $EA$ also points to the left. Let $\alpha$ be its signed angle with the leftward horizontal direction (positive above, negative below), so $-90^\circ<\alpha<90^\circ$ and $\tan\alpha=\frac{h-e}{1/2-a}$. Using $h-y=\frac{x-a}t$, \[t(1-t^2)(h-e)=(x-a)(1-t^2)-(1-2x)t^2=x(1+t^2)-t^2-\frac{1-3t^2}4=\frac{(1+t^2)(4x-1)}4 .\] As $\frac12-a=\frac{1+t^2}{4(1-t^2)}$, this gives $\tan\alpha=\frac{4x-1}{t}$. Replacing $x+ty$ by $\frac{1+t^2}4$ (by $(1)$) in the first product, \[(4x-1)(x+ty)-t(tx-y)=x(1+t^2)-\frac{1+t^2}{4}-t^2x+ty=(x+ty)-\frac{1+t^2}{4}=0,\] again by $(1)$. Hence \[\tan\alpha=\frac{tx-y}{x+ty}.\] Now write $D=\rho(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$ with $\rho=BD$ and $\beta=\angle DBC$; as $D$ is inside the triangle, $0<\beta<3\theta$. Then \[tx-y=\frac{\rho\sin(\theta-\beta)}{\cos\theta},\qquad x+ty=\frac{\rho\cos(\theta-\beta)}{\cos\theta}.\] By $(1)$, $x+ty>0$, so $\cos(\theta-\beta)>0$ and $-90^\circ<\theta-\beta<90^\circ$. Also $\tan\alpha=\tan(\theta-\beta)$, and both angles lie in $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$; therefore $\alpha=\theta-\beta$.

Conclusion. Both rays $EA$ and $ED$ point into the half-plane to the left of $E$, with signed angles $\theta-\beta$ and $-2\theta$ from the leftward horizontal. Hence \[\angle AED=(\theta-\beta)-(-2\theta)=3\theta-\beta=\angle ABC-\angle DBC=\angle ABD,\] the last step because $D$ lies inside $\angle ABC$.

Problem 8.3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Prove that there exist infinitely many positive integers $N$ such that for all positive integers $a, b, c$ the following inequality holds: \[\frac{(abc)^2}{|a-b|+|b-c|+|c-a|} \neq N.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Every odd prime works: divisibility first)

We show that no odd prime $p$ is a value of the expression; there are infinitely many odd primes.

The expression is symmetric in $a,b,c$, so let $a\ge b\ge c$. If $a=c$ the denominator is $0$, so $a>c$ and the denominator is $(a-b)+(b-c)+(a-c)=2(a-c)$. Suppose \[(abc)^2=2p\,(a-c).\] Then $p\mid (abc)^2$, so $p\mid abc$ and $p^2\mid(abc)^2=2p(a-c)$, i.e. $p\mid 2(a-c)$; as $p$ is odd, $p\mid a-c$. Hence $a-c\ge p$ and $a\ge p+1$. Now \[(abc)^2=2p(a-c)<2pa\le 2(a-1)a<2a^2,\] so $(bc)^2<2$, i.e. $b=c=1$. The equation becomes $a^2=2p(a-1)$, so $a-1$ divides $a^2$. Since $a^2=(a-1)(a+1)+1$, this forces $a-1\mid 1$, $a=2$, and then $4=2p$, $p=2$, a contradiction. Thus no odd prime $N=p$ is attained, and there are infinitely many such $N$.

Solution 2 (Every odd prime works: size first)

We show that no odd prime $p$ is attained. As the expression is symmetric, let $a\ge b\ge c$; then $a>c$ (otherwise the denominator vanishes) and the denominator equals $2(a-c)$. Suppose $(abc)^2=2p(a-c)$.

Since $0<a-c<a\le abc$, we get $(abc)^2<2p\cdot abc$, i.e. $abc<2p$. On the other hand $p\mid (abc)^2$, so the prime $p$ divides $abc$. The only multiple of $p$ in $[1,2p)$ is $p$ itself, so $abc=p$, and as $p$ is prime and $a\ge b\ge c$, $(a,b,c)=(p,1,1)$. Substituting, $p^2=2p(p-1)$, i.e. $p=2(p-1)$, $p=2$, contradicting that $p$ is odd. Hence every odd prime $N$ satisfies the requirement, and there are infinitely many of them. (For $N=2$ the value is attained: $a=2,\ b=c=1$ gives $\frac{4}{2}=2$.)

Problem 8.4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

All positive integers from $1$ to $2026$ are written on the board. Mykhailo and Fedir play a game taking turns, with Fedir moving first. On each move, a player erases any one number from the board. The game ends when exactly $3$ numbers remain. If these three numbers can be the side lengths of a triangle, then Mykhailo wins; otherwise, Fedir wins. Who wins if both play optimally?

Show the answer

Fedir wins

Show the solution

Solution (A pairing strategy for Fedir)

Answer: Fedir wins. (Degenerate triples such as $1,2,3$ do not form a triangle.)

There are $2023$ erasures, so Fedir makes the 1st, 3rd, …, 2023rd (last) erasure. Split $\{1,\dots,2026\}$ into the $1013$ pairs \[\{k,\ k+1013\}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots,1013).\] Strategy. Fedir first erases $2026$; its partner $1013$ becomes the single. Afterwards, while more than $4$ numbers remain before his move:

  • if Mykhailo erased a number from a pair, Fedir erases its partner;

  • if Mykhailo erased the single, Fedir erases the larger number of some complete pair, and the smaller one (which is $\le1013$) becomes the new single.

After each such move of Fedir the board consists of complete pairs and one single, and the single is always at most $1013$.

The end. Just before Fedir's last move $4$ numbers remain, obtained from “two complete pairs and a single $s\le1013$” by one move of Mykhailo. Fedir then keeps three numbers as follows.

  • Mykhailo erased $s$: the board is $k,\ j,\ k+1013,\ j+1013$ with $k<j\le1013$. Fedir erases $k+1013$; the remaining $k<j<j+1013$ satisfy $k+j\le 1013+j$, so they are not the sides of a triangle.

  • Mykhailo erased a member of a pair: the board contains a complete pair $k,\ k+1013$ and the single $s\le1013$. Fedir keeps exactly these three. The two smaller ones are $k$ and $s$ (both $\le1013<k+1013$), and $k+s\le k+1013$, so again no triangle.

In every case the final three numbers fail the triangle inequality, so Fedir wins.

Grade 8, Day 2

Problem 8.5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities

In a round-robin chess tournament, $9$ boys and $9$ girls participated. Thus, every pair of participants played exactly one game. It turned out that all $18$ participants scored the same number of points. Prove that there was a boy and a girl who drew their game.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting the boys' points)

A win gives $1$ point, a draw $\frac12$ to each player. Each game distributes exactly $1$ point, and there are $\binom{18}{2}=153$ games, so all players together scored $153$ points; since all $18$ scores are equal, each player scored $\frac{153}{18}=\frac{17}{2}$.

Suppose no game between a boy and a girl was drawn. The $9$ boys scored $9\cdot\frac{17}2=\frac{153}{2}$ points in total. The games among boys give the boys exactly $\binom92=36$ points, so the boys scored $\frac{153}2-36=\frac{81}{2}$ points in the $81$ boy–girl games. But without draws every boy–girl game gives a boy $0$ or $1$ point, so this number is an integer, a contradiction. Hence some boy and some girl drew.

Solution 2 (Odd degrees in the graph of draws)

Each player scored $\frac{17}{2}$ points (the $153$ points of the $153$ games are shared equally by $18$ players).

Suppose no boy–girl game was drawn. Then a boy's points from games against girls form an integer, so his points from games against boys must be (integer)$+\frac12$; that is, he drew an odd number of games against boys. Draw the graph whose vertices are the $9$ boys, two boys joined when their game was drawn. Every vertex has odd degree, so the sum of the degrees is a sum of $9$ odd numbers, which is odd. But the sum of the degrees of any graph is twice the number of edges, an even number. This contradiction shows that some boy–girl game was drawn.

Problem 8.6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Do there exist positive integers $a, b, c$ such that in the decimal representations of the numbers $4^a$, $6^b$, and $7^c$, each digit from $0$ to $9$ appears the same number of times in total?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (The digit sum modulo 3)

No. Suppose each digit $0,1,\dots,9$ occurs exactly $k$ times in total ($k\ge1$). Then the sum of all digits of the three numbers is $k(0+1+\dots+9)=45k$, a multiple of $3$. A number is congruent to its digit sum modulo $3$, so \[4^a+6^b+7^c\equiv 45k\equiv 0\pmod 3 .\] But $4\equiv7\equiv1$ and $6\equiv0\pmod 3$, so $4^a+6^b+7^c\equiv 1+0+1=2\pmod 3$, a contradiction. Hence such $a,b,c$ do not exist.

Problem 8.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

In a convex pentagon $ABCDE$, it is known that $AB + AC = EC + ED$, $\angle BAC = 2\angle CDB$, and $\angle DEC = 2\angle CBD$. Prove that $\angle ABD = \angle EDB$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Unfolding the sums onto one circle)

Put $\delta=\angle CDB$ and $\beta=\angle CBD$, so $\angle BAC=2\delta$ and $\angle DEC=2\beta$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle through $B,C,D$.

Two new points on $\Gamma$. Let $X$ be the point on ray $BA$ beyond $A$ with $AX=AC$, and $Y$ the point on ray $DE$ beyond $E$ with $EY=EC$. Then \[BX=AB+AC=EC+ED=DY .\] Triangle $AXC$ is isosceles with $\angle XAC=180^\circ-2\delta$, so $\angle BXC=\angle AXC=\delta=\angle BDC$. Since the pentagon is convex, it lies on one side of line $BC$, and so does the ray $BA$ beyond $B$; thus $X$ and $D$ are on the same side of $BC$, and $X\in\Gamma$. In the same way $\angle DYC=\beta=\angle DBC$ with $Y,B$ on the same side of $CD$, so $Y\in\Gamma$.

At the vertex $B$ of the convex pentagon the diagonal $BD$ lies inside $\angle CBA$; a point of $\Gamma$ on the arc $DC$ would be seen from $B$ inside $\angle CBD$, so $X$ lies on the arc $\mathcal A$ of $\Gamma$ from $B$ to $D$ not containing $C$. Likewise $Y\in\mathcal A$.

The arcs $BX$ and $DY$ are equal. Measure arcs inside $\mathcal A$. By the inscribed angle theorem, $\overset{\frown}{BX}=2\angle BCX$ and $\overset{\frown}{DY}=2\angle DCY$. As $A$ lies on segment $BX$, $\angle BCX=\angle BCA+\angle ACX=\angle BCA+\delta$; similarly $\angle DCY=\angle DCE+\beta$. At the vertex $C$ the rays $CB,CA,CE,CD$ come in this order, so \[\angle BCA+\angle ACE+\angle ECD=\angle BCD=180^\circ-\beta-\delta,\] and therefore $\angle BCX+\angle DCY=180^\circ-\angle ACE<180^\circ$, i.e. $\overset{\frown}{BX}+\overset{\frown}{DY}<360^\circ$. Equal chords $BX=DY$ cut off arcs that are either equal or add up to $360^\circ$; the second option is excluded, so $\overset{\frown}{BX}=\overset{\frown}{DY}$.

Conclusion. The arc $XD$ inside $\mathcal A$ equals $\mathcal A-\overset{\frown}{BX}=\mathcal A-\overset{\frown}{DY}$, which is the arc $BY$ inside $\mathcal A$. Hence the inscribed angles on these arcs are equal: \[\angle ABD=\angle XBD=\tfrac12\overset{\frown}{XD}=\tfrac12\overset{\frown}{BY}=\angle YDB=\angle EDB .\]

Solution 2 (Law of sines)

Put $\delta=\angle CDB$, $\beta=\angle CBD$ (so $\angle BAC=2\delta$, $\angle DEC=2\beta$), and let $u=\angle ABC$, $v=\angle ACB$, $p=\angle ECD$, $q=\angle EDC$ be angles of triangles $ABC$ and $CDE$. Then $u+v=180^\circ-2\delta$ and $p+q=180^\circ-2\beta$. Let $\rho$ be the circumradius of triangle $BCD$, so $BC=2\rho\sin\delta$ and $CD=2\rho\sin\beta$.

By the law of sines in triangle $ABC$ and the sum-to-product formula, \[AB+AC=\frac{BC(\sin v+\sin u)}{\sin2\delta}=\frac{BC\cdot 2\sin\frac{u+v}2\cos\frac{u-v}2}{2\sin\delta\cos\delta} =\frac{BC}{\sin\delta}\cos\frac{u-v}{2}=2\rho\cos\frac{u-v}2,\] since $\sin\frac{u+v}2=\cos\delta$. In the same way $EC+ED=2\rho\cos\frac{q-p}2$. The hypothesis gives $\cos\frac{u-v}2=\cos\frac{q-p}2$ with both half-differences in $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$, hence $u-v=q-p$ or $u-v=p-q$.

The case $u-v=p-q$ is impossible (unless it coincides with the other case). Indeed, then $2v=(u+v)-(u-v)=180^\circ-2\delta-(p-q)$ and $2p=(p+q)+(p-q)=180^\circ-2\beta+(p-q)$; adding, $v+p=180^\circ-\beta-\delta=\angle BCD$. But in the convex pentagon the rays $CB,CA,CE,CD$ come in this order, so $\angle BCD=v+\angle ACE+p>v+p$.

Hence $u-v=q-p$. Then $2u-2q=(u+v)-(p+q)=2\beta-2\delta$, i.e. $u-\beta=q-\delta$. In the convex pentagon the diagonal $BD$ lies inside $\angle ABC$ and inside $\angle EDC$, so $\angle ABD=u-\beta$ and $\angle EDB=q-\delta$. Therefore $\angle ABD=\angle EDB$.

Problem 8.8

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Mykhailo chose a list of $100$ (not necessarily distinct) real numbers $0 \le a_1 \le a_2 \le \cdots \le a_{100} \le 1$ and computed their arithmetic mean $S$. Fedir does not know the numbers nor the value $S$. He wants to choose some $50$ of these numbers (by selecting their indices) so that their arithmetic mean differs from $S$ by at most $C$. What is the smallest value of $C \ge 0$ for which Fedir can always guarantee this?

Show the answer

$C=\frac{1}{100}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Neighbouring pairs)

Fedir knows nothing except that the list is sorted, so his choice is a fixed set $I$ of $50$ indices.

$C\ge\frac1{100}$. Whatever $I$ is, Mykhailo may have chosen $a_1=\dots=a_{99}=0$, $a_{100}=1$. Then $S=\frac1{100}$, while the mean of the chosen numbers is $\frac1{50}$ (if $100\in I$) or $0$; in both cases it differs from $S$ by exactly $\frac1{100}$.

$C=\frac1{100}$ suffices. Let Fedir take the even indices $2,4,\dots,100$. Then \[\frac{a_2+a_4+\dots+a_{100}}{50}-S=\frac{2(a_2+\dots+a_{100})-(a_1+\dots+a_{100})}{100}=\frac1{100}\sum_{j=1}^{50}(a_{2j}-a_{2j-1}).\] Every bracket is $\ge0$, so the difference is $\ge0$. Adding the nonnegative numbers $a_{2j+1}-a_{2j}$ ($j=1,\dots,49$) we get a telescoping sum: \[\sum_{j=1}^{50}(a_{2j}-a_{2j-1})\le\sum_{j=1}^{50}(a_{2j}-a_{2j-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{49}(a_{2j+1}-a_{2j})=a_{100}-a_1\le1 .\] So the chosen mean differs from $S$ by at most $\frac1{100}$, and the answer is $\boxed{\dfrac{1}{100}}$.

Solution 2 (Reduction to 0–1 staircases)

Fedir's choice is a fixed set $I$ of $50$ indices. For a list $a$ put $\Delta(a)=\frac1{50}\sum_{i\in I}a_i-\frac1{100}\sum_{i=1}^{100}a_i$; this is linear in $a$.

For $k=0,1,\dots,100$ let $s_k$ be the list with $0$ in the first $100-k$ places and $1$ in the last $k$ places, and let $c_k$ be the number of indices of $I$ among the last $k$. Then $\Delta(s_k)=\frac{c_k}{50}-\frac k{100}=\frac{2c_k-k}{100}$.

Every admissible list is a combination of staircases with nonnegative weights of total at most $1$: with $a_0=0$, \[a=\sum_{k=1}^{100}w_k\,s_k,\qquad w_k=a_{101-k}-a_{100-k}\ge0,\qquad \sum_k w_k=a_{100}\le 1 ,\] (the $i$-th entry of the right side is $\sum_{k\ge 101-i}w_k=a_i$). Hence $\Delta(a)=\sum_k w_k\Delta(s_k)$.

Lower bound. For $k=1$, $2c_1-1=\pm1$, so $|\Delta(s_1)|=\frac1{100}$ for every $I$; Mykhailo can choose $a=s_1$.

Upper bound. Choose $I$ so that, going down from index $100$, the indices alternate chosen/not chosen (e.g. all even indices). Then $c_k\in\{\frac{k-1}2,\frac k2,\frac{k+1}{2}\}$, so $|2c_k-k|\le1$ and $|\Delta(s_k)|\le\frac1{100}$ for all $k$. Therefore $|\Delta(a)|\le\frac1{100}\sum_kw_k\le\frac1{100}$ for every admissible list. The smallest $C$ is $\boxed{\dfrac1{100}}$.

Problem 9.1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms · also Grade 10, Day 1, Problem 10.1

Let $a, b, c, d$ be numbers, each having exactly $2026$ nonzero digits after the decimal point. Let $x(n)$ denote the rounding of $x$ to $n$ digits after the decimal point for $n = 1, 2, \ldots, 2026$. Is it possible that:

  • (a) $a(1)+b(1) > c(1)+d(1)$, $a(2)+b(2) < c(2)+d(2)$, and $a(3)+b(3) > c(3)+d(3)$?

  • (b) for even $n$ we have $a(n)+b(n) < c(n)+d(n)$, and for odd $n$ we have $a(n)+b(n) > c(n)+d(n)$?

Show the answer

(a) Yes; (b) Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Truncation plus a round-up bit)

Answer: (a) Yes; (b) Yes. An example for (b) also answers (a), since (a) asks for the cases $n=1,2,3$ of (b).

How rounding works here. Let $x=N.d_1d_2\dots d_{2026}$ with all $d_i\ne0$, and let $T_n(x)$ be $x$ truncated after $n$ digits. For $n<2026$ the discarded tail is $0.d_{n+1}d_{n+2}\dots d_{2026}$ units of $10^{-n}$. If $n+1<2026$ the digits after $d_{n+1}$ are nonzero, so the tail exceeds $\frac12$ exactly when $d_{n+1}\ge5$; if $n+1=2026$ the tail is $\frac{d_{2026}}{10}$, which is never $\frac12$ when $d_{2026}\ne5$. So, for numbers whose last digit is not $5$ (no tie can occur), \[x(n)=T_n(x)+[d_{n+1}\ge5]\cdot10^{-n}\quad(n<2026),\qquad x(2026)=x .\]

The example. Take (digits after the point, positions $1,2,\dots,2026$)

  • $a=0.3131\ldots31$ (odd positions $3$, even positions $1$),

  • $b=0.9696\ldots96$ (odd positions $9$, even positions $6$),

  • $c=0.6363\ldots6364$ (odd positions $6$, even positions $3$, except the last digit, which is $4$),

  • $d=0.6464\ldots64$ (odd positions $6$, even positions $4$).

All digits are nonzero and none is $5$. At every odd position the digits satisfy $3+9=6+6$, and at every even position before the last one $1+6=3+4$. Hence $T_n(a)+T_n(b)=T_n(c)+T_n(d)$ for all $n\le2025$, and \[a(n)+b(n)-c(n)-d(n)=\bigl([d_{n+1}(a)\ge5]+[d_{n+1}(b)\ge5]-[d_{n+1}(c)\ge5]-[d_{n+1}(d)\ge5]\bigr)10^{-n}.\]

  • $n$ odd: position $n+1$ is even, with digits $1,6,3\text{ or }4,4$; only $b$ rounds up, so the difference is $+10^{-n}>0$.

  • $n$ even, $n<2026$: position $n+1$ is odd, with digits $3,9,6,6$; $b,c,d$ round up, so the difference is $(1-2)10^{-n}<0$.

  • $n=2026$: no rounding, and the last digits give $a+b-c-d=(1+6-4-4)10^{-2026}<0$.

So $a(n)+b(n)>c(n)+d(n)$ for odd $n$ and $<$ for even $n$, for all $n=1,\dots,2026$; in particular (a) holds as well.

Problem 9.4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let a positive integer $c$ be given. Consider all sequences of positive integers $(a_n)$ such that for every positive integer $n$, \[a_{n+2} = \gcd(a_{n+1}, a_n) + c.\] Find all positive integers $a$ such that there exists a sequence of this type in which $a$ appears infinitely many times.

Show the solution

Solution (Bounded, eventually periodic, and the maximum of the period)

Answer: exactly the numbers $a=c+d$ and $a=2c+d$ with $d$ a positive divisor of $c$.

These occur infinitely often. Let $d\mid c$ and $a_1=a_2=c+d$. Since $\gcd(c+d,c)=\gcd(d,c)=d$, \[a_3=(c+d)+c=2c+d,\quad a_4=\gcd(c+d,2c+d)+c=\gcd(c+d,c)+c=c+d,\quad a_5=\gcd(2c+d,c+d)+c=c+d,\] and from $(a_4,a_5)=(a_1,a_2)$ the sequence repeats with period $3$: $c+d,\ c+d,\ 2c+d,\dots$. Both values appear infinitely often.

Nothing else does. For $n\ge3$ we have $a_n\ge c+1$; put $b_n=a_n-c\ge1$. Then for $n\ge3$ \[b_{n+2}=\gcd(a_n,a_{n+1})=\gcd(b_n+c,\ b_{n+1}+c).\tag{$*$}\] Two consequences of $(*)$, for $n\ge3$: $b_{n+2}\mid b_{n+1}+c$, and if $b_n\ne b_{n+1}$ then $b_{n+2}$ divides $(b_n+c)-(b_{n+1}+c)$, so $b_{n+2}\le|b_n-b_{n+1}|<\max(b_n,b_{n+1})$.

Periodicity. Each pair $(b_n,b_{n+1})$ determines the rest, and only finitely many pairs occur, so from some point on the sequence is periodic; the values appearing infinitely often are exactly the values in one period.

The period. Let $T$ be the largest value in the period and $b_{k+2}=T$ with $k$ in the periodic part. If $b_k\ne b_{k+1}$, then $T=b_{k+2}<\max(b_k,b_{k+1})\le T$, impossible. Now \[b_{k+3}=\gcd(t+c,t+2c)=\gcd(t+c,c)=t,\qquad b_{k+4}=\gcd(t+2c,t+c)=t,\] so the pattern $t,t,t+c$ repeats from index $k$ on. Together with the first part, the answer is \[\boxed{a\in\{c+d,\ 2c+d\ :\ d\mid c\}}\qquad(d\text{ a positive divisor of }c).\]

Problem 9.6

Prove that there exist infinitely many positive integers that cannot be represented in the form $\gcd(m^n+1, n^m+1)$ for some positive integers $m, n$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Odd multiples of 3)

We show that no odd multiple of $3$ (that is, $3,9,15,21,\dots$, infinitely many numbers) equals $\gcd(m^n+1,n^m+1)$.

Suppose $3$ divides $g=\gcd(m^n+1,n^m+1)$. Then $m^n\equiv-1\equiv2\pmod3$. If $m\equiv0$ or $1\pmod 3$, then $m^n\equiv0$ or $1$; so $m\equiv2\equiv-1\pmod3$, and $(-1)^n\equiv-1$ forces $n$ odd. In the same way $n^m+1\equiv0\pmod3$ forces $m$ odd. But if $m$ and $n$ are both odd, then $m^n+1$ and $n^m+1$ are both even, so $g$ is even. Hence a value of $g$ divisible by $3$ is always even, and no odd multiple of $3$ is a value.

Solution 2 (A quadratic residue argument)

Let $p$ be a prime with $p\equiv3\pmod4$ (for instance $p=3$ or $p=7$). We show: if $p\mid g=\gcd(m^n+1,n^m+1)$, then $g$ is even. Consequently every odd number divisible by $p$ (for example $7,21,35,\dots$) is not a value, and there are infinitely many of these.

For $p\equiv3\pmod 4$, $-1$ is not a square modulo $p$: if $z^2\equiv-1$, then $p\nmid z$ and by Fermat $1\equiv z^{p-1}=(z^2)^{(p-1)/2}\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}=-1\pmod p$, as $\frac{p-1}2$ is odd; impossible.

Now $p\mid m^n+1$ means $m^n\equiv-1\pmod p$. If $n$ were even, $m^n=(m^{n/2})^2$ would be a square $\equiv-1$; so $n$ is odd. Likewise $p\mid n^m+1$ gives $m$ odd. Then $m^n+1$ and $n^m+1$ are even, so $2\mid g$, as claimed.

The parallel gives a ratio. From $DE\parallel BC$ we get $\frac{AE}{AB}=\frac{AD}{AC}$, so \[\frac{BF}{AB}=\frac{BE}{AB}=1-\frac{AE}{AB}=1-\frac{AD}{AC}=\frac{CD}{AC}=\frac{CF}{AC},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{BA}{BF}=\frac{CA}{CF}.\]

Conclusion. In triangle $ABF$ the bisector from $B$ meets the side $AF$ at the point $X$ with $\frac{AX}{XF}=\frac{BA}{BF}$ (angle bisector theorem). In triangle $ACF$ the bisector from $C$ meets $AF$ at the point $Y$ with $\frac{AY}{YF}=\frac{CA}{CF}$. The two ratios are equal, so $X=Y$. This point lies on both bisectors, i.e. on the perpendicular bisectors of $EF$ and $DF$; it is therefore equidistant from $D,E,F$ and equals $O'$. Since $X$ lies on segment $AF$, line $AF$ passes through the circumcenter of $DEF$.

Problem 10.3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Vadym has $n$ blue and $n$ yellow tokens. Initially, all tokens are mixed and arranged into two rows of $n$ tokens each: a top row and a bottom row. In one move, Vadym may choose any positive integer $1 \le k \le n$ and perform one of the following operations:

  • swap any two adjacent tokens in the bottom row;

  • swap the $k$-th token in the top row with the $k$-th token in the bottom row.

What is the minimum number of moves required to guarantee that all yellow tokens are in the top row and all blue tokens are in the bottom row?

Show the answer

$\left\lfloor \frac{(n+1)^2}{4}\right\rfloor$

Show the solution

Solution (Matching for the upper bound, a potential for the lower bound)

Number the columns $1,\dots,n$. Call a token misplaced if it is a blue token in the top row or a yellow token in the bottom row; the numbers of misplaced tokens in the two rows are equal, say $r$.

Upper bound: $r(n+1-r)\le\lfloor(n+1)^2/4\rfloor$ moves always suffice. Let the misplaced blue tokens of the top row be in columns $t_1<\dots<t_r$ and the yellow tokens of the bottom row in columns $s_1<\dots<s_r$. We move the bottom-row yellow tokens, never letting two of them pass each other, so that the $i$-th one goes from $s_i$ to $t_i$; each adjacent swap of a yellow token with a blue neighbour lowers $D=\sum_i|s_i-t_i|$ by $1$. Such a swap exists while $D>0$: if some $s_i<t_i$, take the largest such $i$; the cell to the right of the $i$-th yellow token (column $s_i+1\le t_i\le n$) is blue, because if it held the $(i+1)$-th yellow token, then $s_{i+1}=s_i+1\le t_i<t_{i+1}$ and $i+1$ would also have to move right, contrary to the choice of $i$. Swap them. If all $s_i\ge t_i$, take the smallest $i$ with $s_i>t_i$ and, symmetrically, swap the $i$-th yellow token with the blue token on its left. So $D$ swaps suffice. Since $t_i,s_i\in[i,\,n-r+i]$, each $|s_i-t_i|\le n-r$ and $D\le r(n-r)$. Then $r$ vertical swaps in columns $t_1,\dots,t_r$ finish. In total at most $r(n-r)+r=r(n+1-r)\le\frac{(n+1)^2}4$ moves, and the left side is an integer.

Lower bound. Take $r=\lfloor\frac{n+1}2\rfloor$ and the arrangement: top row blue in columns $1,\dots,r$ and yellow in $r+1,\dots,n$; bottom row blue in columns $1,\dots,n-r$ and yellow in $n-r+1,\dots,n$. For any arrangement define \[\Phi=\sum_{\text{yellow in bottom row, column }j}(j-r)\ +\sum_{\text{blue in top row, column }j}(r+1-j).\] A swap of two adjacent bottom-row tokens changes $\Phi$ by $0$ (same colours) or moves one bottom yellow token by one column, changing $\Phi$ by $\pm1$. A vertical swap in column $j$ changes nothing if the two tokens have the same colour; otherwise it either removes a bottom yellow and a top blue from column $j$ (change $-(j-r)-(r+1-j)=-1$) or creates them ($+1$). So every move changes $\Phi$ by at most $1$. At the goal $\Phi=0$, and initially \[\Phi=\sum_{j=n-r+1}^{n}(j-r)+\sum_{j=1}^{r}(r+1-j)=r(n-2r)+\frac{r(r+1)}2+\frac{r(r+1)}2=r(n+1-r).\] So at least $r(n+1-r)=\lfloor\frac{n+1}2\rfloor\lceil\frac{n+1}2\rceil=\lfloor\frac{(n+1)^2}4\rfloor$ moves are needed.

The answer is $\boxed{\left\lfloor \dfrac{(n+1)^2}{4}\right\rfloor}$.

Problem 10.4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · also Grade 11, Day 1, Problem 11.3

Does there exist a prime number $p > 3$ and a permutation $\{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{p-1}\}$ of the numbers $\{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$ such that the numbers \[\{a_1^{a_2}, a_2^{a_3}, \ldots, a_{p-2}^{a_{p-1}}, a_{p-1}^{a_1}\}\] give pairwise distinct remainders modulo $p$?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (Exponents modulo $p-1$)

No. Suppose such $p$ and $(a_i)$ exist; indices are cyclic ($a_p=a_1$). Put $n=p-1\ge4$ and let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p$; write $a_i\equiv g^{x_i}$ with $x_i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$. As $a_i$ runs over all nonzero residues, $(x_i)$ runs over all residues modulo $n$; also $y_i=a_{i+1}$ runs over $1,\dots,n$, i.e. over all residues modulo $n$. Since $a_i^{a_{i+1}}\equiv g^{x_iy_i}$, the hypothesis says that \[x_iy_i\ (i=1,\dots,n)\ \text{are pairwise different modulo } n .\] So $(x_i)$, $(y_i)$, $(x_iy_i)$ are all complete residue systems modulo $n$.

Lemma. If $q$ is a prime divisor of $n$, then $q\mid x_i\iff q\mid y_i$. Indeed, exactly $n/q$ residues are divisible by $q$, so $q\mid x_i$ for $n/q$ indices, $q\mid y_i$ for $n/q$ indices, and $q\mid x_iy_i$ (i.e. $q\mid x_i$ or $q\mid y_i$) also for exactly $n/q$ indices. The last set is the union of the first two, which therefore coincide.

Case $4\mid n$. By the lemma with $q=2$, for the $n/2$ indices with $x_i$ even also $y_i$ is even, so $4\mid x_iy_i$. But only $n/4$ residues modulo $n$ are divisible by $4$, and $n/2>n/4$: contradiction.

Case $n\equiv2\pmod4$. Then $n=2m$ with $m$ odd and $m>1$ (because $p>3$); let $q$ be an odd prime factor of $m$ and $l=m/q$, which is odd. Let $J$ be the set of indices with $\gcd(x_i,2q)=1$. By the lemma (for $2$ and for $q$), $J$ is also the set of indices with $\gcd(y_i,2q)=1$, and the set of indices with $\gcd(x_iy_i,2q)=1$. Fix $s\not\equiv0\pmod q$; the residues $r$ modulo $n$ with $r$ odd and $r\equiv s\pmod q$ form one class modulo $2q$, so there are $n/(2q)=l$ of them. Hence, as $i$ runs over $J$, each of $x_i$, $y_i$, $x_iy_i$ takes every nonzero residue modulo $q$ exactly $l$ times, and by Wilson's theorem \[\prod_{i\in J}x_i\equiv\prod_{i\in J}y_i\equiv\prod_{i\in J}x_iy_i\equiv\bigl((q-1)!\bigr)^l\equiv(-1)^l=-1\pmod q .\] But $\prod_{J}x_iy_i=\prod_Jx_i\cdot\prod_Jy_i\equiv(-1)(-1)=1\pmod q$, so $1\equiv-1\pmod q$, impossible for odd $q$.

In both cases we reach a contradiction, so no such prime and permutation exist.

Problem 10.5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Prove that for every positive integer $n$ there exists a positive integer divisible by $n$ whose sum of digits is odd.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Multiply by $10^L-1$)

Let $L$ be an odd number with $10^L>n$. We claim that the multiple $n(10^L-1)$ of $n$ has digit sum $9L$, which is odd.

Write $n(10^L-1)=(n-1)\cdot10^L+(10^L-n)$. Here $10^L-n=(10^L-1)-(n-1)$, and since $0\le n-1<10^L$, subtracting $n-1$ from the number $\underbrace{99\ldots9}_{L}$ involves no borrowing: each digit of $10^L-n$ (written with exactly $L$ digits, leading zeros allowed) is $9$ minus the corresponding digit of $n-1$. So the last $L$ digits of $n(10^L-1)$ have sum $9L-s(n-1)$, and the digits in front of them are those of $n-1$, with sum $s(n-1)$ (here $s$ is the digit sum). In total the digit sum is $9L$, which is odd.

Solution 2 (Repeated powers of ten)

$n$ odd. The residues of $1,10,10^2,\dots$ modulo $n$ take finitely many values, so some residue $r$ is taken by infinitely many powers; choose $n$ of them, $10^{e_1},\dots,10^{e_n}$ with distinct exponents. Their sum $X=10^{e_1}+\dots+10^{e_n}\equiv nr\equiv0\pmod n$ is a multiple of $n$, and its decimal digits are $n$ ones and zeros, so its digit sum is $n$, which is odd.

General $n$. Write $n=2^km$ with $m$ odd. Take a multiple $X$ of $m$ with odd digit sum, as above. Then $10^kX$ is divisible by $2^km=n$ and has the same digit sum as $X$, which is odd.

Problem 10.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

In triangle $ABC$, the median $AM$ is drawn, and line $AM$ intersects the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$ again at point $N$. Points $X$ and $Y$ are chosen on line $BC$ such that point $B$ lies on segment $CX$, and point $C$ lies on segment $BY$. It is known that $\angle ABN = 2\angle AXN$ and $\angle ACN = 2\angle AYN$. Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two circles with the same power at $M$)

Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle, $O$ its center, $m=MB=MC$. By the intersecting chords theorem $MA\cdot MN=MB\cdot MC=m^2$.

Locating $X$ and $Y$ on circles. Let $W_B$ be the midpoint of the arc $AN$ of $\Gamma$ that contains $B$, and $W_C$ the midpoint of the other arc $AN$ (it contains $C$). Both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AN$, which passes through $O$, so $W_B$ and $W_C$ are diametrically opposite: $W_B+W_C=2O$ (as vectors). Let $\kappa_B$ be the circle with center $W_B$ through $A$ and $N$. Since $W_B$ and $B$ lie on the same arc, $\angle AW_BN=\angle ABN$; hence every point $Z$ of $\kappa_B$ on the same side of line $AN$ as $W_B$ (and $B$) sees $AN$ at $\angle AZN=\frac12\angle AW_BN=\frac12\angle ABN$, and conversely the points on that side with this property form exactly this arc. The point $X$ is on $B$'s side of line $AN$ (line $AN$ meets $BC$ at $M$, and $X$ lies beyond $B$), and $\angle AXN=\frac12\angle ABN$; so $X\in\kappa_B$. In the same way $Y$ lies on the circle $\kappa_C$ with center $W_C$ through $A$ and $N$.

Coordinates on line $BC$. Take $M$ as origin and $BC$ as the $x$-axis, with $C$ on the positive side; then $O=(0,o)$, so $W_B=(w,\,o+v)$ and $W_C=(-w,\,o-v)$ for some numbers $w,v$. For a circle with center $W$ and radius $r$, the point $(t,0)$ lies on it iff \[t^2-2tW_x+\bigl(|W|^2-r^2\bigr)=0,\] and $|W|^2-r^2$ is the power of $M$. For $\kappa_B$ and $\kappa_C$ this power is $-MA\cdot MN=-m^2$, because $M$ lies on the chord $AN$ of both. So the intersections of line $BC$ with $\kappa_B$ and $\kappa_C$ are given by \[t^2-2wt-m^2=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad t^2+2wt-m^2=0,\] whose roots are negatives of each other. $X=(t_X,0)$ with $t_X<-m$ is a root of the first, so $(-t_X,0)$ lies on $\kappa_C$, beyond $C$. The second equation has roots of opposite signs (their product is $-m^2$), so $\kappa_C$ meets the ray $MC$ in only one point; as $Y$ is such a point, $Y=(-t_X,0)$. Hence $MY=MX$ and \[BX=MX-m=MY-m=CY.\]

Solution 2 (Cotangents in coordinates)

Scale so that $MB=MC=1$, and take $M$ as origin, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(p,q)$ with $q>0$. Put $r=p^2+q^2=MA^2$.

The point $N$. $M$ lies inside the circumcircle on the chord $AN$, between $A$ and $N$, and the intersecting chords theorem gives $MA\cdot MN=MB\cdot MC=1$. Hence $N=-\frac1r A=\bigl(-\frac pr,-\frac qr\bigr)$.

With $\vec{ZA}=(p-z,\,q)$ and $\vec{ZN}=\bigl(-\frac pr-z,\,-\frac qr\bigr)$, \[\vec{ZA}\cdot\vec{ZN}=z^2-1-kz,\qquad \Bigl|(p-z)\Bigl(-\frac qr\Bigr)-q\Bigl(-\frac pr-z\Bigr)\Bigr|=L|z|,\] where $k=p\bigl(1-\frac1r\bigr)$ and $L=q\bigl(1+\frac1r\bigr)>0$. The dot product is $|ZA|\,|ZN|\cos\angle AZN$ and the absolute cross product is $|ZA|\,|ZN|\sin\angle AZN$, so \[\cot\angle AZN=\frac{z^2-1-kz}{L|z|}.\tag{1}\] In particular ($z=-1$ and $z=1$) \[\cot\angle ABN=\frac kL,\qquad \cot\angle ACN=-\frac kL .\]

Halving an angle. For $0^\circ<\varphi<180^\circ$, \[\cot\frac\varphi2=\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}=\cot\varphi+\frac1{\sin\varphi}=\cot\varphi+\sqrt{1+\cot^2\varphi},\] since $\sin\varphi>0$. Put $S=\sqrt{1+k^2/L^2}$.

The point $X$. $B$ lies on segment $CX$, so $X=(-w,0)$ with $w=MX>1$. From $\angle AXN=\frac12\angle ABN$ we get $\cot\angle AXN=\frac kL+S$, while $(1)$ with $z=-w$ gives $\cot\angle AXN=\frac{w^2-1+kw}{Lw}=\frac{1}{L}\bigl(w-\frac1w\bigr)+\frac kL$. Hence \[w-\frac1w=LS .\]

The point $Y$. $C$ lies on segment $BY$, so $Y=(w',0)$ with $w'=MY>1$. From $\angle AYN=\frac12\angle ACN$ we get $\cot\angle AYN=-\frac kL+S$, while $(1)$ with $z=w'$ gives $\frac1L\bigl(w'-\frac1{w'}\bigr)-\frac kL$. Hence \[w'-\frac1{w'}=LS .\]

Conclusion. The function $w\mapsto w-\frac1w$ is strictly increasing for $w>0$, so $w=w'$. Therefore $BX=MX-MB=w-1=w'-1=MY-MC=CY$.

Problem 11.4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

A board $(2n-1) \times (2n-1)$ is colored black and white. In one move, one may choose any rectangle or square inside the board and invert the colors of all its cells. What is the minimum number of moves needed to guarantee that all cells become the same color?

Show the answer

$n^2-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Corner parities)

Setup. Let $m=2n-1$. The board has $(m+1)^2=4n^2$ grid points, in rows and columns numbered $0,\dots,2n-1$. For a grid point $V$ let $\phi(V)\in\{0,1\}$ be the parity of the number of black cells among the (one, two or four) cells having $V$ as a corner. Inverting a rectangle changes $\phi$ exactly at its four corners: at any other grid point it inverts $0$ or $2$ of the touching cells. A rectangle of cells in rows $a..b$ and columns $c..d$ has corners in grid rows $a,b+1$ and grid columns $c,d+1$, so the moves are exactly the operations “choose two grid rows $r_1<r_2$ and two grid columns $c_1<c_2$, and change $\phi$ at the four points $(r_i,c_j)$”. The colouring is determined by $\phi$ (reading the cells row by row from the top-left corner), every grid row and column of $\phi$-values has an even sum (each cell is counted twice), an all-white board has $\phi\equiv0$, and an all-black board has $\phi=1$ exactly at the four corners of the board.

Lower bound $n^2-1$. Colour black the $n^2$ cells whose row and column numbers (from $1$) are both odd (figure). Every grid point is touched by exactly one such cell, so $\phi\equiv1$ at all $4n^2$ points. A move changes $\phi$ at $4$ points, so it changes the number of points with $\phi=1$ by at most $4$. To reach all white ($0$ such points) we need at least $n^2$ moves, to reach all black ($4$ such points) at least $\frac{4n^2-4}4=n^2-1$ moves.

Upper bound $n^2-1$. Phase 1: make all rows of cells equal. This means $\phi=0$ on the inner grid rows $1,\dots,2n-2$. Group them in pairs $(1,2),(3,4),\dots,(2n-3,2n-2)$. For a pair $(i,i+1)$ with $\phi$-rows $u,w$ (each of even weight), let $s$ be the set of columns where both are $1$, enlarged by one column where exactly one of them is $1$ if $|s|$ is odd (such a column exists, since $|u|$ is even). Then $s$, $u+s$, $w+s$ (sums modulo $2$, i.e. symmetric differences of column sets) all have even size. Each column where $u$ or $w$ is $1$ is counted once in $|s|+|u+s|+|w+s|$, except the added column, which is counted twice; so the sum is $|u\cup w|$ or $|u\cup w|+1$, the latter only when $|s|$ was enlarged, i.e. when $|u\cap w|$ is odd, and then $|u\cup w|=|u|+|w|-|u\cap w|$ is odd. Being a sum of even numbers, the sum is even, hence at most $2n$ (there are $2n$ grid columns). Pair up the columns of $s$ and use the moves on grid rows $(i,i+1)$; pair up the columns of $u+s$ and use grid rows $(0,i)$; pair up those of $w+s$ and use grid rows $(0,i+1)$. This clears grid rows $i,i+1$ (it changes only grid row $0$ besides them) with at most $\frac{2n}2=n$ moves. Over the $n-1$ pairs: at most $n(n-1)$ moves.

Phase 2. Now all inner grid rows are $0$, so by the column parities grid rows $0$ and $2n-1$ are equal, say to a vector $z$ of even weight. If $|z|\le2n-2$, pair up its columns and use moves on grid rows $(0,2n-1)$: at most $n-1$ moves give $\phi\equiv0$ (all white). If $|z|=2n$, do the same with the $2n-2$ inner columns: $n-1$ moves leave $\phi=1$ exactly at the four corners (all black).

In total at most $n(n-1)+(n-1)=n^2-1$ moves always suffice, and the answer is $\boxed{n^2-1}$.

Problem 11.5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Into what smallest number of groups can the integers from $1$ to $2026$ be partitioned so that the least common multiple of the numbers in each group does not exceed $2026$? A group may consist of a single number.

Show the answer

$1013$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Doubling chains)

At least $1013$ groups. Let $1014\le x<y\le2026$. If $x$ and $y$ were in one group, the l.c.m. $\ell$ of that group would be a multiple of $y$ with $\ell\le2026<2y$, so $\ell=y$; then $x\mid y$ with $1<\frac yx<2$, impossible. So the $1013$ numbers $1014,\dots,2026$ lie in different groups.

$1013$ groups suffice. Every $k\le2026$ can be doubled until it lands in $[1014,2026]$ (an interval $(N,2N]$ with $N=1013$ contains exactly one number of the form $k\cdot2^j$). Put $k$ into the group $G_y$ of $y=k\cdot2^j\in[1014,2026]$. Every member of $G_y$ divides $y$, so the l.c.m. of $G_y$ is $y\le2026$. There are $1013$ groups, so the answer is $\boxed{1013}$.

Solution 2 (Largest multiples)

Lower bound. Two numbers $x<y$ from $\{1014,\dots,2026\}$ cannot share a group: their l.c.m. is a common multiple of $x$ and $y$; it is not $y$ (as $x\nmid y$, since $1<y/x<2$), so it is at least $2y>2026$. Hence at least $1013$ groups are needed.

Construction. For each $k\le2026$ let $M(k)=k\lfloor2026/k\rfloor$ be its largest multiple not exceeding $2026$. Then $M(k)>2026-k$; for $k\le1013$ also $M(k)\ge2k$ and $M(k)>2026-k\ge1013$, and for $k\ge1014$, $M(k)=k$. So $M(k)\in[1014,2026]$ always. Group the numbers by the value of $M(k)$: all members of the group with value $y$ divide $y$, so its l.c.m. is $y\le2026$. This uses at most $1013$ groups, and the minimum is $\boxed{1013}$.

Problem 11.6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

In each cell $1 \times 1$ of a $2026 \times 2026$ square there is an arrow that points in one of four directions (“up”, “down”, “left”, or “right”). Vadym may move a token from cell $A$ to a neighboring cell $B$ (sharing a side) only if the arrow in cell $A$ points toward cell $B$.

In one press of a button, he may rotate the arrow in any chosen cell by $90^\circ$ clockwise or counterclockwise.

He wants to reach from a cell $A$ to a cell $B$. Determine the minimum possible number of button presses that guarantees that Vadym can reach cell $B$ from cell $A$, regardless of the chosen cells $A$ and $B$ and the initial arrangement of the arrows.

Show the answer

$6075$

Show the solution

Solution (Greedy staircase and a potential)

Rotating an arrow to a direction costs $0$, $1$ or $2$ presses (same, perpendicular, opposite). Presses can all be made before moving, so for a path $A=Z_0,Z_1,\dots,Z_\ell=B$ of neighbouring cells (no cell repeated) the cost is the sum over $i<\ell$ of the cost of turning the arrow of $Z_i$ towards $Z_{i+1}$. Let $m=2026$.

$3(m-1)=6075$ presses always suffice. By symmetry let $B$ be $a\ge0$ columns to the right and $b\ge0$ rows below $A$. At every cell one of the moves “right”, “down” costs at most $1$ (an arrow pointing right or down costs $0$ for that move; an arrow pointing up or left is perpendicular to one of them); call it the preferred move, and every move costs at most $2$. Walk from $A$ inside the rectangle spanned by $A$ and $B$, always making the preferred move, until the column or the row of $B$ is reached; then go straight to $B$. Each preferred move costs $\le1$; if the column of $B$ is reached after $j$ down-moves, at most $b-j\le b$ forced moves remain, each costing $\le2$ (similarly for the row). The path has $a+b$ moves, so the cost is at most $(a+b)+\max(a,b)\le3(m-1)=6075$.

$6075$ may be necessary. Let every arrow point up, $A$ be the top-left and $B$ the bottom-right cell. Number rows from the top and put $\Phi(\text{cell})=2\cdot\text{row}+\text{column}$. A move down costs $2$ and raises $\Phi$ by $2$; right costs $1$, raises $\Phi$ by $1$; left costs $1$, lowers $\Phi$ by $1$; up costs $0$, lowers $\Phi$ by $2$. So along any path of distinct cells, the cost is at least the total increase of $\Phi$, i.e. at least $\Phi(B)-\Phi(A)=3(m-1)$. Presses may also be made between moves and the token may revisit cells; but then cut out every loop of its route (between the first and the last visit of a cell). What remains is a path of distinct cells from $A$ to $B$ in which each cell is left in the direction of its last departure; at that moment its arrow, which started pointing up, points in that direction, so at least the cost above was spent on that cell. Hence at least $3(m-1)$ presses are needed in any case.

Hence the answer is $\boxed{6075}$.

Problem 11.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

Let $O$ be the circumcenter of an acute triangle $ABC$, and $N$ be the midpoint of the larger arc $BC$ of its circumcircle. On the extension of the angle bisector $AL$ of $\angle A$, choose a point $P$ such that line $BO$ is tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $BCP$. Let $Q$ be a point on line $PN$ lying in the same half-plane with respect to line $BC$ as $A$, and suppose $\angle BQC = 90^\circ$. Prove that line $BC$ contains the angle bisector of $\angle PLQ$.

Show the solution

Solution (A pencil of circles through $B$ and $C$)

The vertex $L$ lies on $BC$, so we must show $\angle PLC=\angle QLC$. Since $P$ lies on line $AL$, this says that $Q$ lies on the reflection of line $AL$ in $BC$. We prove this.

Coordinates. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$; take $M=(0,0)$, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, with $A$ in the upper half-plane. Every circle through $B$ and $C$ is \[S_\lambda:\ x^2+y^2+2\lambda y-1=0,\qquad\text{center }(0,-\lambda).\]

  • The circle with diameter $BC$, which contains $Q$ (as $\angle BQC=90^\circ$), is $S_0$.

  • The circumcircle has center $O=(0,h)$ with $h=\cot A>0$ ($ABC$ is acute), so it is $S_{-h}$. Put $R=\sqrt{1+h^2}$ and $\nu=R-h=\frac1{R+h}\in(0,1)$. Its lowest and highest points are $N'=(0,-\nu)$ (midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$) and $N=(0,1/\nu)$ (midpoint of the larger arc).

  • The circle $BCP$ is tangent to $BO$ at $B$, so its center $(0,-\lambda)$ satisfies $(1,-\lambda)\cdot(1,h)=0$, i.e. it is $S_{1/h}$. Since $h=\frac{1-\nu^2}{2\nu}$, this is $\lambda_P=\frac{2\nu}{1-\nu^2}$.

The bisector from $A$ passes through $N'$. Let $L=(\ell,0)$; points of line $AL$ are $Z(s)=(\ell s,\ \nu(s-1))$, with $s=0$ at $N'$, $s=1$ at $L$, and $s>1$ above $BC$. Substituting into $S_\lambda$ and dividing by $s^2$, with $u=1/s$: \[\bigl(\nu^2-2\lambda\nu-1\bigr)u^2+\bigl(2\lambda\nu-2\nu^2\bigr)u+(\ell^2+\nu^2)=0 .\tag{$*$}\] For $\lambda=0$: $(\nu^2-1)u^2-2\nu^2u+(\ell^2+\nu^2)=0$. For $\lambda=\lambda_P$ the first two coefficients become $\frac{(1+\nu^2)^2}{\nu^2-1}$ and $-\frac{2\nu^2(1+\nu^2)}{\nu^2-1}$, so with $v=\frac{1+\nu^2}{\nu^2-1}\,u$ equation $(*)$ turns into \[(\nu^2-1)v^2-2\nu^2v+(\ell^2+\nu^2)=0,\] the same equation as for $\lambda=0$.

Which roots. $N'$ and $L$ are inside $S_{\lambda_P}$ (the value of $x^2+y^2+2\lambda_Py-1$ at $N'$ is $\frac{(1+\nu^2)^2}{\nu^2-1}<0$), so $P$, the intersection below $BC$, has $s_P<0$, i.e. $u_P<0$; then $v_P=\frac{1+\nu^2}{\nu^2-1}u_P>0$. The equation for $\lambda=0$ has roots of opposite signs (product $\frac{\ell^2+\nu^2}{\nu^2-1}<0$): the negative one, $u_*$, belongs to the lower intersection $Q^*$ of line $AL$ with $S_0$, and the positive one to the upper intersection. So $v_P$ is the positive root, and $u_*+v_P=\frac{2\nu^2}{\nu^2-1}$ (sum of roots).

Collinearity. Let $Q_0=(\ell s_*,\ \nu(1-s_*))$ be the reflection of $Q^*=Z(s_*)$ in $BC$; it lies on $S_0$, above $BC$. With $P=(\ell s_P,\nu(s_P-1))$ and $N=(0,1/\nu)$, the determinant of $P-N$ and $Q_0-N$ equals \[\ell\bigl[\nu(s_P+s_*)-2\nu s_Ps_*+(s_*-s_P)/\nu\bigr]=\frac{\ell\,s_Ps_*}{\nu}\Bigl[(\nu^2+1)u_P+(\nu^2-1)u_*-2\nu^2\Bigr] =\frac{\ell\,s_Ps_*(\nu^2-1)}{\nu}\Bigl[v_P+u_*-\frac{2\nu^2}{\nu^2-1}\Bigr]=0 .\] So $N,P,Q_0$ are collinear. ($\ell=0$ is the trivial isosceles case.)

$Q=Q_0$. Reflecting line $NQ_0$ in $BC$ gives the line through $Q^*$ and $(0,\nu)$; the latter is inside $S_0$ (as $\nu<1$), so the segment between them lies inside the disc and crosses $BC$ inside the segment $BC$. Hence line $PN$ meets $BC$ inside the disc of $S_0$, and it meets the circle $S_0$ once above $BC$ and once below. The point $Q$ is on line $PN$, on $S_0$ and above $BC$, so $Q=Q_0$.

Conclusion. $Q$ is the reflection of $Q^*\in$ line $LP$ in $BC$, so $\angle QLC=\angle Q^*LC=\angle PLC$ ($Q^*$ and $P$ lie on the same ray from $L$, both below $BC$). Thus $BC$ bisects $\angle PLQ$.

Problem 11.8

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Find all functions $f : (0, +\infty) \to (0, +\infty)$ such that for all positive $x, y$, \[yf(xf(x)) + xf(yf(y)) = f(x+y).\]

Show the answer

no such functions

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squeezing $f$ near $0$)

Answer: there is no such function. Suppose $f$ exists and put $k(t)=f(tf(t))>0$. The equation reads \[f(x+y)=y\,k(x)+x\,k(y)\qquad(x,y>0).\tag{1}\]

$k$ is bounded on every interval $(0,T]$. For $0<t\le T$ use $(1)$ with $x=t$, $y=2T-t\ge T$: $f(2T)=(2T-t)k(t)+t\,k(2T-t)>T\,k(t)$, so $k(t)<f(2T)/T$. Let $K$ be a bound for $k$ on $(0,1]$.

$f(s)\le Ks$ for $0<s\le2$. Take $x=y=\frac s2\le1$ in $(1)$: $f(s)=s\,k(\frac s2)\le Ks$.

$k(t)\le K^2t^2$ for small $t$. If $0<t\le t_0=\min\bigl(1,\sqrt{2/K}\bigr)$, then $tf(t)\le Kt^2\le2$, so $k(t)=f(tf(t))\le K\cdot tf(t)\le K^2t^2$.

Contradiction. Take $s=1$ and $0<x<t_0$ in $(1)$: $f(1)=(1-x)k(x)+x\,k(1-x)\le K^2x^2+Kx$, using $k\le K$ on $(0,1]$. Letting $x\to0$ gives $f(1)\le0$, impossible. Hence no function satisfies the equation.

Solution 2 (Two ways to split $x+y+z$)

Answer: there is no such function. Suppose $f$ exists and put $h(t)=f(tf(t))$. The equation reads \[f(x+y)=y\,h(x)+x\,h(y)\qquad(x,y>0).\tag{1}\]

An identity for $h$. For $x,y,z>0$ compute $f(x+y+z)$ by $(1)$ once as $f\bigl((x+y)+z\bigr)$ and once as $f\bigl(x+(y+z)\bigr)$: \[z\,h(x+y)+(x+y)\,h(z)=x\,h(y+z)+(y+z)\,h(x).\tag{2}\]

$h$ is affine. Fix $y>0$ and put $A=\frac{h(2y)-h(y)}{y}$. Taking $z=y$ in $(2)$ and dividing by $y$, \[h(x+y)=2h(x)+xA-h(y)\qquad(x>0).\tag{3}\] Applying $(3)$ first at $x+y$ and then at $x$, \[h(x+2y)=2h(x+y)+(x+y)A-h(y)=4h(x)+(3x+y)A-3h(y).\tag{4}\] On the other hand, $(2)$ with $2y$ in place of $y$ and $z=y$ gives $y\,h(x+2y)+(x+2y)h(y)=x\,h(3y)+3y\,h(x)$, that is, \[h(x+2y)=3h(x)+\frac{x\,h(3y)}{y}-\frac{(x+2y)\,h(y)}{y}.\tag{5}\] Subtracting $(5)$ from $(4)$ and solving for $h(x)$: \[h(x)=\Bigl(\frac{h(3y)-h(y)}{y}-3A\Bigr)x+\bigl(h(y)-yA\bigr)\qquad(x>0).\] With $y$ fixed, the brackets are constants: $h(x)=\alpha x+\beta$ for all $x>0$.

$f$ is linear. By $(1)$, $f(x+y)=y(\alpha x+\beta)+x(\alpha y+\beta)=2\alpha xy+\beta(x+y)$. For $s>0$, the choices $x=y=\frac s2$ and $x=\frac s4$, $y=\frac{3s}4$ give $\frac{\alpha s^2}2=\frac{3\alpha s^2}8$, so $\alpha=0$. Every $s>0$ is a sum of two positive numbers, so $f(s)=\beta s$ for all $s>0$, and $\beta>0$ because $f$ is positive.

Contradiction. Now $h(x)=f(xf(x))=f(\beta x^2)=\beta^2x^2$, while $h(x)=\beta$ for all $x$. At $x=1$ and $x=2$ this gives $\beta^2=\beta$ and $4\beta^2=\beta$, so $\beta=0$, which is impossible. Hence no function satisfies the equation.
