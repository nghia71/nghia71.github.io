Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Quadratic solitaire is a single-player game. To start the game, the player chooses two distinct nonzero integers $a$ and $b$ and writes the equation $x^2+ax+b=0$ on a blackboard. On each turn, if the equation currently written on the blackboard has two distinct nonzero integer solutions $x=u$ and $x=v$, then the player erases the equation and replaces it by one of the equations $x^2+ux+v=0$ and $x^2+vx+u=0$ of their choosing. Otherwise, the game ends. Determine all initial choices of $a$ and $b$ such that quadratic solitaire can be played forever.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(1,-2)$ only

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (The constant term never increases)

Write the equation on the board after $t$ turns as $x^2+p_tx+q_t=0$, so $(p_0,q_0)=(a,b)$. If the game does not end at turn $t$, the roots $r,s$ are distinct nonzero integers with $r+s=-p_t$ and $rs=q_t$ (Vieta), and $(p_{t+1},q_{t+1})$ is $(r,s)$ or $(s,r)$.

The monovariant. Since $|r|,|s|\ge1$, \[|q_{t+1}|\le\max(|r|,|s|)\le|r|\,|s|=|q_t|,\] with equality only if one root has absolute value $1$ and $q_{t+1}$ is the other root. Note $q_t\ne0$ always.

The eventual behaviour. Suppose the game lasts forever. The positive integers $|q_t|$ never increase, so from some turn $N$ on they are all equal to some $m$. For $t\ge N$ the equality case holds: the roots at turn $t$ are $\varepsilon_t$ and $q_{t+1}$ with $\varepsilon_t=\pm1$ and $|q_{t+1}|=m$, and $p_{t+1}=\varepsilon_t$. If $m=1$ the roots would be $1$ and $-1$, giving $p_t=0$; but $p_t=\varepsilon_{t-1}=\pm1$ for $t\ge N+1$. So $m\ge2$.

Now take $t\ge N+1$. The equation is $x^2+\varepsilon x+q=0$ with $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{t-1}=\pm1$, $|q|=m$, and its roots are $\varepsilon'=\pm1$ and $q'$ with $|q'|=m$. Vieta gives $\varepsilon'+q'=-\varepsilon$, so $m=|q'|=|\varepsilon+\varepsilon'|\le2$. Hence $m=2$, $\varepsilon'=\varepsilon$ and $q'=-2\varepsilon$; then $q=\varepsilon'q'=-2$. So $q_t=-2$ for every $t\ge N+1$, and from $q'=-2\varepsilon=-2$ we get $\varepsilon=1$. Thus the equation is $x^2+x-2=0$ at every turn $t\ge N+1$.

Going backwards. If the equation at turn $t+1$ is $x^2+x-2=0$, then the two roots at turn $t$ are $1$ and $-2$, so the equation at turn $t$ was $(x-1)(x+2)=x^2+x-2=0$ as well. By backward induction the equation was $x^2+x-2=0$ from the very start, i.e. $(a,b)=(1,-2)$.

It works. $x^2+x-2=(x-1)(x+2)$ has the distinct nonzero roots $1$ and $-2$, and choosing $x^2+1\cdot x+(-2)=0$ writes the same equation again, forever. Hence the only initial choice is $\boxed{(a,b)=(1,-2)}$.

Solution 2 (The sum of squares of the coefficients)

Write the equation on the board as $x^2+sx+t=0$. Suppose the game lasts forever, and look at a turn where the roots are the distinct nonzero integers $u,v$; by Vieta, $s=-(u+v)$ and $t=uv$, and the next equation has coefficients $(u,v)$ or $(v,u)$.

No turn has $uv=-1$. Then $\{u,v\}=\{1,-1\}$, and the next equation is $x^2+x-1=0$ or $x^2-x+1=0$, with discriminants $5$ and $-3$; neither has two integer roots, so the game would stop.

A monovariant. Let $Q=s^2+t^2$. The next equation has $Q'=u^2+v^2$, and \[Q-Q'=(u+v)^2+u^2v^2-u^2-v^2=u^2v^2+2uv=(uv+1)^2-1 .\] As $uv+1$ is an integer different from $0$, this is $\ge0$, with equality exactly when $uv+1=-1$ (note $uv\neq0$), i.e. $uv=-2$.

The stable phase. The non-negative integers $Q$ never increase, so from some turn $N$ on $Q$ stays constant and every turn has $uv=-2$, so $\{u,v\}=\{1,-2\}$ or $\{-1,2\}$. From $\{-1,2\}$ the possible next equations are $x^2-x+2=0$ (no real roots) and $x^2+2x-1=0$ (irrational roots), so the game would stop. From $\{1,-2\}$ the options are $x^2-2x+1=0$ (a double root, the game stops) and $x^2+x-2=0$. Hence for $t\ge N+1$ the board shows $x^2+x-2=0$.

Back to the start. If the board shows $x^2+x-2=0$ at some turn $t\ge1$, then at turn $t-1$ the roots were $1$ and $-2$, so the equation was $(x-1)(x+2)=x^2+x-2=0$ as well. By backward induction the game started with $x^2+x-2=0$, i.e. $(a,b)=(1,-2)$; and this start indeed allows playing forever (rewrite $x^2+x-2=0$ every turn). So the answer is $\boxed{(a,b)=(1,-2)}$.

Solution 3 (A cycle of states and the product of the coefficients)

Write the state $(a,b)$ when the equation $x^2+ax+b=0$ is on the board. A move from $(a,b)$ is possible exactly when this equation has two distinct nonzero integer roots $u,v$, and it leads to $(u,v)$ or to $(v,u)$. So every state that occurs has two distinct nonzero integer entries (the first one by the choice of $a,b$, the later ones because they are distinct nonzero roots).

$(1,-2)$ works. The equation $x^2+x-2=0$ has the roots $1$ and $-2$, so the player can write $x^2+x-2=0$ again, forever.

Now suppose that a play $s_0=(a_0,b_0),\,s_1=(a_1,b_1),\,s_2,\dots$ never ends.

Step 1: $s_0$ lies on a cycle. If $s_{i+1}=(u,v)$, then $u,v$ are nonzero integers, so $|u|\le|uv|$ and $|v|\le|uv|$, that is, $|a_{i+1}|,|b_{i+1}|\le|b_i|$. Hence $|b_i|\le|b_0|$ and $|a_i|\le|b_0|$ for all $i\ge1$: only finitely many states occur, and some state repeats. Each state has at most one possible predecessor: if $s_{i+1}=(a,b)$, then $a$ and $b$ are the roots of the equation of $s_i$, so by Vieta's formulas $s_i=(-(a+b),\,ab)$. Take a repetition $s_i=s_j$ with $i<j$ and $i$ as small as possible. If $i\ge1$, then $s_{i-1}=s_{j-1}$, as both are the predecessor of the same state; this contradicts the choice of $i$. Hence $s_0=s_p$ for some $p\ge1$; below we only use the states $s_0,s_1,\dots,s_p$.

Step 2: every $a_i$ is $\pm1$. The roots of the equation of $s_i$ are $a_{i+1}$ and $b_{i+1}$, so $b_i=a_{i+1}b_{i+1}$. Multiplying the absolute values for $i=0,1,\dots,p-1$ and using $s_p=s_0$, \[\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}|b_i|=\prod_{i=1}^{p}|a_i|\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{p}|b_i|=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}|a_i|\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}|b_i| .\] All $b_i$ are nonzero, so $\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}|a_i|=1$, and since the $a_i$ are nonzero integers, $|a_i|=1$ for $0\le i\le p-1$.

Step 3: every $a_i$ is $1$. Let $0\le i\le p-1$ and suppose $a_i=-1$. The roots of the equation of $s_i$ have sum $-a_i=1$, and one of them is $a_{i+1}$, which is $\pm1$ by Step 2 (note $s_p=s_0$). If $a_{i+1}=1$, the other root is $0$, which is impossible. If $a_{i+1}=-1$, the other root is $b_{i+1}=2$, so $s_{i+1}=(-1,2)$; but $x^2-x+2$ has discriminant $1-8<0$, so the game would end at $s_{i+1}$, a contradiction. Hence $a_i=1$.

Step 4. The roots of the equation of $s_0$ have sum $-a_0=-1$, and one of them is $a_1=1$; so the other one is $b_1=-2$ and $s_1=(1,-2)$. Its only possible predecessor is $(-(1-2),\,1\cdot(-2))=(1,-2)$, and $s_0$ is a predecessor of $s_1$, so $s_0=(1,-2)$.

Hence quadratic solitaire can be played forever exactly for $\boxed{(a,b)=(1,-2)}$.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle. Let $I_B$ and $I_C$ be the excenters of triangle $ABC$ opposite $B$ and $C$, respectively. Let $E$ be a point on line $BA$ such that $\angle EI_BB=90^\circ$. Let $F$ be a point on line $CA$ such that $\angle CI_CF=90^\circ$. Let the circle with center $I_B$ and radius $I_BE$ intersect the segment $I_BB$ at $X$. Let the circle with center $I_C$ and radius $I_CF$ intersect the segment $I_CC$ at $Y$. Prove that $BY$ is perpendicular to $CX$.

The excenter of a triangle $ABC$ opposite vertex $B$ is the center of the circle that is tangent to line segment $AC$, to ray $BA$ beyond $A$, and to ray $BC$ beyond $C$. The excenter opposite $C$ is similarly defined.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Computing two tangents)

Let $a=BC$, $s$ the semiperimeter, $r$ the inradius, $r_b$ the radius of the excircle opposite $B$, and put \[t_B=\tan\tfrac B2,\qquad t_C=\tan\tfrac C2 .\] Since the triangle is acute, $\tfrac B2,\tfrac C2<45^\circ$, so $0<t_B,t_C<1$.

Two standard facts. (a) The excircle opposite $B$ touches line $BC$ at a point $T$ with $BT=s$, and $BI_B$ bisects angle $B$; in the right triangle $BTI_B$ this gives $BI_B\cos\tfrac B2=s$ and $r_b=s\,t_B$. (b) $t_Bt_C=\dfrac{s-a}{s}$. Indeed $t_B=\dfrac r{s-b}$, $t_C=\dfrac r{s-c}$, and Heron's formula gives $r^2s=(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)$.

Locating $X$. In triangle $BI_BE$ the angle at $I_B$ is right and the angle at $B$ is $\tfrac B2$ (the angle between line $BA$ and the bisector $BI_B$), so $I_BX=I_BE=BI_B\,t_B<BI_B$ and $X$ lies on segment $BI_B$ with $BX=BI_B(1-t_B)$. Place $B$ at the origin and $C=(a,0)$, with $A$ above the $x$-axis. As $X$ lies on the bisector of angle $B$, \[X=BX\bigl(\cos\tfrac B2,\ \sin\tfrac B2\bigr)=s(1-t_B)\,\bigl(1,\ t_B\bigr)\] by fact (a). Using fact (b), \[a-s(1-t_B)=s\,t_B-(s-a)=s\,t_B-s\,t_Bt_C=s\,t_B(1-t_C)>0 .\] So $X$ lies above $BC$ and its projection on $BC$ lies between $B$ and $C$, and \[\tan\angle XCB=\frac{s\,t_B(1-t_B)}{s\,t_B(1-t_C)}=\frac{1-t_B}{1-t_C}.\]

The same for $Y$. Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$ (the construction of $Y$ from $I_C$ and $F$ is the mirror image of the construction of $X$), $Y$ lies above $BC$ and \[\tan\angle YBC=\frac{1-t_C}{1-t_B}.\]

Conclusion. The two acute angles $\angle XCB$ and $\angle YBC$ have tangents whose product is $1$, so $\angle XCB+\angle YBC=90^\circ$. The rays $BY$ and $CX$ both point into the half-plane above $BC$, so they meet at a point $Z$, and in triangle $ZBC$ the angle at $Z$ is $180^\circ-90^\circ=90^\circ$. Hence $BY\perp CX$.

Solution 2 (Similar triangles on the circle with diameter $I_BI_C$)

Write $\beta=\angle B$ and $s$ for the semiperimeter. The line $BI_B$ is the internal and $BI_C$ the external bisector of angle $B$, so $\angle I_CBI_B=90^\circ$; likewise $\angle I_BCI_C=90^\circ$. Hence $B$ and $C$ lie on the circle with diameter $I_BI_C$. Both excenters lie on the external bisector of angle $A$, on opposite sides of $A$, and both lie on the same side of $BC$ as $A$.

Where $E$ and $F$ are. In triangle $BI_BE$ the angle at $I_B$ is right, so the angle at $B$ is acute and $E$ lies on ray $BA$ (the ray making the angle $\frac\beta2$ with $BI_B$). The excircle opposite $B$ touches line $BC$ at distance $s$ from $B$, so $BI_B\cos\frac\beta2=s$ and $BE=BI_B/\cos\frac\beta2=s/\cos^2\frac\beta2>s>BA$. So $E$ lies on ray $BA$ beyond $A$, and in the same way $F$ lies on ray $CA$ beyond $A$.

Two pairs of similar triangles. Since $A$ lies between $B$ and $E$ and between $I_C$ and $I_B$, the angles $\angle BAI_C$ and $\angle EAI_B$ are vertical angles. Moreover $\angle ABI_C=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$ (the external bisector halves the angle $180^\circ-\beta$ between ray $BA$ and the extension of $CB$ beyond $B$), and $\angle AEI_B=\angle BEI_B=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$. So triangles $ABI_C$ and $AEI_B$ are similar and \[\frac{BI_C}{EI_B}=\frac{AI_C}{AI_B}.\] Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$, triangles $ACI_B$ and $AFI_C$ are similar and $\dfrac{CI_B}{FI_C}=\dfrac{AI_B}{AI_C}$. Multiplying, and using $I_BX=I_BE$, $I_CY=I_CF$, \[I_BX\cdot I_CY=BI_C\cdot CI_B,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\frac{I_CB}{I_CY}=\frac{I_BX}{I_BC}. \tag{1}\]

A rotation by $90^\circ$. As $Y$ lies on segment $I_CC$, $X$ on segment $I_BB$, and $I_B,I_C$ lie on the same arc $BC$ of the circle with diameter $I_BI_C$, \[\angle BI_CY=\angle BI_CC=\angle BI_BC=\angle XI_BC .\] With (1), triangles $BI_CY$ and $XI_BC$ are similar (SAS), with $B\mapsto X$, $I_C\mapsto I_B$, $Y\mapsto C$. They have the same orientation: $BI_CY$ has the orientation of $BI_CC$, $XI_BC$ that of $BI_BC$, and these agree because $I_C$ and $I_B$ are on the same side of $BC$. So a spiral similarity maps $B,I_C,Y$ to $X,I_B,C$. Its angle of rotation is the angle from $\overrightarrow{I_CB}$ to $\overrightarrow{I_BX}$, which is $\pm90^\circ$ because $I_CB\perp BI_B$. It maps segment $BY$ to segment $XC$, hence $BY\perp CX$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

The sequence of triples of positive integers $(a_0,b_0,c_0)$, $(a_1,b_1,c_1)$, $\ldots$, $(a_{2025},b_{2025},c_{2025})$ satisfies \[(a_{i+1},b_{i+1},c_{i+1})=\bigl(a_i+\gcd(b_i,c_i),\,b_i+\gcd(c_i,a_i),\,c_i+\gcd(a_i,b_i)\bigr)\] for $0\leqslant i\leqslant 2024$. Determine the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$.

Here $\gcd(x,y)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of integers $x$ and $y$.

Show the answer

$3\cdot2^{675}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Halving the triple and counting the halvings)

Write $T_t=(a_t,b_t,c_t)$ and $\Phi(a,b,c)=\bigl(a+\gcd(b,c),\,b+\gcd(c,a),\,c+\gcd(a,b)\bigr)$, so $T_{t+1}=\Phi(T_t)$. Note $\Phi(2a,2b,2c)=2\Phi(a,b,c)$. Let $N=2025$.

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$, $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. For any $g$, \[g(3,5,6)\ \mapsto\ g(4,8,7)\ \mapsto\ g(5,9,11)\ \mapsto\ g(6,10,12)=2g\,(3,5,6),\] so $T_{3m}=2^m(3,5,6)$ for all $m\ge2$, and $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}=3\cdot2^{675}$.

Parity lemma. Call a triple reduced if not all its entries are even. For a reduced triple $r$: if $r$ has exactly one even entry, $\Phi(r)$ has exactly two; if $r$ has exactly two even entries, $\Phi(r)$ is all odd; if $r$ is all odd, $\Phi(r)$ is all even. (A gcd is even only if both arguments are even; e.g. for $a$ even and $b,c$ odd all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ gives odd, even, even.) In particular $\Phi(r)$ is all even if and only if $r$ is all odd.

Reduced sequence. Let $2^{e_t}$ be the largest power of $2$ dividing all of $a_t,b_t,c_t$, $r_t=T_t/2^{e_t}$ (reduced) and $x_t=a_t/2^{e_t}\ge1$, so $a_t=2^{e_t}x_t$. Since $T_{t+1}=2^{e_t}\Phi(r_t)$, the parity lemma gives: if $r_t$ is not all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t$ and $x_{t+1}=x_t+g\ge x_t+1$ ($g$ a gcd); if $r_t$ is all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t+\delta$ with $\delta\ge1$ and $x_{t+1}=(x_t+g)/2^\delta$.

Let $t_1<\dots<t_K$ be the times $t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}$ with $r_t$ all odd, $\delta_i$ the corresponding increments, $d_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, $s=N-1-t_K$ and $y_i=x_{t_i+1}$. By the parity lemma (one even entry $\to$ two $\to$ none) we have $t_1\le2$, $d_i\le3$ and $s\le2$. Hence $2024=t_1+\sum d_i+s\le2+3(K-1)+2$, so $K\ge675$, and $e_N=\sum\delta_i\ge K\ge675$. Put \[D=(2-t_1)+\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)=3K-2025+s\ \ (\ge0).\] Also $x_N\ge y_K+s$, $y_1\ge\frac{x_{t_1}+1}{2^{\delta_1}}\ge\frac{t_1+2}{2^{\delta_1}}$ (as $x_{t_1}\ge x_0+t_1$), and \[y_{i+1}\ge\frac{x_{t_{i+1}}+1}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}\ge\frac{y_i+d_i}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}. \tag{1}\] If $\delta_{j+1}=\dots=\delta_i=1$, the shortfalls $z_i=3-y_i$ satisfy $z_{i+1}\le\frac{z_i+(3-d_i)}{2}$, hence \[z_i\le\frac{z_j}{2^{\,i-j}}+\frac12\sum_{j\le l<i}(3-d_l). \tag{2}\] Since $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N$, it suffices to exclude $e_N=675$ with $x_N\le2$ and $e_N=676$ with $x_N=1$.

$e_N=675$. Then $K=675$, all $\delta_i=1$ and $D=s$. By (2) with $j=1$ and $z_1\le2-\frac{t_1}2\le2$: $z_K\le2^{-673}+\frac12\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)\le2^{-673}+\frac s2$. So $x_N\ge y_K+s=3-z_K+s\ge3-2^{-673}+\frac s2>2$, i.e. $x_N\ge3$.

$e_N=676$, $K=676$, all $\delta_i=1$. Now $D=3+s$ and (2) gives $z_K\le2^{-674}+\frac{3+s}2$, so $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge\frac32-2^{-674}+\frac s2>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

$e_N=676$, $K=675$, one $\delta_j=2$. Here $D=s$. If $j<K$: $y_j\ge1$, so $z_j\le2$ and (2) from $j$ gives $z_K\le1+\frac s2$, hence $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge2+\frac s2\ge2$. If $j=K$ and $s\ge1$: $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge1+1=2$. If $j=K$ and $s=0$: then $D=0$, so $t_1=2$ and all $d_i=3$; by (2) (all earlier $\delta=1$) $z_{K-1}<1$, so $y_{K-1}\ge3$, $x_{t_K}\ge y_{K-1}+2\ge5$ and $x_N=y_K\ge\frac{5+1}{4}>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

In every case $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N\ge3\cdot2^{675}$. Hence the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

Solution 2 (Doubling every three steps)

Write $T_t=(a_t,b_t,c_t)$ and $\Phi(a,b,c)=\bigl(a+\gcd(b,c),\,b+\gcd(c,a),\,c+\gcd(a,b)\bigr)$, so that $T_{t+1}=\Phi(T_t)$. For every positive integer $g$ we have $\Phi(ga,gb,gc)=g\,\Phi(a,b,c)$.

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$ and $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. For every $g$, \[g(3,5,6)\ \mapsto\ g(4,8,7)\ \mapsto\ g(5,9,11)\ \mapsto\ g(6,10,12)=2g\,(3,5,6),\] so $T_{3j}=2^j(3,5,6)$ for all $j\ge2$, and $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}=3\cdot2^{675}$.

Parity. A gcd of two integers is even only if both integers are even. Count the even entries of a triple. If exactly one entry is even, all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes that entry odd and the two odd entries even: two even entries. If exactly two entries are even, the gcd of the two even ones is even and the other two gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes all three entries odd. If all entries are odd, all gcds are odd and $\Phi$ makes all entries even. If all entries are even, so are all gcds and all new entries. So the number of even entries changes by $1\to2\to0\to3$ and $3\to3$; in particular, whatever triple $R$ of positive integers we start with, all entries of $\Phi(\Phi(\Phi(R)))$ are even.

Powers of two. We show by induction on $j$ that $2^j$ divides every entry of $T_t$ for $3j\le t\le 3j+3$. Suppose $T_{3j}=2^jR$ for a triple $R$ of positive integers (true for $j=0$). Then $T_{3j+i}=2^j\Phi^i(R)$ for $i=0,1,2,3$, and all entries of $\Phi^3(R)$ are even, so $T_{3j+3}=2^{j+1}R'$ for a triple $R'$ of positive integers. This proves the claim.

Lower bound. For $3j\le t\le3j+2$ the numbers $b_t,c_t$ are multiples of $2^j$, so $\gcd(b_t,c_t)\ge2^j$ and $a_{t+1}\ge a_t+2^j$. Hence \[a_{3j+3}\ge a_{3j}+3\cdot2^j\qquad(j\ge0).\] With $j=0$ and $j=1$: $a_3\ge a_0+3\ge4$ and $a_6\ge a_3+6\ge10$. But $a_6$ is a multiple of $2^2=4$, so $a_6\ge12=3\cdot2^2$. If $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for some $j\ge2$, then $a_{3j+3}\ge3\cdot2^j+3\cdot2^j=3\cdot2^{j+1}$. By induction $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for all $j\ge2$; in particular $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}\ge3\cdot2^{675}$.

Together with the construction, the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $n$ and $k$ be positive integers such that $n<k<2n$. Suppose $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ are positive integers such that $2^{a_1}+2^{a_2}+\cdots+2^{a_k}$ is divisible by $2^n-1$.

Show that at least three of $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ have the same remainder when divided by $n$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reading off binary digits)

Since $2^n\equiv1\pmod{2^n-1}$, the residue of $2^a$ modulo $2^n-1$ depends only on $a\bmod n$: if $a\equiv r\pmod n$ with $0\le r\le n-1$, then $2^a\equiv2^r$. For $0\le r\le n-1$ let $c_r$ be the number of indices $i$ with $a_i\equiv r\pmod n$. Then $\sum_r c_r=k$ and \[S:=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}c_r2^r\equiv2^{a_1}+\cdots+2^{a_k}\equiv0\pmod{2^n-1}.\] Suppose, for contradiction, that every $c_r\le2$. Then $0<S\le2(2^0+\cdots+2^{n-1})=2(2^n-1)$, with equality only if all $c_r=2$, i.e. $k=2n$, which is excluded. So $S$ is a positive multiple of $2^n-1$ below $2(2^n-1)$, i.e. $S=2^n-1$.

Claim. If $c_0,\dots,c_{m-1}\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $\sum_{r<m}c_r2^r=2^m-1$, then all $c_r=1$. Induction on $m$; $m=1$ is clear. For $m\ge2$, the right side is odd, so $c_0$ is odd, $c_0=1$. Subtracting $1$ and halving gives $\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}c_r2^{r-1}=2^{m-1}-1$, and the induction hypothesis gives $c_1=\cdots=c_{m-1}=1$.

By the claim with $m=n$, every $c_r=1$, so $k=\sum c_r=n$, contradicting $k>n$. Hence some residue class contains at least three of the $a_i$.

Solution 2 (A number and its complement)

As $2^n\equiv1\pmod{2^n-1}$, replace each $a_i$ by its remainder $r_i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ modulo $n$; this does not change the sum modulo $2^n-1$. Assume no remainder occurs three times. Let $U$ be the set of remainders that occur at least once and $W\subseteq U$ the set of those occurring twice, and put \[A=\sum_{r\in U}2^r,\qquad B=\sum_{r\in W}2^r .\] Then $k=|U|+|W|$ and $2^n-1\mid A+B$. Both $A$ and $B$ are at most $2^n-1$, and they are not both equal to $2^n-1$ (that would mean $k=2n$); also $A+B\ge k>0$. Hence $A+B=2^n-1$.

Now $2^n-1$ is the $n$-bit number $11\ldots1$ in binary, and $A$ is an $n$-bit number whose $1$-bits are the positions in $U$. So $B=(2^n-1)-A$ is the number whose $1$-bits are exactly the positions not in $U$ (subtracting from $11\ldots1$ produces no borrows). But the $1$-bits of $B$ are the positions in $W\subseteq U$. A set contained both in $U$ and in its complement is empty, so $W=\varnothing$, $B=0$ and $A=2^n-1$, i.e. $U$ is everything. Then $k=|U|=n$, contradicting $n<k$. So some remainder occurs at least three times.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

There is a row of $n$ paddocks, labelled from left to right with the integers $1$ to $n$, where $n\geq 3$. Skippy the Kangaroo grazes in the paddock according to the following rule:

If Skippy is grazing in paddock $k$, then she makes a sequence of $k$ hops from a paddock to an adjacent paddock. The first of the $k$ hops is always to the right, unless Skippy is in paddock $n$, in which case it is to the left. Each of the following $k-1$ hops is in the same direction as the previous hop, unless Skippy is in paddock $1$ or $n$. Skippy grazes in the paddock that she is in after the $k^{\text{th}}$ hop.

For example, if $n=8$ and Skippy is grazing in paddock $3$, the next $4$ paddocks she will graze in are $6,4,8,2$, in this order. Skippy continues grazing in this way indefinitely. A paddock is overgrazed if Skippy eventually grazes in it, regardless of which paddock she starts in. Determine all $n\geq 3$ for which there exists exactly one overgrazed paddock.

Show the answer

$n=3\cdot2^a$ with $a\ge0$ (i.e. $n=3,6,12,24,\dots$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Folding the doubling map)

Let $f(k)$ be the paddock after grazing in paddock $k$.

The map $f$. If $2k\le n$, the $k$ hops go straight right and $f(k)=2k$. If $\frac n2<k\le n-1$, Skippy reaches $n$ after $n-k$ hops, turns, and makes $2k-n\le n-2$ more hops to the left, so $f(k)=2n-2k$. If $k=n$, she hops left $n-1$ times to paddock $1$ and then once to the right: $f(n)=2$. For an integer $x$ let $\|x\|$ be the distance from $x$ to the nearest multiple of $2n$. Then \[f(k)=\|2k\|\quad(1\le k\le n-1),\qquad f(n)=2 .\] Since $\|2\|y\|\|=\|2y\|$ for every integer $y$, induction gives: if none of $k,f(k),\dots,f^{j-1}(k)$ equals $n$, then $f^j(k)=\|2^jk\|$.

Reformulation. Every orbit of $f$ ends in a cycle. If there are two different cycles, a start on one never visits the other, so no paddock is overgrazed. If there is exactly one cycle, its paddocks are exactly the overgrazed ones (every orbit enters it and runs around it; a paddock off the cycle is missed by a start on the cycle). So there is exactly one overgrazed paddock if and only if $f$ has a fixed point and no other cycle.

Fixed points. $f(n)=2\ne n$. For $k\le n-1$, $\|2k\|=k$ means $2k\equiv\pm k\pmod{2n}$; $k\equiv0$ is impossible, and $3k\equiv0\pmod{2n}$ with $1\le k\le n-1$ means $3\mid n$ and $k=\frac{2n}3$. So we need $3\mid n$; write $n=3\cdot2^a\cdot b$ with $b$ odd, and $k^*=\frac{2n}3=2^{a+1}b$.

If $b>1$, there is a second cycle. Let $k=2^{a+1}<k^*$. As long as the orbit avoids $n$, \[f^j(k)=\|2^{a+1}\cdot2^j\|=2^{a+1}\cdot\|2^j\|_{3b},\] where $\|\cdot\|_{3b}$ is the distance to the nearest multiple of $3b$ (because $2n=2^{a+1}\cdot3b$). These values are multiples of $2^{a+1}$, while $n=2^a\cdot3b$ is not (as $3b$ is odd), so by induction the orbit indeed never meets $n$ and the formula stays valid. As $3b$ is odd, $2^r\equiv1\pmod{3b}$ for some $r\ge1$, and then $f^r(k)=2^{a+1}=k$. So $k$ lies on a cycle different from the fixed point $\{k^*\}$, and no paddock is overgrazed.

If $b=1$, every orbit reaches $k^*=2^{a+1}$. Now $2n=3\cdot2^{a+1}$.

  • If $3\nmid k$: the orbit never meets $n$ before step $a+1$, since $\|2^ik\|=n$ would mean $2^ik\equiv n\pmod{2n}$, forcing $3\mid 2^ik$. Hence $f^{a+1}(k)=\|2^{a+1}k\|$, and $2^{a+1}k\equiv2^{a+1}$ or $2\cdot2^{a+1}\pmod{3\cdot2^{a+1}}$, so in both cases $f^{a+1}(k)=2^{a+1}=k^*$.

  • If $3\mid k$: if the orbit avoided $n$ for $a+1$ steps we would get $f^{a+1}(k)=\|2^{a+1}k\|=0$, which is not a paddock. So the orbit reaches $n$, then $2$, and $3\nmid2$, so by the first case it reaches $k^*$.

So $\{k^*\}$ is the only cycle and $k^*$ is the unique overgrazed paddock.

Therefore exactly one paddock is overgrazed if and only if $\boxed{n=3\cdot2^a,\ a\ge0}$.

Solution 2 (Halving the board)

Let $f_n(k)$ be the paddock after paddock $k$ when there are $n$ paddocks. If $k\le\frac n2$, the $k$ hops go straight right and $f_n(k)=2k$. If $\frac n2<k\le n-1$, Skippy reaches $n$ after $n-k$ hops and makes the other $2k-n\le n-2$ hops to the left, so $f_n(k)=2n-2k$. From $n$ she hops left $n-1$ times and then once right, so $f_n(n)=2$. All values of $f_n$ are even.

Every orbit ends in a cycle. If $f_n$ has two different cycles, a start on one never visits the other, and no paddock is overgrazed; if it has exactly one cycle, the overgrazed paddocks are exactly its paddocks. Call $n$ good if exactly one paddock is overgrazed, i.e. $f_n$ has exactly one cycle and this cycle is a fixed point.

Fixed points. $f_n(n)=2\ne n$; for $k\le\frac n2$, $2k\ne k$; for $\frac n2<k<n$, $2n-2k=k$ means $k=\frac{2n}3$. So $f_n$ has a fixed point only if $3\mid n$. In particular no power of $2$ is good.

Odd $n$. Let $n\ge3$ be odd and $E=\{2,4,\dots,n-1\}$. Then $f_n$ maps $E$ onto $E$: for $2c\in E$, if $c$ is even then $c\in E$ and $f_n(c)=2c$; if $c$ is odd then $k=n-c$ is even with $\frac n2<k\le n-1$, so $k\in E$ and $f_n(k)=2c$. A map of a finite set onto itself is a bijection, so every point of $E$ lies on a cycle; since every orbit is in $E$ after one step, the cycle points are exactly the points of $E$. Hence an odd $n$ is good if and only if $|E|=\frac{n-1}2=1$, i.e. $n=3$ (and indeed $f_3(2)=2$).

Doubling. Let $m\ge3$. For $1\le k\le m-1$ we have $f_{2m}(2k)=2f_m(k)$: if $k\le\frac m2$ both sides equal $4k$, and if $\frac m2<k\le m-1$ both sides equal $4m-4k$. Also $f_{2m}(2m)=2$ and $f_m(m)=2$, $f_m(1)=2$. Every cycle of $f_{2m}$ consists of even numbers.

  • A cycle of $f_m$ not containing $m$ doubles to a cycle of $f_{2m}$ of the same length not containing $2m$, and conversely every cycle of $f_{2m}$ not containing $2m$ halves to such a cycle of $f_m$.

  • A cycle of $f_m$ through $m$ reads $m\to2\to f_m(2)\to\dots\to m$ and has length at least $2$. In $f_{2m}$ we get $2m\to2\to4\to2f_m(2)\to\dots\to2m$ (as $f_{2m}(2)=2f_m(1)=4$): the doubled cycle with an extra point $2$ inserted after $2m$. Conversely a cycle of $f_{2m}$ through $2m$ continues $2m\to2\to4$, and deleting the point $2$ and halving gives a cycle of $f_m$ through $m$.

These correspondences are inverse to each other, so $f_m$ and $f_{2m}$ have the same number of cycles, and the fixed points correspond (a fixed point of $f_m$ is never $m$, and cycles through $m$ correspond to cycles of length at least $3$). Hence, for $m\ge3$, $2m$ is good if and only if $m$ is good.

Conclusion. Write $n=2^a b$ with $b$ odd. If $b=1$, $n$ is a power of $2$ and is not good. If $b\ge3$, all the numbers $b,2b,\dots,2^{a-1}b$ are at least $3$, so by doubling $n$ is good if and only if $b$ is good, i.e. $b=3$. So exactly one paddock is overgrazed if and only if $\boxed{n=3\cdot2^a,\ a\ge0}$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $P$ be a regular $100$-gon. An Aussie triangulation is formed by dividing $P$ into a set $T$ of non-overlapping triangles such that:

  • there are exactly $2025$ different points, including the $100$ vertices of $P$, that are a vertex of at least one triangle in $T$, and

  • no three of these $2025$ points are collinear.

A quokkalateral is a convex quadrilateral that consists of two triangles in $T$ sharing a common side. Two quokkalaterals may share a triangle in $T$. Determine the minimum number of quokkalaterals among all Aussie triangulations.

Show the answer

$1011$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Arrows on non-convex edges, then a near-extremal construction)

Let $m=100$ be the number of vertices of $P$ and $n=2025$ the number of points. Points that are not vertices of $P$ are inner points; edges of triangles that are not sides of $P$ are inner edges.

Counting. The angles of all triangles add up to $360^\circ$ at each of the $n-m$ inner points plus the angle sum $(m-2)180^\circ$ of $P$, so the number of triangles is $|T|=2(n-m)+m-2=2n-m-2=3948$. Each triangle has three sides; sides of $P$ are counted once and inner edges twice, so the number of inner edges is $e=\frac{3|T|-m}2=3n-2m-3=5872$.

Arrows. An inner edge $xy$ is a side of exactly two triangles $xyz$ and $xyw$, and $z,w$ lie on opposite sides of line $xy$. The quadrilateral $xzyw$ has angles less than $180^\circ$ at $z$ and $w$; its angles at $x$ and $y$ are sums of two triangle angles, and they cannot both exceed $180^\circ$ (the four angles sum to $360^\circ$); none equals $180^\circ$ since no three points are collinear. So $xzyw$ is a quokkalateral unless exactly one of the angles at $x$, $y$ exceeds $180^\circ$; in that case we draw an arrow on the edge pointing to that endpoint, its head. Hence \[q=e-(\text{number of arrows}),\] (different inner edges give different pairs of triangles, hence different quokkalaterals) where $q$ is the number of quokkalaterals.

Where arrows point.

  • A vertex of $P$ is never a head: all triangle angles there add up to an angle of $P$, which is less than $180^\circ$.

  • If an inner point $y$ is the head of two edges $yu$, $yv$, these edges are neighbours in the cyclic order of edges around $y$: otherwise the two pairs of angles adjacent to $yu$ and to $yv$ would be disjoint, and together they would exceed $360^\circ$.

  • Consequently an inner point is the head of at most $3$ arrows, and of $3$ only if it has exactly $3$ edges (three edges pairwise neighbouring).

Let $V_j$ be the set of inner points that are heads of exactly $j$ arrows ($j=0,1,2,3$). The number of arrows is $3|V_3|+2|V_2|+|V_1|$, and \[3|V_3|+3|V_2|+2|V_1|\le3(n-m). \tag{1}\] Call a triangle special if two of its sides are arrows with a common head. A point of $V_3$ lies in $3$ special triangles (its three triangles), and a point of $V_2$ in one (the triangle between its two arrows, which are neighbours). A triangle cannot be special for two different vertices, because every side carries at most one arrow and the two arrows of a special triangle already use two of its three sides with the same head. Therefore \[3|V_3|+|V_2|\le|T|=2n-m-2. \tag{2}\] Adding (1) and (2): $2(3|V_3|+2|V_2|+|V_1|)\le5n-4m-2$, so there are at most $\frac{5n-4m-2}2$ arrows and \[q\ge(3n-2m-3)-\frac{5n-4m-2}{2}=\frac{n-4}2=\frac{2021}{2}.\] As $q$ is an integer, $q\ge1011$.

  • 1. Draw the $97$ diagonals $A_1A_3,A_1A_4,\dots,A_1A_{99}$. In each triangle $A_1A_iA_{i+1}$ $(3\le i\le99)$ put one inner point and join it to the three corners ($97$ points).

Draw the path $A_1B_1B_2\dots B_{914}A_3$, join $A_2$ to every $B_j$, and join $A_3$ to every $B_j$. Solution 2 (Removing the degree-three points, and inserting points two at a time)

Let $h=100$ be the number of vertices of $P$, $I=2025-h=1925$ the number of the other points, and call these $I$ points inner points. Since no three of the $2025$ points are collinear, no point lies inside a side of a triangle of $T$, so two triangles of $T$ meet in a common side, a common vertex, or not at all.

Counting. Let $E$ be the number of segments that are sides of triangles of $T$, and $t$ the number of triangles. Euler's formula for the plane graph formed by the points and these segments gives $2025-E+(t+1)=2$. Every triangle has $3$ sides, every side of $P$ belongs to one triangle and every other segment to two, so $3t=2E-h$. Hence $E=2h+3I-3$, $t=h+2I-2$, and there are $E-h=h+3I-3$ inner edges (segments not on the boundary of $P$). These formulas hold for every such triangulation of a convex polygon.

When is an inner edge bad? Let $XY$ be an inner edge with triangles $XYU$ and $XYW$. The quadrilateral $XUYW$ has angles at $X$ and $Y$ equal to $\angle UXY+\angle YXW$ and $\angle UYX+\angle XYW$, which are not $180^\circ$ (no three points collinear), and not both above $180^\circ$ (the four angles add up to $360^\circ$). So $XUYW$ is a quokkalateral unless exactly one of these two angles exceeds $180^\circ$; then call the edge bad and charge it to that endpoint. The total angle of $P$ at a vertex of $P$ is less than $180^\circ$, so edges are charged only to inner points. If $F$ is the number of quokkalaterals, then $F=(h+3I-3)-(\text{number of bad edges})$.

Charges at one inner point. Let $V$ be an inner point of degree $m$, and let $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m$ be the angles at $V$ of its triangles in cyclic order ($\sum\theta_i=360^\circ$). The edge between the triangles with angles $\theta_i,\theta_{i+1}$ is charged to $V$ exactly when $\theta_i+\theta_{i+1}>180^\circ$. Two such pairs cannot be disjoint, since their four angles would add up to more than $360^\circ$. If $m=3$, all three pairs qualify (each $\theta_i<180^\circ$, so $\theta_j+\theta_k=360^\circ-\theta_i>180^\circ$): $V$ gets $3$ charges. If $m\ge4$, a third pair meeting both $\{\theta_{i-1},\theta_i\}$ and $\{\theta_i,\theta_{i+1}\}$ would have to contain $\theta_i$ (it cannot consist of $\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i+1}$, which are not neighbours when $m\ge4$), so pairwise overlapping pairs of consecutive angles are at most two, and two of them are consecutive pairs $\{\theta_{i-1},\theta_i\},\{\theta_i,\theta_{i+1}\}$: so $V$ gets at most $2$ charges, and if it gets $2$, the two charged edges are the two sides at $V$ of one triangle of $T$.

Degree-three points. Let $L$ be the number of inner points of degree $3$, and $H=I-L$. Two inner points of degree $3$ are never adjacent: the edge joining them would be charged to both ends. An inner point $V$ of degree $3$ lies inside the triangle formed by its three neighbours, which is the union of the three triangles at $V$. Remove all these $L$ points, replacing the three triangles around each by the triangle of its neighbours. As no two removed points are adjacent, the replaced triples of triangles are disjoint, and we get a triangulation of $P$ with $H$ inner points, hence with $h+2H-2$ triangles; $L$ of them are the new big triangles and the other $h+2H-2-L$ are triangles of $T$ with no vertex of degree $3$ among the inner points. Call the latter plain.

If an inner point $V$ of degree at least $4$ gets $2$ charges, the two charged edges $VX,VY$ bound a triangle $VXY$ of $T$. This triangle is plain: if $X$ (say) were an inner point of degree $3$, the edge $VX$ would be charged to $X$ as well. Two different points cannot use the same triangle in this way: if $X$ also got $2$ charges through the triangle $VXY$, the edge $VX$ would be charged to both $V$ and $X$. So the number of inner points with $2$ charges is at most $h+2H-2-L$.

Lower bound. The number of bad edges is at most $3L+2H$, and also at most $3L+H+(h+2H-2-L)$. Hence \[F\ge(h+3L+3H-3)-(3L+2H)=h+H-3,\] \[F\ge(h+3L+3H-3)-(2L+3H+h-2)=L-1 .\] Adding, $2F\ge h+H+L-4=h+I-4=2021$, so $F\ge1011$.

Construction. We use orientation signs: for three points the sign of the oriented area tells on which side of the line through two of them the third lies. Whether an inner edge is bad depends only on such signs for its four points, and these signs are not zero. So if a point is moved by a small enough amount, the status (bad or not) of every inner edge whose two triangles keep their vertices is unchanged.

Start. Triangulate $P$ by $97$ diagonals from one vertex into $98$ triangles, put one point inside each (in general position) and join it to the three corners. The $294$ new edges are bad (charged to their inner endpoint of degree $3$), so at most the $97$ diagonals give quokkalaterals: $F\le97$, with $98$ inner points.

Insertion. Let $O$ be an inner point of degree $3$ with neighbours $A,B,C$, so $O$ lies inside triangle $ABC$ and $\overrightarrow{OC}=-\alpha\,\overrightarrow{OA}-\beta\,\overrightarrow{OB}$ with $\alpha,\beta>0$. Choose small numbers $0<\eta<\min(\alpha,\beta,1)$ and $\varepsilon>0$, and put \[P=O+\varepsilon\bigl(\overrightarrow{OC}+\eta\,\overrightarrow{OA}\bigr),\qquad Q=O+\varepsilon\bigl(\overrightarrow{OC}+\eta\,\overrightarrow{OB}\bigr).\] Then $P=(1-\varepsilon-\varepsilon\eta)O+\varepsilon C+\varepsilon\eta A$ lies inside triangle $OCA$, and $Q$ inside triangle $OCB$. Also \[\overrightarrow{OP}=-\varepsilon(\alpha-\eta)\overrightarrow{OA}-\varepsilon\beta\,\overrightarrow{OB},\quad\text{so}\quad \varepsilon(\alpha-\eta)\overrightarrow{OA}+\varepsilon\beta\,\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{OP}=\vec 0\] with positive coefficients: $O$ lies inside triangle $ABP$; likewise $O$ lies inside triangle $ABQ$. Join $P$ to $O,C,A$ and $Q$ to $O,C,B$. We take $\varepsilon$ so small that no three points are collinear and that the edges $CA$ and $CB$, whose inner triangles $OCA,OCB$ become $PCA,QCB$, keep their status (this is the small-move remark above, $O$ being moved to $P$ or $Q$). Now:

  • the six edges at $P$ and $Q$ are bad, since $P$ and $Q$ have degree $3$;

  • $OA$ lies in the triangles $OAB$ and $OAP$, and $O$ is inside $ABP$, so the angle of the quadrilateral at $O$ exceeds $180^\circ$: bad; likewise $OB$ (triangles $OAB$, $OBQ$) is bad;

  • $OC$ (triangles $OCP$, $OCQ$) may be good;

  • every other inner edge keeps both of its triangles, or is $CA$ or $CB$, so its status does not change.

So the number of inner points grows by $2$ and $F$ by at most $1$, and the new points $P,Q$ have degree $3$, so the next insertion is possible. (A later insertion at $P$ or $Q$ replaces $P$ or $Q$ in the triangles $OAP$, $OBQ$ by a point very close to it; by the choice of its own $\varepsilon$ this keeps $OA$ and $OB$ bad.) A one-point version inserts only $P$: then $O$ has the triangles $OAB,OBC,OCP,OPA$; the edges at $P$ and $OA$ are bad as before, $OB$ keeps its two triangles, and only $OC$ may become good. It adds one inner point and at most one quokkalateral.

Starting from the $98$ inner points, $913$ two-point insertions and one one-point insertion give $98+1826+1=1925$ inner points, that is, $2025$ points, and at most $97+913+1=1011$ quokkalaterals.

So the minimum number of quokkalaterals is $\boxed{1011}$.

Solution 1 (An inversion centred at $S$)

Powers of $S$. The line $KL$ is the radical axis of $\omega$ and the circle $(BCI)$, so $S$ has the same power with respect to both. Since $BC$ touches $\omega$ at $D$, the power with respect to $\omega$ is $SD^2$; the power with respect to $(BCI)$ is $\overline{SB}\cdot\overline{SC}$ (signed lengths). Hence \[\overline{SB}\cdot\overline{SC}=SD^2=\overline{SK}\cdot\overline{SL}.\] $S\ne D$: otherwise $\overline{DB}\cdot\overline{DC}=0$, but $D$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$. So all three products are positive: $B,C$ lie on the same side of $S$ on line $BC$, and $K,L$ on the same side of $S$ on line $KL$. In particular $S$ lies outside segment $BC$, hence outside segment $BD$.

The inversion. Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $S$ and radius $SD$. By the displayed equalities, $\Phi$ swaps $B$ and $C$, swaps $K$ and $L$, and fixes $D$. The circle $(BDK)$ does not pass through $S$ (the line $BC$ meets it only at $B$ and $D$), so $\Phi$ maps it to the circle through $\Phi(B),\Phi(D),\Phi(K)$, that is, to $(CDL)$.

The common tangent. Since $B,D$ lie on the same side of $S$, the power $\overline{SB}\cdot\overline{SD}$ of $S$ with respect to $(BDK)$ is positive, so $S$ lies outside $(BDK)$ and there is a tangent line $t$ from $S$ to $(BDK)$, touching it at $T_1$. The inversion maps every line through its centre to itself, and it maps the one common point $T_1$ of $t$ and $(BDK)$ to a single common point $T_2=\Phi(T_1)$ of $t$ and $\Phi\bigl((BDK)\bigr)=(CDL)$. So $t$ meets $(CDL)$ in exactly one point, i.e. it is tangent to $(CDL)$ as well. Thus $t$ is a line through $S$ tangent to both circles.

Solution 2 (Coordinates and a homothety centred at $S$)

Put $D=(0,0)$, $B=(-u,0)$, $C=(v,0)$ and $I=(0,r)$, where $u=BD$, $v=DC$ and $r$ is the inradius. Then $\omega$ is $x^2+y^2-2ry=0$, and the circle $BCI$ is \[x^2+y^2+(u-v)x+\Bigl(\frac{uv}r-r\Bigr)y-uv=0,\] as one checks at $B$, $C$ and $I$. Subtracting the two equations, the common points $K,L$ lie on the line $(u-v)x+\bigl(\frac{uv}r+r\bigr)y=uv$, which is therefore the line $KL$. As $KL$ meets $BC$, we have $u\ne v$, and $S=(s,0)$ with $s=\dfrac{uv}{u-v}$.

The points $K$ and $L$. Every point of $\omega$ other than $D$ has $y>0$, and it is determined by $\tau=x/y$: from $x=\tau y$ and $x^2+y^2=2ry$ we get $y=\frac{2r}{1+\tau^2}$, $x=\frac{2r\tau}{1+\tau^2}$. The line $KL$ does not pass through $D$ (as $uv\ne0$). Substituting into its equation and multiplying by $1+\tau^2$ shows that the parameters $\tau_K,\tau_L$ of $K$ and $L$ are the two roots of \[uv\,\tau^2-2r(u-v)\,\tau-(uv+2r^2)=0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \tau_K+\tau_L=\frac{2r(u-v)}{uv}.\]

The two circles. A circle through $D$ and $B$ has an equation $x^2+y^2+ux+w_1y=0$. For the circle $\omega_1=(BDK)$, using $x_K^2+y_K^2=2ry_K$ and $x_K=\tau_Ky_K$ we get $w_1=-2r-u\tau_K$. Likewise $\omega_2=(CDL)$ is $x^2+y^2-vx+w_2y=0$ with $w_2=-2r+v\tau_L$. Then \[w_1-\frac uv\,w_2=-2r+\frac{2ru}{v}-u(\tau_K+\tau_L)=\frac{2r(u-v)}{v}-\frac{2r(u-v)}{v}=0 .\]

The homothety. Let $\mathcal H$ be the homothety with centre $S$ and ratio $k=u/v>0$, $\mathcal H(Z)=S+k(Z-S)$. A point $(x,0)$ goes to $(s(1-k)+kx,0)$, and $s(1-k)=\frac{uv}{u-v}\cdot\frac{v-u}{v}=-u$; so $\mathcal H(C)=(-u+u,0)=D$ and $\mathcal H(D)=(-u,0)=B$. The centre of $\omega_2$ is $\bigl(\frac v2,-\frac{w_2}2\bigr)$, the point above the midpoint of $CD$ at height $-\frac{w_2}{2}$; its image lies above the midpoint of $\mathcal H(C)\mathcal H(D)=DB$ at height $-\frac{k w_2}{2}=-\frac{w_1}{2}$, so it is $\bigl(-\frac u2,-\frac{w_1}2\bigr)$, the centre of $\omega_1$. Hence $\mathcal H(\omega_2)$ is the circle with the centre of $\omega_1$ through $\mathcal H(D)=B\in\omega_1$, that is, $\mathcal H(\omega_2)=\omega_1$.

The common tangent. The homothety $\mathcal H$ maps every line through $S$ to itself, so $\tau=\mathcal H(\tau)$ is tangent to $\mathcal H(\omega_2)=\omega_1$ as well. Thus $\tau$ is a line through $S$ tangent to the circumcircles of $BDK$ and $CDL$.

There are $2025$ white balls and $2025$ black balls arranged in a row. A balanced segment is a contiguous nonempty segment of balls that contains the same number of white balls as black balls. A balanced removal is the operation of selecting a balanced segment $S$, removing all the balls in $S$, and shifting left the balls that were to the right of $S$ so that the remaining balls form a contiguous row. Determine the smallest positive integer $N$ satisfying the following property: for every configuration of balls and for every integer $k$ satisfying $0\leq k\leq 2025$, there exists a sequence of at most $N$ balanced removals after which there are exactly $2k$ remaining balls.

Show the answer

$N=2$

Show the solution

Solution (Cut along the zero-level walk)

Let $n=2025$. Number the gaps between balls $0,1,\dots,2n$ (gap $t$ is just after the $t$-th ball) and let $P_t$ be the number of white minus the number of black balls among the first $t$ balls. So $P_0=P_{2n}=0$, consecutive values differ by $1$, and the segment of balls between gaps $i<j$ is balanced exactly when $P_i=P_j$.

$N\ge2$. Take the row $BB\,\underbrace{W\cdots W}_{n}\,\underbrace{B\cdots B}_{n-2}$ and $k=1$. One balanced removal leaving $2$ balls would remove all balls except either the first two, or the last two, or the first and the last. These pairs are $BB$, $BB$ and $BB$ (as $n-2\ge2$), never one white and one black, while the removed segment, and hence the remaining pair, would have to be balanced. So one removal is not enough.

$N\le2$. Fix a row and $k$, and put $L=2n-2k$, the number of balls to remove. If $L=0$ do nothing. Otherwise let $z$ be the last gap with $z<L$ and $P_z=0$ (it exists, since $P_0=0$), and let $z'$ be the first gap with $z'\ge L$ and $P_{z'}=0$ (it exists, since $P_{2n}=0$). Between $z$ and $z'$ the walk does not return to $0$, so $P_t$ has constant sign for $z<t<z'$; say $P_t\ge0$ on $[z,z']$ (the other case is symmetric).

Put $\ell=L-z$; it is even and $0<\ell\le z'-z$. For $z\le i\le z'-\ell$ consider $g(i)=P_{i+\ell}-P_i$. Then \[g(z)=P_{z+\ell}\ge0,\qquad g(z'-\ell)=-P_{z'-\ell}\le0,\] all values of $g$ are even (since $\ell$ is even), and $g(i+1)-g(i)\in\{-2,0,2\}$. Hence $g(i)=0$ for some $i$: the balls between gaps $i$ and $i+\ell$ form a balanced segment of length $\ell$ lying between gaps $z$ and $z'$.

Now remove this segment, and then (if $z>0$) the first $z$ balls, which form a balanced segment because $P_z=P_0$ and are still contiguous at the start of the row. Altogether $\ell+z=L$ balls are removed in at most two balanced removals, leaving $2k$ balls.

Hence the smallest $N$ is $\boxed{2}$.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms

For each integer $k\geqslant 3$, prove that there exists a unique $k$-tuplet $(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k)$ of positive real numbers such that $x_1\geqslant x_2\geqslant\cdots\geqslant x_k$, and \[\left\lfloor nx_1+\frac12\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor nx_2+\frac12\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor nx_k+\frac12\right\rfloor=n\] for every integer $n$.

Show the answer

proof; the tuple is $x_i=\dfrac{2^{k-i}}{2^k-1}$ $(1\le i\le k)$

Show the solution

Solution (Balls in unit buckets and a self-similar colour word)

Existence. Let $x_i=\dfrac{2^{k-i}}{2^k-1}$; these are positive and decreasing. Using $\lfloor y+\tfrac12\rfloor=\lfloor2y\rfloor-\lfloor y\rfloor$ (check $y=m+f$ with $f<\frac12$ and with $f\ge\frac12$), the sum telescopes for every integer $n$: \[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2^{k-i}n}{2^k-1}+\frac12\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2^kn}{2^k-1}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2^k-1}\Bigr\rfloor =\Bigl\lfloor n+\frac{n}{2^k-1}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2^k-1}\Bigr\rfloor=n .\]

Uniqueness: first facts. Let $(x_i)$ be any solution. Since $|\lfloor y+\frac12\rfloor-y|\le\frac12$, we get $|n(x_1+\dots+x_k)-n|\le\frac k2$ for all $n$, so $\sum x_i=1$ and every $x_i<1$. Let $H=\mathbb Z+\frac12$. For real $y$, $\lfloor y+\frac12\rfloor+\lfloor-y+\frac12\rfloor$ equals $1$ if $y\in H$ and $0$ otherwise. Adding the hypothesis for $n$ and for $-n$ therefore gives $0=\#\{i:nx_i\in H\}$: no $nx_i$ is a half-integer.

Balls and buckets. For each colour $i$ and each $a\in H$ put a ball of colour $i$ at the point $a/x_i$ of the real line. No ball lies at an integer $N$ (that would mean $Nx_i=a\in H$). For $n\ge1$, the colour-$i$ balls in $(0,n)$ correspond to the $a\in H$ with $0<a<nx_i$, and there are $\lfloor nx_i+\frac12\rfloor$ of them; so $(0,n)$ contains exactly $n$ balls for every $n\ge1$, and each bucket $(N-1,N)$, $N\ge1$, contains exactly one ball. The balls are symmetric under $t\mapsto-t$, so this holds for all $N\in\mathbb Z$. Let $C(N)$ be the colour in bucket $(N-1,N)$; then $C(1-N)=C(N)$. Call $P=C(1)C(2)C(3)\dots$ the positive word; the whole sequence reads (reversed $P$)$\,P$ around $0$.

If $x_i=x_j$ for $i\neq j$, two balls would coincide; so $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_k$. The first positive ball of colour $i$ is at $1/(2x_i)$, so in $P$ the first $i$ comes before the first $i+1$; and $C(1)=1$, because bucket $(0,1)$ holds the smallest positive ball.

Balance lemma. For each colour $i$, any two blocks of $m$ consecutive buckets contain numbers of $i$'s that differ by at most $1$. The block $(N,N+m)$ contains as many $i$'s as there are $a\in H$ in $(Nx_i,(N+m)x_i)$, an open interval of length $mx_i$ with endpoints outside $H$; such an interval contains $\lfloor mx_i\rfloor$ or $\lfloor mx_i\rfloor+1$ half-integers.

Two consequences. A gap of colour $i$ is a maximal run of buckets without $i$ (every colour occurs infinitely often in both directions). (G) Two gaps of colour $i$ differ in length by at most $1$: if the lengths were $g$ and $g'\ge g+2$, the block formed by the shorter gap and its two bounding $i$'s (length $g+2$, two $i$'s) and a block of length $g+2$ inside the longer gap (no $i$) would violate the lemma. (R) In the same way, two maximal runs of $1$'s differ in length by at most $1$.

The words $T_j$. Let $T_1=1$ and $T_j=T_{j-1}\,j\,T_{j-1}$. By induction, $T_j$ has length $2^j-1$, is a palindrome, and its letter at position $p$ is $v_2(p)+1$ ($v_2$ = exponent of $2$). So $T_j$ uses only letters $\le j$, its $1$'s are at the odd positions, consecutive letters $i$ are $2^i$ apart, and $T_{j+1}=T_j\,(j+1)\,T_j$. Consider the statements

  • $A(j)$: $P$ begins with $T_j$; $B(j)$: every letter $\ell>j$ in the whole sequence is immediately preceded and followed by $T_j$.

$A(1)$ holds. For $B(1)$: since $C(1)=1$, the ball $1/(2x_1)$ is below $1$, so consecutive colour-$1$ balls are $1/x_1<2$ apart and lie in buckets at most two apart. So between two $1$'s there is at most one other letter, which is $B(1)$.

Step 1: $A(2)$ (here $k\ge3$ is used). Let $P$ begin with exactly $r\ge1$ ones; the next letter is $2$ (no colour $\ge3$ appears before the first $2$). By the symmetry $C(1-N)=C(N)$ the whole sequence contains $2\,1^{2r}\,2$: a run of $1$'s and a gap of colour $2$, both of length $2r$. By (R) all runs of $1$'s have length $\ge2r-1$, and by (G) all gaps of $2$ have length $\le2r+1$. Colour $3$ occurs; by $B(1)$ it sits between two runs of $1$'s, which gives $(2r-1)+1+(2r-1)=4r-1$ consecutive buckets without a $2$. So $4r-1\le2r+1$, $r=1$, and with $B(1)$, $P$ begins with $121=T_2$.

Step 2: for $2\le j\le k-1$, $A(j)$ and $B(j-1)$ give $B(j)$. By $A(j)$ and symmetry the whole sequence contains $T_jT_j=T_{j-1}\,j\,T_{j-1}T_{j-1}\,j\,T_{j-1}$, hence a gap of colour $j$ of length $2^j-2$; by (G) every gap of $j$ has length $\le2^j-1$. Let $\ell>j$ occur. By $B(j-1)$ we see $T_{j-1}\,\ell\,T_{j-1}$, which has length $2^j-1$ and no letter $j$; so it is a whole gap of $j$, bordered by $j$ on both sides. Applying $B(j-1)$ to these two letters $j$ gives $T_{j-1}\,j\,T_{j-1}\,\ell\,T_{j-1}\,j\,T_{j-1}=T_j\,\ell\,T_j$.

Step 3: for $2\le j\le k-1$, $A(j)$ and $B(j)$ give $A(j+1)$. Let $c=C(2^j)$ be the letter after the initial $T_j$. Then $c\le j+1$, since $T_j$ contains no $j+1$. Colour $j+1$ occurs, so by $B(j)$ the word $W=T_j\,(j+1)\,T_j=T_{j+1}$ occurs; its letter at position $p$ is $v_2(p)+1$.

  • If $c=1$: the block $C(0)C(1)\dots C(2^j)=1\,T_j\,1$ has length $2^j+1$ and $2^{j-1}+2$ ones, while positions $2,3,\dots,2^j+2$ of $W$ (note $2^j+2<2^{j+1}$) form a block of the same length with ones only at $3,5,\dots,2^j+1$, i.e. $2^{j-1}$ ones. This contradicts the balance lemma.

  • If $2\le c=i\le j$: in $P$ the letter $i$ stands at position $2^j-2^{i-1}$ and next at $2^j$, a gap of length $2^{i-1}-1$; in $W$ the letters $i$ at positions $2^{i-1}$ and $3\cdot2^{i-1}$ are consecutive, a gap of length $2^i-1$. The difference $2^{i-1}\ge2$ contradicts (G).

So $c=j+1$, and by $B(j)$ it is followed by $T_j$: $P$ begins with $T_j\,(j+1)\,T_j=T_{j+1}$.

Starting from $A(2)$ and alternating Steps 2 and 3, we obtain $A(k)$ and $B(k-1)$.

Step 4: $P=T_kT_kT_k\cdots$. By $A(k)$ the centre $T_kT_k$ contains a gap of colour $k$ of length $2^k-2$, so all gaps of $k$ have length $2^k-3$, $2^k-2$ or $2^k-1$; by $B(k-1)$ each such gap begins with $T_{k-1}$ and ends with $T_{k-1}$.

  • Length $2^k-3$: the two copies of $T_{k-1}$ overlap in one letter, and the two letters $k-1$ in the gap are $2^{k-1}-2$ apart, a gap of colour $k-1$ of length $2^{k-1}-3$; but $T_k$ contains a gap of $k-1$ of length $2^{k-1}-1$ (positions $2^{k-2}$ and $3\cdot2^{k-2}$). This contradicts (G).

  • Length $2^k-1$: the gap is $T_{k-1}\,w\,T_{k-1}$ with $w\ne k$. If $w=1$ we get a run $111$, while $T_k$ has a run of a single $1$ (at position $3$, between $2$ and $3$), contradicting (R). If $w=i\ge2$, colour $i$ has a gap of length $2^{i-1}-1$ (from position $2^{k-1}-2^{i-1}$ to $w$), while $T_k$ has one of length $2^i-1$, contradicting (G).

So every gap of $k$ is exactly $T_{k-1}T_{k-1}$, and $P=T_{k-1}\,k\,T_{k-1}T_{k-1}\,k\,T_{k-1}\cdots=T_kT_kT_k\cdots$.

Step 5: the values. In $T_k$ colour $i$ occurs $2^{k-i}$ times (positions with $v_2(p)=i-1$). Counting colour-$i$ balls in $(0,n)$ for $n=m(2^k-1)$ in two ways gives $\lfloor m(2^k-1)x_i+\frac12\rfloor=m\,2^{k-i}$, so $|(2^k-1)x_i-2^{k-i}|\le\frac1{2m}$ for every $m\ge1$. Hence \[x_i=\frac{2^{k-i}}{2^k-1}\qquad(1\le i\le k),\] and the solution is unique.

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

In an $n\times n$ board, for each $1\leq i\leq n$ and $1\leq j\leq n$, the cell in the $i$th row from the bottom and $j$th column from the left contains $\gcd(i,j)$ leaves. A koala travels from the bottom left corner to the top right corner. At each step, the koala moves up or to the right by a single cell. The koala eats the leaves in each cell it visits, including the bottom left and top right cells.

Determine the maximum number of leaves that the koala can eat.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{n^2+3n-2}{2}$

Show the solution

Solution (Pairing consecutive anti-diagonals)

Write $(i,j)$ for the cell in row $i$ and column $j$ and $g(i,j)=\gcd(i,j)$. Every step increases $i+j$ by $1$, so the koala's path visits exactly one cell on each anti-diagonal $i+j=s$, $s=2,3,\dots,2n$.

Construction. The staircase $(1,1),(1,2),(2,2),(2,3),\dots,(n-1,n),(n,n)$ visits the diagonal cells $(m,m)$, worth $m$, and the cells $(m,m+1)$, worth $\gcd(m,m+1)=1$. It collects $1+2+\cdots+n+(n-1)=\frac{n^2+3n-2}{2}$ leaves.

Key lemma. Let $A$ be the visited cell with $i+j=2m$ $(1\le m\le n-1)$ and $B$ the next visited cell (with $i+j=2m+1$). Then $g(A)+g(B)\le m+1$.

Proof. $A$ and $B$ share a row or a column, so they share a coordinate $c$, and their other coordinates are consecutive integers. Hence $g(A)$ and $g(B)$ both divide $c$, and a common divisor of them divides two consecutive integers, so $\gcd(g(A),g(B))=1$ and $g(A)g(B)\mid c$.

  • If $g(A)=1$: $g(B)$ is at most the smaller coordinate of $B$, which is at most $m$ because the coordinates of $B$ add up to $2m+1$. So the sum is at most $m+1$.

  • If $g(B)=1$: similarly $g(A)\le m$, since the coordinates of $A$ add up to $2m$.

  • If $g(A),g(B)\ge2$: then $(g(A)-2)(g(B)-2)\ge0$ gives $g(A)+g(B)\le\frac{g(A)g(B)}2+2\le\frac c2+2$. The coordinate $c$ belongs to $A$, whose other coordinate is at least $1$, so $c\le2m-1$; and $c=2m-1$ would force the other coordinate of $A$ to be $1$ and $g(A)=1$. So $c\le2m-2$ and $g(A)+g(B)\le m+1$. $\square$

Upper bound. Group the anti-diagonals as $\{2,3\},\{4,5\},\dots,\{2n-2,2n-1\}$ and $\{2n\}$. By the lemma the cells on the pair $\{2m,2m+1\}$ contribute at most $m+1$, and the last cell $(n,n)$ contributes $n$. So the total is at most \[\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(m+1)+n=\frac{(n-1)n}2+(n-1)+n=\frac{n^2+3n-2}{2}.\] Hence the maximum is $\boxed{\dfrac{n^2+3n-2}{2}}$.

By (1) these are equal, so $X=X'$ lies on line $CH$, and $CX\perp AB$.

Solution 2 (Three pairs of similar right triangles)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of $ABC$; $AB<AC$ gives $\gamma<\beta$. Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (so $B,E,D$ are collinear). We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Where $Q$ and $R$ are. The chords $AQ$ and $AB$ are equal, so the arcs $AQ$ and $AB$ (not containing $C$) both measure $2\gamma$. Since $2\gamma<2\beta$, the point $Q$ lies on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, strictly between $A$ and $C$, and the arc $QC$ measures $2\beta-2\gamma$. Hence chord $BQ$ crosses chord $AC$, so $R$ lies inside both segments, and by inscribed angles \[\angle ABQ=\angle AQB=\angle QCA=\angle ACB=\gamma .\] In particular $\angle BRA=180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma=\beta$.

$T$ is the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$. Since $\angle QCA=\angle ACB$, line $CA$ bisects angle $QCB$, so the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CQ^*=CQ$. The chord $CQ$ subtends the arc $2\beta-2\gamma$ and the chord $CB$ the arc $2\alpha$; as $\beta-\gamma<\alpha$ (because $\beta<90^\circ<\alpha+\gamma$), $CQ^*=CQ<CB$ and $Q^*$ lies on segment $BC$.

Now $RQ=RQ^*$ and $QQ^*\perp AC$, so $QQ^*\parallel BD$. Hence \[\measuredangle RQ^*Q=\measuredangle Q^*QR=\measuredangle(BD,BQ)=\measuredangle DBQ=\measuredangle DSQ=\measuredangle RSQ,\] using the isosceles triangle $RQQ^*$, the parallel lines, the circle $\Gamma$ through $B,D,S,Q$, and the collinearity of $D,R,S$. So $Q^*$ lies on the circle $(QRS)$. It remains to see that $Q^*$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on this circle. Since $R$ lies between $B$ and $Q$, the power of $B$ with respect to the circle is $BR\cdot BQ>0$, so $B$ is outside it. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $QQ^*$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $AC$. Because $\angle QCA=\gamma<90^\circ$ and $\angle QRC=\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the point $M$ lies on ray $CA$ and on ray $RC$, hence strictly between $R$ and $C$: the points $R$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the chord $QQ^*$. As $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $QQ^*$, the chord is seen from $R$ under the angle $\angle QRQ^*=2\angle QRM=2\beta$ and from $C$ under $\angle QCQ^*=2\gamma$. A point on the other side of the chord from $R$ lies inside the circle exactly when it sees the chord under an angle greater than $180^\circ-2\beta$; here $2\gamma>180^\circ-2\beta$ because $\alpha<90^\circ$. So $C$ is inside the circle, and segment $BC$, which joins an outside point to an inside point, meets the circle exactly once. Hence $T=Q^*$, and line $TQ$ is perpendicular to $AC$ at $M$ and parallel to $BD$; moreover $M$ lies strictly between $R$ and $C$.

Positions on $AC$. Since $\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the foot $E$ lies on ray $RA$; hence $R$ lies strictly between $E$ and $M$. Since the triangle is acute, $E$ lies inside segment $AC$, and so do $M$ and $R$.

Three similarities. All of the following triangles are right-angled at $M$ or $E$.

  • $RMX\sim RED$: the lines $RX$ and $RD$ coincide (both are line $RS$), so the angles at $R$ are equal. Hence $\dfrac{MX}{ED}=\dfrac{RM}{RE}$.

  • $RMQ\sim REB$: $Q$, $B$ lie on line $BQ$ through $R$. Hence $\dfrac{RM}{RE}=\dfrac{MQ}{EB}$.

  • $CMQ\sim CEB$: $M$ and $E$ lie on ray $CA$ and $\angle MCQ=\gamma=\angle ECB$. Hence $\dfrac{MQ}{EB}=\dfrac{CM}{CE}$.

Together, $\dfrac{MX}{ED}=\dfrac{CM}{CE}$, so the right triangles $CMX$ and $CED$ are similar (legs in proportion), and \[\angle ACX=\angle MCX=\angle ECD=\angle ACD .\]

The side of $X$. As $R$ is between $E$ and $M$, and $MX\parallel ED$ with $X$, $D$ on a line through $R$, the point $X$ is the image of $D$ under a homothety with centre $R$ and negative ratio; so $X$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$, i.e. $X$ is on the same side as $B$.

Conclusion. The points $B$ and $C$ lie on the same arc $AD$ of $\Gamma$ (the order on $\Gamma$ is $A,D,C,B$), so $\angle ACD=\angle ABD=\angle ABE=90^\circ-\alpha$. Let $F$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$; it lies on side $AB$, on the same side of $AC$ as $B$, and $\angle ACF=90^\circ-\alpha$. The rays $CX$ and $CF$ lie on the same side of line $CA$ and make the same angle with ray $CA$, so they coincide. Hence $X$ lies on the altitude $CF$, and $CX\perp AB$.

Solution 3 (Coordinates: $T$ is the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $B=(u,v)$ with $v>0$, and let $r=u^2+v^2=AB^2$ and $n=(1-u)^2+v^2=BC^2=r+1-2u$. Since $AB<AC$, $r<1$. The angles at $A$ and $C$ are acute, so $0<u<1$, and the angle at $B$ is acute: $\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=r-u>0$. Put $w=\frac{r-u}{v}>0$. We shall use the identity \[rn=(r-u)^2+v^2,\tag{$*$}\] which holds because both sides equal $r^2+r-2ur$ (use $u^2+v^2=r$).

$\Gamma$, $D$, $R$, $Q$. The circle $\Gamma$ is $x^2+y^2=x+wy$ (it passes through $A$, $C$, and through $B$ as $u+wv=r$). The perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$ is $x=u$; on it $\Gamma$ gives $y^2-wy+u^2-u=0$, with roots $v$ and $w-v$, so $D=(u,w-v)$. For a point of $\Gamma$ we have $AZ^2=x^2+y^2=x+wy$; so the points of $\Gamma$ at distance $\sqrt r$ from $A$ are its points on the line $x+wy=r$. Thus $BQ$ is the line $x+wy=r$, and $R=(r,0)$, with $0<r<1$.

Let $Q=(q,z)$. Substituting $x=r-wy$ in $x^2+y^2=r$ gives $(1+w^2)y^2-2rwy+r^2-r=0$, with roots $v$ and $z$; so $vz=-\frac{r(1-r)}{1+w^2}$. By $(*)$, $1+w^2=\frac{v^2+(r-u)^2}{v^2}=\frac{rn}{v^2}$, hence \[z=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}<0,\qquad q=r-wz .\]

The point $T$. Let $T'=(q,-z)$ be the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$. The line $BC$ is $vx+(1-u)y=v$, and since $vw+1-u=r-u+1-u=n$, \[vq-(1-u)z=vr-(vw+1-u)z=vr-nz=vr+v(1-r)=v,\] so $T'$ lies on line $BC$. Its height $-z=\frac{v(1-r)}{n}$ lies strictly between $0$ and $v$, because $1-r<n$ is equivalent to $u<r$. So $T'$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$.

The circle $\omega$ through $Q,R,T'$. As $Q$ and $T'$ are symmetric in $AC$, the centre of $\omega$ is on $AC$, say $(c_0,0)$, and $\omega$ is $f(x,y):=x^2+y^2-2c_0x+2c_0r-r^2=0$ (it passes through $R$). Using $q^2+z^2=AQ^2=r$, the condition $f(Q)=0$ reads $r-r^2=2c_0(q-r)=-2c_0wz$, so \[2c_0=\frac{r(1-r)}{-wz}=\frac{rn}{vw}=\frac{rn}{r-u}.\]

$S$ lies on $\omega$. Let $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-x-wy$, so $\Gamma$ is $g=0$, and $\ell=f-g=(1-2c_0)x+wy+2c_0r-r^2$, an affine function. Since $S\in\Gamma$, we have $S\in\omega$ if and only if $\ell(S)=0$. Write the points of line $DR$ as $R+\mu(D-R)$. Then $g(R+\mu(D-R))$ is a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $|D-R|^2$, constant term $g(R)=r^2-r$, and roots $\mu=1$ (the point $D$) and $\mu_S$ (the point $S$); so $\mu_S=\frac{r^2-r}{|D-R|^2}$. As $\ell$ is affine, $\ell(S)=\ell(R)+\mu_S\bigl(\ell(D)-\ell(R)\bigr)$. Here $\ell(R)=r-r^2$ and, using $2c_0(u-r)=-rn$ and $wv=r-u$, \[\ell(D)-\ell(R)=(1-2c_0)(u-r)+w(w-v)=(u-r)+rn+w^2-wv=rn+w^2-2wv .\] By $(*)$, $|D-R|^2=(u-r)^2+(w-v)^2=rn+w^2-2wv$ as well. Hence $\ell(S)=r-r^2+(r^2-r)=0$, and $S\in\omega$. So $\omega$ is the circle $QRS$.

$T=T'$. The power of $C$ with respect to $\omega$ is $f(1,0)=(1-r)(1+r-2c_0)$, where \[1+r-2c_0=\frac{(1+r)(r-u)-r(r+1-2u)}{r-u}=-\frac{u(1-r)}{r-u}<0;\] so $C$ lies inside $\omega$. Hence $C$ lies between the two points where line $BC$ meets $\omega$; one of them is $T'$, on the side of $B$, so the other one is beyond $C$. Thus $T'$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on $\omega$, and $T=T'=(q,-z)$.

The point $X$. The line $TQ$ is $x=q$. The line $RS$ is the line $RD$, which is not vertical as $u\ne r$; its point with $x=q$ is $R+\mu(D-R)$ with $\mu=\frac{q-r}{u-r}=\frac{-wz}{-vw}=\frac zv$, so $X=\bigl(q,\frac{z(w-v)}{v}\bigr)$.

Conclusion. We have $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(q-1)+z(w-v)$. With $q-1=r-1-wz$ this is $u(r-1)+z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)$. Now $v\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=(r-u)(1-u)-v^2=r-u-ur+u^2-(r-u^2)=-u(1+r-2u)=-un$, so \[z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{un}{v}\Bigr)=u(1-r),\] and $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(r-1)+u(1-r)=0$. Hence $CX$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $S$ be a set of positive integers, possibly infinite, such that no positive integer greater than $1$ divides all elements of $S$. Determine all non-periodic infinite sequences $a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots$ of positive integers such that, for all positive integers $n$,

  • $a_n\leqslant|a_{n+\ell}-\ell|$ for all $\ell$ in $S$, and

  • $a_n=|a_{n+\ell}-\ell|$ for at least one $\ell$ in $S$.

We say that an infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots$ is periodic if there exists a positive integer $t$ such that $a_n=a_{n+t}$ for all positive integer $n$.

Show the answer

$a_n=n+c$ for all $n$, where $c\ge0$ is an integer

Show the solution

Solution (Unbounded, then eventually above every shift, then eventually linear)

The answer works. For an integer $c\ge0$ let $a_n=n+c$. These are positive integers, the sequence is strictly increasing (so not periodic), and $|a_{n+\ell}-\ell|=|n+c|=a_n$ for every $\ell\in S$, so both conditions hold.

From now on let $(a_n)$ be a non-periodic sequence with the two properties. Together they say \[a_n=\min_{\ell\in S}\,|a_{n+\ell}-\ell|\qquad\text{for every }n\ge1, \tag{$*$}\] and the minimum is attained.

A finite part of $S$. The greatest common divisor of the first $j$ elements of $S$ (in increasing order) is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers, so it is eventually constant; its final value divides every element of $S$, hence equals $1$. So some finite set $T\subseteq S$ has $\gcd T=1$. Let $M=\max T$ and $K=\bigl(\sum_{t\in T}t\bigr)^2$.

Fact: every integer $k>K$ is a sum of elements of $T$ (repetitions allowed). By Bézout there are integers $w_t$ with $\sum_{t\in T}w_tt=k$. If some $w_j<0$, the positive terms satisfy $\sum_{w_t>0}w_tt\ge k>K$; if every positive coefficient were at most $j-1$, this sum would be at most $(j-1)\sum_{t\in T}t<K$. So some $w_i\ge j$, and we replace $w_i$ by $w_i-j$ and $w_j$ by $w_j+i$: the value $\sum w_tt$ is unchanged, $w_i$ stays nonnegative, and the total $\sum_{w_t<0}|w_t|$ of the negative coefficients strictly decreases. After finitely many such steps all coefficients are nonnegative.

Step 1: the sequence is unbounded. Suppose $a_n\le C$ for all $n$. If $\ell\in S$ and $\ell>2C$, then $|a_{n+\ell}-\ell|\ge\ell-C>C\ge a_n$, so the minimum in $(*)$ is attained at some $\ell\le2C$. Hence $a_n$ is determined by the block $W_n=(a_{n+1},a_{n+2},\dots,a_{n+2C})$: there is a fixed rule $F$ with $a_n=F(W_n)$, and therefore a fixed rule $G$ with $W_{n-1}=G(W_n)$ (compute $a_n=F(W_n)$ and drop the last entry). The blocks take only finitely many values, so some block value $W$ occurs as $W_N$ for infinitely many $N$. The sequence $W,G(W),G^2(W),\dots$ takes finitely many values, so there are $J\ge0$ and $P\ge1$ with $G^{j+P}(W)=G^{j}(W)$ for all $j\ge J$. For any $n\ge1$ choose such an $N$ with $N\ge n+P+J$; then $W_n=G^{N-n}(W)$ and $W_{n+P}=G^{N-n-P}(W)$ are equal, since both exponents are at least $J$ and differ by $P$. So $a_n=F(W_n)=F(W_{n+P})=a_{n+P}$ for all $n$, and the sequence is periodic, a contradiction.

Step 2: $a_n>M$ for all large $n$. If $a_p\le M$, $t\in T$ and $p-t\ge1$, then by the first condition $a_{p-t}\le|a_p-t|\le M-1$, because both $a_p$ and $t$ lie in $[1,M]$. Now suppose $a_n\le M$ for infinitely many $n$, and let $m\ge1$. Pick such an $n$ with $n-m>K$ and write $n-m=t_1+\dots+t_r$ with $t_i\in T$ (Fact). Going from index $n$ down to $n-t_1$, then to $n-t_1-t_2$, and so on, all indices stay $\ge m$ and the value stays at most $M$; so $a_m\le M$. Hence the sequence is bounded, contradicting Step 1. So there is $N$ with $a_n>M$ for all $n>N$.

Step 3: a monotonicity. Put $b_n=a_n-n$. Let $n>N$ and $t\in T$. If $a_{n+t}<t$, the first condition would give $a_n\le t-a_{n+t}<t\le M<a_n$, which is absurd. So $a_{n+t}\ge t$ and $a_n\le a_{n+t}-t$, that is, $b_n\le b_{n+t}$. Using the Fact, we get \[b_n\le b_{n+k}\qquad\text{for all }n>N\text{ and all }k>K. \tag{1}\]

Step 4: $(b_n)$ is eventually constant.

  • Some $B_0$ satisfies $b_m\le B_0$ for infinitely many $m$. Suppose instead that for every $B$ only finitely many $m$ have $b_m\le B$. Take $B\ge\max(0,b_1)$, and let $m^*$ be the largest $m$ with $b_m\le B$ (the set is finite and contains $1$). Choose $\ell\in S$ attaining the minimum in $(*)$ for $n=m^*$. Then $b_{m^*+\ell}>B\ge0$, so $a_{m^*+\ell}=b_{m^*+\ell}+m^*+\ell>\ell$ and $a_{m^*}=a_{m^*+\ell}-\ell$, i.e. $b_{m^*}=b_{m^*+\ell}>B$, a contradiction. So there is $B_0$ with $b_m\le B_0$ for infinitely many $m$.

  • Bounded. For $n>N$ pick such an $m$ with $m>n+K$; by (1), $b_n\le b_m\le B_0$. Also by (1), $b_n\ge b_{N+1}$ for $n>N+1+K$. So for large $n$ the integers $b_n$ lie in a fixed finite set.

  • Constant. Let $v$ be the largest value taken by $b_n$ for infinitely many $n$. For large $n$, $b_n\le v$ (larger values occur only finitely often). Also, choosing $j>N$ with $b_j=v$ and $n>j+K$, (1) gives $b_n\ge v$. Hence $b_n=v$ for all large $n$.

Step 5: going back to the start. Let $c=v$ and let $L$ be the least index with $b_n=c$ for all $n\ge L$. Suppose $L\ge2$. Choose $\ell\in S$ attaining the minimum in $(*)$ for $n=L-1$. Since $L-1+\ell\ge L$, we have $a_{L-1+\ell}=L-1+\ell+c$, so $a_{L-1}=|L-1+c|$. As $L+c=a_L\ge1$, the number $L-1+c$ is nonnegative, so $a_{L-1}=L-1+c$ and $b_{L-1}=c$, contradicting the choice of $L$. Hence $L=1$: $a_n=n+c$ for all $n$, and $c=a_1-1\ge0$.

Therefore the sequences are exactly \[\boxed{a_n=n+c\ \ (n\ge1),\quad c\ge0\text{ an integer}}.\]

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle such that $\angle A$ is obtuse. Let $D$ and $E$ be points on sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively, such that $BDEC$ is cyclic. Suppose the circumcircle of triangle $ADE$ intersects side $BC$ at two points $X$ and $Y$, where $B,X,Y$ and $C$ lie in that order. Let the circumcircles of triangles $BDX$ and $CEY$ be $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, respectively. Let $P$ be a point such that $PD$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $PE$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Let $Q$ be a point such that $QB$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $QC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Prove that points $A$, $P$ and $Q$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Three points on a radical axis)

We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circle through $A,D,E,X,Y$.

Step 1: $AX=AY$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. By the tangent-chord angle and then the cyclic quadrilateral $BDEC$, \[\measuredangle(t,AB)=\measuredangle(t,AD)=\measuredangle(EA,ED)=\measuredangle(EC,ED)=\measuredangle(BC,BD)=\measuredangle(BC,AB),\] so $t\parallel BC$. A tangent parallel to the chord $XY$ touches $\omega$ at the midpoint of an arc $XY$, so $AX=AY$, and in directed angles \[\measuredangle AXY=\measuredangle XYA. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $QB=QC$. By the tangent-chord angle in $\Omega$ and in $\Gamma$, then the circle $\omega$, then (1): \[\measuredangle(BQ,BC)=\measuredangle(DB,DX)=\measuredangle(DA,DX)=\measuredangle(YA,YX),\qquad \measuredangle(CB,CQ)=\measuredangle(EY,EC)=\measuredangle(EY,EA)=\measuredangle(XY,XA).\] By (1) these are equal, so in triangle $QBC$ the angles at $B$ and $C$ are equal (as directed angles $\measuredangle QBC=\measuredangle BCQ$), and $QB=QC$.

Step 3: $PD=PE$. Similarly, \[\measuredangle(DP,DE)=\measuredangle(DP,DX)+\measuredangle(DX,DE)=\measuredangle(BD,BX)+\measuredangle(YX,YE)=\measuredangle(BA,YE),\] \[\measuredangle(ED,EP)=\measuredangle(ED,EY)+\measuredangle(EY,EP)=\measuredangle(XD,XY)+\measuredangle(CY,CE)=\measuredangle(XD,CA),\] using the tangent-chord angles in $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, the circle $\omega$, and $BX=CY=BC$ as lines. The two right sides are equal if and only if $\measuredangle(CA,YE)=\measuredangle(XD,BA)$, i.e. $\measuredangle(EA,EY)=\measuredangle(DX,DA)$. In $\omega$, $\measuredangle(EA,EY)=\measuredangle(XA,XY)$ and $\measuredangle(DX,DA)=\measuredangle(YX,YA)$, which are equal by (1). Hence $\measuredangle PDE=\measuredangle DEP$ and $PD=PE$.

Step 4: the radical axis. $\Omega\ne\Gamma$ (a circle cannot meet line $BC$ in the four points $B,X,Y,C$). The power of $A$ with respect to $\Omega$ is $AD\cdot AB$ and with respect to $\Gamma$ is $AE\cdot AC$; these are equal because $BDEC$ is cyclic. The powers of $P$ are $PD^2$ and $PE^2$, and those of $Q$ are $QB^2$ and $QC^2$; they are equal by Steps 3 and 2. So $A$, $P$, $Q$ all lie on the radical axis of $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, which is a line.

Solution 2 (Oblique coordinates along $BA$ and $BC$)

Scale so that $BC=1$ and put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(u,v)$ with $v>0$, $X=(p,0)$, $Y=(q,0)$, $0<p<q<1$. Let $\lambda=BA^2=u^2+v^2$ and $\mu=CA^2=(u-1)^2+v^2$.

The points $D,E$. The circle $ADE$ passes through $X$ and $Y$. The power of $B$ with respect to it is $BX\cdot BY=pq=BD\cdot BA$, so $D=B+\frac{pq}{\lambda}\overrightarrow{BA}$; likewise $E=C+\frac{(1-p)(1-q)}{\mu}\overrightarrow{CA}$. The power of $A$ with respect to the circle $BDEC$ gives $AD\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC$, that is, $\lambda-pq=\mu-(1-p)(1-q)$; expanding, this says $2u=p+q$. Put $\delta=\lambda-pq=\mu-(1-p)(1-q)=AD\cdot AB>0$. The angle at $A$ is obtuse: $\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}=\lambda-u<0$, so \[\delta<u-pq=\tfrac{p+q}2-pq<q-pq=q(1-p).\tag{1}\]

Two oblique coordinate systems. Give the point $B+h\,\overrightarrow{BA}+l\,\overrightarrow{BC}=(uh+l,\,vh)$ the coordinates $(h,l)$. Then line $BA$ is $l=0$, line $BC$ is $h=0$, $A=(1,0)$, $D=(h_D,0)$ with $h_D=\frac{pq}\lambda$, and $X=(0,p)$. Also put $m=l+h-1$; then the same point is $C+h\,\overrightarrow{CA}+m\,\overrightarrow{BC}=(1+(u-1)h+m,\,vh)$, so in the coordinates $(h,m)$ line $CA$ is $m=0$, $C=(0,0)$, $E=(h_E,0)$ with $h_E=\frac{(1-p)(1-q)}\mu$, and $Y=(0,q-1)$.

The circles. In the coordinates $(h,l)$, $x^2+y^2=\lambda h^2+2uhl+l^2$, and a circle through $B$ is $x^2+y^2+\alpha x+\beta y=0$. Fitting $D$ and $X$, the circle $\Omega$ is \[\lambda h^2+2uhl+l^2-pq\,h-p\,l=0 .\] In the coordinates $(h,m)$, $(x-1)^2+y^2=\mu h^2+2(u-1)hm+m^2$, and fitting $C$, $E$ and $Y$, the circle $\Gamma$ is \[\mu h^2+2(u-1)hm+m^2-(1-p)(1-q)\,h+(1-q)\,m=0 .\] (At $D$: $h_D(\lambda h_D-pq)=0$; at $X$: $p^2-p^2=0$; at $E$: $h_E(\mu h_E-(1-p)(1-q))=0$; at $Y$: $(q-1)^2+(1-q)(q-1)=0$.)

The tangents. Affine coordinates map lines to lines, so the tangent to a curve $\Phi=0$ at a point $Z_0$ of it is $\partial_1\Phi(Z_0)(h-h_0)+\partial_2\Phi(Z_0)(l-l_0)=0$ in either system.

  • At $B$: $pq\,h+p\,l=0$, i.e. $l=-qh$. At $C$: $-(1-p)(1-q)h+(1-q)m=0$, i.e. $m=(1-p)h$.

  • At $D$: the partial derivatives are $2\lambda h_D-pq=pq$ and $2uh_D-p$; multiplying the tangent equation by $\lambda/(pq)$ it becomes $f_1=0$, where \[f_1=\lambda h+\Bigl(2u-\frac\lambda q\Bigr)l-pq .\]

  • At $E$: the partial derivatives are $(1-p)(1-q)$ and $2(u-1)h_E+1-q$; multiplying by $\mu/((1-p)(1-q))$ the tangent becomes $f_2=0$, where \[f_2=\mu h+\Bigl(2(u-1)+\frac{\mu}{1-p}\Bigr)m-(1-p)(1-q),\qquad m=l+h-1 .\]

So $P$ satisfies $f_1=f_2=0$. For $Q$: $l=-qh$ and $l=m-h+1=1-ph$, so $h=\frac1{p-q}$; thus $Q$ has $h_Q=\frac1{p-q}\ne1$, $l_Q=-qh_Q$, $m_Q=(1-p)h_Q$, and $Q\ne A$.

The line $AQ$. Let $g=f_1-f_2$, an affine function of $(h,l)$, with $g(P)=0$.

  • At $A$ ($h=1$, $l=m=0$): $f_1=\lambda-pq=\delta$ and $f_2=\mu-(1-p)(1-q)=\delta$, so $g(A)=0$.

  • At $Q$, with $h=h_Q$: $f_1=2\lambda h-2uqh-pq$ and $f_2=2\mu h+2(u-1)(1-p)h-(1-p)(1-q)$. Using $\lambda-\mu=2u-1$ and $2u=p+q$, \[g(Q)=2h\bigl(u(1+p-q)-p\bigr)+1-p-q=h(q-p)(1-p-q)+1-p-q=0,\] because $2u(1+p-q)-2p=(p+q)(1+p-q)-2p=(q-p)(1-p-q)$ and $h(q-p)=-1$.

  • The coefficient of $l$ in $g$ is $\bigl(2u-\frac\lambda q\bigr)-\bigl(2(u-1)+\frac\mu{1-p}\bigr)=2-\frac\lambda q-\frac\mu{1-p}$. With $\lambda=pq+\delta$ and $\mu=(1-p)(1-q)+\delta$ it equals $(1-p+q)\Bigl(1-\frac{\delta}{q(1-p)}\Bigr)$, which is positive by (1).

Show the solution

Solution (Counting visits to multiples of 3)

Call rows and columns lines; $n=1000$. A line is open if its number of lollies is a multiple of $3$. Every lolly raises the count of one row and one column; the line Bluey chose was open just before. Call a placement wasteful if both its row and its column were open just before it. Let $T$ be the total number of lollies and $W$ the number of wasteful placements.

When does Bluey stop? An open line has a multiple of $3$ lollies, so at most $999<1000$, hence an empty cell. So Bluey stops exactly when no line is open. In the final position let $n_L$ be the count of line $L$; then $n_L\equiv1$ or $2\pmod 3$ for all $2n$ lines. Let $k$ be the number of lines with $n_L\equiv2$, of which $x$ are rows and $y$ are columns.

The identity $T=4n-3W-k$. For a line $L$, the number of placements made in $L$ while $L$ was open equals the number of multiples of $3$ among $0,1,\dots,n_L-1$, which is $\lceil n_L/3\rceil$. A non-wasteful placement is made while exactly one of its two lines is open, a wasteful one while both are. Hence \[\sum_L\Bigl\lceil\frac{n_L}{3}\Bigr\rceil=T+W.\] If $n_L\equiv\rho_L\in\{1,2\}\pmod3$ then $\lceil n_L/3\rceil=\frac{n_L-\rho_L}3+1$. Using $\sum_Ln_L=2T$ (each lolly is counted in one row and one column) and $\sum_L\rho_L=2n+k$, \[\frac{2T-2n-k}{3}+2n=T+W\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=4n-3W-k.\]

Upper bound. The first placement is wasteful (everything is empty), so $W\ge1$.

  • If the last placement is also wasteful (and $T\ge2$), then $W\ge2$ and $T\le4n-6$.

  • Otherwise, in the last placement the chosen line goes to a count $\equiv1$, and the other line was not open, so it had count $\equiv1$ or $\equiv2$. In the second case it would end with count $\equiv0$, i.e. open, which is impossible at the end. So it goes from $\equiv1$ to $\equiv2$, and $k\ge1$. Moreover the sum of the row counts is $T$ and so is the sum of the column counts; modulo $3$ these sums are $n+x$ and $n+y$, so $x\equiv y\pmod3$. With $x+y=k\ge1$ this rules out $k=1$, so $k\ge2$ and $T\le4n-3-2=4n-5$.

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Prove that there are finitely many prime numbers $p$ such that

  • $p-8$ is a perfect square, and

  • for all odd positive integers $k<\sqrt{p}$, the number $p-k^2$ has at most two distinct prime factors.

Show the answer

proof (in fact the primes are exactly $17,89,233,1097$)

Show the solution

Solution (Three values of $p-k^2$ divisible by one small prime)

Call a prime $p$ good if it has both properties. Write $p=n^2+8$. Since $p=9$ is not prime, $p$ is odd and $n$ is odd with $n\ge3$, so $p\ge17$. We show that all but finitely many $n$ are impossible.

Step 1: the shape of $n$ (use $k=3$). As $3<\sqrt p$, the number $p-9=(n-1)(n+1)$ has at most two prime factors; it is even, so $(n-1)(n+1)=2^aq^b$ with $q$ an odd prime and $a\ge3$, $b\ge0$. The numbers $n\pm1$ are even with $\gcd(n-1,n+1)=2$, so one of them is exactly divisible by $2$, and their odd parts are coprime with product $q^b$. Hence $\{n-1,n+1\}$ is $\{2,\,2^{a-1}q^b\}$ or $\{2^{a-1},\,2q^b\}$. The first option gives $n=3$. In the second there are two cases.

Case 1: $n-1=2^{a-1}$, $n+1=2q^b$. Then $q^b-1=2^{a-2}$, and $b\ge1$ (otherwise $n=1$). If $b$ is even, $(q^{b/2}-1)(q^{b/2}+1)=2^{a-2}$ is a product of two powers of $2$ that differ by $2$, so they are $2$ and $4$; then $q^b=9$, $n=17$, $p=297=3^3\cdot11$, not prime. If $b$ is odd, the factor $1+q+\dots+q^{b-1}$ of $q^b-1$ is odd and a power of $2$, so it is $1$ and $b=1$. Then $q=2^{a-2}+1$ with $a\ge3$. If $a-2$ had an odd divisor $d>1$, say $a-2=de$, then $2^e+1$ would be a proper divisor of $q$; so $a-2=2^c$. If $c\ge4$, then $16\mid 2^c$ and $2^{16}\equiv1\pmod{17}$ give $2^{a-2}\equiv1$, $n=2\cdot2^{a-2}+1\equiv3$ and $p=n^2+8\equiv17\equiv0\pmod{17}$, impossible for the prime $p>17$. So $c\le3$, i.e. $a-2\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, and $n=2^{a-1}+1\in\{5,9,33,513\}$: finitely many.

Case 2: $n+1=2^{a-1}$, $n-1=2q^b$. Then $q^b=2^{a-2}-1$. If $a$ is even, $(2^{(a-2)/2}-1)(2^{(a-2)/2}+1)=q^b$ is a product of two powers of $q$ differing by $2$; the smaller one divides $2$, so it is $1$, giving $n=7$ and $p=57=3\cdot19$, not prime. So $a$ is odd and $n=2^{a-1}-1$.

Step 2: a small prime factor of $n$ modulo which $p$ is a square. We claim that in Case 2, apart from finitely many $n$, there is a prime $r\ge5$ with $r\mid n$, $r\le\sqrt n$, and $p$ a quadratic residue modulo $r$. For a prime $r\mid n$ we have $p\equiv8=2\cdot2^2\pmod r$, so $p$ is a quadratic residue modulo $r$ exactly when $2$ is.

  • If $a-1$ is not a power of $2$, write $a-1=2^es$ with $s>1$ odd; here $e\ge1$ because $a$ is odd. Then $2^s-1$ divides $2^{a-1}-1=n$, and $2^s-1\le2^{(a-1)/2}-1<\sqrt n$. Let $r$ be a prime factor of $2^s-1$. From $2^s\equiv1$ we get $2\equiv2^{s+1}=\bigl(2^{(s+1)/2}\bigr)^2\pmod r$, so $2$ is a square modulo $r$. Also $r\ne3$, because $2^s\equiv2\pmod 3$ for odd $s$.

  • If $a-1=2^d$ with $d\ge4$, then $16\mid a-1$, so $17\mid 2^{16}-1\mid n$; moreover $6^2=36\equiv2\pmod{17}$, and $17\le\sqrt n$ as $n\ge2^{16}-1$. Take $r=17$.

The remaining values $d\le3$ give only $n\in\{3,15,255\}$.

Step 3: the contradiction. Take such $n$ and $r$, and choose $m$ with $0\le m<r$ and $m^2\equiv p\pmod r$. Replacing $m$ by $r-m$ if necessary, $m$ is odd (note $m\ne0$ since $r\nmid p$), so $1\le m\le r-2$. As $r\ge5$ and $r^2\le n$, we have $n\ge5r$ and \[p>n^2\ge25r^2 .\] Consider \[U_1=p-m^2,\qquad U_2=p-(4r-m)^2,\qquad U_3=p-(4r+m)^2 .\] The numbers $m,4r-m,4r+m$ are odd and less than $5r<\sqrt p$, so each $U_i$ is positive and has at most two distinct prime factors. Each $U_i$ is even (difference of two odd numbers) and divisible by $r$ (because $(4r\pm m)^2\equiv m^2\equiv p\pmod r$). Hence each $U_i$ has the form $2^\alpha r^\beta$. Their differences are \[U_1-U_2=8r(2r-m),\qquad U_1-U_3=8r(2r+m),\qquad U_2-U_3=16rm .\] Since $m$ and $2r\pm m$ are odd and not divisible by $r$ (as $0<m<r$), each pairwise greatest common divisor divides $16r$. Therefore at most one $U_i$ is divisible by $32$ and at most one is divisible by $r^2$, so some $U_i$ has $\alpha\le4$ and $\beta\le1$, i.e. $U_i\le16r$. The smallest of the three is $U_3$, so $U_3\le16r$, and with $4r+m\le5r-2$, \[p\le(5r-2)^2+16r=25r^2-4r+4<25r^2,\] a contradiction.

Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $P$ be a convex polyhedron. Anne wants to colour all vertices of $P$ either red or blue. We call an edge of $P$ checkered if it connects two vertices of different colours. We call a face of $P$ diverse if at least half of its edges are checkered. Show that if every vertex of $P$ is a vertex of exactly three faces, then Anne can find a colouring where every face of $P$ is diverse.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A colouring with the most checkered edges)

There are finitely many colourings; choose one with the largest possible number of checkered edges.

Every vertex has at most one non-checkered edge. Each vertex $v$ lies on exactly three faces, hence on exactly three edges. If two or three of these edges were non-checkered, recolour $v$ alone. The edges at $v$ swap status (checkered $\leftrightarrow$ non-checkered) and no other edge changes, so the number of checkered edges goes up by at least $2-1=1$, contradicting maximality.

Every face is diverse. The boundary of a face with $m$ edges is a cycle $v_1v_2\ldots v_m$. Two consecutive edges of this cycle share a vertex, so by the previous paragraph they are not both non-checkered. A set of edges of an $m$-cycle with no two consecutive has at most $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ elements, so at least $m-\lfloor m/2\rfloor\ge m/2$ edges of the face are checkered. Hence every face is diverse, and this colouring works.

Solution 2 (The four colour theorem and a Tait colouring)

Local structure. Around a vertex $V$ the faces and edges at $V$ alternate. As $V$ lies on exactly three faces, it has exactly three edges, and each of them separates two of the three faces at $V$; so every two faces at $V$ share an edge.

Colouring the faces. By the four colour theorem (applied to the planar graph whose vertices are the faces of $P$, two faces being joined when they share an edge), the faces can be coloured with four colours so that faces with a common edge get different colours. Use as colours the four pairs $00,01,10,11$ of bits, and label every edge with the bitwise sum modulo $2$ of the colours of its two faces. The two faces are different colours, so every label is $01$, $10$ or $11$.

One edge of each label at every vertex. Let the three faces at $V$ have colours $c_1,c_2,c_3$. They are pairwise different, since every two of these faces share an edge. The edges at $V$ have the labels $c_1+c_2$, $c_2+c_3$, $c_3+c_1$, and these are different: for example $c_1+c_2=c_2+c_3$ would give $c_1=c_3$. So $V$ has exactly one edge of each of the labels $01,10,11$.

Colouring the vertices. Let $G$ be the set of edges labelled $01$ or $10$. Every vertex lies on exactly two edges of $G$, one of each label. Hence $G$ splits into disjoint cycles through all vertices, and along each cycle the labels alternate, so each cycle has even length. Colour the vertices of each cycle alternately red and blue. Then every edge of $G$ joins two consecutive vertices of a cycle and is checkered.

Every face is diverse. Every uncheckered edge has label $11$, and no two edges labelled $11$ share a vertex (each vertex has only one). Let a face have $s$ edges; its boundary is a cycle with $s$ vertices. Its uncheckered edges share no vertex, and each uses two of these $s$ vertices, so there are at most $s/2$ of them. Hence at least half of the edges of every face are checkered, and every face is diverse.

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities

Determine all integers $n\geqslant 2$ such that the polynomial \[P_n(x,y,z)=x^n+y^n+z^n-xyz^{n-2}-xy^{n-2}z-x^{n-2}yz\] can be written as $P_n(x,y,z)=Q_n(x,y,z)\cdot R_n(x,y,z)$, where $Q_n(x,y,z)$ and $R_n(x,y,z)$ are non-constant polynomials with integer coefficients.

$n=3$ only

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Specialisations and Eisenstein)

$n=3$ works: $P_3=x^3+y^3+z^3-3xyz=(x+y+z)(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx)$.

From now on $n\ne3$ and suppose $P_n=QR$ with non-constant $Q,R\in\mathbb Z[x,y,z]$. Since $P_n$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, so are $Q$ and $R$ (the products of the lowest-degree parts and of the highest-degree parts of $Q,R$ are the lowest- and highest-degree parts of $P_n$); let their degrees be $d$ and $n-d$, with $1\le d\le n-1$.

Pure powers occur in $Q$ and $R$. Let $\alpha,\beta$ be the degrees of $Q,R$ in $x$ alone. Then $\alpha\le d$, $\beta\le n-d$, and $\alpha+\beta=n$ because $P_n$ contains $x^n$. So $\alpha=d$: the only monomial of degree $d$ in $Q$ that has $x$-degree $d$ is $x^d$, hence $Q$ contains $x^d$ with a nonzero coefficient; likewise $y^d$ and $z^d$, and similarly for $R$. Consequently every specialisation used below has the full degree: $Q(x,y,0)$ is a binary form of degree $d$, and $Q(x,1,1)$ has degree exactly $d$ in $x$.

$n=2$ and $n=4$. $P_2(x,y,0)=x^2-xy+y^2$ and $P_4(x,y,0)=x^4+y^4$ are irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ (the first has discriminant $-3$; the second is $y^4\Phi_8(x/y)$). But $Q(x,y,0)R(x,y,0)$ would be a factorisation into forms of degrees $d,n-d\ge1$. Contradiction.

$n\ge5$. Then $P_n(x,y,0)=x^n+y^n$. Put $y=z=1$: \[P_n(x,1,1)=x^n-x^{n-2}-2x+2=(x-1)(x^{n-1}+x^{n-2}-2)=(x-1)^2h(x),\] \[h(x)=x^{n-2}+2x^{n-3}+2x^{n-4}+\cdots+2x+2 .\] By Eisenstein's criterion at $2$, $h$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, of degree $n-2$, and $h(1)\ne0$. Now $Q(x,1,1)R(x,1,1)=(x-1)^2h(x)$, so $h$ divides one factor; renaming, $h\mid R(x,1,1)$, whence $n-d\ge n-2$, i.e. $d\le2<n-2$. Then $h\nmid Q(x,1,1)$, so $Q(x,1,1)\mid(x-1)^2$ and $Q(x,1,1)=c(x-1)^d$ with $c\ne0$. Since $P_n$ is symmetric, the same argument (with $y$ or $z$ as the free variable; $h$ can only divide the factor of degree $n-d$) gives $Q(1,y,1)=c'(y-1)^d$ and $Q(1,1,z)=c''(z-1)^d$.

Also $Q(x,y,0)$ divides $x^n+y^n$, and its degree is $d$.

Case $d=1$. $Q=\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z$ with $\alpha\beta\gamma\ne0$. The rational number $t=-\beta/\alpha$ satisfies $t^n=-1$ (as $\alpha x+\beta y$ divides $x^n+y^n$), so $t=-1$ and $\alpha=\beta$; similarly $\beta=\gamma$. But then $Q(x,1,1)=\alpha(x+2)$, not a multiple of $x-1$. Contradiction.

Case $d=2$. $x^n+y^n$ has at most one rational linear factor ($x+y$, when $n$ is odd, and only to the first power), so $Q(x,y,0)$ is a constant times an irreducible rational quadratic form dividing $x^n+y^n$. Its roots $x/y$ are roots of unity $\zeta$ with $\zeta^n=-1$ and of degree $2$, i.e. of order $3$, $4$ or $6$. Order $3$ is impossible ($\zeta^n$ would be a cube root of unity), so the form is $x^2+y^2$ (needs $n\equiv2\pmod 4$) or $x^2-xy+y^2$ (needs $n\equiv3\pmod 6$); which one is decided by $n$. The same holds for $Q(x,0,z)$ and $Q(0,y,z)$, and comparing the coefficients of $x^2,y^2,z^2$ shows that \[Q=c\,(x^2+y^2+z^2)+u\,(xy+yz+zx),\qquad u\in\{0,-c\}.\] Then $Q(x,1,1)=cx^2+2ux+2c+u$ must equal $c(x-1)^2$, so $u=-c$, $n\equiv3\pmod6$, $n\ge9$ and $Q=c(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx)$. Put $y=1$, $z=-1$ (with $n$ odd): \[P_n(x,1,-1)=x^n+x^{n-2}+2x=x\bigl(x^{n-3}(x^2+1)+2\bigr),\qquad Q(x,1,-1)=c(x^2+3).\] So $x^2+3$ divides $x^{n-3}(x^2+1)+2$. Substituting a root ($x^2=-3$, and $n-3$ is even) gives $-2(-3)^{(n-3)/2}+2=0$, i.e. $(-3)^{(n-3)/2}=1$, which forces $n=3$. Contradiction.

Hence $P_n$ factors exactly for $\boxed{n=3}$.

Solution 2 (Substituting $z=-y$ and working modulo $2$)

$n=3$ works: $P_3=(x+y+z)(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx)$.

Pure powers. Suppose $P_n=QR$ with non-constant $Q,R\in\mathbb Z[x,y,z]$. Comparing lowest- and highest-degree parts, $Q$ and $R$ are homogeneous, of degrees $d$ and $n-d$ with $1\le d\le n-1$. The substitution $z=-y$. Lemma. $S_j$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Z$. For $j=1$, $S_1=x^2+3$ has no rational root. Let $j\ge2$ and suppose $S_j=gh$ with monic non-constant $g,h\in\mathbb Z[x]$. As $g(0)h(0)=2$, one of them, say $g$, has $g(0)=\pm1$. Modulo $2$, $S_j\equiv x^{2j-2}(x+1)^2$, and $x$ does not divide $g$ modulo $2$, so by unique factorisation in $\mathbb F_2[x]$ the reduction of $g$ divides $(x+1)^2$, and $\deg g\le2$. If $\deg g=1$, then $g=x\mp1$ and $S_j(\pm1)=0$; but $S_j(\pm1)=4$. If $\deg g=2$, then $g\equiv x^2+1\pmod 2$, so $g=x^2+2ux+c$ with $c=g(0)=\pm1$. If $(u,c)\ne(0,1)$, the discriminant $4(u^2-c)$ is $\ge0$, so $g$ has a real root, while $S_j(x)\ge2$ for all real $x$. If $g=x^2+1$, then $S_j(i)=(-1)^j+(-1)^{j-1}+2=2\ne0$. Each case is a contradiction.

Even $n$. Then $Q(x,1,-1)\,R(x,1,-1)=S_{n/2}(x)$ would split the irreducible $S_{n/2}$ into factors of degrees $d,n-d\ge1$. So $P_n$ does not factor.

Odd $n\ge5$. Now $Q(x,1,-1)\,R(x,1,-1)=xS_{(n-1)/2}(x)$, and $S_{(n-1)/2}$ is irreducible of degree $n-1$. It divides one factor, say $R(x,1,-1)$; then $n-d\ge n-1$, so $d=1$ and $Q(x,1,-1)=cx$ with $c\ne0$. Writing $Q=\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z$, this gives $\beta=\gamma$. As $P_n$ is symmetric, $P_n(1,t,-1)=P_n(t,1,-1)=tS_{(n-1)/2}(t)$. The linear polynomial $Q(1,t,-1)=\alpha-\gamma+\beta t$ is not divisible by $S_{(n-1)/2}$ (degree $n-1\ge4$), so $S_{(n-1)/2}$ divides $R(1,t,-1)$, whence $Q(1,t,-1)=c't$ and $\alpha=\gamma$. Thus $Q=\alpha(x+y+z)$ and $P_n(2,-1,-1)=0$. But for odd $n$, \[P_n(2,-1,-1)=2^n-2-2-2-2^{n-2}=3\cdot2^{n-2}-6,\] which vanishes only for $n=3$. Contradiction.

Hence $P_n$ factors exactly for $\boxed{n=3}$.

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral with circumcircle $\Omega$ such that $CD>BD>BC$. The tangents to $\Omega$ at $B$ and $C$ meet at a point $S$. A line $\ell$ is called coastal if:

  • $\ell$ does not pass through $A$,

  • $\ell$ intersects ray $SD$ at a point $X_\ell$ beyond $D$, and

  • there is a circle tangent to line $\ell$, line $X_\ell A$, segment $SB$ and segment $SC$.

Prove that there is a circle that is tangent to $\Omega$ and all coastal lines.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pole of $SD$, a homothety from $S$, and a homothety from $U$)

Let $O$ be the centre of $\Omega$. For a point $P\ne O$, its polar is taken with respect to $\Omega$; we use La Hire's principle ($P$ lies on the polar of $Q$ iff $Q$ lies on the polar of $P$) and Brocard's theorem: for four distinct points of $\Omega$, the three intersection points of opposite sides of the complete quadrangle form a self-polar triangle (with points at infinity allowed). Directed lengths are written with a bar.

The configuration. If $D$ were on the minor arc $BC$ we would have $BD<BC$; so the minor arc $BC$, which is the part of $\Omega$ seen from $S$, contains neither $D$ nor (by the order $A,B,C,D$) $A$. Hence line $SD$ meets $\Omega$ at $D$ and at a point of the minor arc $BC$, so $A\notin SD$, and the points of ray $SD$ beyond $D$ lie outside $\Omega$. Moreover $O\notin SD$: otherwise $D$ would be on the symmetry axis $SO$ of $B$ and $C$, and $BD=CD$.

The circle $\Gamma$. Let $K$ be the pole of line $SD$; it lies outside $\Omega$ because $SD$ is a secant. Line $KA$ is not tangent at $A$ (that would put $A$ on the polar $SD$ of $K$), so it meets $\Omega$ again at a point $U\ne A$. Let $\nu$ be the homothety with centre $U$ and ratio $\kappa=\overline{UA}/\overline{UK}$ (so $\nu(K)=A$; $\kappa\ne0,1$), and let $\Gamma=\nu(\Omega)$. Since $U\in\Omega$, $\Gamma$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $U$. We show that every coastal line is tangent to $\Gamma$.

Setup for a coastal line. Let $\ell$ be coastal, $X=X_\ell$, and $\omega$ a circle tangent to $\ell$, to line $XA$ and to the segments $SB$, $SC$. Both $\omega$ and $\Omega$ are inscribed in angle $BSC$, so a homothety $\rho$ with centre $S$ and positive ratio maps $\omega$ to $\Omega$.

Case $\omega=\Omega$. Then line $XA$ is the tangent at $A$ and $\ell$ is the other tangent from $X$, touching at $T$. As $X$ lies on $SD$, the polar of $K$, the point $K$ lies on the polar $AT$ of $X$; so $T$ is the second point of $KA$ on $\Omega$, i.e. $T=U$. The homothety $\nu$ fixes $U$ and maps the tangent to $\Omega$ at $U$ to the parallel tangent of $\Gamma$ at $U$, i.e. to itself. So $\ell$ touches $\Gamma$ at $U$.

Case $\omega\ne\Omega$. Then the ratio of $\rho$ is not $1$. Put $Y=\rho(X)\in SD$. The lines $XA$ and $\ell$ (tangents to $\omega$ through $X$) are mapped to the two tangents from $Y$ to $\Omega$; let $E$ and $F$ be their points of contact, so $YE\parallel XA$ and $YF\parallel\ell$, and $E\ne F$.

  • $E\ne A$: $S\notin XA$ (else $XA=SD\ni A$), so $YE$ is a line parallel to and different from $XA$, and it cannot contain $A\in XA$.

  • $K\in EF$: $Y$ lies on $SD$, the polar of $K$, so $K$ lies on the polar of $Y$, which is $EF$.

  • Since $K\in AU$, $K\in EF$ and $K\notin\Omega$: if $F=A$ then $E=U$ (otherwise the lines $AU$, $AE$ meet only at $A$), and conversely if $E=U$ then $F=A$; and $F\ne U$ (otherwise $AU$, $EU$ would meet only at $U$, as $E\ne A$).

Key claim: if $L$ is the intersection of lines $UF$ and $\ell$, then $AL\parallel EF$ and $\nu(F)=L$.

Main case: $A,E,U,F$ are four distinct points. By Brocard's theorem applied to them, the point $J=AE\cap UF$ lies on the polar of $AU\cap EF=K$, that is, on line $SD$ (if $AE\parallel UF$, this means that $AE$ and $UF$ are parallel to $SD$). Let $\tau$ be the homothety with centre $J$ (a translation along $SD$ in the parallel case) that maps $A$ to $E$. It fixes the lines $SD$ and $UF$ through $J$. It maps line $XA$ to the parallel line through $E$, which is $YE$; hence $\tau(X)=\tau(XA\cap SD)=YE\cap SD=Y$. Then $\tau(\ell)$ is the line through $Y$ parallel to $\ell$, namely $YF$. Line $\ell$ is not parallel to $UF$ (otherwise $YF=\tau(\ell)$ would be parallel to $UF$ and share the point $F$ with it, so $U$ would lie on the tangent at $F$, forcing $U=F$); so $L$ exists and $\tau(L)=YF\cap UF=F$. As $\tau$ maps $A\mapsto E$ and $L\mapsto F$, we get $AL\parallel EF$. Finally $A$ lies on line $UK$ with $\overline{UA}=\kappa\,\overline{UK}$, $L$ lies on line $UF$, and $AL\parallel KF$ (as $K\in EF$, $K\ne F$); by the intercept theorem $\overline{UL}=\kappa\,\overline{UF}$, i.e. $L=\nu(F)$.

Special case: $E=U$, $F=A$. Then $XA\parallel YE$ is parallel to the tangent at $U$, $\ell\parallel YF$ is parallel to the tangent at $A$, $Y$ is the intersection of these two tangents, and $L=\ell\cap UA$. Let $M=AU\cap SD$. The homothety with centre $M$ taking $Y$ to $X$ maps the tangent $YU$ to the parallel line through $X$, which is $XA$, so it maps $U\mapsto A$; and it maps the tangent $YA$ to $\ell$, so $A\mapsto L$. Hence, with a coordinate on line $AU$ having $M=0$ and $A=a$, $U=u$: $L=a^2/u$. Since $M$ is on the polar of $K$ and $K\in AU$, the pair $K,M$ divides $AU$ harmonically, which gives $K=\frac{2au}{a+u}$. A direct computation now yields $(L-u)(K-u)=(a-u)^2$, i.e. $\overline{UL}=\kappa\,\overline{UA}$, so again $L=\nu(F)$. (If $AU\parallel SD$, the homothety is a translation, $K$ is the midpoint of $AU$, $L=2A-U$, and the same relation holds.)

Conclusion. In every case $L=\nu(F)$ lies on $\ell$. The homothety $\nu$ maps $\Omega$ to $\Gamma$ and the tangent of $\Omega$ at $F$, which is the line $YF$, to the tangent of $\Gamma$ at $L$, a line through $L$ parallel to $YF$. Since $\ell\parallel YF$ and $L\in\ell$, this tangent is $\ell$. Therefore every coastal line is tangent to the fixed circle $\Gamma$, which is tangent to $\Omega$ at $U$.

Solution 2 (A fixed circle from the tangent equations)

Put $S$ at the origin with ray $SD$ as the positive $x$-axis. Let $2\gamma=\angle BSC$ ($0<\gamma<90^\circ$), let $(e,f)$ be the unit vector along the internal bisector of $\angle BSC$, and $d=\sin\gamma$.

Circles tangent to the segments $SB$ and $SC$. Let a circle with centre $K$ be tangent to segments $SB$ and $SC$, and let $\hat b,\hat c$ be the unit vectors along $SB,SC$. Its points of tangency are the feet of $K$ on the two lines, $(K\cdot\hat b)\hat b$ and $(K\cdot\hat c)\hat c$; they lie on the rays $SB,SC$ and are not $S$ (two different lines cannot touch a circle at the same point), so $K\cdot\hat b>0$ and $K\cdot\hat c>0$. As $K$ is equally far from both lines, it lies on one of the two bisectors. On the external bisector $K\cdot\hat b=-K\cdot\hat c$, and on the ray of the internal bisector opposite to the angle both are negative. Hence $K=t(e,f)$ with $t>0$, and the radius is the distance to $SB$, namely $t\sin\gamma=dt$. In particular $\Omega$ (tangent to $SB$ at $B$ and to $SC$ at $C$) has centre $U=t_0(e,f)$ and radius $dt_0$ for some $t_0>0$.

Position of $D$. The circle $\Omega$ lies in the angular region $BSC$ and touches its sides only at $B$ and $C$. So $D$ lies strictly inside the angle, the angle between $(1,0)$ and $(e,f)$ is less than $\gamma$, and $f^2<d^2$. Also $f\ne0$: otherwise $SD$ is the bisector, and the reflection in it fixes $\Omega$ and $D$ and swaps $B$ and $C$, giving $DB=DC$, contrary to $CD>BD$. Put \[F=\frac{d^2+f^2}{d^2-f^2},\qquad G=\frac{2df}{d^2-f^2}\ne0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad F^2-G^2=1,\quad (F+1)f=dG .\] Let $A=(a,b)$.

If $b=0$. Then $A$ is on line $SD$. For a coastal line $\ell$ the point $X_\ell$ is on this line and $X_\ell\ne A$ (as $\ell$ avoids $A$), so $X_\ell A$ is the $x$-axis. A circle with centre $t(e,f)$, $t>0$, and radius $dt$ is at distance $|f|t<dt$ from the $x$-axis, so the $x$-axis is not tangent to it. Hence there are no coastal lines, and any circle tangent to $\Omega$ does the job.

If $b\ne0$. Let $\kappa$ be the circle with centre $V=(a,-Fb)$ and radius $\rho=|Gb|>0$. We show that $\kappa$ touches every coastal line and $\Omega$.

Tangency to a coastal line. Let $\ell$ be coastal, $X=X_\ell=(x,0)$ with $x>0$, and let the circle tangent to $\ell$, $XA$, $SB$, $SC$ have centre $K=t(e,f)$, $t>0$, and radius $dt$. For each of the two lines $\ell$ and $XA$ through $X$ choose the unit normal $\nu$ for which $\nu\cdot(K-X)=-dt$ (the signed distance of $K$ from the line is $-dt$); write $\nu=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ for $\ell$ and $\nu=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ for $XA$. As $\nu\cdot X=x\cos\theta$ (resp. $x\cos\varphi$), \[x\cos\theta=t(e\cos\theta+f\sin\theta+d),\qquad x\cos\varphi=t(e\cos\varphi+f\sin\varphi+d).\] Multiplying the first equation by $e\cos\varphi+f\sin\varphi+d$, the second by $e\cos\theta+f\sin\theta+d$, subtracting and dividing by $x\ne0$ gives $\cos\theta\,(f\sin\varphi+d)=\cos\varphi\,(f\sin\theta+d)$, that is, \[f\sin(\varphi-\theta)+d(\cos\theta-\cos\varphi)=0.\] The lines $\ell\ne XA$ both pass through $X$, so their normals are not parallel and $\sin\frac{\varphi-\theta}2\ne0$. Using $\sin(\varphi-\theta)=2\sin\frac{\varphi-\theta}2\cos\frac{\varphi-\theta}2$ and $\cos\theta-\cos\varphi=2\sin\frac{\varphi+\theta}2\sin\frac{\varphi-\theta}2$, we get $f\cos\frac{\varphi-\theta}2+d\sin\frac{\varphi+\theta}2=0$. With $c_1=\cos\frac\theta2$, $s_1=\sin\frac\theta2$, $c_2=\cos\frac\varphi2$, $s_2=\sin\frac\varphi2$ this reads \[c_2(fc_1+ds_1)+s_2(dc_1+fs_1)=0 .\] The numbers $fc_1+ds_1$ and $dc_1+fs_1$ are not both $0$ (the system has determinant $f^2-d^2\ne0$ and $(c_1,s_1)\ne(0,0)$), so $(c_2,s_2)=\lambda\bigl(dc_1+fs_1,\,-(fc_1+ds_1)\bigr)$ for some $\lambda\ne0$. Hence \[\cos\varphi=c_2^2-s_2^2=\lambda^2(d^2-f^2)(c_1^2-s_1^2)=\lambda^2(d^2-f^2)\cos\theta,\] \[\sin\varphi=2c_2s_2=-2\lambda^2(dc_1+fs_1)(fc_1+ds_1)=-\lambda^2\bigl(2df+(d^2+f^2)\sin\theta\bigr).\] The line $XA$ contains $A$, which is off the $x$-axis, so it is not the $x$-axis and $\cos\varphi\ne0$; then also $\cos\theta\ne0$, and \[\tan\varphi\cos\theta=-\frac{2df+(d^2+f^2)\sin\theta}{d^2-f^2}=-G-F\sin\theta .\] Since $A$ is on $XA$, $a\cos\varphi+b\sin\varphi=x\cos\varphi$, i.e. $x=a+b\tan\varphi$. Therefore \[x\cos\theta=a\cos\theta-Fb\sin\theta-Gb,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \nu\cdot(V-X)=Gb\] for the normal $\nu$ of $\ell$. So the distance from $V$ to $\ell$ is $|Gb|=\rho$, and $\ell$ is tangent to $\kappa$.

Tangency to $\Omega$. As $A\in\Omega$, $(a-t_0e)^2+(b-t_0f)^2=d^2t_0^2$. Hence, using $F^2-1=G^2$ and $(F+1)f=dG$, \begin{align*}|V-U|^2&=(a-t_0e)^2+(Fb+t_0f)^2=d^2t_0^2-(b-t_0f)^2+(Fb+t_0f)^2\\&=d^2t_0^2+G^2b^2+2dGbt_0=(dt_0+Gb)^2 .\end{align*} So $|UV|=|dt_0+Gb|$, which is either the sum $dt_0+\rho$ or the difference $|dt_0-\rho|$ of the radii. Moreover $V\ne U$: otherwise $|dt_0+Gb|=0$ and $Fb=-t_0f$, so $Gt_0f/F=dt_0$, i.e. $Gf=dF$, i.e. $2df^2=d(d^2+f^2)$, i.e. $f^2=d^2$, which is false. Two circles with different centres whose distance is the sum or the difference of the radii are tangent. Hence $\kappa$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and to every coastal line.

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Let $n\geq 2$ be a given integer. Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that \[f(x-f(y))=f(x+y^n)+f(f(y)+y^n),\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}.\]

Show the answer

$f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=-x^n$

Show the solution

Solution (The group of periods)

Both functions work: for $f\equiv0$ both sides vanish, and for $f(x)=-x^n$ we have $f(y)+y^n=0$, so the equation reads $f(x+y^n)=f(x+y^n)+f(0)$, true since $f(0)=0$.

Now let $f$ be any solution, $a=f(0)$ and $c_y=f(y)+y^n$. Writing $u=x+y^n$, the equation becomes \[f(u-c_y)=f(u)+f(c_y)\qquad\text{for all }u,y. \tag{1}\] With $u=c_y$: $a=2f(c_y)$, so $f(c_y)=a/2$ and (1) reads $f(u-c_y)=f(u)+\tfrac a2$. For $y=0$ we have $c_0=a$, so also $f(u-a)=f(u)+\tfrac a2$. Comparing, $f(u-c_y)=f(u-a)$ for all $u$, i.e. \[p_y:=a-c_y\ \text{ is a period of } f\ \text{ for every } y. \tag{2}\] Let $G$ be the set of all periods of $f$ (numbers $p$ with $f(u+p)=f(u)$ for all $u$). It is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb R$.

Lemma. If $G$ contains all values $h(y)$, $y\in\mathbb R$, of a non-constant polynomial $h$, then $G=\mathbb R$. Indeed, a non-constant real polynomial takes all values in some half-line $[m,\infty)$ or $(-\infty,m]$, and every real number is a difference of two numbers from such a half-line.

Step 1: $a=0$. By (2), $f(y)=a-y^n-p_y$ with $p_y\in G$. Using $f(y+a)=f(y)-\tfrac a2$ (the relation $f(u-a)=f(u)+\tfrac a2$ with $u=y+a$): \[a-(y+a)^n-p_{y+a}=a-y^n-p_y-\tfrac a2\ \Longrightarrow\ (y+a)^n-y^n-\tfrac a2=p_y-p_{y+a}\in G.\] If $a\ne0$, the left side is a polynomial in $y$ of degree $n-1\ge1$, so $G=\mathbb R$ by the lemma; then $a$ is a period, contradicting $f(u-a)=f(u)+\frac a2\ne f(u)$. So $a=0$.

Step 2. Now (2) says $c_y=f(y)+y^n\in G$ for all $y$, i.e. $f(y)=-y^n+g_y$ with $g_y\in G$. If $G=\{0\}$, then $f(y)=-y^n$. Otherwise pick $g\in G$, $g\ne0$. Since $f(y+g)=f(y)$, \[-(y+g)^n+g_{y+g}=-y^n+g_y\ \Longrightarrow\ (y+g)^n-y^n=g_{y+g}-g_y\in G\] for all $y$; the left side is a polynomial of degree $n-1\ge1$ in $y$, so $G=\mathbb R$ by the lemma. Then $f$ is constant, $f\equiv f(0)=0$.

Hence the solutions are $\boxed{f\equiv0}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=-x^n}$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Let $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ be the set of positive integers. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}$ such that for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, and every positive divisor $d$ of $n$, there exists a positive divisor $e$ of $n$ such that $n$ divides $d+f(e)$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=-n$ for all $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Residues as a bijection, and two large primes)

$f(n)=-n$ works: for $d\mid n$ take $e=d$; then $d+f(e)=0$.

Now let $f$ satisfy the condition; write $D(n)$ for the set of positive divisors of $n$ and $\tau(n)=|D(n)|$.

Step 1: a bijection. Fix $n$. The numbers $-d$ $(d\in D(n))$ are $\tau(n)$ different residues modulo $n$ (the divisors below $n$ are distinct and nonzero modulo $n$, and $-n\equiv0$). Each of them must be congruent to some $f(e)$ with $e\in D(n)$, and there are only $\tau(n)$ such values. Hence \[\{f(e)\bmod n:\ e\in D(n)\}=\{-d\bmod n:\ d\in D(n)\},\quad\text{and the }f(e)\ (e\in D(n))\text{ are pairwise incongruent mod }n. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $f(m)=0$ or $f(m)=-d$ for some $d\in D(m)$. Let $a=f(m)$ and pick a prime $q>|a|+m$; then $q\nmid m$. By (1) for $n=mq$, $a\equiv-d\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid mq$, i.e. $d=d'$ or $d=d'q$ with $d'\mid m$. In the first case $mq\mid a+d'$ and $|a+d'|\le|a|+m<mq$, so $a=-d'$. In the second case $q\mid a$, so $a=0$ (as $q>|a|$).

Step 3: $f(m)=-m$ for all $m$, by strong induction. Let $m\ge1$ and assume $f(d)=-d$ for all $d<m$ (nothing is assumed for $m=1$). By (1) for $n=m$, the values $f(d)=-d$ $(d\in D(m),\ d<m)$ already occupy all the required residues except $0$, so $f(m)\equiv0\pmod m$, and by Step 2, $f(m)\in\{0,-m\}$.

Suppose $f(m)=0$. Let $q$ be any prime not dividing $m$ and $n=mq$, so $D(n)=D(m)\cup qD(m)$. The residue $-m$ is required by (1), and $-m\not\equiv0$. It is not $f(d)=-d$ for $d<m$, $d\mid m$, nor $f(m)=0$. So $f(dq)\equiv-m\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid m$. By Step 2, $f(dq)$ is $0$ or $-c$ with $c\mid dq$, $0<c\le dq\le mq$; the congruence then forces $f(dq)=-m$ and $m\mid dq$, hence $m\mid d$ and $d=m$. So \[f(mq)=-m\quad\text{for every prime } q\nmid m.\] Take two different such primes $q,r$ and $N=mqr$. Both $mq$ and $mr$ divide $N$ and $f(mq)=f(mr)=-m$, contradicting the incongruence in (1) for $N$. Hence $f(m)=-m$, completing the induction.

The only function is $\boxed{f(n)=-n}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral with $\angle ADC>90^\circ$. Suppose that points $X$ and $Y$ lie on diagonal $AC$ such that \[\angle ADX=\angle YDC=90^\circ,\quad\angle CAD=\angle CBX,\quad\text{and}\quad\angle DCA=\angle YBA.\] Let $O$ be the circumcentre of triangle $BXY$. Suppose point $R\neq O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$ such that lines $OR$ and $AC$ are parallel.

Prove that line $RO$ bisects angle $\angle DRB$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A homothety centred at $D$ and the Apollonius circle)

We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines (mod $180^\circ$). Place $AC$ horizontally with $A$ on the left and $D$ above; by convexity $B$ is below $AC$. As $\angle ADC>90^\circ$, the feet of the rays $DX\perp DA$ and $DY\perp DC$ lie inside segment $AC$, in the order $A,Y,X,C$.

Step 1: two right angles at $B$. Let $DX$ and $DY$ meet the circle $(ACD)$ again at $U$ and $V$. From $\angle ADU=\angle VDC=90^\circ$, both $AU$ and $CV$ are diameters, so $AVUC$ is a rectangle: $VU\parallel AC$, $VU=AC$, and $U,V$ lie below $AC$. Triangles $ACD$ and $BCX$ are both oriented counterclockwise, so the given $\angle CAD=\angle CBX$ holds for directed angles. Hence \[\measuredangle CBX=\measuredangle CAD=\measuredangle CUD=\measuredangle CUX,\] and $B,U,C,X$ are concyclic. Since $\angle XCU=\angle ACU=90^\circ$, $UX$ is a diameter of this circle and $\angle XBU=90^\circ$. In the same way (triangles $CAD$, $BAY$ are both clockwise) $\measuredangle YBA=\measuredangle DCA=\measuredangle DVA=\measuredangle YVA$, so $A,V,B,Y$ are concyclic, $VY$ is a diameter (as $\angle YAV=90^\circ$) and $\angle YBV=90^\circ$.

Step 2: a homothety. Let $\omega$ be the circle $BXY$ and $B_1$ the point of $\omega$ opposite $B$; the line through $X$ perpendicular to $BX$ passes through $B_1$, and so does the line through $Y$ perpendicular to $BY$. Let $h$ be the homothety centred at $D$ with $h(X)=U$; since $VU\parallel YX$ and $D,Y,V$ are collinear, also $h(Y)=V$. Its ratio $k$ satisfies $|k|=UV/XY=AC/XY>1$. The image of the line through $X$ perpendicular to $BX$ is the parallel line through $U$, which is $UB$ (because $\angle XBU=90^\circ$); likewise the line through $Y$ perpendicular to $BY$ goes to $VB$. These two lines are different (otherwise $BX\parallel BY$) and meet at $B$, so $h(B_1)=B$. Thus $h$ maps $\omega=(XYB_1)$ to the circle $(UVB)$, and its centre $O$ to the centre $O'$ of $(UVB)$. So $D,B_1,B$ are collinear, $D,O,O'$ are collinear, and as $O$ is the midpoint of $BB_1$, the five points $D,B_1,O,B,O'$ lie on one line $g$.

Step 3: a right angle at $R$. $O'$ is equidistant from $U$ and $V$, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $UV$, which is the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $AC$. Also $R\in m$ and $RO\parallel AC\perp m$. If $R=O'$, then $R$ and $O$ would both lie on $g$, so $g=RO$ would be parallel to $AC$; but $g$ passes through $D$ and $B$, which lie on opposite sides of $AC$. Hence $R\ne O'$ and $\angle ORO'=90^\circ$.

Step 4: equal ratios. Use a coordinate on $g$ with $D=0$ and $B_1=b$. Then $B=kb$, $O=\frac{1+k}2b$ and $O'=k\cdot\frac{1+k}2b$, so \[\frac{OB}{OD}=\frac{|1-k|}{|1+k|}=\frac{|k|\,|k-1|}{|k|\,|k+1|}=\frac{O'B}{O'D}=:\lambda .\] Here $\lambda\ne1$, and since $|k|>1$ the numbers $O-B=\frac{1-k}2b$ and $O-D=\frac{1+k}2b$ have opposite signs: $O$ lies between $B$ and $D$.

Step 5: conclusion. The points $Z$ with $ZB:ZD=\lambda$ form the Apollonius circle of $B,D$, whose diameter is the segment between the two points of line $BD$ dividing $BD$ internally and externally in the ratio $\lambda$, namely $O$ and $O'$. As $\angle ORO'=90^\circ$, $R$ lies on this circle, and $R\ne O,O'$ means $R$ is not on line $BD$. Hence $RB:RD=OB:OD$ with $O$ on segment $BD$, and by the converse of the angle bisector theorem, line $RO$ bisects angle $DRB$.

Solution 2 (An isosceles trapezoid and the reflection of $B$)

We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines (mod $180^\circ$). Place $AC$ horizontally with $A$ on the left and $D$ above; by convexity $B$ is below $AC$. As $\angle ADC>90^\circ$, the feet of the rays $DX\perp DA$ and $DY\perp DC$ lie inside segment $AC$, in the order $A,Y,X,C$.

Two right angles at $B$. Let $DX$ and $DY$ meet the circle $(ACD)$ again at $U$ and $V$. From $\angle ADU=\angle VDC=90^\circ$, both $AU$ and $CV$ are diameters, so $AVUC$ is a rectangle: $VU\parallel AC$, $VU=AC$, and $U,V$ lie below $AC$. Triangles $ACD$ and $BCX$ are both oriented counterclockwise, so the given $\angle CAD=\angle CBX$ holds for directed angles. Hence \[\measuredangle CBX=\measuredangle CAD=\measuredangle CUD=\measuredangle CUX,\] and $B,U,C,X$ are concyclic. Since $\angle XCU=\angle ACU=90^\circ$, $UX$ is a diameter of this circle and $\angle XBU=90^\circ$. In the same way (triangles $CAD$, $BAY$ are both clockwise) $\measuredangle YBA=\measuredangle DCA=\measuredangle DVA=\measuredangle YVA$, so $A,V,B,Y$ are concyclic, $VY$ is a diameter (as $\angle YAV=90^\circ$) and $\angle YBV=90^\circ$.

Setup. Let $\omega$ be the circle $BXY$ (centre $O$), and let $O_1$, $O_2$ be the midpoints of $VY$ and $UX$, the centres of the circles $AVBY$ and $BUCX$.

A trapezoid. $VUXY$ is a trapezoid with $VU\parallel YX$, and $O_1O_2$ is its midline: $O_1O_2\parallel AC$ and $\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}=\frac12(\overrightarrow{VU}+\overrightarrow{YX})$, which points the same way as $\overrightarrow{YX}$ and is longer ($\frac12(AC+XY)>XY$). Measure positions along $AC$ from $A$, with $Y,X,C$ at $y,x,c$. The projections onto $AC$ are: $O_1\mapsto\frac y2$ (as $V$ projects to $A$), $O_2\mapsto\frac{x+c}2$ (as $U$ projects to $C$), $O\mapsto\frac{x+y}2$ ($O$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$), $R\mapsto\frac c2$. Since $\frac y2+\frac{x+c}2=\frac{x+y}2+\frac c2$, the reflection $\sigma$ in the line perpendicular to $AC$ at position $\frac{x+y+c}4$ swaps $O_1\leftrightarrow O_2$ and $O\leftrightarrow R$ (it keeps each of the lines $O_1O_2$ and $RO$, both parallel to $AC$). These two lines are different: otherwise $O$ would lie on $O_1O_2$, and since $OO_1\perp BY$, $OO_2\perp BX$ (lines of centres of circles through $B,Y$ and through $B,X$), we would get $BX\parallel BY$; note $O\ne O_1$, because the circle $AVBY$ cannot pass through the three collinear points $A,Y,X$.

Parallel sides. Let $Q$ be the reflection of $B$ in line $RO$; since $O\in RO$, $Q$ lies on $\omega$. Let $t$ be the line through $B$ perpendicular to $AC$; it is the line $BQ$, or the tangent to $\omega$ at $B$ if $Q=B$. Reflections reverse directed angles, rotating both lines by $90^\circ$ keeps a directed angle, and $\omega$ gives inscribed angles; so \[\measuredangle(O_1O_2,O_1R)=-\measuredangle(O_2O_1,O_2O)=-\measuredangle(t,BX)=-\measuredangle(YQ,YX)=\measuredangle(YX,YQ),\] using $O_2O_1\perp t$ and $O_2O\perp BX$. As $O_1O_2\parallel YX$, this gives $O_1R\parallel YQ$. Similarly $\measuredangle(O_2O_1,O_2R)=-\measuredangle(O_1O_2,O_1O)=-\measuredangle(t,BY)=\measuredangle(XY,XQ)$, so $O_2R\parallel XQ$. (If $Q$ were $Y$, the second chain would give $O_1O\parallel O_1O_2$, which we excluded; similarly $Q\ne X$. So $Q\notin XY$ and the lines $YQ$, $XQ$ are different.)

The homothety. Lines $O_1Y$ and $O_2X$ are the lines $VY$ and $UX$, which meet at $D$. Let $g$ be the homothety centred at $D$ with $g(O_1)=Y$. It maps line $O_1O_2$ to the parallel line through $Y$, namely $YX$, so $g(O_2)=X$, and its ratio is $s=XY/O_1O_2\in(0,1)$ (the vectors $O_1O_2$ and $YX$ point the same way). It maps line $O_1R$ to the parallel line $YQ$ and line $O_2R$ to $XQ$; hence $g(R)=Q$. So $Q$ lies on segment $DR$, strictly between $D$ and $R$. If $Q=B$, then $B$ would be on line $RO\parallel AC$ and on line $RD$, so $DB\parallel AC$, impossible; thus $Q\ne B$.

Therefore ray $RQ$ is ray $RD$, and since $Q$ is the mirror image of $B$ in line $RO$, the line $RO$ bisects angle $BRQ$, which is angle $DRB$.

Solution 3 (Coordinates with $D$ at the origin)

Put $D=(0,0)$ and scale so that $AC$ is the line $y=1$; let $A=(a,1)$ and $C=(c,1)$ with $a<c$. As $\angle ADC>90^\circ$, $\overrightarrow{DA}\cdot\overrightarrow{DC}=ac+1<0$, so $a<0<c$. Put $\alpha=a+c$ and $\beta=ac<-1$.

$X$, $Y$, $B$. For $X=(p,1)$ the condition $\angle ADX=90^\circ$ is $ap+1=0$, so $p=-1/a$; likewise $Y=(q,1)$ with $q=-1/c$. Then $a<q<p<c$: indeed $q-a=-\frac{1+\beta}{c}>0$, $c-p=\frac{1+\beta}{a}>0$ and $p-q=\frac1c-\frac1a>0$. As $ABCD$ is convex, $B$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$: $B=(b,1+t)$ with $t>0$.

The angle conditions. For an angle $\angle ZWZ'$ we have $\cot\angle ZWZ'=\dfrac{\overrightarrow{WZ}\cdot\overrightarrow{WZ'}}{|\det(\overrightarrow{WZ},\overrightarrow{WZ'})|}$, and two angles in $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ are equal exactly when their cotangents are. We find $\cot\angle CAD=\frac{(c-a)(-a)}{c-a}=-a$, $\cot\angle DCA=\frac{-c(a-c)}{c-a}=c$, \[\cot\angle CBX=\frac{(c-b)(p-b)+t^2}{t(c-p)},\qquad \cot\angle YBA=\frac{(a-b)(q-b)+t^2}{t(q-a)} .\] As $-a(c-p)=-ac-1$ and $c(q-a)=-1-ac$, the two given equalities become \[(b-c)(b-p)+t^2=-(\beta+1)t,\tag{1}\] \[(b-a)(b-q)+t^2=-(\beta+1)t.\tag{2}\] Subtracting, $b(a+q-c-p)=aq-cp=\frac{c^2-a^2}{ac}$. Since $a+q-c-p=(a-c)+\frac{c-a}{ac}=\frac{(a-c)(\beta-1)}{\beta}$, this gives \[b=\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}.\] Then $b-c=\frac{a(1+c^2)}{1-\beta}$ and $c-q=\frac{1+c^2}{c}$, so $c-q=(b-c)\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}$ and $b-q=(b-c)+(c-q)=\frac{b-c}{\beta}$. With (1), \[(b-p)(b-q)=\frac{(b-p)(b-c)}{\beta}=-\frac{t^2+(\beta+1)t}{\beta}.\]

The points $O$ and $R$. The centre $O$ of the circle $BXY$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$: $O=\bigl(\frac{p+q}2,k\bigr)$. From $OX=OB$, \[\Bigl(\frac{p-q}2\Bigr)^2+(1-k)^2=\Bigl(b-\frac{p+q}2\Bigr)^2+(1+t-k)^2,\] that is, $0=(b-p)(b-q)+t^2+2t(1-k)$. Hence $2t(k-1)=t^2-\frac{t^2+(\beta+1)t}{\beta}$; dividing by $t$ and multiplying by $\beta$, $2\beta k=2\beta+\beta t-t-\beta-1=(\beta-1)(1+t)$, so \[k=\frac{(\beta-1)(1+t)}{2\beta}>0 .\] The point $R$ is on the perpendicular bisector $x=\frac\alpha2$ of $AC$ and on the line $y=k$ through $O$ parallel to $AC$: $R=(\sigma,k)$ with $\sigma=\frac\alpha2$. As $\frac{p+q}2=-\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}$ and $\beta\ne-1$, the condition $R\ne O$ means $\alpha\ne0$, so $\sigma\ne0$.

The bisector. We have $b-\sigma=\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}-\frac\alpha2=\sigma\cdot\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}$ and $1+t-k=(1+t)\bigl(1-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta}\bigr)=(1+t)\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}$. So the slopes of $RD$ and $RB$ are \[\frac{0-k}{0-\sigma}=\frac k\sigma,\qquad \frac{1+t-k}{b-\sigma}=\frac{(1+t)(\beta+1)}{2\beta}\cdot\frac{1-\beta}{\sigma(1+\beta)}=\frac{(1+t)(1-\beta)}{2\beta\sigma}=-\frac k\sigma .\] They are opposite and nonzero, so the lines $RD$ and $RB$ are mirror images in the horizontal line $RO$. Moreover $\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}<0$, so $b-\sigma$ and $0-\sigma$ have the same sign: $D$ and $B$ lie on the same side of the vertical line through $R$. Hence the rays $RD$ and $RB$ are mirror images in line $RO$, and line $RO$ bisects $\angle DRB$.

Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

The sunshine cost of a sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of integers is the largest possible value of \[\left|(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_i)-a_j\right|\] as $i$ and $j$ vary over all integers $1,2,\ldots,100$.

Determine the smallest possible sunshine cost over all sequences $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of pairwise distinct integers.

Show the answer

$75$

Show the solution

Solution (Prefix sums are trapped in a short interval)

Write $P_i=a_1+\cdots+a_i$, let $A=\max a_j$, $B=\min a_j$, $D=A-B$, and let $M$ be the sunshine cost. As the $a_j$ are $100$ distinct integers, $D\ge99$.

Construction with cost $75$. Take $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, followed by the pairs $j,-j$ for $j=1,2,\dots,50$, $j\ne25$ (that is $2+2\cdot49=100$ distinct integers). The prefix sums are $-25$ after $a_1,a_2$ and after every complete pair, and $-25+j$ in the middle of the pair $j,-j$; so they range over $[-25,25]$, while the terms range over $[-50,50]$. Hence $M=\max(25-(-50),\,50-(-25))=75$.

Lower bound. For every $i$ and $j$, $|P_i-a_j|\le M$; with $j$ giving $A$ and $B$ this means \[P_i\in I:=[A-M,\ B+M]\qquad(1\le i\le100),\] an interval of length $L=2M-D$ (so $L\ge0$). For $i\ge2$, $a_i=P_i-P_{i-1}$ is a difference of two points of $I$, so $|a_i|\le L$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-L,L]$, which forces $2L+1\ge99$, i.e. $L\ge49$.

Suppose $M\le74$. Then $D=2M-L\le148-49=99$, so $D=99$, and then $L=2M-99\ge49$ forces $M=74$, $L=49$. So $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ are exactly the integers $-49,\dots,49$, and since all $100$ terms are consecutive integers ($D=99$), $a_1=50$ or $a_1=-50$. But then $|P_1-a_j|=|a_1-a_j|=99>74$ for $a_j=\mp49$. Contradiction. So $M\ge75$.

The smallest sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ be any $n$-tuple of distinct integers. An inversion is a pair $(i,j)$ of integers satisfying $1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n$ and $a_i>a_j$. An inversion is called odd if $j-i$ is odd. Let $A$ be the number of all inversions, and let $B$ be the number of odd inversions.

  • (a) Prove that $A\leqslant 5B$ for all $n$ and all $n$-tuples $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$.

  • (b) Prove that there exist an $n$ and an $n$-tuple $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ for which $A>4.99B$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Charging each even inversion to a middle point)

Call an inversion $(i,j)$ even if $j-i$ is even; let $E=A-B$ be the number of even inversions.

(a) We show $E\le4B$, which is equivalent to $A\le5B$. Let $(i,j)$ be an even inversion, $j-i=2d$. Put \[k=\begin{cases}i+d,& d\text{ odd},\\ i+d-1,& d\text{ even}.\end{cases}\] Then $i<k<j$ and $k-i$, $j-k$ are odd. Since $a_i>a_j$, we have $a_i>a_k$ or $a_k>a_j$; so $(i,k)$ or $(k,j)$ is an odd inversion. Assign $(i,j)$ to $(i,k)$ if $a_i>a_k$, and to $(k,j)$ otherwise.

Now fix an odd inversion $(u,v)$, $h=v-u$ (odd), and count the even inversions assigned to it.

  • As $(i,k)=(u,v)$: then $i=u$ and $k-i=h$ equals $d$ (if $d$ is odd) or $d-1$ (if $d$ is even), so $d\in\{h,h+1\}$ and $j=u+2d$ takes at most $2$ values.

  • As $(k,j)=(u,v)$: then $j=v$ and $j-k=2d-(k-i)=h$ equals $d$ ($d$ odd) or $d+1$ ($d$ even), so $d\in\{h,h-1\}$ and $i=v-2d$ takes at most $2$ values.

Hence every odd inversion receives at most $4$ even inversions, and $E\le4B$, i.e. $A=E+B\le5B$.

(b) Let $m$ and $C$ be even positive integers, $n=Cm$, and use positions $x=0,1,\dots,n-1$ (shift by $1$ for the indexing of the problem). For a position $x$ let $c(x)=\lfloor x/m\rfloor$ be its block and define its band \[\beta(x)=\begin{cases}c(x),& x\equiv c(x)\pmod 2,\\ c(x)-1,& \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad a_x=n\,\beta(x)-x .\] Properties. Always $x\equiv\beta(x)\pmod2$; band $b$ consists of the positions of parity $b$ in blocks $b$ and $b+1$. Since $0\le x<n$, $a_x>a_y$ holds exactly when $\beta(x)>\beta(y)$, or $\beta(x)=\beta(y)$ and $x<y$; in particular the $a_x$ are distinct.

Which pairs $x<y$ are inversions? All pairs in the same band (they are at even distance). If $\beta(x)>\beta(y)$ with $x<y$, then $c(x)-1\le c(y)-1\le\beta(y)<\beta(x)\le c(x)$ forces $c(y)=c(x)=:c$, $\beta(x)=c$, $\beta(y)=c-1$: $x$ and $y$ lie in the same block, $x$ has parity $c$, $y$ the other parity (odd distance). So \[E=\sum_{\text{bands}}\binom{|\text{band}|}{2},\qquad B=\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\#\{x<y\text{ in block }c:\ x\equiv c,\ y\not\equiv c\pmod 2\}.\] Bands $0,\dots,C-2$ have $m$ elements, bands $-1$ and $C-1$ have $\frac m2$, so $E=(C-1)\frac{m(m-1)}2+\frac{m(m-2)}4$. A block starts at an even position; for even $c$ the pairs counted are (even offset, later odd offset), $\frac{m}{2}\cdot\frac{m/2+1}{2}$ of them, and for odd $c$ they are $\frac m2\cdot\frac{m/2-1}2$. As $C$ is even, $B=\frac C2\cdot\frac{m^2}{4}=\frac{Cm^2}8$. Therefore \[\frac AB=1+\frac EB=1+\frac{4(C-1)(m-1)+2(m-2)}{Cm}=5-\frac4m-\frac2C .\] With $m=C=1000$ (so $n=10^6$) this is $4.994>4.99$, as required.

Solution 2 (A periodic example built from residues modulo $2m+1$)

Call an inversion $(i,j)$ even if $j-i$ is even; let $E=A-B$ be the number of even inversions.

(a) We show $E\le4B$, which is equivalent to $A\le5B$. Let $(i,j)$ be an even inversion, $j-i=2s$, and put $c=i+s$ if $s$ is odd and $c=i+s-1$ if $s$ is even. Then $i<c<j$, and $c-i$ and $j-c$ are odd. As $a_i>a_j$, we have $a_i>a_c$ or $a_c>a_j$; assign $(i,j)$ to the odd inversion $(i,c)$ in the first case and to $(c,j)$ in the second. Fix an odd inversion $(x,y)$, $y-x=g$. If $(i,j)$ is assigned to it as $(i,c)$, then $i=x$ and $g=c-i\in\{s,s-1\}$, so $s\in\{g,g+1\}$ and $j=x+2s$ takes at most $2$ values. If it is assigned as $(c,j)$, then $j=y$ and $g=j-c\in\{s,s+1\}$, so $s\in\{g,g-1\}$ and $i=y-2s$ takes at most $2$ values. So each odd inversion receives at most $4$ even inversions, $E\le4B$ and $A\le5B$.

(b) Let $m,T$ be positive integers, $k=2m+1$ and $n=kT$; we index the terms by $i=0,1,\dots,n-1$ (shift by $1$ for the problem's indexing). As $k$ is odd, there is a unique $r_i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}$ with $2r_i\equiv-i\pmod k$. Put \[a_i=n\,(i+3r_i)+i .\] These are distinct, since $a_i\equiv i\pmod n$.

Criterion. Let $0\le i<j\le n-1$, $d=j-i$ and $\delta=r_i-r_j$. If $i+3r_i>j+3r_j$, then $a_i-a_j\ge n+i-j>0$; otherwise $a_i-a_j\le i-j<0$. So $(i,j)$ is an inversion if and only if $3\delta>d$. Since $2\delta\equiv j-i=d$ and $2(m+1)=k+1\equiv1\pmod k$, we have $\delta\equiv(m+1)d\pmod k$. Let $\rho_d\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ be the remainder of $(m+1)d$ modulo $k$. As $|\delta|<k$, either $\delta=\rho_d$ (when $r_i\ge\rho_d$) or $\delta=\rho_d-k<0$ (when $r_i<\rho_d$). Hence \[(i,i+d)\text{ is an inversion}\iff 3\rho_d>d\ \text{ and }\ r_i\ge\rho_d .\]

The distances that occur. Note $\rho_d\le2m$, so $3\rho_d>d$ forces $d<6m$.

  • $d=2s$: $(m+1)2s=s(k+1)\equiv s$. For $1\le s\le2m$, $\rho_d=s$ and $3s>2s$: these distances occur. For $k\le s<2k$, $\rho_d=s-k$ and $3(s-k)>2s$ would need $s>3k$; larger $s$ give $d\ge4k>6m$. So no other even distance occurs.

  • $d=2s+1$: $(m+1)(2s+1)\equiv s+m+1$. For $0\le s\le m-1$, $\rho_d=s+m+1$ and $3(s+m+1)>2s+1$: these occur. For $m\le s\le3m$, $\rho_d=s-m$ and $3(s-m)>2s+1$ would need $s>3m+1$; larger $s$ give $d>6m$. So no other odd distance occurs.

All occurring distances are less than $2k$.

Counting. The map $i\mapsto r_i$ is a bijection from any $k$ consecutive integers onto $\{0,\dots,k-1\}$, so among $k$ consecutive values of $i$ exactly $k-\rho$ have $r_i\ge\rho$. For an occurring odd distance $d=2s+1$, the starting points $i$ lie in $[0,n-d)\subset[0,n)$, which consists of $T$ blocks of $k$ consecutive integers; so there are at most $T(k-\rho_d)=T(m-s)$ inversions at distance $d$, and \[B\le T\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}(m-s)=T\,\frac{m(m+1)}2 .\] For every occurring distance $d<2k$, the interval $[0,n-d)$ contains the $T-2$ blocks of $[0,k(T-2))$, so \[A\ge(T-2)\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{2m}(k-s)+\frac{m(m+1)}2\Bigr)=(T-2)\Bigl(m(2m+1)+\frac{m(m+1)}2\Bigr).\] Hence $A\ge\bigl(1-\frac2T\bigr)\bigl(1+\frac{2(2m+1)}{m+1}\bigr)B=\bigl(1-\frac2T\bigr)\bigl(5-\frac2{m+1}\bigr)B$. For $m=250$ and $T=20000$ (so $n=10\,020\,000$), $\bigl(1-\frac1{10000}\bigr)\bigl(5-\frac2{251}\bigr)>0.9999\cdot4.992>4.9915$, and $B>0$ (for instance $(i,i+1)$ is an inversion whenever $r_i\ge m+1$). So $A>4.99B$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

For positive integers $n$ and $k$, let $f_k(n)$ denote the remainder when $n$ is divided by $2^k-1$. A positive integer $n$ is grouse if \[f_1(n)\leq f_2(n)\leq f_3(n)\leq f_4(n)\leq\cdots\]

  • (a) Prove that there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • (b) Prove that there exists a positive integer $M$ such that there are at most $\dfrac{M}{2025^{2025}}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A greedy chain of multiples; grouse numbers hug the powers of two)

Write $d_i=2^i-1$. Always $f_1(n)=0$, and $f_i(n)=n$ as soon as $d_i>n$.

The greedy chain. Fix $k\ge1$. Put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,k-2,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$. Then $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_k$ and $x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. More generally, starting the chain at any $x_i$, summing these steps gives \[0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i. \tag{1}\]

(a) We show that $x_1$ is grouse; since $x_1\ge x_k=2^k-1$ and $k$ is arbitrary, this gives infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • For $i\le k$: $x_i$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and $0\le x_1-x_i<d_i$ by (1), so $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$. As $x_i$ does not increase with $i$, the values $f_1(x_1)\le f_2(x_1)\le\dots\le f_k(x_1)$ are non-decreasing.

  • For $i>k$: by (1), $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge x_1-x_k=f_k(x_1)$.

(b) Claim. If $2^k\le n<2^{k+1}$ and $n$ is grouse, then $n>2^{k+1}-3\cdot2^{k/2}$. (For $k=0$ this is trivial, so let $k\ge1$.)

Step 1: $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$ for $1\le i\le k$. The number $n-f_i(n)$ is the largest multiple of $d_i$ not exceeding $n$. For $i=k$: $d_k\le n$, so $n-f_k(n)\ge d_k=x_k$. If $n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, then, since $n$ is grouse, $n-f_i(n)\ge n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, and as $n-f_i(n)$ is a multiple of $d_i$, the definition of $x_i$ gives $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$.

Step 2: $x_i=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ for $k/2\le i\le k$. For $i=k$ both sides equal $d_k$. Suppose it holds for $i+1$, where $k/2\le i<k$. The number $y=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and \[y-x_{i+1}=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)-(2^{i+1}-1)(2^{k-i}-1)=2^i-2^{k-i},\] which lies in $[0,d_i)$ because $i\ge k-i$ and $k-i\ge1$. So $y$ is the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is at least $x_{i+1}$, i.e. $y=x_i$.

Step 3. Take $i=\lceil k/2\rceil$. Then $n\ge n-f_i(n)\ge x_i=2^{k+1}-\bigl(2^i+2^{k+1-i}\bigr)+1$. If $k=2t$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=3\cdot2^t=3\cdot2^{k/2}$; if $k=2t-1$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=2^{t+1}=2\sqrt2\cdot2^{k/2}<3\cdot2^{k/2}$. This proves the claim.

Counting. By the claim, the block $[2^k,2^{k+1})$ contains at most $3\cdot2^{k/2}$ grouse numbers. Let $M=2^{b+1}$. The grouse numbers below $M$ lie in the blocks with $k=0,1,\dots,b$, so there are at most \[\sum_{k=0}^{b}3\cdot2^{k/2}<\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\,2^{(b+1)/2}=\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\sqrt M\] of them. This is at most $M/2025^{2025}$ as soon as $\sqrt M\ge3\cdot2025^{2025}/(\sqrt2-1)$, which holds for $b$ large enough. Such an $M$ proves (b).

Solution 2 (Part (b) by forbidden residue classes and the Chinese remainder theorem)

(a) Write $d_i=2^i-1$; then $f_i(n)=n$ whenever $d_i>n$. Fix $k\ge1$, put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$; so $x_{i+1}\le x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. Summing these steps, $0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j<i}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i$. Hence for $i\le k$ we get $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$, which does not decrease as $i$ grows; and for $i>k$ we get $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge f_k(x_1)$. Thus $x_1\ge2^k-1$ is grouse, and as $k$ is arbitrary there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

(b) we only use the inequality $f_{2k-1}(n)\le f_{2k+1}(n)$, which every grouse number satisfies.

Forbidden classes. Let $k\ge2$, $a=2^{2k-1}-1$ and $b=2^{2k+1}-1$. Recall $\gcd(2^u-1,2^v-1)=2^{\gcd(u,v)}-1$; since $\gcd(2k-1,2k+1)=1$, the numbers $a,b$ are coprime. For each pair of integers $0\le j<i\le a-1$ (there are $\binom a2$ pairs; note $j<a<b$) the Chinese remainder theorem gives one residue class modulo $ab$ consisting of the $n$ with $n\equiv i\pmod a$ and $n\equiv j\pmod b$, that is, $f_{2k-1}(n)=i>j=f_{2k+1}(n)$. No grouse number lies in these $\binom a2$ classes. So the fraction of classes modulo $ab$ that may contain grouse numbers is at most \[1-\frac{\binom a2}{ab}=1-\frac{a-1}{2b}=1-\frac{2^{2k-2}-1}{2^{2k+1}-1}=1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8(2^{2k+1}-1)}\le1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8\cdot31}=\frac{28}{31},\] using $2^{2k+1}-1\ge31$.

Many independent moduli. Choose $k_1=2$ and $k_j=\prod_{i<j}(2k_i-1)(2k_i+1)$ for $j\ge2$. Each $k_j$ is at least $2$, and every earlier number $2k_i\pm1$ divides $k_j$, so it is coprime to $2k_j\pm1$; also $\gcd(2k_j-1,2k_j+1)=1$. Hence the exponents $2k_j\pm1$ $(j=1,\dots,s)$ are pairwise coprime, and so are the numbers $a_j=2^{2k_j-1}-1$, $b_j=2^{2k_j+1}-1$. Put $M=\prod_{j=1}^{s}a_jb_j$.

Counting. The numbers $0,1,\dots,M-1$ represent each class modulo $M$ once. If such a number is grouse, its class modulo each $a_jb_j$ is not forbidden. By the Chinese remainder theorem the classes modulo $M$ correspond to tuples of classes modulo $a_1b_1,\dots,a_sb_s$, so at most \[\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{28}{31}\,a_jb_j=\Bigl(\frac{28}{31}\Bigr)^{s}M\] numbers below $M$ are grouse. Choosing $s$ with $(28/31)^s\le2025^{-2025}$ gives at most $M/2025^{2025}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

For a positive integer $n$, let $a_n$ and $b_n$ be the coprime positive integers with \[\frac{a_n}{b_n}=1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac1n.\] Show that the following two assertions are equivalent (i.e., both true or both false):

  • (i) There are infinitely many $n$ for which $b_n$ is not divisible by $2027$.

  • (ii) There are infinitely many $n$ for which $b_n$ is not divisible by $2027^{2025}$.

Show the solution

Solution (Splitting off the multiples of $p$)

Let $p=2027$, which is prime, and write $H_n=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$ and $v=v_p$ for the $p$-adic valuation of a nonzero rational number. Since $a_n/b_n$ is in lowest terms, $p^k\mid b_n$ if and only if $v(H_n)\le-k$ (for $k\ge1$). So (i) says that $v(H_n)\ge0$ for infinitely many $n$, and (ii) says that $v(H_n)\ge-2024$ for infinitely many $n$. Clearly (i) implies (ii); we prove that (ii) implies (i) by contraposition.

Key relation. Let $m=\lfloor n/p\rfloor\ge1$. The multiples of $p$ up to $n$ are $p,2p,\dots,mp$, so \[H_n=S+\frac1p\,H_m,\qquad S=\sum_{\substack{k\le n\\ p\nmid k}}\frac1k .\] Every term of $S$ has a denominator prime to $p$, so $S=0$ or $v(S)\ge0$. If $v(H_m)<0$, then $v\bigl(\tfrac1pH_m\bigr)=v(H_m)-1<0\le v(S)$, and the valuation of a sum of two numbers with different valuations is the smaller one: \[v(H_n)=v(H_m)-1\qquad\text{whenever } v(H_{\lfloor n/p\rfloor})<0 .\]

Contraposition. Suppose (i) fails: there is $N_0$ with $v(H_n)\le-1$ for all $n\ge N_0$. We show by induction on $j\ge0$ that \[v(H_n)\le-(j+1)\qquad\text{for all } n\ge p^jN_0 .\] For $j=0$ this is the assumption. If it holds for $j$ and $n\ge p^{j+1}N_0$, then $m=\lfloor n/p\rfloor\ge p^jN_0$, so $v(H_m)\le-(j+1)<0$ and the key relation gives $v(H_n)=v(H_m)-1\le-(j+2)$.

With $j=2024$: $v(H_n)\le-2025$, i.e. $p^{2025}\mid b_n$, for all $n\ge p^{2024}N_0$. So (ii) fails as well. Hence (i) and (ii) are equivalent.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with circumcircle $k$. Let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ and suppose that $|AD|=|BC|$. Let $X$ be the foot of the altitude from $D$ on $AB$ and let $W$ be the intersection of the angular bisector of $\angle ADC$ with $AC$. Let $Y\neq B$ be a point on $k$ such that $BY\parallel AC$. Let $XY$ intersect $k$ again in $Z\neq Y$. Finally, let $S\neq W$ be the second intersection of $AC$ with the circumcircle of $YWZ$. Show that $\angle DZS=90^\circ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle centred at $A$ through $D$)

Let $h=AD=BC$, let $\kappa$ be the circle with centre $A$ and radius $h$ (it touches $BC$ at $D$), and let $V$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AC$. As the triangle is acute, $X$ lies on segment $AB$ and $V$ on segment $AC$. We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines.

Step 1: $Y\in\kappa$. The perpendicular bisector of $AC$ passes through the centre of $k$ and is perpendicular to the chord $BY$ (as $BY\parallel AC$), so it is also the perpendicular bisector of $BY$. The reflection in it swaps $A\leftrightarrow C$ and $B\leftrightarrow Y$, so $AY=CB=h$.

Step 2: $Z\in\kappa$, and $Y,V,Z$ are collinear. In the right triangles $ADB$ and $ADC$ with altitudes $DX$ and $DV$ we have $AX\cdot AB=h^2=AV\cdot AC$. Hence $X$ has the same power with respect to $k$ and $\kappa$: \[XA^2-h^2=XA^2-XA\cdot AB=-XA\cdot XB,\] and likewise $V$. So line $XV$ is the radical axis of $k$ and $\kappa$; it contains their common point $Y$. Thus line $XY$ is this radical axis, it meets $k$ exactly in the common points of $k$ and $\kappa$, and its second intersection $Z$ with $k$ lies on $\kappa$. Also $V$ lies on line $YZ$.

Step 3: $\angle WDS=90^\circ$. Let $\sigma$ be the circle $(YWZS)$. Since $V$ lies on line $YZ$ and on line $WS$, \[\overline{VW}\cdot\overline{VS}=\overline{VY}\cdot\overline{VZ}=VA^2-h^2=-VD^2,\] the last step by Pythagoras in triangle $AVD$. So $V$ lies between $W$ and $S$ and $VW\cdot VS=VD^2$; the right triangles $WVD$ and $DVS$ are similar, and $\angle WDS=\angle WDV+\angle VDS=\angle WDV+\angle VWD=90^\circ$. As $DW$ bisects the right angle $ADC$, line $DS$ makes $45^\circ$ with $DA$ on the side of $B$.

Step 4: the second point $M'$ of line $DS$ on $\kappa$ lies on line $BY$. Triangle $ADM'$ is isosceles ($AD=AM'=h$) with $\angle ADM'=45^\circ$, so $\angle DAM'=90^\circ$: $AM'\parallel BC$ and $M'$ is on the side of $B$. With $D$ as origin, $\mathbf e$ the unit vector towards $C$ and $\mathbf n$ the unit vector towards $A$: $B=-DB\,\mathbf e$, $A=h\mathbf n$, $M'=h\mathbf n-h\mathbf e$, so \[M'-B=h\mathbf n-(h-DB)\mathbf e=h\mathbf n-DC\,\mathbf e=-(C-A),\] using $h=BC=DB+DC$. Hence $BM'\parallel AC$, i.e. $M'$ lies on line $BY$. Moreover $M'\ne Y$: otherwise $AY\parallel BC$ and $BY\parallel AC$, so $ACBY$ would be a parallelogram inscribed in $k$, hence a rectangle, and $\angle ACB=90^\circ$.

Step 5: $D,V,S,Z$ are concyclic. Using Step 2, the circle $\kappa$ through $Z,Y,D,M'$, Step 4 and Step 3: \[\measuredangle(ZV,ZD)=\measuredangle(ZY,ZD)=\measuredangle(M'Y,M'D)=\measuredangle(AC,SD)=\measuredangle(SV,SD).\] So $Z$ lies on the circle through $D,V,S$. Since $\angle DVS=90^\circ$, $DS$ is a diameter of that circle, and therefore $\angle DZS=90^\circ$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates adapted to the parallel lines $BY$ and $AC$)

Scale so that $AD=BC=1$ and put $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,1)$, $B=(-u,0)$, $C=(v,0)$ with $u,v>0$ (the foot $D$ lies between $B$ and $C$, as the triangle is acute) and $u+v=1$.

New coordinates. Put $h=x+vy+u$ and $t=vx-y+uv$. The linear part sends $(1,0)$ to $(1,v)$ and $(0,1)$ to $(v,-1)$, two perpendicular vectors of the same length $\sqrt{1+v^2}$; so $(x,y)\mapsto(h,t)$ is a similarity. It multiplies all lengths by $\sqrt{1+v^2}$, keeps right angles, and maps lines and circles to lines and circles. From now on we work in the coordinates $(h,t)$ as Cartesian coordinates. Then \[B=(0,0),\quad D=(u,uv),\quad A=(1,uv-1),\quad C=(1,v),\] and line $AC$ is $h=1$.

The circle $k$ and $Y$. Let $L=1+v-uv^2$. The circle $t(t+u^2)+h(h-L)=0$ passes through $B$; through $C$, since $v(v+u^2)+1-L=v^2+uv(u+v)-v=v(v+u-1)=0$; and through $A$, since $uv-1+u^2=u-1=-v$ gives $(uv-1)(-v)+1-L=-uv^2+v-v+uv^2=0$. So it is $k$. The line $BY$ is parallel to $AC$, so it is $h=0$; on it $t(t+u^2)=0$, and $Y=(0,-u^2)$.

$X$ and $Z$. In the original coordinates the foot of $D$ on $AB$ is $X=\bigl(\frac{-u}{1+u^2},\frac{u^2}{1+u^2}\bigr)$, so $h_X=\frac{-u+vu^2+u+u^3}{1+u^2}=\frac{u^2}{1+u^2}$. As $X$ is on line $AB$, which is $t=(uv-1)h$ in the new coordinates, the slope of $YX$ is $\frac{t_X+u^2}{h_X}=uv-1+(1+u^2)=u(u+v)=u$. So line $YX$ is $t=uh-u^2$. Substituting into the equation of $k$ gives $h\bigl((1+u^2)h-u^3-L\bigr)=0$; the root $h=0$ is $Y$, so \[h_Z=\frac{L+u^3}{1+u^2}=\frac{2(1-u+u^2)}{1+u^2},\qquad t_Z=uh_Z-u^2,\] using $v=1-u$. Note $h_Z-1=\frac{(1-u)^2}{1+u^2}=\frac{v^2}{1+u^2}>0$.

$W$ and $S$. The line $YX$ meets $AC$ at $H=(1,uv)$. The bisector of the right angle $ADC$ is $y=x$ in the original coordinates, so $W=\bigl(\frac v{1+v},\frac v{1+v}\bigr)$ and $t_W=\frac{v^2-v}{1+v}+uv=uv-\frac{uv}{1+v}$. So $W=(1,t_W)$ with $t_H-t_W=\frac{uv}{1+v}$. Now compute the power of $H$ with respect to the circle $YWZ$ in two ways. Along line $YX$, with direction vector $(1,u)$, it is $\overrightarrow{HY}\cdot\overrightarrow{HZ}=(0-1)(h_Z-1)(1+u^2)=-v^2$. Along line $AC$ it is $(t_W-t_H)(t_S-t_H)$. Hence \[t_S-t_H=\frac{-v^2}{t_W-t_H}=\frac{v(1+v)}{u},\qquad S=\Bigl(1,\ uv+\frac{v(1+v)}u\Bigr),\] and indeed $S\ne W$.

The right angle. We have $\overrightarrow{DZ}=(h_Z-u,\ t_Z-uv)=(h_Z-u,\ u(h_Z-1))$ and $\overrightarrow{ZS}=(1-h_Z,\ t_S-t_Z)$, so \[\overrightarrow{DZ}\cdot\overrightarrow{ZS}=(1-h_Z)\bigl(h_Z-u-u(t_S-t_Z)\bigr).\] Here $u(t_S-t_Z)=u^2v+v(1+v)-u^2h_Z+u^3$, so the bracket is \[(1+u^2)h_Z-u-u^2(u+v)-v-v^2=2(1-u+u^2)-1-u^2-(1-u)^2=0 .\] The points $D,Z,S$ are distinct ($h_Z\ne1$, and $Z\ne D$ because $t_Z=u(h_Z-1)+uv\ne uv$). Hence $\angle DZS=90^\circ$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

On a $45\times 45$ square grid there is an echidna. From any cell, the echidna can move to any other cell that shares a side with that cell. The echidna starts in one of the cells and makes a sequence of $2024$ moves, after which it has visited each cell exactly once. Prove that it is possible to write the numbers from $1$ to $2025$, one in each cell, in such a way that:

  • For any pair of adjacent cells in the same row, the cell containing the larger number was visited earlier.

  • For any pair of adjacent cells in the same column, the cell containing the larger number was visited later.

Show the solution

Solution (No directed cycle: a switch count on the enclosed region)

Let $t(c)\in\{0,1,\dots,2024\}$ be the time at which cell $c$ is visited. Form a directed graph $D$ on the cells: for two cells $u,v$ in the same row and adjacent, draw $u\to v$ if $t(u)>t(v)$; for two cells $u,v$ in the same column and adjacent, draw $u\to v$ if $t(u)<t(v)$. A labelling as required is exactly a numbering in which every arrow goes from a smaller to a larger number. Such a numbering exists if $D$ has no directed cycle (number the cells along a topological order: repeatedly give the next number to a cell with no incoming arrow from unnumbered cells). So it suffices to show that $D$ is acyclic.

We picture cells as their centres (lattice points) and adjacencies as unit segments. The echidna's route is a simple lattice path through all points; for times $p<q$ write $\Gamma[p,q]$ for the part of the route between times $p$ and $q$. We use repeatedly: two grid paths with no common cell do not meet at all, since unit segments of the lattice meet only at lattice points.

Lemma 1 (no directed unit square). Suppose the four cells at the corners of a unit square form a directed cycle. Around the square the arrows alternate between horizontal and vertical, so at every corner one arrow is horizontal and one vertical. A horizontal arrow points to the earlier cell and a vertical arrow to the later one. Hence at a corner whose incoming arrow is horizontal and outgoing arrow vertical, the corner is earlier than both its neighbours on the square, and the two corners of this type are opposite; call them $b,d$ and the other two $a,c$. So $\max(t(b),t(d))<\min(t(a),t(c))$. Then the route segment $\Gamma_1$ between $b$ and $d$ uses only times below $\min(t(a),t(c))$, and the segment $\Gamma_2$ between $a$ and $c$ only times above; they are disjoint and avoid the other two corners, so neither uses a side of the square. Close $\Gamma_1$ by the diagonal $bd$ to a closed curve $J$. Near the diagonal, $a$ and $c$ lie on different sides of $J$, but $\Gamma_2$ joins them without meeting $\Gamma_1$ or the diagonal (it stays outside the open square). This contradicts the Jordan curve theorem.

Lemma 2 (no source or sink with four neighbours). Let $x$ have neighbours $L,R$ in its row and $U,W$ in its column. If all four arrows at $x$ point out of $x$, then $t(L),t(R)<t(x)<t(U),t(W)$; if all point in, the inequalities are reversed. In the first case the route segment $\Gamma_1$ between $L$ and $R$ uses only times $<t(x)$, and the segment $\Gamma_2$ between $U$ and $W$ only times $>t(x)$ (the second case is the same with the roles exchanged). Close $\Gamma_1$ by the segments $Lx$ and $xR$ to a closed curve $J$. Near $x$, $J$ is a straight horizontal line, so $U$ and $W$ lie on different sides of $J$; yet $\Gamma_2$ joins them without meeting $\Gamma_1$, $x$, or the segments $Lx,xR$. Contradiction.

The counting. Suppose $D$ has a directed cycle; take a simple one, $C$. It is a simple closed lattice polygon. Let $H$ be the plane graph formed by all lattice points on or inside $C$ (all are cells of the board) and all unit segments between them lying inside or on $C$, with the orientations of $D$. Its bounded faces are unit squares and its outer face is bounded by $C$.

For a vertex $v$ let $s_v$ be the number of switches at $v$: consecutive edges around $v$ of which one points in and one points out. For a face $f$ let $s_f$ be the number of switches along its boundary: consecutive boundary edges that point in opposite directions along the boundary walk. Each angle (a pair of consecutive edges at a vertex $v$, lying in a face $f$) is a switch at $v$ or a switch of $f$, but not both: if one edge enters $v$ and the other leaves, the boundary of $f$ passes through $v$ in a consistent direction; if both enter or both leave, the direction reverses at $v$. There are $2E$ angles, so $\sum_vs_v+\sum_fs_f=2E$, and Euler's formula $V-E+F=2$ gives \[\sum_v\Bigl(1-\frac{s_v}2\Bigr)+\sum_f\Bigl(1-\frac{s_f}2\Bigr)=2 .\] All $s$-values are even. Now estimate each term:

  • the outer face: $C$ is directed, so $s=0$ and the term is $1$;

  • a unit-square face: by Lemma 1 it is not a directed cycle, so $s_f\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$;

  • a vertex on $C$: it has an incoming and an outgoing edge of $C$, so $s_v\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$;

  • a vertex strictly inside $C$: all its four neighbours are in $H$, and by Lemma 2 it is neither a source nor a sink, so $s_v\ge2$ and the term is $\le0$.

The left side is therefore at most $1$, a contradiction. Hence $D$ is acyclic, and the required numbering exists.
