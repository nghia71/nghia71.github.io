Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that \[f(x+yf(x+y))=f(x)f(y)+y^2\] for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x+1$ and $f(x)=1-x$

Show the solution

Solution (The substitutions $y=-x$ and $x+y=\pm1$)

Both functions work: for $f(x)=x+1$ both sides equal $x+xy+y^2+y+1$, and for $f(x)=1-x$ both sides equal $1-x-y+xy+y^2$. Conversely, let $f$ be a solution and write $P(x,y)$ for the equation.

$P(x,0)$ gives $f(x)=f(x)f(0)$. If $f(0)\ne1$, then $f\equiv0$, and $P(0,1)$ reads $0=1$; so $f(0)=1$. Now $P(x,-x)$ gives $f(x-xf(0))=f(0)$, i.e. \[1=f(x)f(-x)+x^2\quad\text{for all }x. \tag{1}\] With $x=1$: $f(1)f(-1)=0$.

Case $f(1)=0$. $P(x,1-x)$ gives $f(x)=f(x)f(1-x)+(1-x)^2$, i.e. \[f(x)\bigl(1-f(1-x)\bigr)=(1-x)^2, \tag{2}\] and replacing $x$ by $1-x$: $f(1-x)\bigl(1-f(x)\bigr)=x^2$. Subtracting, $f(x)-f(1-x)=(1-x)^2-x^2=1-2x$. Substituting $f(1-x)=f(x)-1+2x$ into (2): \[f(x)\bigl(2-2x-f(x)\bigr)=(1-x)^2\iff\bigl(f(x)-(1-x)\bigr)^2=0,\] so $f(x)=1-x$ for all $x$.

Case $f(-1)=0$. The function $g(x)=f(-x)$ is also a solution: $P(-x,-y)$ reads $f\bigl(-x-yf(-x-y)\bigr)=f(-x)f(-y)+y^2$, that is, $g\bigl(x+yg(x+y)\bigr)=g(x)g(y)+y^2$. Since $g(1)=f(-1)=0$, the first case gives $g(x)=1-x$, so $f(x)=g(-x)=1+x$.

Hence the solutions are \[\boxed{f(x)=x+1\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)=1-x}\]

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

The mixture $M(A)$ of a finite sequence $A=(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ is the set of sums of all the non-empty blocks of consecutive terms, i.e., \[M(A)=\{a_i+a_{i+1}+\dots+a_j\mid 1\le i\le j\le n\}.\] For example, the mixture of the sequence $A=(2,3,2)$ is the set $M(A)=\{2,3,5,7\}$. For each $n\ge 1$, let $k(n)$ be the smallest possible value such that there exist two finite sequences $A=(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ and $B=(b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n)$ of positive integers whose mixtures $M(A)$ and $M(B)$ are disjoint and \[(a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n)+(b_1+b_2+\dots+b_n)=k(n).\] Determine $k(n)$ for all $n\ge 1$.

Show the answer

$k(n)=n^2+2n$

Show the solution

Solution (Partial sums and a sumset)

Example. $A=(1,1,\dots,1)$ and $B=(n+1,n+1,\dots,n+1)$ have $M(A)=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ and $M(B)=\{n+1,2(n+1),\dots,n(n+1)\}$, which are disjoint, and the total is $n+n(n+1)=n^2+2n$.

Lower bound. Let $p_0=0<p_1<\dots<p_n$ be the partial sums of $A$ ($p_j=a_1+\dots+a_j$) and $q_0=0<q_1<\dots<q_n$ those of $B$. Then $M(A)=\{p_j-p_i:0\le i<j\le n\}$ and $M(B)=\{q_j-q_i:0\le i<j\le n\}$.

The $(n+1)^2$ numbers $p_i+q_j$ ($0\le i,j\le n$) are pairwise distinct. Indeed, let $p_i+q_j=p_k+q_l$ with $(i,j)\ne(k,l)$. If $i=k$ then $q_j=q_l$ and $j=l$; so $i\ne k$, say $i>k$. Then $p_i-p_k=q_l-q_j>0$ is an element of both $M(A)$ and $M(B)$, which is impossible.

All these numbers lie in $\{0,1,\dots,p_n+q_n\}$, so $(n+1)^2\le p_n+q_n+1$, i.e. $(a_1+\dots+a_n)+(b_1+\dots+b_n)\ge n^2+2n$.

Hence \[\boxed{k(n)=n^2+2n}\]

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$ and let $O$ be its circumcentre. Points $P$ and $Q$ lie on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$ and satisfy $PA=PB$ and $QA=QC$. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of $PQ$ and $BC$, respectively. Let $R\ne A$ be the second intersection of line $AN$ with the circumcircle of $ABC$. Prove that the perpendicular bisectors of the three line segments $BC$, $MO$, and $NR$ intersect at one point.

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates with origin $N$)

Choose coordinates with $N=(0,0)$, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and $A=(p,q)$, $q>0$; since $AB<AC$, $p<0$. The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ is the line $x=0$, and the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$ is $x=p$.

The points. $O=(0,k)$ with $OA^2=OB^2$, i.e. $p^2+(q-k)^2=1+k^2$, so $k=\dfrac{p^2+q^2-1}{2q}$. The point $P=(p,y_P)$ satisfies $(q-y_P)^2=(p+1)^2+y_P^2$, so $y_P=\dfrac{q^2-(p+1)^2}{2q}$; similarly $Q=(p,y_Q)$ with $y_Q=\dfrac{q^2-(p-1)^2}{2q}$. Hence \[M=(p,\mu),\qquad \mu=\frac{y_P+y_Q}2=\frac{q^2-p^2-1}{2q}.\] Since $N$ is inside the circumcircle, $NA\cdot NR=NB\cdot NC=1$ with $R$ on the ray opposite to $NA$, so $R=-\dfrac{A}{p^2+q^2}= \Bigl(\dfrac{-p}{p^2+q^2},\dfrac{-q}{p^2+q^2}\Bigr)$.

The common point. Let $T=(0,\tau)$ with $\tau=-\dfrac1{2q}$; it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$.

  • $TR^2=|R|^2-2\tau R_y+\tau^2=\dfrac{1+2\tau q}{p^2+q^2}+\tau^2=\tau^2=TN^2$, because $1+2\tau q=0$. So $T$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $NR$.

  • $TO^2-TM^2=(k-\tau)^2-p^2-(\mu-\tau)^2=(k-\mu)(k+\mu-2\tau)-p^2$. Here $k-\mu=\dfrac{p^2}{q}$ and $k+\mu-2\tau=\dfrac{q^2-1}{q}+\dfrac1q=q$, so $TO^2-TM^2=p^2-p^2=0$. As $k-\mu=p^2/q\ne0$, $M\ne O$, and $T$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $MO$.

Hence the three perpendicular bisectors pass through $T$. (The hypothesis that $ABC$ is acute was not used.)

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Let $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ be an infinite sequence of positive rational numbers such that for all $n\ge 1$ we have $a_{n+1}=\dfrac{p_n+1}{q_n+1}$, where $\dfrac{p_n}{q_n}$ is the reduced fraction form of $a_n$. Determine all possible values of $a_1$ such that there exists a positive integer $k$ for which the product $a_1a_2\cdots a_k$ is an integer.

Remark: The reduced fraction form of a positive rational number $r$ is the unique representation $r=\dfrac{p}{q}$ where $p$ and $q$ are coprime positive integers.

Show the answer

all rational $a_1\ge1$

Show the solution

Solution (The difference $p_n-q_n$ only shrinks)

$a_1<1$ fails. If $p_n<q_n$ then $p_n+1<q_n+1$, so $a_{n+1}<1$. Hence all terms lie in $(0,1)$ and so does every product $a_1\cdots a_k$; it is never an integer.

$a_1\ge1$ works. If $a_1=1$, take $k=1$. Let $a_1>1$ and put $d_n=p_n-q_n\ge1$. Let $g_n=\gcd(p_n+1,q_n+1)$; it divides $(p_n+1)-(q_n+1)=d_n$, and $p_{n+1}=(p_n+1)/g_n$, $q_{n+1}=(q_n+1)/g_n$, so \[d_{n+1}=d_n/g_n .\] Thus $(d_n)$ is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers, and it decreases whenever $g_n>1$.

Claim: $d_N=1$ for some $N$. Otherwise $d_n$ is eventually constant, equal to some $d\ge2$, say for $n\ge n_0$; then $g_n=1$ and $q_{n+1}=q_n+1$ for $n\ge n_0$. Let $r$ be a prime factor of $d$. Among the consecutive integers $q_{n_0}+1,q_{n_0}+2,\dots$ there is a multiple of $r$, say $r\mid q_n+1$ with $n\ge n_0$; then also $r\mid q_n+1+d=p_n+1$, so $r\mid g_n$, a contradiction.

So $a_N=\frac{Q+1}{Q}$ for some $Q\ge1$. Consecutive integers are coprime, so the following terms are $a_{N+j}=\frac{Q+j+1}{Q+j}$, and the product telescopes: \[a_1a_2\cdots a_{N+j}=\frac uv\cdot\frac{Q+j+1}{Q},\qquad\text{where }\frac uv=a_1\cdots a_{N-1}\ (\,=1\text{ if }N=1).\] Choose $j=2vQ-Q-1\ge0$; then $Q+j+1=2vQ$ and the product equals $2u$, an integer.

Hence the answer is \[\boxed{\text{all rational numbers }a_1\ge1}\]

Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfying the inequality \[f(x)f(y+z)\ge x(f(y)+f(z))\] for all real numbers $x,y,z$.

Show the answer

$f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=x$

Show the solution

Solution (First $f(0)=0$, then oddness)

Both work: for $f\equiv0$ the inequality reads $0\ge0$, and for $f(x)=x$ it reads $x(y+z)\ge x(y+z)$. Conversely let $f$ be a solution and $c=f(0)$. Note that $g(x)=-f(-x)$ is also a solution: $g(x)g(y+z)=f(-x)f(-y-z)\ge(-x)\bigl(f(-y)+f(-z)\bigr)=x\bigl(g(y)+g(z)\bigr)$.

$c=0$. Suppose $c>0$. With $x=0$: $cf(y+z)\ge0$, so $f\ge0$. With $y=z=0$: $cf(x)\ge2cx$, so $f(x)\ge2x$. With $x=1$, $z=-y$: $cf(1)\ge f(y)+f(-y)\ge f(y)$ for all $y$. So $f$ is bounded above by $cf(1)$, while $f(y)\ge2y$ is unbounded; contradiction. If $c<0$, the solution $g$ has $g(0)=-c>0$, impossible by the same argument. Hence $c=0$.

Conclusion. With $z=-y$: $0=f(x)f(0)\ge x\bigl(f(y)+f(-y)\bigr)$ for all $x$; taking $x=\pm1$ gives $f(-y)=-f(y)$. With $z=0$: $f(x)f(y)\ge xf(y)$, i.e. \[\bigl(f(x)-x\bigr)f(y)\ge0\quad\text{for all }x,y.\] If $f\not\equiv0$, choose $y_0$ with $f(y_0)\ne0$; since $f(-y_0)=-f(y_0)$, $f$ takes a positive and a negative value, so $f(x)-x\ge0$ and $f(x)-x\le0$, i.e. $f(x)=x$. Hence \[\boxed{f\equiv0\quad\text{or}\quad f(x)=x}\]

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Find all positive rational numbers $q$ for which there exist positive integers $a,b,c$ and $n$ such that $a+b+c=qn$ and $ab+c=n^2$.

Show the answer

all rational numbers $q>2$

Show the solution

Solution (AM–GM bound and a square construction)

$q>2$ is necessary. Since $c=n^2-ab\ge1$, we have $ab<n^2$, so $1\le\sqrt{ab}<n$. By AM–GM, $a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}$, hence \[qn=a+b+c\ge2\sqrt{ab}+n^2-ab=n^2+1-\bigl(\sqrt{ab}-1\bigr)^2>n^2+1-(n-1)^2=2n,\] because $0\le\sqrt{ab}-1<n-1$. So $q>2$.

Every rational $q>2$ occurs. Write $q=2+\frac uv$ with positive integers $u,v$, and put \[n=4uv,\quad a=n-2u,\quad b=n+2u,\quad c=4u^2 .\] Then $a=2u(2v-1)\ge1$, $ab+c=n^2-4u^2+4u^2=n^2$, and $a+b+c=2n+4u^2=n\bigl(2+\frac{4u^2}{4uv}\bigr)=qn$.

Hence the answer is \[\boxed{\text{all rational numbers }q>2}\]

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Consider an infinite square grid consisting of white cells. Let $n$ be a positive integer. Kekec coloured $4n+1$ of the cells red such that both the set $R$ of red cells and the set $W$ of white cells are connected.

The resultant red shape is a $k$-gon. An example with $5$ red cells forming a $6$-gon is shown.

Determine the largest possible value of $k$ in terms of $n$.

Remark: A set $S$ of cells is said to be connected, if for any two cells $a,b\in S$, there exists an integer $m\ge 1$ and cells $c_1,c_2,\dots,c_m\in S$ where $c_1=a$, $c_m=b$, and cells $c_i,c_{i+1}$ for $1\le i\le m-1$ share an edge.

Show the answer

$k=8n+4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Perimeter of a connected shape)

Let $m=4n+1$. Since $R$ and $W$ are both connected, the boundary of the red shape is a single closed polygon with $k$ sides (it may touch itself at a vertex; the bound below does not use that it is simple).

Upper bound. Every side of the polygon consists of whole unit edges separating a red cell from a white cell, and different sides use different unit edges, so $k\le P$, the number of such unit edges. Each red cell has $4$ edges, and an edge between two red cells is counted twice; so $P=4m-2e$, where $e$ is the number of pairs of red cells sharing an edge. As $R$ is connected, the graph of red cells (adjacent when they share an edge) is connected, so it has at least $m-1$ edges: $e\ge m-1$. Hence \[k\le P\le 4m-2(m-1)=2m+2=8n+4.\]

Construction. Using (column, row) coordinates, colour red the cells $(i,0)$ for $1\le i\le 2n+1$ and $(2j,1)$, $(2j,-1)$ for $1\le j\le n$: a row of $2n+1$ cells with a "tooth" above and below every even position (figure: $n=2$). These are $4n+1$ cells, $R$ is connected, and $W$ is connected (every white cell in rows $-1,0,1$ can step vertically or horizontally out of the strip of rows $-1,0,1$). There are exactly $e=2n+2n=m-1$ adjacent red pairs, so $P=8n+4$.

No two consecutive boundary unit edges are collinear, so each of them is a whole side and $k=P=8n+4$:

  • the horizontal boundary edges are the top and bottom edges of the row cells at odd positions and the outer edges of the teeth; along the row, two such edges are separated by a tooth, and next to the outer edge of a tooth the cells are white on both sides of the line;

  • the vertical boundary edges are the two end edges of the row and the side edges of the teeth; the continuation of an end edge, or the outer continuation of a tooth's side, runs between two white cells, and the inner continuation of a tooth's side runs between two red cells of the row.

Hence the largest possible value is \[\boxed{k=8n+4}\]

Solution 2 (Count cell corners and use the angle sum)

Let $m=4n+1$, and let cell $(x,y)$ be the unit square $[x,x+1]\times[y,y+1]$.

The boundary is a simple polygon. The statement treats the boundary of the red shape as a polygon; we check that it is a single closed curve without self-contact, so that the angle sum of a polygon applies.

  • There are no holes: a hole would be a set of white cells enclosed by red cells, and these could not be joined to the white cells far away, against the connectedness of $W$. As $R$ is connected, the boundary is a single closed curve.

  • There is no pinch, that is, a grid vertex $v$ where two red cells $U,V$ meet only at $v$ and the other two cells $U',V'$ at $v$ are white. Suppose there were. Join the centres of a chain of red cells from $U$ to $V$ (consecutive cells sharing an edge) by segments, and close it by the two half-diagonals from the centres of $U$ and $V$ to $v$. Every piece of this closed curve lies inside red cells and meets the grid lines only at midpoints of edges between two red cells or at $v$; so the curve does not cross itself. Near $v$ it is the diagonal through $v$, so the centres of $U'$ and $V'$ (on the other diagonal) lie on opposite sides of it, and by the Jordan curve theorem every path from $U'$ to $V'$ meets the curve. But a chain of white cells from $U'$ to $V'$, joined through centres and edge midpoints, stays inside white cells and away from all grid vertices, so it misses the curve. This contradicts the connectedness of $W$.

Two counts. Let $c$ and $d$ be the numbers of vertices of the polygon with interior angle $90^\circ$ and $270^\circ$; so $k=c+d$. The angle sum gives $90^\circ c+270^\circ d=(c+d-2)\cdot180^\circ$, that is, \[c-d=4.\] Now classify the grid vertices by the number of red cells among the four cells around them. With exactly one red cell, the vertex is a $90^\circ$ vertex of the polygon; with three, a $270^\circ$ vertex; with two, the two red cells share an edge (there is no pinch) and the boundary passes straight through the vertex; with four, the vertex is inside the shape. Let $s$ and $i$ be the numbers of vertices with two and with four red cells. Counting the pairs (red cell, one of its four corners) by vertices gives \[4m=c+3d+2s+4i .\] Substituting $c=d+4$ and $m=4n+1$: $16n+4=4+4d+2s+4i$, so $d=4n-\frac s2-i\le4n$. Hence \[k=c+d=2d+4\le8n+4.\]

Construction. Colour red the cells $(0,j)$ for $0\le j\le 2n$ (a column of $2n+1$ cells) and the cells $(-1,j)$ and $(1,j)$ for the odd $j$ with $1\le j\le 2n-1$ (one cell on each side of every second column cell; figure: $n=2$). These are $(2n+1)+2n=4n+1$ cells. $R$ is connected, since each side cell shares an edge with the column. $W$ is connected: a white cell of column $0$ lies above or below the whole shape, a white cell $(\pm1,j)$ can step sideways to $(\pm2,j)$, and all white cells outside the columns $-1,0,1$ or outside the rows $0,\dots,2n$ are joined around the shape.

We count the vertices with three red cells. A $2\times2$ block of cells inside the columns $1,2$ (or $-2,-1$) contains at most one red cell, because the side cells are never in adjacent rows. A block in the columns $0,1$ with rows $y-1,y$ contains three red cells exactly when both column cells $(0,y-1),(0,y)$ are red, i.e. $1\le y\le 2n$; then exactly one of $y-1,y$ is odd and lies in $[1,2n-1]$, so exactly one of $(1,y-1),(1,y)$ is red. This gives $2n$ such vertices on the right of the column and, symmetrically, $2n$ on the left: $d=4n$. By the first part ($c=d+4$), the polygon has $k=2d+4=8n+4$ sides.

Hence the largest possible value is \[\boxed{k=8n+4}\]

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A treasure chest has $2n-1$ locks on it labeled from $1$ to $2n-1$. The $i^{th}$ lock has $i$ keys that can open that lock, but none of the other locks. There are $2n-1$ people such that each one of them owns $n$ keys (one person can own multiple keys opening the same lock).

Determine the smallest positive integer $k$, such that no matter how the keys are distributed to the $2n-1$ people, there exists a group of $k$ people that can open all the locks together.

Show the answer

$k=\left\lfloor\frac{3n-1}{2}\right\rfloor$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Small locks first, then a forest)

Let $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$; note $\lfloor\frac{3n-1}2\rfloor=2n-1-m$. Call lock $i$ small if $i\le n$ and big if $i>n$. A big lock has more than $n$ keys, so it has at least two holders.

Lower bound: a distribution needing $2n-1-m$ people. Let $s=2n-1-2m$ ($s=n$ for odd $n$, $s=n-1$ for even $n$). Take $s$ "solo" people $P_1,\dots,P_s$ and $m$ pairs $Q_i,Q_i'$ ($1\le i\le m$). Person $P_j$ gets all $j$ keys of lock $j$; person $Q_i$ gets $n$ keys of lock $2n-i$ and $Q_i'$ gets the other $n-i\ge1$ keys of lock $2n-i$. The remaining locks $s+1,\dots,2n-1-m$ are distributed arbitrarily to fill the remaining places ($n-j$ for $P_j$, $i$ for $Q_i'$); the numbers match because there are $n(2n-1)$ keys and places in total. Now lock $j\le s$ is opened only by $P_j$, and lock $2n-i$ only by $Q_i$ or $Q_i'$. So every group opening all locks contains all $s$ solo people and one person of each pair: at least $s+m=2n-1-m$ people.

Upper bound. Fix a distribution. For every small lock choose one holder, and let $G_1$ be the set of chosen people, $|G_1|\le n$. Let $W$ be the set of the other $2n-1-|G_1|$ people, and $U$ the set of big locks none of whose holders is in $G_1$. Every lock in $U$ has all its holders in $W$, at least two of them. For $S\subseteq W$ let $i(S)$ be the number of locks of $U$ whose holders all lie in $S$; their keys, at least $n+1$ per lock, are held by the people of $S$, so $(n+1)\,i(S)\le n|S|$, and therefore \[i(S)\le|S|-1\qquad\text{for every non-empty }S\subseteq W. \tag{$*$}\]

Lemma. Let $W$ be a finite set and $U$ a list of subsets of $W$ ("edges", repetitions allowed), each with at least two elements, such that $(*)$ holds, where $i(S)$ counts the edges contained in $S$. Then $W$ can be coloured with two colours so that every edge contains both colours.

Proof. (i) While some edge $e$ has $|e|\ge3$, we replace it by $e\setminus\{v\}$ for a suitable $v\in e$, keeping $(*)$. Such $v$ exists: otherwise, for every $v\in e$ the replacement violates $(*)$ for some $S_v$ with $e\setminus\{v\}\subseteq S_v$, $v\notin S_v$ and $i(S_v)=|S_v|-1$. Take $v\ne w$ in $e$, put $S=S_v\cup S_w$ and $T=S_v\cap S_w\supseteq e\setminus\{v,w\}\ne\varnothing$. Edges inside $S_v$ or inside $S_w$ lie inside $S$, those inside both lie inside $T$, and $e$ lies inside $S$ but inside neither $S_v$ nor $S_w$. Hence \[i(S)\ge i(S_v)+i(S_w)-i(T)+1\ge(|S_v|-1)+(|S_w|-1)-(|T|-1)+1=|S|,\] contradicting $(*)$. Each replacement lowers the total size of the edges, so the process stops. A colouring that is good for the smaller edges is good for the original ones, so we may assume all edges have two elements. (ii) Now $U$ is a multigraph on $W$ in which every set $S$ spans at most $|S|-1$ edges. It has no cycle (a cycle, or a double edge, on $r$ vertices spans at least $r$ edges), so it is a forest; colour each tree by the parity of the distance from a fixed vertex. Adjacent vertices get different colours. $\square$

By the lemma, colour $W$ so that every lock of $U$ has holders of both colours, and let $W'$ be the smaller colour class, $|W'|\le\lfloor|W|/2\rfloor$. The group $G=G_1\cup W'$ opens every lock: small locks through $G_1$, big locks not in $U$ through $G_1$, and locks of $U$ through $W'$. Its size is at most \[|G_1|+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2n-1-|G_1|}2\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2n-1+|G_1|}2\Bigr\rfloor\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac{3n-1}2\Bigr\rfloor.\]

If $G$ is smaller, we enlarge it by arbitrary further people to a group of exactly $\lfloor\frac{3n-1}2\rfloor\le2n-1$ people, which still opens all locks. Therefore the smallest $k$ is \[\boxed{k=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{3n-1}{2}\Bigr\rfloor}\] that is, $\frac{3n-1}2$ for odd $n$ and $\frac{3n}2-1$ for even $n$.

Solution 2 (Distinct representatives and distances to a free person)

Let $t=\lfloor\frac{n-1}2\rfloor$. Note that $n+t=\lfloor\frac{3n-1}2\rfloor$ (for $n=2t+1$ both equal $3t+1$, for $n=2t+2$ both equal $3t+2$). We show that the answer is $n+t$.

A distribution that needs $n+t$ people. Call the people $P_1,\dots,P_{2n-1}$. For $i=1,\dots,n$ give all $i$ keys of lock $i$ to $P_i$. For $j=1,\dots,t$ give $n$ keys of lock $n+j$ to $P_{n+2j-1}$ and its other $j$ keys to $P_{n+2j}$; these people exist because $n+2t\le2n-1$. Nobody has more than $n$ keys so far, and the keys of the locks $n+t+1,\dots,2n-1$ can fill all the remaining places in any way, because there are $1+2+\dots+(2n-1)=n(2n-1)$ keys and $n(2n-1)$ places. Now lock $i\le n$ is opened only by $P_i$, and lock $n+j$ only by $P_{n+2j-1}$ or $P_{n+2j}$. These $n+t$ sets of people are pairwise disjoint, so a group that opens all locks has at least $n+t$ people.

Every distribution allows $n+t$ people. Fix a distribution. For each lock $i\le n$ choose one holder of a key of it, and let $S$ be the set of chosen people, $s=|S|\le n$. Let $W$ be the set of the other $2n-1-s$ people and $U$ the set of locks with no holder in $S$. Every lock of $U$ is a lock $L>n$, so it has at least $n+1$ keys, all held by people of $W$. For $L\in U$ let $H(L)\subseteq W$ be the set of its holders.

Union bound. For any $r\ge1$ locks of $U$, the union of their sets $H(L)$ has at least $r+1$ people. Indeed, these people hold all the at least $(n+1)r$ keys of these locks and each holds at most $n$ keys, so there are more than $r$ of them.

Representatives. By the union bound, any $r$ locks of $U$ have at least $r$ holders in total, so by Hall's marriage theorem we can choose for every $L\in U$ a holder $\rho(L)\in H(L)$, with different locks getting different people. A person of $W$ who is not of the form $\rho(L)$ is called free.

Arrows. For every $L\in U$ draw an arrow from $\rho(L)$ to each other person of $H(L)$. The arrows leaving a representative $p=\rho(L)$ all belong to its own lock $L$. We claim that from every representative some free person can be reached along arrows. If not, let $Z$ be the set of people reachable from some representative $p$ (including $p$); all of them are representatives. Let $T$ be the set of locks they represent, so $|T|=|Z|$. For $L\in T$ every holder of $L$ is reached by an arrow from $\rho(L)\in Z$, so $H(L)\subseteq Z$. Thus the $|T|$ locks of $T$ have only $|Z|=|T|$ holders in total, against the union bound.

Colouring. For $p\in W$ let $\delta(p)$ be $0$ if $p$ is free, and otherwise the smallest number of arrows on a path from $p$ to a free person. If $p=\rho(L)$, the first arrow of a shortest such path goes from $p$ to a holder $q$ of $L$ with $\delta(q)=\delta(p)-1$. Colour every person of $W$ by the parity of $\delta$. Then every lock $L\in U$ has the holders $p=\rho(L)$ and $q$ of different colours. Let $W'$ be the smaller colour class, so $|W'|\le\lfloor\frac{2n-1-s}2\rfloor$, and $W'$ contains a holder of every lock of $U$.

The group $S\cup W'$ opens every lock: a lock outside $U$ has a holder in $S$, and a lock of $U$ has one in $W'$. Its size is at most \[s+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2n-1-s}2\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2n-1+s}2\Bigr\rfloor\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac{3n-1}2\Bigr\rfloor=n+t,\] and adding further people (as $n+t\le2n-1$) gives a group of exactly $n+t$ people that opens all locks. Therefore \[\boxed{k=n+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-1}2\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{3n-1}{2}\Bigr\rfloor}\]

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $ABCD$ ($AD\parallel BC$, $AD<BC$) be an isosceles trapezoid. Let $O$ be the circumcenter of triangle $ABC$. Let $X$ be the point diametrically opposite to $B$ on circle $ABO$, and let $Y$ be the point diametrically opposite to $C$ on circle $ACO$.

Show that points $A$, $D$, $X$, and $Y$ are concyclic.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Reflect $X$ in the axis of symmetry)

Let $\omega$ be the circle through $A,B,C,D$ (an isosceles trapezoid is cyclic), with centre $O$, and let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the common perpendicular bisector $s$ of $AD$ and $BC$; it fixes $O$ and swaps $A\leftrightarrow D$, $B\leftrightarrow C$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(p,q)$ modulo $180^\circ$.

The case $O\in BC$. Then $BC$ is a diameter of $\omega$ and $\angle BAC=90^\circ$. The lines through $O$ perpendicular to $OB$ and to $OC$ are both $s$, so $X,Y\in s$. Also $XA\perp AB$ and $YA\perp AC$, so $X$ lies on line $AC$ and $Y$ on line $AB$ ($X\ne Y$, as these lines meet only at $A\notin s$); hence $\angle XAY=90^\circ$. So $A$ lies on the circle with diameter $XY$; its centre is on $s$, so this circle is symmetric in $s$ and also contains $D$. From now on let $O\notin BC$.

Since $BX$ and $CY$ are diameters, $XA\perp AB$, $XO\perp OB$, $YA\perp AC$, $YO\perp OC$. Put $X'=\sigma(X)$. Then $X'$ is diametrically opposite to $C$ on circle $DCO$, so $X'O\perp OC$: both $X'$ and $Y$ lie on the line $m$ through $O$ perpendicular to $OC$.

A known angle. Let $C_1$ be the point of $\omega$ opposite to $C$. As $\angle CAC_1=90^\circ$, \[\measuredangle(CO,CA)=\measuredangle(C_1C,C_1A)+\measuredangle(C_1A,CA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+90^\circ. \tag{1}\]

$A,X,X',Y$ are concyclic. The segment $XX'$ is perpendicular to $s$, hence parallel to $BC$, so \[\measuredangle(XX',XA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(BA,XA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+90^\circ.\] The lines $YX'=m$ and $YA$ are the lines $OC$ and $AC$ turned by $90^\circ$, so by (1) \[\measuredangle(YX',YA)=\measuredangle(CO,CA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+90^\circ.\] The two angles are equal, so $A,X,X',Y$ lie on a circle $\Gamma$. (Here $X\ne X'$: as $O\notin BC$, the line $XO\perp OB$ is not $s$, so it meets $s$ only at $O$, and $X\ne O$ because $\angle BAO<90^\circ$. If $Y=X'$, let $\Gamma$ be the circle $AXX'$; the next paragraph applies unchanged. If $A$ coincides with $X$, $X'$ or $Y$, then two of the points $A,D,X,Y$ coincide ($X'=A$ means $X=D$) and the statement degenerates; we exclude this.)

$D$ lies on $\Gamma$. The perpendicular bisector of the chord $XX'$ of $\Gamma$ is $s$, so the centre of $\Gamma$ is on $s$ and $\sigma(\Gamma)=\Gamma$. Hence $D=\sigma(A)\in\Gamma$, and $A,D,X,Y$ are concyclic.

Solution 2 (Trigonometric coordinates)

Take $\omega$ to be the unit circle centred at $O=(0,0)$, with the axis of symmetry as the $y$-axis: \[B=(-\sin b,-\cos b),\ C=(\sin b,-\cos b),\ A=(-\sin a,\cos a),\ D=(\sin a,\cos a),\qquad 0<a,b<\pi,\ \sin a<\sin b.\] In particular $a\ne b$, so $\cos a\ne\cos b$.

$X$. $OX\perp OB$ means $X=\lambda(-\cos b,\sin b)$; then $X\cdot B=0$, and $AX\perp AB$ reads $(X-A)\cdot(B-A)=-X\cdot A-A\cdot B+1=0$. Since $X\cdot A=\lambda\sin(a+b)$, $A\cdot B=-\cos(a+b)$, and $\sin(a+b)\ne0$ ($a+b=\pi$ would give $A=B$), we get $\lambda=\dfrac{1+\cos(a+b)}{\sin(a+b)}=\cot\dfrac{a+b}2$.

$Y$. Similarly $Y=\mu(\cos b,\sin b)$ with $\mu\sin(b-a)=1+\cos(b-a)$, i.e. $\mu=\cot\dfrac{b-a}2$.

The circle. Let $\Gamma:\ x^2+y^2-2\kappa y+c=0$ with $\kappa=\dfrac1{\cos a-\cos b}$, $c=\dfrac{\cos a+\cos b}{\cos a-\cos b}$. For $A$ and $D$: $1-2\kappa\cos a+c=\dfrac{(\cos a-\cos b)-2\cos a+(\cos a+\cos b)}{\cos a-\cos b}=0$. For $X$ and $Y$ (note $|X|=|\lambda|$, $X_y=\lambda\sin b$, $|Y|=|\mu|$, $Y_y=\mu\sin b$) we must show that $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are roots of $T^2-2\kappa\sin b\,T+c=0$, i.e. that $\lambda+\mu=2\kappa\sin b$ and $\lambda\mu=c$. With $\alpha=\frac{a+b}2$, $\beta=\frac{b-a}2$ and $2\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\cos a-\cos b$, $2\cos\alpha\cos\beta=\cos a+\cos b$: \[\lambda+\mu=\frac{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}{\sin\alpha\sin\beta}=\frac{2\sin b}{\cos a-\cos b}=2\kappa\sin b,\qquad \lambda\mu=\frac{\cos\alpha\cos\beta}{\sin\alpha\sin\beta}=\frac{\cos a+\cos b}{\cos a-\cos b}=c.\] Hence $A,D,X,Y$ all lie on $\Gamma$.

Solution 3 (Inversion in the circumcircle)

An isosceles trapezoid is cyclic, so $A,B,C,D$ lie on the circumcircle $\omega$ of $ABC$; let $\rho$ be its radius. Take coordinates \[B=(-1,0),\quad C=(1,0),\quad A=(-u,v),\quad D=(u,v),\qquad 0<u<1,\ v>0\] ($u<1$ because $AD<BC$). Then $O=(0,o)$, and $OA^2=OB^2$ reads $u^2+(v-o)^2=1+o^2$, that is, $2vo=u^2+v^2-1$; also $\rho^2=1+o^2$.

The circles $ABO$ and $ACO$. $O$ is not on line $AB$: otherwise $AB$ would be a diameter of $\omega$, but the point of $\omega$ opposite to $B$ is $(1,2o)$, whose first coordinate is not $-u$. In the same way $O$ is not on line $AC$ (the point opposite to $C$ is $(-1,2o)$). Also $X\ne O$: otherwise $BO$ would be a diameter of circle $ABO$ and $\angle BAO=90^\circ$, but $\angle BAO$ is a base angle of the isosceles triangle $ABO$, hence acute. Similarly $Y\ne O$.

Inversion. Invert in $\omega$ (centre $O$, radius $\rho$); it fixes $A,B,C,D$. Let $X',Y'$ be the images of $X,Y$. The circle $ABO$ passes through the centre $O$, so it is mapped into the line $AB$; hence $X'\in AB$. As $BX$ is a diameter of circle $ABO$, $\angle BOX=90^\circ$, and $X'$ is on ray $OX$, so $OX'\perp OB$. Likewise $Y'\in AC$ and $OY'\perp OC$.

Write $X'=B+t(B-A)$. Since $OA=OB=\rho$, \[(B-O)\cdot(B-A)=\rho^2-(B-O)\cdot(A-O)=\rho^2-\frac{2\rho^2-AB^2}{2}=\frac{AB^2}2,\] so $0=(X'-O)\cdot(B-O)=\rho^2+t\,\frac{AB^2}2$, i.e. $t\,AB^2=-2\rho^2$. In the same way $Y'=C+s(C-A)$ with $s\,AC^2=-2\rho^2$.

A circle through $A,D,X',Y'$. For a point $P$ with second coordinate $y_P$ put \[\varphi(P)=\bigl(OP^2-\rho^2\bigr)-\frac{2\rho^2}{v}\,(y_P-v).\] The equation $\varphi(P)=0$ has the form $x^2+y^2+(\text{linear terms})=0$. Since $A,D\in\omega$ and $y_A=y_D=v$, we get $\varphi(A)=\varphi(D)=0$. For $X'$: the power $OX'^2-\rho^2$ of $X'$ with respect to $\omega$ equals $(A-X')\cdot(B-X')$, because $A,B$ are the points of $\omega$ on the line through $X'$; here $A-X'=-(1+t)(B-A)$ and $B-X'=-t(B-A)$, so the power is $t(1+t)AB^2$. Also $y_{X'}=-tv$, so $y_{X'}-v=-(1+t)v$. Hence \[\varphi(X')=t(1+t)AB^2+2\rho^2(1+t)=(1+t)\bigl(t\,AB^2+2\rho^2\bigr)=0.\] The same computation with $C$, $s$, $AC$ gives $\varphi(Y')=0$. So the set $\Gamma$ given by $\varphi=0$ contains the two distinct points $A,D$; an equation of this form with at least two solutions describes a circle. Finally, using $2vo=u^2+v^2-1$, \[\varphi(O)=-\rho^2-\frac{2\rho^2}{v}(o-v)=\frac{\rho^2\,(v^2-2vo)}{v^2}=\frac{\rho^2(1-u^2)}{v^2}>0,\] so $O\notin\Gamma$.

Back. An inversion maps a circle not through its centre to a circle. So the image of $\Gamma$ is a circle, and it contains the images of $A,D,X',Y'$, namely $A,D,X,Y$. Hence $A,D,X,Y$ are concyclic.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

We are given an acute-angled triangle $ABC$. Let $I_a$ be its $A$-excenter. Points $D$ and $E$ lie on the lines $I_aB$ and $I_aC$, respectively, and satisfy $DA=DB$ and $EA=EC$. The circumcircle of $I_aDE$ intersects line $BC$ at points $F$ and $G$. Prove that the circumcircles of $ABC$ and $AFG$ are tangent.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Powers of $B$ and $C$ give isogonal lines)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ the angles of $ABC$, $\omega$ its circumcircle and $\Omega$ the circle $I_aDE$. The circles $ABC$ and $AFG$ meet at $A$; we show they are tangent there.

Step 1: lengths. The line $I_aB$ is the external bisector at $B$; its ray towards $I_a$ makes the angle $\angle ABI_a=\beta+\frac{180^\circ-\beta}2=90^\circ+\frac\beta2$ with $BA$. As $D$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, its projection to line $AB$ is the midpoint of $AB$, on ray $BA$, so $\angle ABD$ is acute: $D$ is on the opposite ray, $B$ lies between $D$ and $I_a$, and $\angle ABD=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$. Hence $BD=\dfrac{c/2}{\cos(90^\circ-\beta/2)}=\dfrac{c}{2\sin(\beta/2)}$. In triangle $BI_aC$ the angles at $B$, $C$ are $90^\circ-\frac\beta2$, $90^\circ-\frac\gamma2$, so the angle at $I_a$ is $90^\circ-\frac\alpha2$ and, by the law of sines, $BI_a=\dfrac{a\cos(\gamma/2)}{\cos(\alpha/2)}$. Symmetrically $C$ lies between $E$ and $I_a$, $CE=\dfrac{b}{2\sin(\gamma/2)}$, $CI_a=\dfrac{a\cos(\beta/2)}{\cos(\alpha/2)}$.

Step 2: powers. $B$ is an interior point of the chord $DI_a$ of $\Omega$, so $B$ is inside $\Omega$ with power $-BD\cdot BI_a$; likewise $C$ is inside $\Omega$. So line $BC$ meets $\Omega$ at a point beyond $B$ and a point beyond $C$; call them $G$ and $F$ respectively (the statement is symmetric in $F,G$). Then $BF\cdot BG=BD\cdot BI_a$ and $CF\cdot CG=CE\cdot CI_a$, so \[\frac{BF\cdot BG}{CF\cdot CG}=\frac{c\cos(\gamma/2)\,\sin(\gamma/2)}{b\cos(\beta/2)\,\sin(\beta/2)}=\frac cb\cdot\frac{\sin\gamma}{\sin\beta}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}.\]

Step 3: $\angle CAF=\angle BAG$. Let $\varphi=\angle CAF$ and $\psi=\angle BAG$. As $F$ is beyond $C$, $\angle BAF=\alpha+\varphi$ and $\angle ACF=180^\circ-\gamma$; the law of sines in $ABF$ and $ACF$ gives $\dfrac{BF}{CF}=\dfrac{\sin(\alpha+\varphi)\sin\gamma}{\sin\varphi\,\sin\beta}=\dfrac{c\,\sin(\alpha+\varphi)}{b\,\sin\varphi}$, and in the same way $\dfrac{CG}{BG}=\dfrac{b\,\sin(\alpha+\psi)}{c\,\sin\psi}$. Dividing and using Step 2, \[\frac{\sin(\alpha+\varphi)}{\sin\varphi}=\frac{\sin(\alpha+\psi)}{\sin\psi},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad \sin\alpha\cot\varphi+\cos\alpha=\sin\alpha\cot\psi+\cos\alpha,\] so $\cot\varphi=\cot\psi$ and $\varphi=\psi$ (both lie in $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$).

Step 4: tangency. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$, and $t_B$ its ray on the side of line $AB$ opposite to $C$; then $\angle(t_B,AB)=\gamma$. In triangle $AFC$, $\angle AFC=180^\circ-(180^\circ-\gamma)-\varphi=\gamma-\varphi$, so $\varphi<\gamma$. The ray $AG$ is on the same side of $AB$ as $t_B$ (since $G$ is beyond $B$) and makes the angle $\psi=\varphi<\gamma$ with $AB$, so it lies between $AB$ and $t_B$ and \[\angle(t_B,AG)=\gamma-\varphi=\angle AFG.\] Moreover $F$ lies on the side of line $AG$ opposite to $t_B$: on line $BC$ the points $B$ and $F$ lie on the same side of $G$, so $F$ is on the same side of line $AG$ as $B$, and the ray $AG$, lying between the rays $AB$ and $t_B$, separates $B$ from $t_B$. The angle between $t$ and the chord $AG$ equals the inscribed angle on $AG$ from the other side, so $t$ is tangent to circle $AFG$ at $A$ (converse of the tangent–chord theorem). Thus the circles $ABC$ and $AFG$ have the common tangent $t$ at their common point $A$: they are tangent.

Solution 2 (The radical axis is the tangent at $A$)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $\beta,\gamma$ the angles at $B,C$ of triangle $ABC$, let $\omega$ be its circumcircle with centre $O$, let $\Omega$ be the circle $I_aDE$, and let $r_a$ be the distance from $I_a$ to $BC$.

The powers of $B$ and $C$ with respect to $\Omega$. The line $BI_a$ is the external bisector at $B$: the ray $BI_a$ bisects the angle $180^\circ-\beta$ between ray $BC$ and the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$, so it makes the obtuse angle $90^\circ+\frac\beta2$ with ray $BA$, and the opposite ray makes the acute angle $90^\circ-\frac\beta2$ with $BA$. Since $DA=DB$, the foot of $D$ on line $AB$ is the midpoint of $AB$, on ray $BA$; hence $D$ lies on the ray opposite to $BI_a$, $B$ is between $D$ and $I_a$, and $BD\cos(90^\circ-\frac\beta2)=\frac c2$, that is, $BD=\dfrac{c}{2\sin(\beta/2)}$. As $\angle I_aBC=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$, $BI_a=\dfrac{r_a}{\cos(\beta/2)}$. So $D\ne I_a$, similarly $E\ne I_a$, and $\Omega$ exists because $D,E$ lie on the different lines $I_aB$, $I_aC$. The point $B$ is inside the chord $DI_a$ of $\Omega$, so its power is \[\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(B)=-BD\cdot BI_a=-\frac{c\,r_a}{2\sin\frac\beta2\cos\frac\beta2}=-\frac{c\,r_a}{\sin\beta},\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad \operatorname{pow}_\Omega(C)=-\frac{b\,r_a}{\sin\gamma}.\] By the law of sines $b\sin\gamma=c\sin\beta$, so \[\frac{\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(B)}{c^2}=\frac{\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(C)}{b^2}=-\lambda,\qquad \lambda=\frac{r_a}{c\sin\beta}>0.\] In particular $B$ and $C$ are inside $\Omega$, so line $BC$ meets $\Omega$ at two points $F\ne G$; the circle $\eta=AFG$ exists since $A\notin BC$.

An affine function. For a circle $\kappa$ with centre $K$ and radius $R$ write $\operatorname{pow}_\kappa(P)=PK^2-R^2$. The difference $\ell(P)=\operatorname{pow}_\eta(P)-\operatorname{pow}_\omega(P)$ has no quadratic terms: it is an affine function of the coordinates of $P$. We know its values at $A,B,C$:

  • $\ell(A)=0$, as $A$ lies on both circles;

  • for a point $P$ of line $BC$, $\operatorname{pow}_\eta(P)$ and $\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(P)$ both equal the signed product $(F-P)\cdot(G-P)$, since both circles meet line $BC$ at $F,G$; so $\ell(B)=\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(B)-0=-\lambda c^2$ and $\ell(C)=-\lambda b^2$.

Compare with the affine function $g(P)=2(A-O)\cdot(P-A)$. Clearly $g(A)=0$, and since $OA=OB$, $2(A-O)\cdot(A-B)=OA^2-OB^2+AB^2=c^2$, so $g(B)=-c^2$; likewise $g(C)=-b^2$. Two affine functions that agree at three non-collinear points are equal, so $\ell=\lambda g$.

Conclusion. A common point $P$ of $\omega$ and $\eta$ has $\ell(P)=0$, hence $g(P)=0$: it lies on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $OA$, which is the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ and meets $\omega$ only at $A$. So $\omega$ and $\eta$ have exactly one common point, $A$; two circles with exactly one common point are tangent there. Hence the circumcircles of $ABC$ and $AFG$ are tangent at $A$.

Solution 3 (Inversion at $A$: two parallel lines)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $\beta,\gamma$ the angles at $B,C$ of triangle $ABC$, let $\Omega$ be the circle $I_aDE$, and let $r_a$ be the distance from $I_a$ to $BC$.

The powers of $B$ and $C$ with respect to $\Omega$. The ray $BI_a$ bisects the angle $180^\circ-\beta$ between ray $BC$ and the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$, so it makes the angle $90^\circ+\frac\beta2$ with ray $BA$, and the opposite ray makes the acute angle $90^\circ-\frac\beta2$ with $BA$. As $DA=DB$, the foot of $D$ on line $AB$ is the midpoint of $AB$, on ray $BA$; hence $D$ is on the ray opposite to $BI_a$, $B$ is between $D$ and $I_a$, and $BD=\dfrac{c}{2\sin(\beta/2)}$. Since $\angle I_aBC=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$, $BI_a=\dfrac{r_a}{\cos(\beta/2)}$. In the same way $C$ is between $E$ and $I_a$ with $CE=\dfrac{b}{2\sin(\gamma/2)}$, $CI_a=\dfrac{r_a}{\cos(\gamma/2)}$; $\Omega$ exists since $D\ne I_a$ and $E\ne I_a$ lie on the different lines $I_aB$, $I_aC$. Thus $B$ and $C$ are inside $\Omega$, line $BC$ meets $\Omega$ at two points $F,G$, the points $B,C$ lie strictly between $F$ and $G$, and by the power of a point and the law of sines ($c\sin\beta=b\sin\gamma$) \[\frac{BF\cdot BG}{c^2}=\frac{BD\cdot BI_a}{c^2}=\frac{r_a}{c\sin\beta}=\frac{r_a}{b\sin\gamma}=\frac{CE\cdot CI_a}{b^2}=\frac{CF\cdot CG}{b^2}.\] Call this common value $\lambda$.

Inversion. Invert with centre $A$ and radius $1$, and write $P'$ for the image of $P$; recall $P'Q'=\dfrac{PQ}{AP\cdot AQ}$. Line $BC$ becomes a circle $\kappa$ through $A$ containing $B',C',F',G'$; the circle $ABC$ becomes the line $B'C'$, and the circle $AFG$ the line $F'G'$. The inversion maps line $BC$ continuously and bijectively onto $\kappa$ without $A$, so the segment $FG$ goes to the arc $F'G'$ of $\kappa$ not containing $A$; as $B,C$ are inside segment $FG$, the points $B',C'$ lie on this arc, on the same side of line $F'G'$. Therefore the inscribed angles $\angle F'B'G'$ and $\angle F'C'G'$ are equal, say to $\theta$. Moreover \[B'F'\cdot B'G'=\frac{BF\cdot BG}{c^2\,AF\cdot AG}=\frac{\lambda}{AF\cdot AG}=\frac{CF\cdot CG}{b^2\,AF\cdot AG}=C'F'\cdot C'G'.\] So the triangles $F'B'G'$ and $F'C'G'$ have equal areas $\frac12B'F'\cdot B'G'\sin\theta=\frac12C'F'\cdot C'G'\sin\theta$. They share the base $F'G'$, so $B'$ and $C'$ are at the same distance from line $F'G'$, on the same side: $B'C'\parallel F'G'$. These lines are different, because a line meets $\kappa$ in at most two of the four distinct points $B',C',F',G'$.

Conclusion. A common point $P\ne A$ of the circles $ABC$ and $AFG$ would give a common point $P'$ of the distinct parallel lines $B'C'$ and $F'G'$, which is impossible. So the two circles have only the point $A$ in common, and therefore they are tangent at $A$.

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation

Let $\alpha$ be a positive rational number. In the beginning, the number $2026$ is written on the blackboard. In each step, if $x$ is the number currently written on the blackboard, we may replace it with one of the numbers $x+\alpha$, $x-\alpha$ and $\dfrac{1}{x}$ (the latter if $x\ne 0$). We call a rational number $y$ reachable if it can appear on the blackboard after a finite number of steps.

Determine all positive rational numbers $\alpha$ for which all rational numbers are reachable.

Show the answer

$\alpha=\frac1k$, $k$ a positive integer

Show the solution

Solution (Euclid's algorithm and residues modulo a prime)

Every step can be undone ($x\pm\alpha$ by the opposite step, $x\mapsto\frac1x$ by itself), so if $y$ can be reached from $x$, then $x$ can be reached from $y$, and reachability is transitive.

$\alpha=\frac1k$ works. Adding $\alpha$ $k$ times adds $1$, so the moves $x\mapsto x\pm1$ and $x\mapsto\frac1x$ are available. We show by induction on $b$ that every rational $y=\frac ab$ ($b\ge1$, lowest terms) can be brought to $0$. If $b=1$, $y$ is an integer: add or subtract $1$ repeatedly. If $b>1$, subtract $\lfloor y\rfloor$ to get $\frac{a'}b$ with $0<a'<b$, then invert to get $\frac b{a'}$, whose denominator $a'$ is smaller than $b$. Since $2026$ and every rational $y$ can be brought to $0$, every $y$ is reachable from $2026$ (go from $2026$ to $0$, then reverse the path from $y$ to $0$).

Other $\alpha$ fail. Let $\alpha=\frac pq$ in lowest terms with $p\ge2$, and let $r$ be a prime factor of $p$. For a rational $x$ define $\rho(x)\in\{0,1,\dots,r-1,\infty\}$: if $x=\frac uv$ with $r\nmid v$, let $\rho(x)$ be the residue of $uv^{-1}$ modulo $r$ (it does not depend on the choice of $u,v$); if no such representation exists ($r$ divides the reduced denominator), let $\rho(x)=\infty$. Then:

  • $\rho(x\pm\alpha)=\rho(x)$: if $x=\frac uv$ with $r\nmid v$, then $x\pm\alpha=\frac{uq\pm pv}{vq}$ with $r\nmid vq$ ($r\nmid q$ because $\gcd(p,q)=1$) and $(uq\pm pv)(vq)^{-1}\equiv uv^{-1}\pmod r$; if $r$ divides the reduced denominator of $x$, it still divides that of $x\pm\alpha$.

  • $\rho(1/x)=1/\rho(x)$, with the conventions $1/0=\infty$, $1/\infty=0$.

Hence every reachable number has $\rho\in\{\rho(2026),1/\rho(2026)\}$, at most two values. But $\rho$ takes all $r+1\ge3$ values (on $0,1,\dots,r-1$ and $\frac1r$), so some rationals are not reachable.

Therefore all rationals are reachable exactly for \[\boxed{\alpha=\tfrac1k,\quad k=1,2,3,\dots}\]

Problem 8

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Determine all positive integers $n$, which have no prime divisors greater than $2026$, and such that for any two odd and coprime positive divisors of $n$, their arithmetic mean is also a divisor of $n$.

Show the answer

$n=2^a$ $(a\ge0)$, $3\cdot2^a$ $(a\ge1)$, $7\cdot2^a$ $(a\ge2)$, $15\cdot2^a$ $(a\ge3)$, $31\cdot2^a$ $(a\ge4)$, $127\cdot2^a$ $(a\ge6)$

Show the solution

Solution (The odd part is $2^c-1$, then split it)

Write $n=2^am$ with $m$ odd. The odd divisors of $n$ are the divisors of $m$, and for coprime odd divisors $d,d'$ the number $\frac{d+d'}2$ is an integer that must divide $n$. If an odd number $t$ divides $n$, then $t\mid m$.

Step 1: $m=2^c-1$ and $2^{c-1}\mid n$. Take $d=1$, $d'=m$: $\frac{m+1}2\mid n$. As $\gcd(m+1,m)=1$, $\frac{m+1}2$ is coprime to $m$, so it divides $2^a$. Hence $m=2^c-1$ for some $c\ge1$, and $2^{c-1}\mid n$.

Step 2: if $m$ has two distinct prime factors, then $m=15$. Let $m=uv$ with coprime $u,v>1$. Then $\frac{u+v}2\mid n$, and $u+v$ is coprime to $m$ (a prime of $m$ divides exactly one of $u,v$), so $u+v=2^s$; as $u,v\ge3$ are distinct odd numbers, $u+v\ge8$, so $s\ge3$. Also $2^c=uv+1>u+v=2^s$ (because $(u-1)(v-1)>0$), so $c>s$, and \[u^2-1=u(u+v)-(uv+1)=2^su-2^c=2^s\bigl(u-2^{c-s}\bigr).\] So $2^s\mid(u-1)(u+1)$. One of $u\pm1$ is $\equiv2\pmod4$, so the other is a multiple of $2^{s-1}$; but $0<u-1<u+1\le u+v-2<2^s$, so that factor equals $2^{s-1}$. Thus $u=2^t\pm1$ and $v=2^s-u=2^t\mp1$ with $t=s-1\ge2$, and $m=4^t-1$; in particular $t$ is determined by $m$.

Apply this to $u=$ the full power of one prime factor of $m$, and to $u'=$ the full power of another: both $u$ and $u'$ belong to $\{2^t-1,2^t+1\}$ and are different, so $\{u,u'\}=\{2^t-1,2^t+1\}$ and $m=uu'$ has exactly two prime factors. Now $w=2^t+1\equiv1\pmod4$, so $\frac{w+1}2=2^{t-1}+1$ is odd; it divides $n$ (take $d=w$, $d'=1$), hence $m$, and it is coprime to $w$, so it divides $2^t-1=2(2^{t-1}+1)-3$. Hence $2^{t-1}+1\mid3$, so $t=2$ and $m=3\cdot5=15$.

Step 3: if $m>1$ is a prime power, it is a prime. If $p^2\mid m$, take $d=p^2$, $d'=1$: since $p^2\equiv1\pmod4$, $\frac{p^2+1}2$ is odd and greater than $1$, so it divides $m$; but it is coprime to $p$, and $m$ is a power of $p$, a contradiction. So $m=p=2^c-1$ is a prime, and as $p\le2026$, $c\le10$; checking $c=2,\dots,10$ gives $p\in\{3,7,31,127\}$ (for $c=4,6,8,9,10$, $2^c-1=15,63,255,511,1023$ is composite).

Step 4: the powers of $2$, and verification.

  • $m=1$: the only odd divisor is $1$, and $\frac{1+1}2=1\mid n$. All $n=2^a$, $a\ge0$, work.

  • $m=p=2^c-1$ prime: the coprime pairs are $(1,1)$ and $(1,p)$, with means $1$ and $2^{c-1}$. So $n=2^ap$ works iff $a\ge c-1$: $3\cdot2^a$ ($a\ge1$), $7\cdot2^a$ ($a\ge2$), $31\cdot2^a$ ($a\ge4$), $127\cdot2^a$ ($a\ge6$).

  • $m=15$: the coprime pairs $(1,1),(1,3),(1,5),(1,15),(3,5)$ have means $1,2,3,8,4$; so $n=15\cdot2^a$ works iff $a\ge3$.

These are all the numbers with the required property: \[\boxed{\begin{gathered}n=2^a\ (a\ge0),\quad 3\cdot2^a\ (a\ge1),\quad 7\cdot2^a\ (a\ge2),\\ 15\cdot2^a\ (a\ge3),\quad 31\cdot2^a\ (a\ge4),\quad 127\cdot2^a\ (a\ge6)\end{gathered}}\]
