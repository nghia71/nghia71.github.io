First (Correspondence) Round

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

Let triangles $T$ and $ABC$ be symmetric about a point $P$. Points $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ are the reflections of $A$, $B$, $C$ about the corresponding sidelines of $T$. It is known that two of these points coincide. Prove that all three points coincide.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Let $T=A_1B_1C_1$ with $A_1=2P-A$, $B_1=2P-B$, $C_1=2P-C$. The half-turn about $P$ maps the line $BC$ to the line $B_1C_1$, so $B_1C_1\parallel BC$; similarly $C_1A_1\parallel CA$ and $A_1B_1\parallel AB$. The reflection of $A$ in a line parallel to $BC$ lies on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$, the $A$-altitude. Hence $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ lie on the altitudes from $A$, $B$, $C$ respectively.

Suppose $A'=B'$ (the other cases are the same up to renaming). Two different altitudes meet only at the orthocentre $H$, so $A'=B'=H$.

Put the origin at the circumcentre $O$ and denote the position vector of a point by the same letter. Then $H=A+B+C$, because $(A+B+C-A)\cdot(B-C)=|B|^2-|C|^2=0$ and similarly for the other vertices.

Claim. Let $\ell_A$ be the line through the points $A+B$ and $A+C$. Then $\ell_A\parallel BC$, and the reflection of $A$ in $\ell_A$ is $H$. Moreover, $\ell_A$ is the only line parallel to $BC$ with this property.

Indeed, $\ell_A$ has direction $(A+C)-(A+B)=C-B$. The midpoint of $AH$ is $A+\frac{B+C}2$, the midpoint of $A+B$ and $A+C$, so it lies on $\ell_A$; and $H-A=B+C$ is perpendicular to $BC$ because $(B+C)\cdot(B-C)=|B|^2-|C|^2=0$. So $A$ and $H$ are symmetric in $\ell_A$. A line parallel to $BC$ is determined by the point where it crosses the altitude $AH$, and that point must be the midpoint of $AH$; this gives uniqueness.

In the same way define $\ell_B$ (through $B+C$ and $B+A$) and $\ell_C$ (through $C+A$ and $C+B$).

Since $B_1C_1\parallel BC$ and the reflection of $A$ in $B_1C_1$ is $H$, the claim gives $B_1C_1=\ell_A$; likewise $C_1A_1=\ell_B$. The point $A+B$ lies on both $\ell_A$ and $\ell_B$, and these lines are not parallel, so \[C_1=A+B,\qquad 2P=C+C_1=A+B+C=H .\] Then $A_1=2P-A=B+C$ and $B_1=2P-B=A+C$, so $A_1B_1=\ell_C$, and by the claim the reflection of $C$ in $A_1B_1$ is $H$. Thus $C'=H=A'=B'$: all three points coincide (at the orthocentre). $\blacksquare$

Remark. The argument shows that this happens only when $P$ is the nine-point centre $\frac{O+H}2$; then $T$ is the triangle formed by the reflections of $O$ in the sides of $ABC$.

Solution 2 (Signed distances)

For a side line, say $BC$, let $d_a(X)$ be the signed distance from a point $X$ to $BC$, positive on the side of $A$; it is an affine function of $X$, and $d_a(A)=h_a$ (the altitude). Define $d_b,d_c$ in the same way.

Where the reflection lands. The half-turn about $P$ maps $BC$ to the side line $B_1C_1$ of $T$, so $X\in B_1C_1$ iff $2P-X\in BC$, i.e. $d_a(2P-X)=2d_a(P)-d_a(X)=0$. Thus $B_1C_1=\{X: d_a(X)=2d_a(P)\}$, a line parallel to $BC$. Reflecting $A$ in it keeps $A$ on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$ (the $A$-altitude) and gives a point $A'$ with $d_a(A')=4d_a(P)-h_a$.

The orthocentre $H$ lies on the same altitude, so \[A'=H\iff 4d_a(P)=h_a+d_a(H).\tag{1}\]

The nine-point centre. Let $O$ be the circumcentre, $M_a$ the midpoint of $BC$ and $N$ the midpoint of $OH$. The well-known relation $\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OM_a}$ gives $d_a(H)-h_a=2\bigl(d_a(M_a)-d_a(O)\bigr)=-2d_a(O)$. Hence \[4d_a(N)=2d_a(O)+2d_a(H)=\bigl(h_a-d_a(H)\bigr)+2d_a(H)=h_a+d_a(H).\] Comparing with (1): $A'=H$ if and only if $d_a(P)=d_a(N)$, i.e. iff $P$ lies on the line through $N$ parallel to $BC$. The same holds for $B'$ and $C'$ with the lines through $N$ parallel to $CA$ and $AB$.

Conclusion. Each of $A',B',C'$ lies on the corresponding altitude, so if two of them coincide, say $A'=B'$, the common point is the intersection $H$ of two altitudes. Then $P$ lies on the line through $N$ parallel to $BC$ and on the line through $N$ parallel to $CA$, so $P=N$. Consequently $d_c(P)=d_c(N)$ and also $C'=H$. All three points coincide. $\blacksquare$

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

An isosceles trapezoid $ABCD$ is circumscribed around a circle touching the lateral side $AB$ at point $T$. The segments $TC$ and $TD$ meet this circle for the second time at points $P$, $Q$ respectively. Find all possible values of $TP/PC+TQ/QD$.

Show the answer

$8$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of a point and the law of cosines)

Let the bases be $BC$ and $AD$ and put $b=\frac{BC}2$, $a=\frac{AD}2$. By symmetry the circle touches $BC$ and $AD$ at their midpoints, so the tangent lengths are $b$ from $B$ and $C$ and $a$ from $A$ and $D$. In particular $BT=b$ and $AT=a$.

The point $C$ lies outside the circle and $T$ on it, so $P$ lies on the segment $TC$ and, by the power of $C$, $CP\cdot CT=b^2$. Hence \[\frac{TP}{PC}=\frac{CT-CP}{CP}=\frac{CT^2}{CP\cdot CT}-1=\frac{CT^2}{b^2}-1,\] and similarly $\frac{TQ}{QD}=\frac{DT^2}{a^2}-1$. In triangle $TBC$ we have $TB=b$, $BC=2b$, so $CT^2=b^2+4b^2-4b^2\cos B=b^2(5-4\cos B)$. In triangle $TAD$ we have $TA=a$, $AD=2a$ and $\cos A=-\cos B$ (as $AD\parallel BC$), so $DT^2=a^2(5+4\cos B)$. Therefore \[\frac{TP}{PC}+\frac{TQ}{QD}=(4-4\cos B)+(4+4\cos B)=\boxed{8}\] for every such trapezoid.

Solution 2 (Stewart and Ptolemy)

Keep the notation $a=\frac{AD}2$, $b=\frac{BC}2$ (bases $BC$, $AD$); the tangent lengths give $AT=a$, $BT=b$, $AB=a+b$, and the powers of $C$ and $D$ are $b^2$ and $a^2$. As in any secant through a point outside the circle, $CP\cdot CT=b^2$ gives $\frac{TP}{PC}=\frac{CT^2}{b^2}-1$, and likewise $\frac{TQ}{QD}=\frac{DT^2}{a^2}-1$. So we need $\frac{CT^2}{b^2}+\frac{DT^2}{a^2}$.

Let $d=AC=BD$. The isosceles trapezoid is cyclic, so Ptolemy gives $d^2=AB\cdot CD+AD\cdot BC=(a+b)^2+4ab$.

Stewart's theorem for the cevian $CT$ of triangle $ABC$ ($AT=a$, $TB=b$) and the cevian $DT$ of triangle $ABD$ gives \[CT^2=\frac{b\,d^2+a\cdot 4b^2}{a+b}-ab,\qquad DT^2=\frac{b\cdot 4a^2+a\,d^2}{a+b}-ab .\] Hence \[\frac{CT^2}{b^2}+\frac{DT^2}{a^2}=\frac{4a+4b}{a+b}+\frac{d^2}{a+b}\Bigl(\frac1b+\frac1a\Bigr)-\frac ab-\frac ba =4+\frac{d^2-a^2-b^2}{ab}=4+\frac{6ab}{ab}=10,\] and the requested sum is $10-2=\boxed{8}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $AK$ be the bisector of a triangle $ABC$, $N$ be a point on $AC$ such that $\angle NKC=\angle CAB/2$, and $L$ be the midpoint of $KN$. Prove that $\angle KBN=\angle LAK$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A parallelogram and SAS similarity)

Let $\alpha=\frac12\angle CAB$ and $\beta=\angle ABC$. The exterior angle of triangle $ABK$ gives $\angle AKC=\beta+\alpha>\alpha=\angle NKC$, so the ray $KN$ lies inside the angle $AKC$, $N$ lies on the segment $AC$, and $\angle AKN=\angle AKC-\angle NKC=\beta$.

Step 1. Triangles $ABK$ and $AKN$ are similar ($A\to A$, $B\to K$, $K\to N$): they have $\angle BAK=\angle KAN=\alpha$ and $\angle ABK=\angle AKN=\beta$. Hence \[\frac{BK}{KN}=\frac{AK}{AN}.\tag{1}\]

Step 2. Let $S$ be the point such that $AKSN$ is a parallelogram. Its diagonals bisect each other, so $L$ (the midpoint of $KN$) is also the midpoint of $AS$, and $\angle LAK=\angle SAK$. Moreover $KS=AN$ and $KS\parallel AN$.

Compare triangles $KBN$ and $KAS$. By (1), $\frac{KB}{KA}=\frac{KN}{AN}=\frac{KN}{KS}$. Since $K$ lies between $B$ and $C$, $\angle BKN=180^\circ-\angle NKC=180^\circ-\alpha$; and consecutive angles of the parallelogram give $\angle AKS=180^\circ-\angle KAN=180^\circ-\alpha$. So the triangles are similar (SAS), with $B\leftrightarrow A$, $N\leftrightarrow S$, and therefore \[\angle KBN=\angle KAS=\angle KAL. \qquad\blacksquare\]

Solution 2 (Complex numbers)

A length relation. Since $\angle NKC=\frac12\angle CAB=\angle KAC$ and the angle at $C$ is common, triangles $CKN$ and $CAK$ are similar, so $CN=\frac{CK^2}{CA}$ and $AN=CA-\frac{CK^2}{CA}$. The bisector-length formula $AK^2=AB\cdot AC-BK\cdot KC$ and the bisector theorem $\frac{BK}{AB}=\frac{CK}{CA}$ give \[\frac{AK^2}{AB}=AC-\frac{BK\cdot KC}{AB}=AC-\frac{CK^2}{CA}=AN,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad AN\cdot AB=AK^2 .\]

Coordinates. Put $A=0$ and let the bisector $AK$ be the positive real axis. With $\theta=\frac12\angle CAB$, $k=AK$, $m=AB$: \[K=k,\qquad B=m e^{-i\theta},\qquad N=\frac{k^2}{m}e^{i\theta},\qquad L=\frac{K+N}2 .\] Then \[\frac{N-B}{K-B}=\frac{\frac{k^2}{m}e^{i\theta}-me^{-i\theta}}{k-me^{-i\theta}} =\frac{k^2e^{2i\theta}-m^2}{m\,(ke^{i\theta}-m)}=\frac{ke^{i\theta}+m}{m},\] \[\frac{L}{K}=\frac{k+\frac{k^2}{m}e^{i\theta}}{2k}=\frac{m+ke^{i\theta}}{2m}.\] So $\dfrac{N-B}{K-B}=2\,\dfrac{L-A}{K-A}$; the two quotients differ by a positive real factor and have the same argument. The rotation carrying the ray $BK$ to the ray $BN$ is therefore the rotation carrying the ray $AK$ to the ray $AL$, and $\angle KBN=\angle KAL$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

The diagonals of a circumscribed quadrilateral $ABCD$ meet at point $X$. Prove that there exists a common tangent to the incircles of triangles $ABC$, $BCD$, and $AXD$.

Show the solution

Solution (Tangent lengths decide the existence of a common tangent)

Lemma. Let $\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3$ be circles with non-collinear centres $O_i$ and radii $r_i$, and choose signs $\varepsilon_i=\pm1$ (the side of the wanted line on which $\omega_i$ should lie). Suppose that for each pair $i,j$ there is some line touching $\omega_i,\omega_j$ with the circles on the sides $\varepsilon_i,\varepsilon_j$, and let $t_{ij}$ be the distance between its two points of tangency, so that $t_{ij}^2=O_iO_j^2-(\varepsilon_ir_i-\varepsilon_jr_j)^2$. If one of $t_{12},t_{13},t_{23}$ is the sum of the other two, then there is a line touching all three circles (with $\omega_i$ on side $\varepsilon_i$).

Proof. Write a line as $\{X:\ n\cdot X=c\}$ with $|n|=1$. It touches $\omega_i$ with $\omega_i$ on the prescribed side iff $n\cdot O_i-c=\rho_i$, where $\rho_i=\varepsilon_i r_i$. Put $u=O_2-O_1$, $v=O_3-O_1$, $\delta=\rho_2-\rho_1$, $\eta=\rho_3-\rho_1$. We need a vector $n$ with $n\cdot u=\delta$, $n\cdot v=\eta$, $|n|=1$ (then $c=n\cdot O_1-\rho_1$). As $u,v$ are independent, exactly one $n$ satisfies the two linear equations, and inverting the Gram matrix gives, with $G=|u|^2|v|^2-(u\cdot v)^2>0$, \[G\,|n|^2=\delta^2|v|^2-2\delta\eta\,(u\cdot v)+\eta^2|u|^2 .\] Hence $|n|=1$ iff $(|u|^2-\delta^2)(|v|^2-\eta^2)-(u\cdot v-\delta\eta)^2=0$ (expand both sides). Here $|u|^2-\delta^2=t_{12}^2$, $|v|^2-\eta^2=t_{13}^2$ and $|u-v|^2-(\delta-\eta)^2=t_{23}^2$, so $u\cdot v-\delta\eta=\frac12(t_{12}^2+t_{13}^2-t_{23}^2)$ and the condition becomes \[4t_{12}^2t_{13}^2=(t_{12}^2+t_{13}^2-t_{23}^2)^2,\] which factors as \[(t_{12}+t_{13}+t_{23})(t_{12}+t_{13}-t_{23})(t_{12}-t_{13}+t_{23})(-t_{12}+t_{13}+t_{23})=0,\] which holds when one length is the sum of the other two. $\square$

The three circles. Let $\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3$ be the incircles of $ABC$, $BCD$, $AXD$ and let them touch the lines as follows: $\omega_1$ touches $BC$ at $E_1$ and $AC$ at $K$; $\omega_2$ touches $BC$ at $E_2$ and $BD$ at $L$; $\omega_3$ touches $AC$ at $K_3$ and $BD$ at $L_3$. We use the signs $\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2=+1$, $\varepsilon_3=-1$:

  • $BC$ touches $\omega_1,\omega_2$, which lie on the same side of it (the side of $A$ and $D$), so $t_{12}=E_1E_2$;

  • $AC$ touches $\omega_1$ (inside $ABC$) and $\omega_3$ (inside $AXD\subset ACD$), which lie on opposite sides, so $t_{13}=KK_3$;

  • $BD$ touches $\omega_2$ (inside $BCD$) and $\omega_3$ (inside $ABD$), on opposite sides, so $t_{23}=LL_3$.

Computing the lengths. Recall that the incircle of a triangle $UVW$ touches $VW$ at distance $\frac12(UV+VW-UW)$ from $V$.

  • $CE_1=\frac12(CA+CB-AB)$ and $CE_2=\frac12(CB+CD-BD)$, so $E_1E_2=\frac12(AC+BD-AB-CD)$; this is positive because $AC+BD=(XA+XB)+(XC+XD)>AB+CD$.

  • $AK=\frac12(AB+AC-BC)$ and $AK_3=\frac12(AX+AD-XD)$. By Pitot's theorem $AB+CD=BC+AD$, so $AK-AK_3=\frac12(AB-BC-AD+XC+XD)=\frac12(XC+XD-CD)>0$, and $KK_3=\frac12(XC+XD-CD)$.

  • $DL=\frac12(DB+DC-BC)$ and $DL_3=\frac12(DX+DA-AX)$; again by Pitot, $LL_3=DL-DL_3=\frac12(XA+XB-AB)$.

Therefore \[t_{13}+t_{23}=\tfrac12\bigl(XA+XB+XC+XD-AB-CD\bigr)=\tfrac12(AC+BD-AB-CD)=t_{12}.\]

Conclusion. If the incentres $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are not collinear, the lemma gives a line touching all three incircles (with $\omega_1,\omega_2$ on one side and $\omega_3$ on the other).

If $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are collinear, we pass to a limit. Scale the figure so that the incircle of $ABCD$ is the unit circle. A circumscribed quadrilateral with this incircle is determined by its four points of tangency, i.e. by angles $\theta_1<\theta_2<\theta_3<\theta_4<\theta_1+2\pi$ whose consecutive differences (including $\theta_1+2\pi-\theta_4$) are less than $\pi$; these parameters form a convex, hence connected, open set. The vertices, the point $X$, the side lengths and therefore the incentres $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are real-analytic functions of the parameters, and so is the determinant $\delta$ that expresses the collinearity of $I_1,I_2,I_3$. It is not identically zero: for the square $A=(0,1)$, $B=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $D=(1,1)$ (up to scaling) the incentres are $(1-\frac1{\sqrt2},1-\frac1{\sqrt2})$, $(\frac1{\sqrt2},1-\frac1{\sqrt2})$ and $(\frac12,\frac32-\frac1{\sqrt2})$; the first two lie on a horizontal line that misses the third, so they are not collinear. A real-analytic function on a connected open set that vanishes on a nonempty open subset vanishes identically, so the zeros of $\delta$ have no interior points, and our quadrilateral is the limit of circumscribed quadrilaterals $Q_k$ for which the incentres are not collinear. For each $Q_k$ the argument above gives a line $\{X:\ n_k\cdot X=c_k\}$, $|n_k|=1$, with $n_k\cdot O_i^{(k)}-c_k=\rho_i^{(k)}$ for $i=1,2,3$ (the same signs $\varepsilon_i$, since the configuration is the same). The numbers $c_k$ are bounded, so a subsequence of $(n_k,c_k)$ converges to some $(n,c)$ with $|n|=1$, and in the limit $n\cdot O_i-c=\rho_i$ for $i=1,2,3$: the line $n\cdot X=c$ touches all three incircles. $\blacksquare$

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios

Let $I$ be the incenter of a triangle $ABC$. The perpendicular bisector to $AI$ meets $BC$ at point $D$; the line $AD$ meets for the second time the circumcircle of $ABC$ at point $X$. Prove that $|BX-CX|=AX$.

Show the solution

Solution (Menelaus, the ratio lemma and Ptolemy)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths and assume $b\ne c$ (if $b=c$, the perpendicular bisector of $AI$ is parallel to $BC$ and $D$ does not exist).

Where the perpendicular bisector cuts the sides. Let the perpendicular bisector $\ell$ of $AI$ meet $AB$ and $AC$ at $P_c$ and $P_b$. Since $P_cA=P_cI$, we get $\angle P_cIA=\angle P_cAI=\angle IAC$, so $IP_c\parallel AC$; similarly $IP_b\parallel AB$. The line through $I$ parallel to $AC$ consists of points at distance $r$ (the inradius) from $AC$, while $B$ is at distance $h_b=\frac{2\Delta}{b}$; hence \[AP_c=c\cdot\frac{r}{h_b}=\frac{bc\,r}{2\Delta}=\frac{bc}{a+b+c}\qquad(\Delta=rs),\] and in the same way $AP_b=\frac{bc}{a+b+c}$. Consequently $P_cB=\frac{c(a+c)}{a+b+c}$ and $P_bC=\frac{b(a+b)}{a+b+c}$; both points are inside the sides.

The point $D$. Menelaus' theorem for triangle $ABC$ and the line $\ell$ gives \[\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{BP_c}{P_cA}\cdot\frac{AP_b}{P_bC}=\frac{a+c}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{a+b}=\frac{c(a+c)}{b(a+b)},\] and since $\ell$ meets the sides $AB$, $AC$ at interior points, it meets the line $BC$ outside the segment $BC$. So $D$ lies outside the circumcircle.

The ratio $BX:CX$. From $DB\cdot DC=DA\cdot DX$ and the common angle at $D$, triangles $DAB\sim DCX$ and $DAC\sim DBX$, so $\frac{AB}{CX}=\frac{DA}{DC}$ and $\frac{AC}{BX}=\frac{DA}{DB}$. Dividing, $\frac{AB\cdot BX}{AC\cdot CX}=\frac{DB}{DC}$, hence \[\frac{BX}{CX}=\frac bc\cdot\frac{c(a+c)}{b(a+b)}=\frac{a+c}{a+b};\qquad\text{write } BX=k(a+c),\ CX=k(a+b).\]

Ptolemy. The line $AX$ does not separate $B$ and $C$ (it meets $BC$ outside the segment), so $X$ lies on the arc $AB$ not containing $C$ or on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$. In the first case $AXBC$ is a cyclic quadrilateral and Ptolemy gives $AB\cdot XC=AX\cdot BC+XB\cdot CA$, i.e. \[a\cdot AX=c\,k(a+b)-b\,k(a+c)=k\,a(c-b);\] in the second case $AXCB$ is cyclic and $AC\cdot XB=AX\cdot BC+XC\cdot AB$ gives $a\cdot AX=k\,a(b-c)$. In both cases \[AX=k\,|b-c|=|k(a+c)-k(a+b)|=|BX-CX|.\qquad\blacksquare\]

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $O$ be the circumcenter of a triangle $ABC$, $I$ be its incenter, $H$ be the orthocenter, and $N$ be the Nagel point. Prove that $IN=IH$ if and only if $ONH$ is a right angle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Put the origin at $O$ and denote position vectors by the point names. Then $H=A+B+C=3G$ ($G$ the centroid). The Nagel point satisfies $N=3G-2I$: in barycentric coordinates $N=\frac{(s-a)A+(s-b)B+(s-c)C}{s}$ and $I=\frac{aA+bB+cC}{2s}$, and $3G-2I=\bigl(1-\frac as\bigr)A+\bigl(1-\frac bs\bigr)B+\bigl(1-\frac cs\bigr)C=N$. Hence \[N=H-2I .\] Now \[IN^2-IH^2=|H-3I|^2-|H-I|^2=-4\,H\cdot I+8|I|^2,\] \[\overrightarrow{NO}\cdot\overrightarrow{NH}=(-N)\cdot(H-N)=-(H-2I)\cdot 2I=-2\,H\cdot I+4|I|^2 .\] So $IN^2-IH^2=2\,\overrightarrow{NO}\cdot\overrightarrow{NH}$. Therefore $IN=IH$ iff $\overrightarrow{NO}\cdot\overrightarrow{NH}=0$, i.e. iff $\angle ONH=90^\circ$. (We assume the triangle is not equilateral; otherwise $O=H=N=I$ and the angle is not defined. Then also $N\ne H$, as $N=H-2I$ and $I\ne O$, and $N\ne O$: $N=O$ would mean $H=2I$, i.e. $I$ is the nine-point centre $\frac{O+H}2$; by Feuerbach's theorem the distance between these centres is $\frac R2-r$, so $R=2r$, and Euler's formula $OI^2=R^2-2Rr=0$ makes the triangle equilateral. So the angle $ONH$ is always defined.) $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (A homothety and two projections)

Let $G$ be the centroid and $h$ the homothety with centre $G$ and ratio $-2$. It maps $O$ to $H$ (Euler line, $GH=2\,GO$) and $I$ to the Nagel point $N$ ($I,G,N$ are collinear with $GN=2\,GI$: the Nagel line). Hence \[\overrightarrow{NH}=h(O)-h(I)=-2\,(O-I)=2\,\overrightarrow{OI},\qquad\text{so } OI\parallel NH\ \text{ and }\ \overrightarrow{OI}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{NH}.\] (If the triangle is equilateral, all these points coincide and the angle is undefined; we exclude this case. Then $I\ne O$, so $N\ne H$. Also $N\ne O$: otherwise $\overrightarrow{OI}=\frac12\overrightarrow{OH}$, i.e. $I$ is the nine-point centre (the midpoint of $OH$); by Feuerbach's theorem the distance between these centres is $\frac R2-r$, so $R=2r$, and Euler's formula $OI^2=R^2-2Rr=0$ makes the triangle equilateral. So the angle $ONH$ is defined.)

Project orthogonally onto the line $NH$; write $X^*$ for the projection of $X$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of $NH$. Because $OI\parallel NH$, projection does not change the vector $\overrightarrow{OI}$, so \[I^*=O^*+\tfrac12\overrightarrow{NH}.\] Now

  • $\angle ONH=90^\circ$ iff $ON\perp NH$ iff $O^*=N$;

  • $IN=IH$ iff $I$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $NH$ iff $I^*=M$.

If $O^*=N$ then $I^*=N+\frac12\overrightarrow{NH}=M$; conversely, if $I^*=M$ then $O^*=M-\frac12\overrightarrow{NH}=N$. So the two conditions are equivalent. $\blacksquare$

Problem 7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

The side $AB$ of a triangle $ABC$ touches the incircle and the excircle at points $P$ and $Q$ respectively. Let $T$ be the projection of the midpoint of $AB$ to the bisector of angle $C$. Prove that $C$, $P$, $Q$, $T$ are concyclic.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of the foot of the bisector)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, assume $a\ne b$ (otherwise $P=Q$ and the statement degenerates), let $I$, $I_c$ be the incentre and the excentre opposite $C$, and let the bisector of angle $C$ meet $AB$ at $L$. Both $I$ and $I_c$ lie on this bisector, on different sides of $AB$. Since $AP=s-a=BQ$, the midpoint $M$ of $AB$ is also the midpoint of $PQ$.

A harmonic relation. In triangle $ACL$ the internal and external bisectors at $A$ meet $CL$ at $I$ and $I_c$, so $\frac{CI}{IL}=\frac{CA}{AL}=\frac{CI_c}{I_cL}$. Use a coordinate on the line $CL$ with $L=0$: $C=p>0$, $I=i\in(0,p)$, $I_c=j<0$. The equality reads $\frac{p-i}{i}=\frac{p-j}{-j}$, i.e. \[2ij=p\,(i+j).\tag{1}\]

Projections. Let $\varphi$ be the angle between the lines $CL$ and $AB$, and orient $AB$ so that the unit vectors $w$ (along $LC$) and $u$ (along $AB$) satisfy $u\cdot w=\cos\varphi$. Since $P,Q$ are the feet of $I,I_c$ on $AB$, their coordinates on $AB$ (origin $L$) are $i\cos\varphi$ and $j\cos\varphi$; so $M$ has coordinate $\frac{i+j}2\cos\varphi$, and its projection $T$ on $CL$ has coordinate $\frac{i+j}2\cos^2\varphi$ on $CL$.

Power of $L$. With signed lengths, \[LP\cdot LQ=ij\cos^2\varphi,\qquad LC\cdot LT=p\cdot\frac{i+j}2\cos^2\varphi=ij\cos^2\varphi\quad\text{by (1)}.\] The equality $LP\cdot LQ=LC\cdot LT$ for two different lines through $L$ is the converse of the intersecting-chords theorem, so $C,P,Q,T$ are concyclic. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Explicit lengths)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$ ($a\ne b$), $\gamma=\frac12\angle C$, and let the bisector of $C$ meet $AB$ at $L$. It suffices to check $LP\cdot LQ=LC\cdot LT$ with signed lengths, by the converse of the intersecting-chords theorem.

On the bisector. Measure from $C$ along the bisector. The classical length is $CL=\frac{2ab\cos\gamma}{a+b}$. The point $T$ is the projection of the midpoint $M$ of $AB$, and $\overrightarrow{CM}=\frac12(\overrightarrow{CA}+\overrightarrow{CB})$ projects to $\frac12(b\cos\gamma+a\cos\gamma)$, so $CT=\frac{(a+b)\cos\gamma}2\ge CL$ (as $(a+b)^2\ge4ab$). Hence $L$ lies between $C$ and $T$ and \[LC\cdot LT=-\frac{2ab\cos\gamma}{a+b}\Bigl(\frac{(a+b)\cos\gamma}2-\frac{2ab\cos\gamma}{a+b}\Bigr)=-\frac{ab\,(a-b)^2\cos^2\gamma}{(a+b)^2}.\]

On $AB$. Measure from $L$ towards $A$: $LA=\frac{bc}{a+b}$, $AP=s-a$, $AQ=s-b$. So the signed distances of $P$ and $Q$ from $L$ are \[\frac{bc}{a+b}-(s-a)=\frac{(a-b)(a+b-c)}{2(a+b)},\qquad \frac{bc}{a+b}-(s-b)=\frac{(b-a)(a+b+c)}{2(a+b)},\] and their product is $-\frac{(a-b)^2\bigl((a+b)^2-c^2\bigr)}{4(a+b)^2}$. By the law of cosines $(a+b)^2-c^2=2ab(1+\cos C)=4ab\cos^2\gamma$, so \[LP\cdot LQ=-\frac{ab\,(a-b)^2\cos^2\gamma}{(a+b)^2}=LC\cdot LT .\] Hence $C,P,Q,T$ lie on one circle. $\blacksquare$

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle B=30^\circ$, $O$ be the circumcenter of $ABC$, $I$ be its incenter. The circles $AIB$, $CIB$ meet $BC$, $AB$ respectively at points $D$, $E$. Prove that $D$ is the orthocenter of triangle $OEI$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Directions of the lines)

Write $\alpha=\angle A$, $\gamma=\angle C$, so $\alpha+\gamma=150^\circ$; let $R$ be the circumradius.

The points $D$ and $E$. The centre of the circle $AIB$ is the midpoint of the arc $AB$, which lies on the bisector $CI$; so this circle is symmetric in the line $CI$. The reflection in $CI$ swaps the lines $CA$ and $CB$, hence maps $A$ to the point of line $CB$ at distance $CA$ from $C$, which is on the circle; this is $D$. (If $CA=CB$, i.e. $\alpha=30^\circ$, the circle $AIB$ touches $BC$ at $B$; then $D=B$, which is again the reflection of $A$ in $CI$, and the argument below is unchanged. The same holds for $E$ when $\gamma=30^\circ$.) So $D$ is the reflection of $A$ in $CI$; likewise $E$ is the reflection of $C$ in $AI$. Since $AC=2R\sin30^\circ=R$, we get \[CD=CA=R=CO,\qquad AE=AC=R=AO .\]

Directions. Put $B$ at the origin, $C$ on the positive $x$-axis and $A$ above it, and describe a line by its direction angle, measured counterclockwise from the ray $BC$ and taken modulo $180^\circ$. Reflecting a line of direction $\theta$ in a line of direction $\varphi$ gives direction $2\varphi-\theta$.

  • $CI$: the ray $CI$ makes the angle $\frac\gamma2$ with $CB$, so its direction is $180^\circ-\frac\gamma2$.

  • $AI$: the rays $AB$ and $AC$ have directions $210^\circ$ and $360^\circ-\gamma$ (they form the angle $150^\circ-\gamma=\alpha$), so the bisector has direction $210^\circ+\frac\alpha2\equiv 105^\circ-\frac\gamma2$.

  • $EI$: $I$ lies on $AI$ and $E$ is the reflection of $C$, so $EI$ is the reflection of $CI$ in $AI$; its direction is $2(105^\circ-\frac\gamma2)-(180^\circ-\frac\gamma2)=30^\circ-\frac\gamma2$.

  • $OD$: the ray $CD$ is the ray $CB$ (direction $180^\circ$), and the ray $CO$ has direction $90^\circ+\alpha$ (as $\angle OCB=|90^\circ-\alpha|$, with $O$ on the side of $A$ exactly when $\alpha<90^\circ$). In the isosceles triangle $OCD$ ($CO=CD$) the base $OD$ is perpendicular to the bisector of the angle $OCD$, whose direction is $135^\circ+\frac\alpha2$. So $OD$ has direction $45^\circ+\frac\alpha2=120^\circ-\frac\gamma2$.

The directions of $OD$ and $EI$ differ by $90^\circ$, so $OD\perp EI$.

The second altitude. The whole configuration is symmetric under exchanging the roles of $A$ and $C$ (then $D$ and $E$ are exchanged), and the argument above used only $\angle B=30^\circ$. Applied to the mirror image it gives $OE\perp DI$.

So $D$ lies on the altitude of triangle $OEI$ from $O$ and on the altitude from $I$, i.e. $D$ is its orthocentre. (When $\alpha=90^\circ$ we get $D=O$, and the two relations say that the angle $EOI$ is right, which is the same statement. If $\gamma=90^\circ$ then $E=O$ and the triangle $OEI$ degenerates; this case is excluded.) $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the bisector of angle B)

Write $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $k=\cos\frac{\angle B}2=\cos15^\circ$, $h=\sin\frac{\angle B}2=\sin15^\circ$. Put $B$ at the origin with the bisector $BI$ along the positive $x$-axis, $A$ on the ray of direction $(k,h)$ and $C$ on the ray of direction $(k,-h)$: \[A=c\,(k,h),\qquad C=a\,(k,-h),\qquad I=(i,0),\quad i=BI .\]

Two facts about $i$. The inradius is the distance from $I$ to the line $BA$, i.e. $r=i\sin\frac{\angle B}2=ih$. The area of $ABC$ is $\frac12ac\sin\angle B=ackh$ and also $\frac12r(a+b+c)$, so \[i=\frac{2ack}{a+b+c},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad b=\frac{2ack}{i}-a-c. \tag{1}\] By the law of cosines and $1+\cos\angle B=2k^2$, $b^2=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos\angle B=(a+c)^2-4ack^2$. Substituting (1), \[\frac{4a^2c^2k^2}{i^2}-\frac{4ack(a+c)}{i}+4ack^2=0,\qquad\text{and after multiplying by }\frac{i^2}{4ack}:\qquad F:=ack-(a+c)i+ki^2=0. \tag{2}\]

The circumcentre. $OB=OA$ and $OB=OC$ with $B$ at the origin mean $O\cdot A=\frac12|A|^2$ and $O\cdot C=\frac12|C|^2$, i.e. $kO_x+hO_y=\frac c2$ and $kO_x-hO_y=\frac a2$. Hence \[O=\Bigl(\frac{a+c}{4k},\ \frac{c-a}{4h}\Bigr).\]

The points $D$ and $E$. A circle through the origin $B$ has an equation $x^2+y^2=\lambda x+\mu y$, and it passes through $I$ iff $\lambda=i$. The points of the line $BC$ are $t(k,-h)$; substituting gives $t^2=t(ik-\mu h)$, so the second common point with $BC$ has parameter $t=ik-\mu h$. Similarly the second common point with the line $BA$ (points $t(k,h)$) has $t=ik+\mu h$. For the circle $AIB$ the point $A$ ($t=c$ on $BA$) gives $\mu h=c-ik$, so $D=(ik-\mu h)(k,-h)$, and for the circle $CIB$ the point $C$ ($t=a$ on $BC$) gives $\mu h=ik-a$, so $E=(ik+\mu h)(k,h)$: \[D=(2ik-c)\,(k,-h),\qquad E=(2ik-a)\,(k,h).\]

The altitude from $E$. We have $D-E=\bigl((a-c)k,\ -(4ik-a-c)h\bigr)$ and $I-O=\bigl(i-\frac{a+c}{4k},\ \frac{a-c}{4h}\bigr)$, so \[(D-E)\cdot(I-O)=(a-c)ki-\frac{(a-c)(a+c)}4-\frac{(a-c)(4ik-a-c)}4=0 .\] So $D$ lies on the line through $E$ perpendicular to $OI$, the altitude of triangle $OEI$ from $E$. (This part holds for every angle $B$.)

The altitude from $I$. Now $D-I=\bigl(2ik^2-ck-i,\ -(2ik-c)h\bigr)$ and $4k(E-O)=\bigl(8ik^3-4ak^2-a-c,\ 4kh(2ik-a)-\frac{(c-a)k}h\bigr)$, so \[4k\,(D-I)\cdot(E-O)=(2ik^2-ck-i)(8ik^3-4ak^2-a-c)-4kh^2(2ik-c)(2ik-a)+k(c-a)(2ik-c).\] Replacing $h^2$ by $1-k^2$ and expanding, this equals \[8ack^3-2ack+(a+c)i\,(1+8k^2-16k^4)+i^2(32k^5-24k^3),\] which can be regrouped (expand to check) as \[4k\,(D-I)\cdot(E-O)=8k^2(4k^2-3)\,F+(16k^4-16k^2+1)\bigl((a+c)i-2ack\bigr).\] Here $F=0$ by (2), and $16k^4-16k^2+1=1-16k^2h^2=1-4\sin^2\angle B=0$ because $\angle B=30^\circ$ (we used $2kh=\sin\angle B$). Hence $(D-I)\cdot(E-O)=0$: $D$ lies on the line through $I$ perpendicular to $OE$, the altitude of triangle $OEI$ from $I$.

Conclusion. The two altitudes from $E$ and from $I$ of the (non-degenerate) triangle $OEI$ meet at its orthocentre, and $D$ lies on both, so $D$ is the orthocentre of $OEI$. (If $\angle C=90^\circ$, then $E=O$ and the triangle degenerates; this case is excluded, as in the statement.) $\blacksquare$

Problem 9

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $ABCD$ be a circumscribed quadrilateral with incenter $I$. The circles $BID$ and $AIC$ meet at point $P$, and the rays $AB$ and $DC$ meet at point $Q$. Let $R$ be the midpoint of $PI$. Prove that the quadrilateral $ARQD$ is cyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (Inversion in the incircle)

Let the incircle (centre $I$, radius $1$) touch $AB$, $BC$, $CD$, $DA$ at $K$, $L$, $M$, $N$; put the origin at $I$, so $K,L,M,N$ are unit vectors. Let $X\mapsto X^*$ be the inversion in the incircle.

Images of the vertices. The tangents from $A$ touch at $K$ and $N$, and the chord $KN$ is perpendicular to $IA$ at its midpoint $A_0=\frac{K+N}2$; from the right triangle $IKA$ we get $IA_0\cdot IA=IK^2=1$, so $A^*=\frac{K+N}2$. Similarly \[A^*=\tfrac{K+N}2,\quad B^*=\tfrac{K+L}2,\quad C^*=\tfrac{L+M}2,\quad D^*=\tfrac{M+N}2 .\]

The point $P$. The circles $BID$ and $AIC$ pass through the centre of inversion, so they become the lines $B^*D^*$ and $A^*C^*$, and $P^*$ is their common point. $A^*B^*C^*D^*$ is the Varignon parallelogram of $KLMN$, so its diagonals $A^*C^*$ and $B^*D^*$ meet at their common midpoint: $P^*=\frac{K+L+M+N}4$. Since $R$ lies on the ray $IP$ with $IR=\frac12IP$, its image lies on the same ray with $IR^*=2\,IP^*$, so \[R^*=2P^*=\tfrac{K+L+M+N}2 .\]

The point $Q$. The line $AB$ touches the incircle at $K$, so it becomes the circle with diameter $IK$; likewise $CD$ becomes the circle with diameter $IM$. Their second common point is the point $F$ with $\angle IFK=\angle IFM=90^\circ$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $I$ to $KM$, which is the midpoint of the chord $KM$. So $Q^*=\frac{K+M}2$.

Concyclicity. The points $A^*=\frac{K+N}2$, $D^*=\frac{M+N}2$, $Q^*=\frac{K+M}2$ are the midpoints of the sides of triangle $KMN$, whose circumcentre is $I$ (origin). They lie on its nine-point circle, with centre $\frac{K+M+N}2$ and radius $\frac12$. And \[\Bigl|R^*-\tfrac{K+M+N}2\Bigr|=\Bigl|\tfrac L2\Bigr|=\tfrac12,\] so $R^*$ lies on the same circle. This circle does not pass through $I$: that would mean $|K+M+N|=1$, i.e. the orthocentre of $KMN$ lies on its circumcircle, i.e. $KMN$ has a right angle; since $K,N$ and $M,N$ are never antipodal, this forces $K,M$ antipodal, i.e. $AB\parallel CD$, when $Q$ does not exist. Inverting back, $A$, $R$, $Q$, $D$ lie on one circle. $\blacksquare$

Problem 10

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 43: Locus, Transformation, and Construction Geometry

A circle $\omega$, a point $A$ on it, and a point $B$ are given. Let $X$ be an arbitrary point of $\omega$, and $T$ be the common point of tangents to the circle $ABX$ at $X$ and $B$. Find the locus of points $T$.

Show the answer

Let $O$ be the centre of $\omega$, let $X_0$ be the second common point of the line $AB$ with $\omega$ ($X_0=A$ if $AB$ touches $\omega$), and let $X_0^*$ be the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite $X_0$. If $B\notin\omega$, the locus is the line through the midpoint $M_0$ of $BX_0$ perpendicular to $BX_0^*$, except for the points that would come from the forbidden positions $X=X_0$ and $X=A$: the point $M_0$, and the centre $S$ of the circle through $B$ tangent to the line $OA$ at $A$ (there is no such point if $B$ lies on the line $OA$, and $S=M_0$ if $AB$ touches $\omega$). If $B\in\omega$, the locus is the tangent to $\omega$ at $B$ without $B$ and without the common point of the tangents at $A$ and $B$ (if they meet).

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Orthogonal circles)

Let $O$ be the centre of $\omega$. The case $B\in\omega$ is easy: then the circle $ABX$ is $\omega$ itself, $T$ is the common point of the tangents to $\omega$ at $B$ and $X$, and as $X$ runs over $\omega\setminus\{A,B\}$ it covers the tangent at $B$ except $B$ and except the point coming from $X=A$. From now on $B\notin\omega$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

The fixed point $A_1$. Let $\sigma$ be the circle through $B$ tangent to the line $OA$ at $A$ (if $B\in OA$, let $\sigma$ be the line $OA$). It crosses $\omega$ at $A$ (at a right angle), so it meets $\omega$ at a second point $A_1$, and $A_1\ne B$ since $B\notin\omega$.

Key relation. For $X\in\omega$, $X\ne A,A_1$: \[\measuredangle(A_1B,A_1X)-\measuredangle(AB,AX)=90^\circ.\tag{1}\] Indeed, $\measuredangle(A_1B,A_1X)=\measuredangle(A_1B,A_1A)+\measuredangle(A_1A,A_1X)$. By the tangent-chord theorem in $\sigma$ (tangent $AO$ at $A$), $\measuredangle(A_1B,A_1A)=\measuredangle(AB,AO)$, and in $\omega$ (tangent $\tau\perp AO$ at $A$), $\measuredangle(A_1A,A_1X)=\measuredangle(\tau,AX)$. So the sum is $\measuredangle(AB,AO)+\measuredangle(AO,AX)+\measuredangle(\tau,AO)=\measuredangle(AB,AX)+90^\circ$.

$T$ is equidistant from $B$ and $A_1$. Let $X\ne A$, $A,B,X$ not collinear, and let $\Gamma$ be the circle $BXA_1$ (for $X\ne A_1$). The tangent $t$ to the circle $ABX$ at $B$ satisfies $\measuredangle(t,BX)=\measuredangle(AB,AX)$, and the tangent $t'$ to $\Gamma$ at $B$ satisfies $\measuredangle(t',BX)=\measuredangle(A_1B,A_1X)$. By (1), $t\perp t'$: the circles $ABX$ and $\Gamma$ are orthogonal (at $B$, hence also at $X$). Therefore the tangents to the circle $ABX$ at $B$ and at $X$ are radii of $\Gamma$, and they meet at its centre: $T$ is the centre of $\Gamma$, so $TB=TA_1$. If $B,X,A_1$ are collinear, the same computation shows that both tangents are perpendicular to that line, so they are parallel and there is no point $T$. For $X=A_1$ the circle $ABX$ is $\sigma$, and $TB=TA_1$ because tangent segments from $T$ to $\sigma$ are equal.

So every point $T$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $\ell$ of $BA_1$.

Which points of $\ell$ occur. Let $T\in\ell$ and let $\Gamma$ be the circle with centre $T$ through $B$ and $A_1$; let $X$ be its second common point with $\omega$ ($X=A_1$ if $\Gamma$ touches $\omega$).

  • If $X\ne A_1$, $X\ne A$ and $A,B,X$ are not collinear, then by (1) the circle $ABX$ is orthogonal to $\Gamma$, so its tangents at $B$ and $X$ pass through $T$: the point $T$ is obtained from $X$.

  • If $X=A_1$, then $T$ lies on the line $OA_1$. As $\sigma$ is orthogonal to $\omega$ at both common points, the tangent to $\sigma$ at $A_1$ is $A_1O$, and $T$ is the point of it with $TA_1=TB$, i.e. the common point of the tangents to $\sigma$ at $A_1$ and $B$; again $T$ is obtained (from $X=A_1$).

  • If $X=A$, then $TA=TB=TA_1$ and $T$ is the centre $S$ of $\sigma$.

  • If $X=X_0$ (the second point of the line $AB$ on $\omega$), then (1) gives $\angle BA_1X_0=90^\circ$, so $T$ is the midpoint $M_0$ of $BX_0$.

A point $T$ obtained from some $X'$ is the centre of a circle through $B,X',A_1$, so $X'$ is the second common point $X$ above. Hence $S$ and $M_0$ are not obtained (they would require $X'=A$ or $X'=X_0$, which are excluded), and every other point of $\ell$ is. (If $B$ lies on the line $OA$, then $\sigma$ is that line, $X=A$ gives no finite point $T$, and only $M_0$ is missing. If $AB$ touches $\omega$, then $X_0=A$ and $S=M_0$.)

Finally, $\angle BA_1X_0=90^\circ$ and $\angle X_0A_1X_0^*=90^\circ$ show that $A_1$ lies on the line $BX_0^*$; so $\ell$ is the line through $M_0$ perpendicular to $BX_0^*$. Answer: the locus is this line without the points $M_0$ and $S$ (a single point if $S=M_0$; only $M_0$ if $S$ does not exist).

Solution 2 (Inversion at B)

Let $O$ be the centre of $\omega$ and assume $B\notin\omega$ (if $B\in\omega$, the circle $ABX$ is $\omega$ and $T$ runs over the tangent at $B$, without $B$ and without the point given by $X=A$). Invert about $B$ (any radius $k$); denote images by primes. Then $\omega'$ is a circle not through $B$, containing $A'$ and $X'$.

The circle $ABX$ becomes the line $A'X'$; its tangent at $B$ becomes the line $m$ through $B$ parallel to $A'X'$; its tangent at $X$ becomes the circle $\kappa$ through $B$ tangent to the line $A'X'$ at $X'$. So $T'$ is the second common point of $m$ and $\kappa$. Let $n$ be the line through $X'$ perpendicular to $A'X'$. Both $\kappa$ and $m$ are symmetric in $n$, hence $T'$ is the reflection of $B$ in $n$.

Let $A''$ be the point of $\omega'$ opposite to $A'$. Since $\angle A'X'A''=90^\circ$, the line $n$ is the line $X'A''$ (for $X'=A''$ it is the tangent at $A''$). Hence $T'$ is the reflection of $B$ in a line through the fixed point $A''$, and $T'$ lies on the circle $\Gamma'$ with centre $A''$ through $B$. Conversely, every line through $A''$ is $X'A''$ for exactly one $X'\in\omega'$, and every point of $\Gamma'$ is the reflection of $B$ in exactly one line through $A''$. The excluded positions are $X'=A'$ (that is $X=A$), $X'$ on the line $BA'$ (that is $X=X_0$, the second point of $AB$ on $\omega$), and the point $T'=B$ (the tangents are parallel). So $T'$ runs over $\Gamma'$ minus $B$ and minus the two points given by $X=A$ and $X=X_0$.

Invert back. $\Gamma'$ passes through $B$, so its image is a line $\ell$, perpendicular to $BA''$, at distance $\frac{k^2}{2\,BA''}$ from $B$: it is the perpendicular bisector of $BA_1$, where $A_1$ is the inverse of $A''$ ($BA_1=\frac{k^2}{BA''}$). Since $A''\in\omega'$, $A_1\in\omega$. The line $A'A''$ passes through the centre of $\omega'$, so it is orthogonal to $\omega'$; its inverse is a circle through $B$, $A$, $A_1$ orthogonal to $\omega$, i.e. tangent to $OA$ at $A$. So $A_1$ is the second common point of $\omega$ with the circle $\sigma$ through $B$ tangent to $OA$ at $A$.

The two excluded points of $\ell$ are the limits for $X=A$ (the circle $ABX$ becomes the circle through $B$ tangent to $\omega$ at $A$, and $T$ the point of the tangent at $A$ equidistant from $A$ and $B$, which is the centre $S$ of $\sigma$) and for $X=X_0$ (the circle degenerates to the line $AB$ and $T$ becomes the midpoint $M_0$ of $BX_0$). Both lie on $\ell$, so $M_0$ is the midpoint of the hypotenuse of the right triangle $BA_1X_0$, i.e. $\angle BA_1X_0=90^\circ$, and $A_1$ lies on the line $BX_0^*$ ($X_0^*$ opposite to $X_0$ on $\omega$).

Answer: the perpendicular bisector of $BA_1$, which is the line through the midpoint of $BX_0$ perpendicular to $BX_0^*$, without the midpoint of $BX_0$ and the centre of $\sigma$ (if $B$ lies on the line $OA$, then $\sigma$ is a line and only the midpoint of $BX_0$ is missing; if $AB$ touches $\omega$, the two excluded points coincide).

Problem 11

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $P$ and $Q$ lie on the side $AC$ of a triangle $ABC$, in the order $A$, $P$, $Q$, $C$, in such a way that $PQ=AC/2$. The point $B'$ is the reflection of $B$ about $AC$. Let $D$ and $E$ be the points on the lines $BP$ and $BQ$ such that the lines $AD$ and $CE$ touch the circles $APB'$ and $CQB'$ respectively. Prove that the circumcircle of triangle $BDE$ touches $AC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of a point)

The points are in the order $A,P,Q,C$, so $\overrightarrow{PQ}=\frac12\overrightarrow{AC}$. Let $Y$ be the reflection of $A$ in $P$; then $Y=2P-A=2Q-C$ is also the reflection of $C$ in $Q$, and $PY=PA$, $QY=QC$.

Step 1: $PA^2=PD\cdot PB$. We use directed angles modulo $180^\circ$. Since $AD$ touches the circle $APB'$ at $A$, $\measuredangle(AD,AP)=\measuredangle(B'A,B'P)$. Reflection in $AC$ reverses directed angles and maps $B'$ to $B$, so $\measuredangle(B'A,B'P)=\measuredangle(BP,BA)=\measuredangle(BD,BA)$. Hence $\measuredangle(AP,AD)=\measuredangle(BA,BD)$, which says that $AP$ touches the circle $ABD$ at $A$. The power of $P$ with respect to this circle gives $PA^2=PD\cdot PB$ (in particular $D$ lies on the ray $PB$). In the same way $QC^2=QE\cdot QB$.

Step 2. Suppose first that $D\ne B$. Consider the circle through $B$, $D$, $Y$ (these points are not collinear, since $Y\ne P$ lies on $AC$). The line $PB$ meets it at $B$ and $D$, so the power of $P$ is $PD\cdot PB=PA^2=PY^2$. The line $PY$ meets the circle at $Y$ and at a second point $Y_1$ with $PY\cdot PY_1=PY^2$, so $Y_1=Y$: the circle $BDY$ touches $AC$ at $Y$. Likewise, if $E\ne B$, then $QE\cdot QB=QC^2=QY^2$ shows that the circle $BEY$ touches $AC$ at $Y$.

Step 3. There is only one circle through $B$ touching the line $AC$ at $Y$ (its centre is the common point of the perpendicular to $AC$ at $Y$ and the perpendicular bisector of $BY$); call it $\Gamma$. By Step 2, $D\in\Gamma$ if $D\ne B$ and $E\in\Gamma$ if $E\ne B$, and of course $B\in\Gamma$. So $B,D,E$ all lie on $\Gamma$, and the circle $BDE$ is $\Gamma$, which touches $AC$ at $Y$. (If $D=E=B$, which happens when $PA=PB$ and $QB=QC$, the circle $BDE$ is not defined; the statement presupposes that this is not the case.) $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

The points are in the order $A,P,Q,C$, so $\overrightarrow{PQ}=\frac12\overrightarrow{AC}$. Put $A=(0,0)$, let $AC$ be the $x$-axis, $B=(u,v)$ with $v\ne0$, $B'=(u,-v)$ and $P=(p,0)$, $p>0$.

The point $D$. A circle through $A$ and $P$ has an equation $x^2+y^2-px+fy=0$; it passes through $B'$ when $f=\frac{u^2+v^2-pu}{v}$. Its tangent at $A$ is $-px+fy=0$. Points of the line $BP$ are $(p+t(u-p),\,tv)$; substituting, \[-p\bigl(p+t(u-p)\bigr)+ftv=0\iff t\bigl(u^2+v^2-2pu+p^2\bigr)=p^2\iff t=\frac{p^2}{(u-p)^2+v^2}.\] So $D=(p+t(u-p),\,tv)$ with $t\,\bigl((u-p)^2+v^2\bigr)=p^2$.

The tangent circle. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle through $B$ touching the $x$-axis at $Y=(2p,0)$: \[\Gamma:\ (x-2p)^2+y^2+gy=0,\qquad g=-\frac{(u-2p)^2+v^2}{v}.\] For $D$: \[\bigl(t(u-p)-p\bigr)^2+t^2v^2+gtv=t^2\bigl((u-p)^2+v^2\bigr)-2tp(u-p)+p^2-t\bigl((u-2p)^2+v^2\bigr).\] Using $t\bigl((u-p)^2+v^2\bigr)=p^2$, this equals $t\bigl(p^2-2pu+2p^2-(u-2p)^2-v^2\bigr)+p^2=-t\bigl((u-p)^2+v^2\bigr)+p^2=0$. So $D\in\Gamma$.

The same computation with the origin at $C$ and the $x$-axis reversed shows that $E$ lies on the circle through $B$ touching $AC$ at the reflection of $C$ in $Q$. That point is $2Q-C=2P-A=Y$ (because $Q-P=\frac12(C-A)$), and there is only one circle through $B$ touching $AC$ at $Y$; it is $\Gamma$. Hence $B,D,E\in\Gamma$, and the circle $BDE$ touches $AC$ (at $Y$). $\blacksquare$

Problem 12

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

The vertices of a right-angled triangle $ABC$ are points with integer coordinates. Its incircle centered at $I$ touches $AB$, $BC$ at points $C'$, $A'$ respectively. The lines $AA'$ and $CC'$ meet at point $G$. Prove that the line $IG$ passes through some point with integer coordinates.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates along the legs)

The incircle touches the three sides, and the cevians from the vertices to the opposite touch points are concurrent (Ceva: the tangent lengths cancel); so $G$ is the Gergonne point, and it lies on the cevians from any two vertices. The right angle may be at any vertex; call it $V$, call the other vertices $U$, $W$, and let the incircle touch $VW$ at $U_1$ and $VU$ at $W_1$. Then $G=UU_1\cap WW_1$. We show that $IG$ passes through \[D=U+W-V,\] the fourth vertex of the rectangle with sides $VU$, $VW$; it has integer coordinates because $U$, $V$, $W$ do.

Take (non-lattice) Cartesian axes along $VW$ and $VU$: $V=(0,0)$, $W=(a,0)$, $U=(0,c)$, $D=(a,c)$. With inradius $r$ we have $I=(r,r)$, $U_1=(r,0)$, $W_1=(0,r)$, and $r<a$, $r<c$. The lines $UU_1$ and $WW_1$ are \[\frac xr+\frac yc=1,\qquad \frac xa+\frac yr=1 .\] Solving, $G=\Bigl(\dfrac{ra(c-r)}{ac-r^2},\ \dfrac{rc(a-r)}{ac-r^2}\Bigr)$ (one checks both equations directly). Then \[x_G-r=-\frac{r^2(a-r)}{ac-r^2},\qquad y_G-r=-\frac{r^2(c-r)}{ac-r^2},\] so $(x_G-r)(c-r)=(y_G-r)(a-r)$, which is exactly the condition that $G$ lies on the line through $I=(r,r)$ and $D=(a,c)$. Hence the line $IG$ passes through the lattice point $D$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Pappus with points at infinity)

As in any triangle, the cevians to the touch points of the incircle concur at the Gergonne point $G$ (Ceva). Let the right angle be at $V$, the other vertices $U,W$, and let the incircle touch $VW$ at $U_1$ and $VU$ at $W_1$; then $G=UU_1\cap WW_1$. Let $D=U+W-V$ (a lattice point), so $VUDW$ is a rectangle.

Since $IW_1\perp VU$ and $VU\perp VW$, the line $IW_1$ is parallel to $VW$; likewise $IU_1\parallel VU$. Let $\infty_1$ and $\infty_2$ be the points at infinity of the directions $VW$ and $VU$ (in the projective plane, parallel lines meet at the point at infinity of their direction).

Apply Pappus' theorem to the points $U, W_1, \infty_2$ on the line $VU$ and $W, U_1, \infty_1$ on the line $VW$:

  • $UU_1\cap W_1W=G$;

  • $U\infty_1\cap\infty_2W$ = (line through $U$ parallel to $VW$) $\cap$ (line through $W$ parallel to $VU$) $=D$;

  • $W_1\infty_1\cap\infty_2U_1$ = (line through $W_1$ parallel to $VW$) $\cap$ (line through $U_1$ parallel to $VU$) $=I$.

Pappus says these three points are collinear. So the line $IG$ passes through the lattice point $D$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 13

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $A_1\ldots A_n$ be a convex polygon. The points $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ in some order are vertices of two closed broken lines. What is the least upper bound of the ratio of their lengths?

Show the answer

$\boxed{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ (for $n\ge4$ the ratio is always smaller than $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ but comes arbitrarily close to it; for $n=3$ it always equals $1$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting crossings of cutting chords)

Let $p$ be the perimeter and $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. "Closed broken line" means a closed polygonal path whose vertices are $A_1,\dots,A_n$ in some order.

The shortest one is the polygon. Take a shortest closed broken line. If two of its edges $XY$, $ZW$ crossed, replacing them by $XZ$, $YW$ or by $XW$, $YZ$ (the choice that keeps one closed path) would make it shorter (triangle inequality). So it has no crossings. If it had an edge $XY$ that is a diagonal of the polygon, there would be vertices on both sides of $XY$; the rest of the path, going from $Y$ to $X$ through all of them, would contain an edge joining vertices on different sides, and such a segment crosses the chord $XY$ (convex position) — a contradiction. So all edges are sides: the shortest closed broken line is the polygon itself, of length $p$. Hence the ratio of two broken lines is at most $\frac{L}{p}$, where $L$ is the length of the longest one.

Upper bound. Parametrise the boundary of the polygon by arc length, a circle of length $p$. For boundary points $X,Y$ let $\delta(X,Y)\le\frac p2$ be the length of the shorter boundary arc between them; then $|XY|\le\delta(X,Y)$. For $t\in[0,\frac p2)$ cut the boundary at the points with parameters $t$ and $t+\frac p2$ into two halves. The set of $t$ for which $X$ and $Y$ fall into different halves has measure exactly $\delta(X,Y)$. Therefore, for a closed broken line with edges $e_1,\dots,e_n$, \[L=\sum|e_k|\le\sum\delta(e_k)=\int_0^{p/2}c(t)\,dt,\] where $c(t)$ is the number of edges joining the two halves of cut $t$. A closed path crosses between the halves an even number of times, so $c(t)$ is even and at most $n$, i.e. $c(t)\le2m$. Hence $L\le 2m\cdot\frac p2=mp$.

Strictness for $n\ge4$. If all edges of the broken line are sides, $L=p<mp$. Otherwise some edge $XY$ is a diagonal; both boundary arcs between $X$ and $Y$ contain another vertex $Z$, so $\delta(X,Y)\ge XZ+ZY>XY$, and $L<mp$. (For $n=3$ there is only one closed broken line, the triangle, and the ratio is $1=m$.)

The bound is approached. Take $\varepsilon>0$ small and put the vertices on the ellipse $x=\cos\varphi$, $y=\varepsilon\sin\varphi$: $n-m$ of them with $\varphi$ within $\varepsilon$ of $\pi$ (near $(-1,0)$) and $m$ of them with $\varphi$ within $\varepsilon$ of $0$ (near $(1,0)$). They are in convex position and the polygon lies in the rectangle $[-1,1]\times[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$, so $p<4+4\varepsilon$. A broken line that alternates $L_1R_1L_2R_2\dots$ between the left and right groups (with one extra left vertex when $n$ is odd) has $2m$ edges of length at least $2\cos\varepsilon$. Its ratio to the perimeter exceeds $\frac{4m\cos\varepsilon}{4+4\varepsilon}\to m$.

So the ratio can be made as close to $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ as we like, and for $n\ge4$ it is always smaller: the least upper bound is $\boxed{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ (not attained for $n\ge4$; for $n=3$ the ratio is always $1$).

Solution 2 (Projections (Cauchy))

Let $p$ be the perimeter and $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$.

Projections of one segment and of the polygon. For a direction $\theta$ let $\pi_\theta$ be the orthogonal projection onto a line of direction $\theta$. A segment of length $\ell$ satisfies $\int_0^\pi|\pi_\theta(\text{segment})|\,d\theta=2\ell$. The boundary of the convex polygon covers its projection, an interval of length $w(\theta)$, exactly twice, so $\sum_{\text{sides}}|\pi_\theta|=2w(\theta)$; integrating, $2p=2\int_0^\pi w(\theta)\,d\theta$, i.e. $p=\int_0^\pi w(\theta)\,d\theta$.

A closed walk on an interval. Let $x_1,\dots,x_n$ (cyclically) be numbers in an interval of length $w$ that contains $\min x_i$ and $\max x_i$ as its ends. Split the cyclic sequence of steps into maximal monotone runs. Their number is even (ups and downs alternate), at most $n$, hence at most $2m$; each run has total variation at most $w$; and if the values are not all equal there are at least two runs, which together go from the minimum to the maximum and back. Therefore \[2w\le\sum_i|x_{i+1}-x_i|\le 2m\,w .\]

Integration. For a closed broken line of length $L$ through the vertices, project it in direction $\theta$: the projected vertices fill the projection of the polygon, so by the walk lemma $2w(\theta)\le\sum_k|\pi_\theta(e_k)|\le2m\,w(\theta)$. Integrating over $\theta\in[0,\pi)$ and using the two formulas above, \[2p\le 2L\le 2m\,p,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad p\le L\le mp .\] So the ratio of two closed broken lines is at most $m$.

For $n\ge4$ the inequality is strict: in all but finitely many directions the projections of the vertices are distinct, so only one vertex projects to the top end of the interval; then at most one run can end at the top, and if $m\ge2$ the bound $2mw$ is not reached. Integrating a strict inequality over a set of positive measure gives $L<mp$. (For $n=3$ the only broken line is the triangle.)

The bound is approached. Let $\varepsilon>0$ be small. Put $n-m$ vertices on the ellipse $x=\cos\varphi$, $y=\varepsilon\sin\varphi$ with $\varphi$ within $\varepsilon$ of $\pi$, and $m$ vertices with $\varphi$ within $\varepsilon$ of $0$. They are in convex position and the polygon lies in $[-1,1]\times[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$, so $p\le4+4\varepsilon$, while the polygon itself is a closed broken line of length $p$. The broken line that alternates between the two groups (with one extra left vertex if $n$ is odd) has $2m$ edges of length at least $2\cos\varepsilon$, so its ratio to $p$ is at least $\frac{4m\cos\varepsilon}{4+4\varepsilon}$, which tends to $m$ as $\varepsilon\to0$.

Hence the least upper bound is $\boxed{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, not attained for $n\ge4$ (for $n=3$ the ratio is always $1$).

Problem 14

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

A triangle $ABC$ ($AB<AC$) is given. Let $P$ be a point on the ray $BA$ such that $BP=AC$, and $Q$ be a point on the ray $CA$ such that $CQ=AB$. Let $BB_1$ and $CC_1$ be the perpendiculars to the line $PQ$. Prove that the circles $(CB_1Q)$, $(BC_1P)$ and the external bisector of angle $BAC$ have a common point.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The point W and two right angles)

Let $b=AC$, $c=AB$ ($c<b$), $\alpha=\frac12\angle BAC$, and let $\ell$ be the external bisector of angle $A$.

Shape of the figure. Since $BP=b>c$, the point $P$ lies on the ray $BA$ beyond $A$, with $AP=b-c$; since $CQ=c<b$, $Q$ lies on the segment $CA$ with $AQ=b-c$. So the triangle $APQ$ is isosceles; its apex angle $\angle PAQ=180^\circ-\angle BAC$ is bisected by $\ell$. Hence $\ell$ is the axis of symmetry of $APQ$, and $PQ\perp\ell$. Let $W$ be the common point of the perpendicular to $AB$ at $P$ and the perpendicular to $AC$ at $Q$; by the symmetry in $\ell$, $W\in\ell$.

We show that $W$ lies on both circles: since $\angle CQW=90^\circ$ and $\angle BPW=90^\circ$, it suffices to prove $\angle CB_1W=90^\circ$ and $\angle BC_1W=90^\circ$, because then $Q,B_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $CW$ (which is therefore the circle $CB_1Q$) and $P,C_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $BW$.

Coordinates. Put $A=(0,0)$ with the internal bisector along the $x$-axis and $\ell$ along the $y$-axis: \[B=c(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha),\qquad C=b(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha),\] \[P=(b-c)(-\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha),\qquad Q=(b-c)(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha).\] So $PQ$ is the line $y=(b-c)\sin\alpha$, and $B_1=(c\cos\alpha,(b-c)\sin\alpha)$, $C_1=(b\cos\alpha,(b-c)\sin\alpha)$. (If $b=2c$ then $B_1=Q$ and the circle $(CB_1Q)$ is not determined; the statement presupposes $b\ne2c$.) The condition $\overrightarrow{QW}\perp AC$ for $W=(0,w)$ gives $(b-c)-w\sin\alpha=0$, so $W=\bigl(0,\frac{b-c}{\sin\alpha}\bigr)$. Now \begin{align*} \overrightarrow{B_1C}\cdot\overrightarrow{B_1W}&=(b-c)\cos\alpha\cdot(-c\cos\alpha)+c\sin\alpha\Bigl(\frac{b-c}{\sin\alpha}-(b-c)\sin\alpha\Bigr)\\ &=-c(b-c)\cos^2\alpha+c(b-c)\cos^2\alpha=0,\\ \overrightarrow{C_1B}\cdot\overrightarrow{C_1W}&=(c-b)\cos\alpha\cdot(-b\cos\alpha)+(-b\sin\alpha)\Bigl(\frac{b-c}{\sin\alpha}-(b-c)\sin\alpha\Bigr)\\ &=b(b-c)\cos^2\alpha-b(b-c)\cos^2\alpha=0. \end{align*} So $\angle CB_1W=\angle BC_1W=90^\circ$, and the circles $(CB_1Q)$, $(BC_1P)$ and the external bisector $\ell$ all pass through $W$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Projections on the external bisector and the power of A)

Let $b=AC$, $c=AB$ ($c<b$), $\alpha=\frac12\angle BAC$, and let $\ell$ be the external bisector of angle $A$. We assume $b\ne2c$; otherwise $B_1=Q$ and the circle $(CB_1Q)$ is not determined. Write $e_B=\frac1c\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $e_C=\frac1b\overrightarrow{AC}$ for the unit vectors along the sides. The vector $e_B+e_C$ points along the internal bisector, and $e_B-e_C$ is perpendicular to it (the two unit vectors have equal lengths), so $e_B-e_C$ is parallel to $\ell$.

The line $PQ$. Since $BP=b>c$, the point $P$ lies on the ray $BA$ beyond $A$, with $AP=b-c$; since $CQ=c<b$, the point $Q$ lies on the segment $AC$, with $AQ=b-c$. The triangle $APQ$ is isosceles with apex angle $\angle PAQ=180^\circ-\angle BAC$, which $\ell$ bisects, so $PQ\perp\ell$. Hence the perpendiculars $BB_1$ and $CC_1$ to $PQ$ are parallel to $\ell$.

Two more points of the circles. Let $U$, $V$ be the projections of $B$, $C$ on $\ell$. They lie on opposite sides of $A$, because $B$ and $C$ lie on opposite sides of the internal bisector. The line $AB$ makes the angle $90^\circ-\alpha$ with $\ell$, so $AU=c\sin\alpha$; likewise $AV=b\sin\alpha$.

The vector $\overrightarrow{AU}$ is the component of $\overrightarrow{AB}=c\,e_B$ along $\ell$. The vector $\overrightarrow{CC_1}$ is parallel to $\ell$ and perpendicular to $PQ$, and $Q\in PQ$, so it is the component of $\overrightarrow{CQ}=-c\,e_C$ along $\ell$. Since $e_B-e_C\parallel\ell$, the vectors $e_B$ and $-e_C$ have the same component along $\ell$; hence $\overrightarrow{AU}=\overrightarrow{CC_1}$, so $ACC_1U$ is a parallelogram and $\overrightarrow{UC_1}=\overrightarrow{AC}$. In the same way $\overrightarrow{AV}$ and $\overrightarrow{BB_1}$ are the components along $\ell$ of $b\,e_C$ and of $\overrightarrow{BP}=-b\,e_B$, so $\overrightarrow{AV}=\overrightarrow{BB_1}$ and $\overrightarrow{VB_1}=\overrightarrow{AB}$.

The segments $BU$ and $PC_1$ are both perpendicular to $\ell$, and their midpoints differ by \[\tfrac12\bigl(\overrightarrow{BP}+\overrightarrow{UC_1}\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(-b\,e_B+b\,e_C\bigr),\] a vector parallel to $\ell$. So the parallel segments $BU$ and $PC_1$ have the same perpendicular bisector $m$. The circle through $B$ and $P$ with centre on $m$ (it exists: $m$ is parallel to $\ell$, and the perpendicular bisector of $BP$ is not, because $AB$ is not perpendicular to $\ell$) is symmetric in $m$, so it also passes through $U$ and $C_1$, the mirror images of $B$ and $P$. Thus $U$ lies on the circle $(BC_1P)$. Similarly, the midpoints of $CV$ and $QB_1$ differ by $\frac12(\overrightarrow{CQ}+\overrightarrow{VB_1})=\frac12(-c\,e_C+c\,e_B)$, again a vector parallel to $\ell$, so $V$ lies on the circle $(CB_1Q)$.

The power of $A$. Measure signed lengths on $\ell$ in the direction from $A$ towards $V$; then $\overline{AU}=-c\sin\alpha$ and $\overline{AV}=b\sin\alpha$. On the line $BP$ the point $A$ lies between $B$ and $P$, so the power of $A$ with respect to the circle $(BC_1P)$ is $-AB\cdot AP=-c(b-c)$. On the line $AC$ the point $A$ lies outside the segment $QC$, so its power with respect to $(CB_1Q)$ is $AQ\cdot AC=b(b-c)$. Let $W_1$, $W_2$ be the second common points of $\ell$ with the two circles ($W_i$ is the point of tangency if $\ell$ touches the circle). Then \[\overline{AW_1}=\frac{-c(b-c)}{\overline{AU}}=\frac{b-c}{\sin\alpha},\qquad \overline{AW_2}=\frac{b(b-c)}{\overline{AV}}=\frac{b-c}{\sin\alpha}.\] So $W_1=W_2$: the circles $(CB_1Q)$, $(BC_1P)$ and the external bisector of angle $BAC$ have a common point. $\blacksquare$

Problem 15

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Prove that the Nagel point of a triangle lies on its incircle if and only if the bisectors of two angles meet the side of the Gergonne triangle cutting the third vertex, at two points such that the segment between them equals a half of this side.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A chord of the circle on AB, and a distance formula)

Let the incircle touch $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, let $s$ be the semiperimeter and $r$ the inradius. The side of the Gergonne triangle "cutting off" the vertex $C$ is $A'B'$; the bisectors of the other two angles are $AI$ and $BI$. We prove: they meet the line $A'B'$ at points $X,Y$ with $XY=\frac12A'B'$ iff $s=2c$; and $N$ lies on the incircle iff $(s-2a)(s-2b)(s-2c)=0$. Together these give the statement.

Lemma. The line $AI$ meets $A'B'$ at the foot $X$ of the perpendicular from $B$ to $AI$. Indeed, let $X$ be that foot. As $\angle BXI=\angle BA'I=90^\circ$, the points $B,X,A',I$ lie on the circle with diameter $BI$. Use directed angles $\measuredangle(m,n)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$, with $A,B,C$ labelled counterclockwise; then the angle $\angle AIB=90^\circ+\frac C2$ gives $\measuredangle(IA,IB)=90^\circ+\frac C2$, and the ray $CI$ is obtained from the ray $CA$, and the ray $CB$ from the ray $CI$, by turning counterclockwise through $\frac C2$, so $\measuredangle(CI,CB)=\frac C2$. If $X=A'$ (which happens only when $CA=CB$), there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, since $B,X,A',I$ are concyclic and $IX$ is the line $AI$, \[\measuredangle(A'X,BC)=\measuredangle(A'X,A'B)=\measuredangle(IX,IB)=\measuredangle(IA,IB)=90^\circ+\tfrac C2 .\] Since $A'B'\perp CI$ (the tangents $CA'$, $CB'$ are equal), $\measuredangle(A'B',BC)=\measuredangle(A'B',CI)+\measuredangle(CI,CB)=90^\circ+\frac C2$. So the lines $A'X$ and $A'B'$ through $A'$ are parallel, i.e. they coincide, and $X\in A'B'$. Similarly $BI$ meets $A'B'$ at the foot $Y$ of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BI$.

The bisector condition. $\angle AXB=\angle AYB=90^\circ$, so $X,Y$ lie on the circle with diameter $AB=c$, and $XY=c\sin\angle XAY$. The angle between $AX=AI$ and $AY\perp BI$ is $90^\circ$ minus the angle between $AI$ and $BI$, which is $90^\circ-\frac C2$; so $\sin\angle XAY=\sin\frac C2$ and $XY=c\sin\frac C2$. On the other hand $CA'=CB'=s-c$, so $A'B'=2(s-c)\sin\frac C2$. Hence \[XY=\tfrac12A'B'\iff c=s-c\iff s=2c .\]

Distance $IN$. In barycentric coordinates $N=\frac{(s-a,\,s-b,\,s-c)}{s}$ and $I=\frac{(a,b,c)}{2s}$, so the displacement $N-I$ has coordinates $(u,v,w)=\frac{(b+c-2a,\ c+a-2b,\ a+b-2c)}{2s}$ (sum $0$), and $IN^2=-(a^2vw+b^2wu+c^2uv)$. Write $x=s-a$, $y=s-b$, $z=s-c$, so that $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$, $s=x+y+z$ and \[u=\frac{2x-y-z}{2s},\qquad v=\frac{2y-z-x}{2s},\qquad w=\frac{2z-x-y}{2s}.\] Using $r^2=\frac{xyz}{x+y+z}$ (Heron's formula and $\Delta=rs$), a direct expansion of $-(a^2vw+b^2wu+c^2uv)-r^2$ gives \[IN^2-r^2=\frac{(x-y-z)(x-y+z)(x+y-z)}{x+y+z}=\frac{(s-2a)(s-2b)(s-2c)}{s}.\] (Indeed $x-y-z=s-2a$, $x-y+z=-(s-2b)$, $x+y-z=-(s-2c)$.)

Conclusion. $N$ lies on the incircle iff $IN=r$ iff one of $s-2a$, $s-2b$, $s-2c$ vanishes, iff for one of the vertices (the one opposite the side equal to $\frac s2$) the bisectors of the other two angles cut out on the corresponding side of the Gergonne triangle a segment equal to half of that side. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Homothety at a vertex and Stewart)

Let the incircle (centre $I$, radius $r$) touch $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, and let $s$ be the semiperimeter.

The bisector condition. Let $X$, $Y$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $B$ to $AI$ and from $A$ to $BI$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and $A,B,C$ counterclockwise, the circle with diameter $BI$ through $X$ and $A'$ gives (for $X\ne A'$) $\measuredangle(A'X,BC)=\measuredangle(IX,IB)=\measuredangle(IA,IB)=90^\circ+\frac C2=\measuredangle(A'B',CI)+\measuredangle(CI,CB)=\measuredangle(A'B',BC)$, so $X\in A'B'$ (trivially if $X=A'$); likewise $Y\in A'B'$. So $X$, $Y$ are the points where the bisectors from $A$, $B$ meet $A'B'$. They lie on the circle with diameter $AB$, and the lines $AX=AI$ and $AY\perp BI$ make the angle $90^\circ-\bigl(90^\circ-\frac C2\bigr)=\frac C2$, so $XY=c\sin\frac C2$. Since $CA'=CB'=s-c$, $A'B'=2(s-c)\sin\frac C2$, and the condition $XY=\frac12A'B'$ for the vertex $C$ holds iff $s=2c$; similarly for $A$ and $B$. It remains to prove: $N$ lies on the incircle iff $s=2a$, $s=2b$ or $s=2c$.

$N$ on a cevian. Let $Q$ be the point where the excircle opposite $C$ touches $AB$; then $AQ=s-b$, $BQ=s-a$, and from the barycentric coordinates of $N$ we get $N=\frac csQ+\frac{s-c}sC$, so $N$ lies on $CQ$ with $CN=\frac cs\,CQ$.

The homothety with centre $C$ and ratio $\frac{r}{r_c}=\frac{s-c}{s}$ (where $r=\frac\Delta s$, $r_c=\frac\Delta{s-c}$) maps the excircle to the incircle, and maps $Q$ (where the tangent is $AB$, on the side facing $C$) to the point $K$ of the incircle diametrically opposite $C'$. So $K\in CQ$ and $CK=\frac{s-c}{s}CQ$. Let $K_2$ be the second common point of the line $CQ$ with the incircle; the power of $C$ gives $CK\cdot CK_2=(s-c)^2$.

The two possibilities. $N$ lies on the incircle iff $N=K$ or $N=K_2$.

  • $N=K$ iff $\frac cs=\frac{s-c}s$ iff $s=2c$.

  • $N=K_2$ iff $CN\cdot CK=(s-c)^2$ iff $\frac cs\cdot\frac{s-c}s\,CQ^2=(s-c)^2$ iff $c\,CQ^2=s^2(s-c)$. By Stewart's theorem, $c\,CQ^2=b^2(s-a)+a^2(s-b)-c(s-a)(s-b)$, and a direct expansion gives \begin{align*} &b^2(s-a)+a^2(s-b)-c(s-a)(s-b)-s^2(s-c)\\ &\quad=\tfrac18(a+b+c)(a+c-3b)(3a-b-c)=-(s-2a)(s-2b)\,s . \end{align*} So $N=K_2$ iff $s=2a$ or $s=2b$.

Hence $N$ lies on the incircle iff $s=2a$, $2b$ or $2c$, i.e. iff the bisector condition holds for one of the vertices. $\blacksquare$

Problem 16

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

The line passing through the common point of the diagonals of a trapezoid $ABCD$ and parallel to its bases meets the lateral side $AB$ at point $M$. Let $K$ be the projection of $M$ to $CD$. Prove that $KM$ bisects the angle $AKB$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Projecting onto CD)

Let the bases be $AD$ and $BC$, $O$ the common point of the diagonals and $M\in AB$ with $MO\parallel AD$.

The ratio $AM:MB$. From $MO\parallel AD$ in triangle $BAD$ and $MO\parallel BC$ in triangle $ABC$: $MO=AD\cdot\frac{BM}{BA}=BC\cdot\frac{AM}{AB}$, so \[\frac{AM}{MB}=\frac{AD}{BC}.\]

Projections. Let $A_1$, $B_1$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $A$, $B$ to the line $CD$. Orthogonal projection onto $CD$ preserves ratios along a line, and $K$ is the projection of $M$; hence $K$ lies between $A_1$ and $B_1$ and $\frac{A_1K}{KB_1}=\frac{AM}{MB}=\frac{AD}{BC}$. Also $AA_1=AD\sin\varphi$ and $BB_1=BC\sin\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between the parallel lines $AD$, $BC$ and the line $CD$; so $\frac{AA_1}{BB_1}=\frac{AD}{BC}$.

Hence the right triangles $AA_1K$ and $BB_1K$ are similar ($\frac{AA_1}{A_1K}=\frac{BB_1}{B_1K}$), and $\angle AKA_1=\angle BKB_1$. The points $A,B$ lie on the same side of $CD$ and $A_1,B_1$ on opposite sides of $K$, while $KM\perp CD$. Therefore \[\angle AKM=90^\circ-\angle AKA_1=90^\circ-\angle BKB_1=\angle BKM,\] i.e. $KM$ bisects the angle $AKB$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (A harmonic division and the Apollonius circle)

Let the bases be $AD$, $BC$ with $AD\ne BC$ (otherwise $ABCD$ is a parallelogram, $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, and $KM\perp CD\parallel AB$, so $KM$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ and bisects the angle $AKB$ of the isosceles triangle $AKB$), $O=AC\cap BD$, and $M\in AB$ with $MO\parallel AD$.

From $MO\parallel AD$ in triangle $BAD$ and $MO\parallel BC$ in triangle $ABC$, $MO=AD\cdot\frac{BM}{BA}=BC\cdot\frac{AM}{AB}$, so $\frac{AM}{MB}=\frac{AD}{BC}$. The lines $AB$ and $CD$ meet at a point $S$, the centre of the homothety taking $BC$ to $AD$, so $\frac{SA}{SB}=\frac{AD}{BC}$. Thus $M$ and $S$ divide $AB$ internally and externally in the same ratio $k=\frac{AD}{BC}$.

The locus of points $Z$ with $\frac{ZA}{ZB}=k$ is the Apollonius circle; it passes through $M$ and $S$ and has $MS$ as a diameter. The point $K$ lies on the line $CD$, which passes through $S$, and $\angle MKS=90^\circ$; so $K$ lies on the circle with diameter $MS$, and $\frac{KA}{KB}=k=\frac{MA}{MB}$.

By the converse of the angle bisector theorem (applied in triangle $AKB$ to the point $M$ of the side $AB$), $KM$ bisects the angle $AKB$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 17

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $O$ and $H$ be the circumcenter and the orthocenter of an acute-angled triangle $ABC$. The tangents to the circumcircle of $ABC$ at $B$ and $C$ meet at point $T$. Points $X$ and $Y$ lie on $AB$ and $AC$ in such a way that $\angle AOX=\angle AOY=\angle BTC$. Prove that $HT\perp XY$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Equal power differences)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles and $R$ the circumradius. Since $\angle BOC=2\alpha$ and $\angle OBT=\angle OCT=90^\circ$, we have $\angle BTC=180^\circ-2\alpha$ and $OT=\frac R{\cos\alpha}$; $T$ lies on the ray from $O$ through the midpoint of $BC$. The points $X$ and $Y$ lie on different sides of $AO$ (on the sides of $B$ and of $C$).

The criterion. For a point $Z$ put $f(Z)=HZ^2-TZ^2$. This is an affine function of $Z$, constant exactly on the lines perpendicular to $HT$. So $HT\perp XY$ iff $f(X)=f(Y)$.

The length $AX$. In triangle $AOX$: $\angle OAX=\angle OAB=90^\circ-\gamma$ and $\angle AOX=180^\circ-2\alpha$, so $\angle AXO=2\alpha+\gamma-90^\circ=90^\circ+\alpha-\beta$, and the law of sines gives \[AX=\frac{R\sin2\alpha}{\cos(\alpha-\beta)},\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad AY=\frac{R\sin2\alpha}{\cos(\alpha-\gamma)} .\]

Computing $f$ on the line $AB$. Let $u$ be the unit vector from $A$ to $B$ and $Z=A+xu$. Then \[f(Z)=AH^2-AT^2-2x\,\bigl(\overrightarrow{AH}-\overrightarrow{AT}\bigr)\cdot u .\] We have $\overrightarrow{AH}\cdot u=AH\cos\angle HAB=2R\cos\alpha\sin\beta$. Next $\overrightarrow{AT}=\overrightarrow{AO}+\overrightarrow{OT}$; the projection of $O$ on $AB$ is the midpoint of $AB$, so $\overrightarrow{AO}\cdot u=\frac c2=R\sin\gamma$; and $\overrightarrow{OT}$ has length $\frac R{\cos\alpha}$ and is perpendicular to $BC$, pointing away from $A$, so $\overrightarrow{OT}\cdot u=\frac{R\sin\beta}{\cos\alpha}$ (walking from $A$ to $B$ one approaches $BC$ by $c\sin\beta$). With $\sin\gamma=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta$: \begin{align*} \bigl(\overrightarrow{AH}-\overrightarrow{AT}\bigr)\cdot u&=\frac{R}{\cos\alpha}\bigl(\sin\beta\cos2\alpha-\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\cos^2\!\alpha\sin\beta\bigr)\\ &=-\frac{R\sin\alpha\cos(\alpha-\beta)}{\cos\alpha}. \end{align*} Hence \[f(X)=AH^2-AT^2+2\cdot\frac{R\sin2\alpha}{\cos(\alpha-\beta)}\cdot\frac{R\sin\alpha\cos(\alpha-\beta)}{\cos\alpha}=AH^2-AT^2+4R^2\sin^2\alpha .\] The same computation on the line $AC$ (exchange $\beta$ and $\gamma$) gives $f(Y)=AH^2-AT^2+4R^2\sin^2\alpha$. So $f(X)=f(Y)$ and $HT\perp XY$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Reflections of H in the sides)

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles (all acute), $\Omega$ the circumcircle and $R$ its radius. Since $\angle BOC=2\alpha$ and $\angle OBT=\angle OCT=90^\circ$, we have $\angle BTC=180^\circ-2\alpha$, $\angle BOT=\angle TOC=\alpha$ and $R=TO\cos\alpha$.

The points $X$ and $Y$. Let $U$ be the reflection of $H$ in $AB$; it is well known that $U$ lies on $\Omega$ (it is the second common point of the altitude from $C$ with $\Omega$), on the arc $AB$ not containing $C$. The triangle $AOU$ is isosceles ($OA=OU=R$). The reflection in $AB$ gives $\angle BAU=\angle BAH=90^\circ-\beta$; also $\angle OAB=90^\circ-\gamma$, and the ray $AB$ lies inside the angle $OAU$, because $O$ (inside the triangle) and $U$ lie on different sides of $AB$. Hence the base angle is $\angle OAU=(90^\circ-\gamma)+(90^\circ-\beta)=\alpha$, and $\angle AOU=180^\circ-2\alpha=\angle BTC$. The segment $OU$ joins $O$, which lies inside the triangle, to the point $U$ of the arc $AB$, so it crosses the chord $AB$ at a point of the segment $AB$. Moreover, as a point $Z$ moves along the segment $AB$ from $A$ to $B$, the angle $\angle AOZ$ increases from $0$ to $\angle AOB=2\gamma$, and $180^\circ-2\alpha<2\gamma$ (as $\beta<90^\circ$), so there is exactly one point $Z$ of the segment $AB$ with $\angle AOZ=180^\circ-2\alpha$. Hence $X$ is the common point of $OU$ and $AB$, and in the same way $Y$ is the common point of $OV$ and $AC$, where $V$ is the reflection of $H$ in $AC$ (a point of $\Omega$ on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$).

Since $X$ lies on the mirror $AB$, $HX=UX$, and since $X$ lies on the segment $OU$, $UX=R-OX$. So \[HX=R-OX,\qquad HY=R-OY.\] In triangle $AOX$ we have $\angle OAX=90^\circ-\gamma$ and $\angle AOX=180^\circ-2\alpha$, so $\angle AXO=2\alpha+\gamma-90^\circ=90^\circ+\alpha-\beta$, and the law of sines gives \[OX=\frac{R\cos\gamma}{\cos(\alpha-\beta)},\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad OY=\frac{R\cos\beta}{\cos(\alpha-\gamma)}.\]

The criterion. $HT\perp XY$ iff $TX^2-TY^2=HX^2-HY^2$, i.e. iff \[TX^2+HY^2=TY^2+HX^2.\tag{1}\]

The lengths $TX$ and $TY$. The ray $OT$ passes through the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$. Going around $\Omega$ in one direction we meet this midpoint, then $C$, then $V$; the central angles are $\angle TOC=\alpha$ and $\angle COV=2\angle CAV=2(90^\circ-\gamma)$. So the rays $OT$ and $OV=OY$ form the angle $\alpha+180^\circ-2\gamma$ (or its supplement to $360^\circ$, which has the same cosine), and the law of cosines in triangle $TOY$, with $\cos(180^\circ+\alpha-2\gamma)=-\cos(2\gamma-\alpha)$, gives \[TY^2=TO^2+OY^2+2\,TO\cdot OY\cos(2\gamma-\alpha).\] In the same way, going from the midpoint through $B$ to $U$ (central angles $\angle TOB=\alpha$ and $\angle BOU=2\angle BAU=180^\circ-2\beta$), \[TX^2=TO^2+OX^2+2\,TO\cdot OX\cos(2\beta-\alpha).\]

The computation. Substituting these and $HX=R-OX$, $HY=R-OY$, the difference of the two sides of (1) is \[\bigl(OX^2-(R-OX)^2\bigr)-\bigl(OY^2-(R-OY)^2\bigr)+2\,TO\bigl(OX\cos(2\beta-\alpha)-OY\cos(2\gamma-\alpha)\bigr),\] and with $OX^2-(R-OX)^2=2R\cdot OX-R^2$ and $R=TO\cos\alpha$ it equals \[2\,TO\Bigl(OX\bigl(\cos\alpha+\cos(2\beta-\alpha)\bigr)-OY\bigl(\cos\alpha+\cos(2\gamma-\alpha)\bigr)\Bigr) =4\,TO\bigl(OX\cos\beta\cos(\beta-\alpha)-OY\cos\gamma\cos(\gamma-\alpha)\bigr).\] By the formulas for $OX$ and $OY$, $OX\cos\beta\cos(\beta-\alpha)=R\cos\beta\cos\gamma=OY\cos\gamma\cos(\gamma-\alpha)$. So the difference is $0$, (1) holds, and $HT\perp XY$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 3 (Complex numbers on the circumcircle)

Take the circumcircle as the unit circle, so $O=0$, and put $A=1$, $B=b=e^{i\beta}$, $C=c=e^{-i\gamma}$ with $B$ above and $C$ below the real axis. A bar denotes complex conjugation; for $|z|=1$, $\bar z=1/z$. The arc $AB$ not containing $C$ measures $2\angle C$, so $\beta=2\angle C$; likewise $\gamma=2\angle B$. As the triangle is acute, $0<\beta<\pi$ and $0<\gamma<\pi$.

The angle. In the quadrilateral $OBTC$ the angles at $B$ and $C$ are right and $\angle BOC=2\angle A$, so \[\tau:=\angle BTC=180^\circ-2\angle A=2\angle B+2\angle C-180^\circ=\beta+\gamma-\pi .\] Here $\tau>0$ (as $\angle A<90^\circ$), $\tau<\beta$ (as $\gamma<\pi$) and $\tau<\gamma$.

The rays $OX$ and $OY$. The segment $AB$ lies in the angle between the rays from $O$ of arguments $0$ and $\beta<\pi$, and its points have all the arguments from $0$ to $\beta$. Since $0<\tau<\beta$, the only ray from $O$ making the angle $\tau$ with $OA$ that meets the segment $AB$ is the ray of argument $\tau$; its direction is \[d=e^{i\tau}=e^{i(\beta+\gamma)}e^{-i\pi}=-\frac bc .\] In the same way $Y$ lies on the ray of direction $e^{-i\tau}=-\frac cb$.

The points $X$, $Y$. A point $z$ lies on the line through $0$ with unit direction $d$ iff $z/d$ is real, i.e. $z\bar d=\bar zd$, i.e. $z=d^2\bar z$. So $x=\frac{b^2}{c^2}\bar x$ and $y=\frac{c^2}{b^2}\bar y$. The chord through two points $p,q$ of the unit circle is $z+pq\,\bar z=p+q$ (both $p$ and $q$ satisfy it), so the line $AB$ is $z+b\bar z=1+b$. Substituting $\bar x=\frac{c^2}{b^2}x$ gives $x\bigl(1+\frac{c^2}b\bigr)=1+b$; the coefficient is nonzero because the line $OX$ meets the line $AB$ in exactly one point. Hence, and likewise for $Y$, \[x=\frac{b(1+b)}{b+c^2},\qquad y=\frac{c(1+c)}{c+b^2}.\]

The points $T$, $H$. The tangent at $b$ is $z+b^2\bar z=2b$ and the tangent at $c$ is $z+c^2\bar z=2c$. Eliminating $\bar z$: $z(b^2-c^2)=2bc(b-c)$, so $T=\frac{2bc}{b+c}$ ($b+c\ne0$, since $BC$ is not a diameter). With the circumcentre at the origin, $H=1+b+c$.

The computation. First \[H-T=\frac{(1+b+c)(b+c)-2bc}{b+c}=\frac{b+c+b^2+c^2}{b+c}.\] Next, $b(1+b)(c+b^2)-c(1+c)(b+c^2)=b^4-c^4+b^3-c^3+b^2c-bc^2=(b-c)(b+c)(b^2+c^2+b+c)$, so \[x-y=\frac{(b-c)(b+c)(b^2+c^2+b+c)}{(b+c^2)(b^2+c)}=w\,(H-T),\qquad w=\frac{(b-c)(b+c)^2}{(b+c^2)(b^2+c)} .\] The points $X\ne A$ and $Y\ne A$ lie on the different lines $AB$ and $AC$, so $X\ne Y$; hence $x-y\ne0$, and therefore $H\ne T$ and $w\ne0$.

$w$ is purely imaginary. Using $\bar b=\frac1b$, $\bar c=\frac1c$ and multiplying numerator and denominator by $b^3c^3$, \[\bar w=\frac{\bigl(\frac1b-\frac1c\bigr)\bigl(\frac1b+\frac1c\bigr)^2}{\bigl(\frac1b+\frac1{c^2}\bigr)\bigl(\frac1{b^2}+\frac1c\bigr)}=\frac{(c-b)(b+c)^2}{(c^2+b)(c+b^2)}=-w .\] So $w$ is a nonzero purely imaginary number, and $x-y=w(H-T)$ says that the vector $\overrightarrow{YX}$ is the vector $\overrightarrow{TH}$ turned through $90^\circ$ and scaled. Therefore $HT\perp XY$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 18

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 53: Barycentric and Projective-Flavored Geometry

A point $P$ inside a triangle $ABC$ is given. The lines $BP$, $CP$ meet the circle $ABC$ for the second time at points $E$, $F$ respectively. The circle $\Omega$ passes through $P$, $E$ and meets $AC$ at points $B_1$, $B_2$. The lines $PB_1$, $PB_2$ meet $AB$ at points $C_1$, $C_2$. Prove that $C_1$, $C_2$, $P$, $F$ are concyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (Products of distances and a central projection)

Let $B_0=BP\cap AC$ and $Z=CP\cap AB$. On the line $BE$ the order is $B,P,B_0,E$; on $CF$ it is $C,P,Z,F$. All products of lengths below are signed (along the line in question).

Goal. The lines $AB$ and $CF$ meet at $Z$, so by the converse of the intersecting-chords theorem it suffices to prove \[ZC_1\cdot ZC_2=ZP\cdot ZF .\tag{1}\]

What we know. $B_0$ lies on the line $PE$, a secant of $\Omega$, so its power gives $B_0B_1\cdot B_0B_2=B_0P\cdot B_0E=:k$.

Two auxiliary points. Let $V\in AC$ with $PV\parallel AB$ and $W\in AB$ with $PW\parallel AC$. The homothety with centre $B_0$ taking $B$ to $P$ takes $A$ to $V$, so $\overrightarrow{B_0V}=\frac{B_0P}{B_0B}\overrightarrow{B_0A}$; with the power of $B_0$ with respect to the circle $ABC$ ($B_0A\cdot B_0C=B_0B\cdot B_0E$) this gives \[B_0C\cdot B_0V=B_0P\cdot B_0E=k.\tag{2}\] In the same way (homothety with centre $Z$ taking $C$ to $P$, and $ZA\cdot ZB=ZC\cdot ZF$) \[ZB\cdot ZW=ZP\cdot ZF.\tag{3}\]

The central projection. Use coordinates $x$ on $AC$ (origin $B_0$) and $y$ on $AB$ (origin $Z$), and let $\pi$ map a point of $AC$ to the common point of $AB$ with its join to $P$. A central projection preserves cross-ratios, so in these coordinates it is a linear-fractional map. It sends $C$ (coordinate $c$) to $Z$ ($y=0$) and $V$ (coordinate $v$) to the point at infinity, hence \[y=\lambda\,\frac{x-c}{x-v}\] for a constant $\lambda$. It sends $B_0$ ($x=0$) to $B$ and the point at infinity to $W$, so $ZB=\lambda\frac cv$ and $ZW=\lambda$.

The computation. Let $x_1,x_2$ be the coordinates of $B_1,B_2$; then $x_1x_2=k=cv$ by (2). Since $C_i=\pi(B_i)$, \[ZC_1\cdot ZC_2=\lambda^2\frac{(x_1-c)(x_2-c)}{(x_1-v)(x_2-v)}=\lambda^2\frac{cv-c(x_1+x_2)+c^2}{cv-v(x_1+x_2)+v^2}=\lambda^2\frac cv=ZB\cdot ZW,\] the factor $c+v-(x_1+x_2)$ cancelling (it vanishes only when $\{B_1,B_2\}=\{C,V\}$, a degenerate position with $C_2$ at infinity). By (3) this equals $ZP\cdot ZF$, which is (1). Hence $C_1,C_2,P,F$ are concyclic. $\blacksquare$

Problem 19

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 53: Barycentric and Projective-Flavored Geometry

The incircle of a triangle $ABC$ centered at $I$ touches $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at points $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ respectively. Let $A_b$, $A_c$, $B_a$, $C_a$ be the midpoints of segments $A'B$, $A'C$, $B'A$, $C'A$ respectively. The lines $A_bB_a$ and $A_cC_a$ meet at point $P$. Prove that the reflection of $I$ about $P$ lies on $AA'$.

Show the solution

Solution (Barycentric coordinates)

Use barycentric coordinates with respect to $ABC$ and the tangent lengths $x=s-a$, $y=s-b$, $z=s-c$ (so $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$, $s=x+y+z$). Then \[A'=\tfrac{(0,\,z,\,y)}{a},\quad B'=\tfrac{(z,\,0,\,x)}{b},\quad C'=\tfrac{(y,\,x,\,0)}{c},\quad I=\tfrac{(a,\,b,\,c)}{2s}.\]

The point $P$. Points of the line $A_bB_a$ are $\frac12\bigl[(1-t)(A'+B)+t(A+B')\bigr]$ and points of $A_cC_a$ are $\frac12\bigl[(1-u)(A'+C)+u(A+C')\bigr]$. For $P$ we need \[(1-t)(A'+B)+t(A+B')=(1-u)(A'+C)+u(A+C').\] Comparing the $A$- and $B$-coordinates (the sums of coordinates agree automatically): \[t\,\frac{x+2z}{x+z}=u\,\frac{x+2y}{x+y},\qquad (1-t)\,\frac{y+2z}{y+z}=(1-u)\frac{z}{y+z}+u\,\frac{x}{x+y}.\] With $\Sigma=xy+yz+zx$ the solution is \[t=\frac{(x+2y)(x+z)(y+z)}{2s\Sigma},\qquad u=\frac{(x+y)(x+2z)(y+z)}{2s\Sigma}\] (substitute to check).

The reflection. The reflection of $I$ in $P$ is $J=2P-I$, whose coordinates are those of $(1-t)(A'+B)+t(A+B')$ minus those of $I$: \[J_B=(1-t)\frac{y+2z}{y+z}-\frac{z+x}{2s},\qquad J_C=(1-t)\frac{y}{y+z}+t\,\frac{x}{x+z}-\frac{x+y}{2s}.\] Substituting $t$ and simplifying, \[J_B=\frac{z\,\Phi}{2(y+z)\,s\,\Sigma},\qquad J_C=\frac{y\,\Phi}{2(y+z)\,s\,\Sigma},\] where $\Phi=x^2y+x^2z+xy^2+4xyz+xz^2-y^2z-yz^2$. So $J_B:J_C=z:y$. The points of the line $AA'$ are exactly those whose $B$- and $C$-coordinates are in the ratio $z:y$ (as for $A'$). Hence $J$ lies on $AA'$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 20

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

The altitudes $AA_1$, $BB_1$, $CC_1$ of a triangle $ABC$ meet its circumcircle for the second time at points $A_2$, $B_2$, $C_2$ respectively. Let $A_3$ be the common point of circles $ABC$ and $AB_1C_1$, distinct from $A$; the points $B_3$, $C_3$ are defined similarly; $A_4$, $B_4$, $C_4$ are the feet of altitudes of triangle $A_1B_1C_1$. Prove that the lines $AA_4$, $BB_4$, $CC_4$, $A_2A_3$, $B_2B_3$, $C_2C_3$ concur.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The incircle of A2B2C2 and an inversion at H)

We assume, as the official solution does, that the triangle $ABC$ is acute, and that it is scalene (if, say, $AB=AC$, the circle $AB_1C_1$ touches the circumcircle at $A$ and $A_3$ is not defined). Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle, $O$ its centre, $R$ its radius, $H$ the orthocentre, and $d=OH$; $d>0$ because the triangle is not equilateral.

Step 1: the triangle $A_2B_2C_2$. As is well known, $A_2$ is the reflection of $H$ in $BC$: $\angle BCA_2=\angle BAA_2=90^\circ-\angle B=\angle BCH$, and $CB\perp HA_2$. So $A_1,B_1,C_1$ are the midpoints of $HA_2,HB_2,HC_2$, and the homothety $\varphi$ with centre $H$ and ratio $2$ maps the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ to $A_2B_2C_2$. Moreover $AB_2=AH=AC_2$ ($B_2$, $C_2$ are the reflections of $H$ in $CA$, $AB$), so $A$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $B_2C_2$. Also $\angle B_2A_2A=\angle B_2BA=90^\circ-\angle A=\angle ACC_2=\angle AA_2C_2$, so $A_2A$ bisects the angle $B_2A_2C_2$. Hence the bisectors $A_2A$, $B_2B$, $C_2C$ of the triangle $A_2B_2C_2$ meet at $H$: $H$ is its incentre, and $A$ is the midpoint of the arc $B_2C_2$ of $\Omega$ not containing $A_2$; similarly for $B$ and $C$. Let $\rho$ be the incircle of $A_2B_2C_2$ (centre $H$, radius $r'$), touching $B_2C_2$, $C_2A_2$, $A_2B_2$ at $T_a$, $T_b$, $T_c$. Euler's formula for the triangle $A_2B_2C_2$ (whose circumcircle is $\Omega$) gives $d^2=R^2-2Rr'$, i.e. $r'=\frac{R^2-d^2}{2R}$.

Step 2: $AA_4$ passes through $T_a$. The homothety $\varphi$ maps $A_4$ to the foot $A_4'$ of the altitude from $A_2$ in $A_2B_2C_2$, maps $A$ to $J=2A-H$, and maps $T_a$ to $H_a=2T_a-H$, the reflection of $H$ in $B_2C_2$. By the incentre-excentre lemma, the arc midpoint $A$ is the centre of a circle through $B_2$, $C_2$, the incentre $H$ and the excentre $J_a$ opposite $A_2$, with $HJ_a$ a diameter; so $J=J_a$. It suffices to show that $A_4'$, $H_a$, $J_a$ are collinear. Let $D$ be the common point of $A_2H$ and $B_2C_2$. In the triangle $A_2B_2D$ the lines $B_2H$ and $B_2J_a$ are the internal and external bisectors at $B_2$, so $(A_2,D;H,J_a)=-1$. If $A_4'=D$, the line $A_2D$ is perpendicular to $B_2C_2$ and contains $A_4'$, $H$, $J_a$ and also $H_a$. Otherwise the lines $A_4'A_2$, $A_4'D$, $A_4'H$, $A_4'J_a$ form a harmonic pencil whose first two lines are perpendicular, so $A_4'D=B_2C_2$ bisects an angle between $A_4'H$ and $A_4'J_a$: the reflection of the line $A_4'H$ in $B_2C_2$ is the line $A_4'J_a$. The reflection of $A_4'H$ passes through $H_a$, so $H_a\in A_4'J_a$, and applying $\varphi^{-1}$ gives $T_a\in AA_4$. Likewise $T_b\in BB_4$ and $T_c\in CC_4$.

Step 3: $AA_4$, $BB_4$, $CC_4$ meet at the centre $Z$ of the positive homothety taking $\rho$ to $\Omega$. Since $A_2T_b=A_2T_c$ and $HT_b=HT_c$, the line $A_2H$ is the perpendicular bisector of $T_bT_c$, so $T_bT_c\perp A_2H$. By Step 1, $BC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $HA_2$, so $T_bT_c\parallel BC$. Similarly $T_cT_a\parallel CA$ and $T_aT_b\parallel AB$. The triangles $T_aT_bT_c$ and $ABC$ have parallel corresponding sides and circumradii $r'<R$, so a homothety $\eta$ (not a translation) maps the first to the second, $\rho$ to $\Omega$, $H$ to $O$, and $T_a\mapsto A$, $T_b\mapsto B$, $T_c\mapsto C$. The lines $AT_a$, $BT_b$, $CT_c$, i.e. $AA_4$, $BB_4$, $CC_4$, pass through its centre $Z$. The vectors $\overrightarrow{HT_a}$ and $\overrightarrow{OA}=\eta(\overrightarrow{HT_a})$ are both perpendicular to $B_2C_2$ and point to the side of $B_2C_2$ not containing $A_2$ (for $\overrightarrow{OA}$: $A$ is the midpoint of the arc on that side), so the ratio of $\eta$ is $+\frac R{r'}$. From $\overrightarrow{ZO}=\frac R{r'}\overrightarrow{ZH}$ we get that $Z$ lies on the ray from $O$ through $H$, beyond $H$, with \[HZ=\frac{r'}{R-r'}\,d=\frac{d\,(R^2-d^2)}{R^2+d^2}.\]

Step 4: $A_2A_3$ passes through $Z$. The chord $AA_2$ passes through $H$, so $HA\cdot HA_2=R^2-d^2$ (the power of $H$), and $HA_2=2HA_1$, so $HA\cdot HA_1=\frac12(R^2-d^2)$; the same holds for $B$ and $C$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $H$ and power $-\frac12(R^2-d^2)$, i.e. the inversion in the circle with centre $H$ and radius $\sqrt{(R^2-d^2)/2}$ followed by the half-turn about $H$. As $H$ lies between $A$ and $A_1$, $\iota$ swaps $A\leftrightarrow A_1$, and likewise $B\leftrightarrow B_1$, $C\leftrightarrow C_1$; so it maps $\Omega$ to the circle $A_1B_1C_1$, the nine-point circle $\nu$ (centre $N$, the midpoint of $OH$; radius $\frac R2$). It maps $A_2$ to the point of the segment $HA$ at distance $\frac{HA\cdot HA_1}{HA_2}=\frac{HA}2$, i.e. to the midpoint $E_a$ of $AH$. The circle $AB_1C_1$ has diameter $AH$ (as $\angle AB_1H=\angle AC_1H=90^\circ$) and passes through $H$, so its image is a line: the line through $\iota(A)=A_1$ perpendicular to $AH$, namely $BC$. Thus $\iota(A_3)$ is a common point of $BC$ and $\nu$ different from $\iota(A)=A_1$; these common points are $A_1$ and the midpoint $M_a$ of $BC$, and $M_a\ne A_1$ because $AB\ne AC$. So $\iota(A_3)=M_a$.

The line $A_2A_3$ does not pass through $H$ (otherwise $A_3$ would lie on $AA_2\cap\Omega=\{A,A_2\}$, whereas $\iota(A_3)=M_a$ differs from $\iota(A)=A_1$ and from $\iota(A_2)=E_a$, which is at distance $R$ from $M_a$, see below). So $\iota$ maps it to the circle $\kappa_a$ through $H$, $E_a$, $M_a$. With $O$ as the origin, $H=A+B+C$ and $E_a-M_a=\frac{A+H}2-\frac{B+C}2=A$ has length $R$, so $E_aM_a$ is a diameter of $\nu$, and $N$ is its midpoint. Hence the power of $N$ with respect to $\kappa_a$ is $\overline{NE_a}\cdot\overline{NM_a}=-\frac{R^2}4$. The line $OH$ passes through $N$ and through $H\in\kappa_a$, so its second common point $W$ with $\kappa_a$ satisfies $\overline{NH}\cdot\overline{NW}=-\frac{R^2}4$; this condition does not depend on the vertex. Hence $\kappa_a$, $\kappa_b$, $\kappa_c$ pass through the same point $W$ (and $W\ne H$, since the power is negative while $\overline{NH}^2>0$), and the lines $A_2A_3$, $B_2B_3$, $C_2C_3$ pass through $\iota(W)$.

Measure signed distances on the line $OH$ from $H$ towards $O$. Then $N=\frac d2$ and $\overline{NH}=-\frac d2$, so $\overline{NW}=\frac{R^2}{2d}$, $W=\frac{R^2+d^2}{2d}$, and \[\iota(W)=-\frac{R^2-d^2}2\cdot\frac{2d}{R^2+d^2}=-\frac{d\,(R^2-d^2)}{R^2+d^2}.\] By Step 3 this is exactly the point $Z$.

Therefore all six lines pass through $Z$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 2 (Ceva for the first three lines, barycentrics for the rest)

We assume, as the official solution does, that the triangle is acute, and that it is scalene (if, say, $AB=AC$, the circle $AB_1C_1$ touches the circumcircle at $A$ and $A_3$ is not defined). Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be its angles. Step 1: $AA_4$, $BB_4$, $CC_4$ concur at $Z=(\tan\alpha\cos2\alpha:\tan\beta\cos2\beta:\tan\gamma\cos2\gamma)$. $B,C,B_1,C_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, so $\angle AB_1C_1=\beta$, $\angle AC_1B_1=\gamma$: triangle $AB_1C_1$ is similar to $ABC$ ($B_1\leftrightarrow B$, $C_1\leftrightarrow C$) by a similarity that reflects in the bisector of angle $A$. Hence if $U\in B_1C_1$ divides $C_1B_1$ in the (signed) ratio $\lambda$, the reflection of the line $AU$ in the bisector of $A$ meets $BC$ at the point dividing $CB$ in the ratio $\lambda$; and isogonal cevians $AX$, $AX'$ satisfy $\frac{BX}{XC}\cdot\frac{BX'}{X'C}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}$. So $AU$ meets $BC$ at a point $U'$ with $\frac{BU'}{U'C}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}\lambda$.

For $U=A_4$ (the foot from $A_1$): the orthic triangle has $A_1C_1=b\cos\beta$, $A_1B_1=c\cos\gamma$, and angles $180^\circ-2\gamma$ at $C_1$ and $180^\circ-2\beta$ at $B_1$, so $\lambda=\frac{C_1A_4}{A_4B_1}=\frac{b\cos\beta\cos2\gamma}{c\cos\gamma\cos2\beta}$ (signed). Therefore \[\frac{BU'}{U'C}=\frac{c\cos\beta\cos2\gamma}{b\cos\gamma\cos2\beta}=\frac{\tan\gamma\cos2\gamma}{\tan\beta\cos2\beta}.\] The cyclic product of these ratios is $1$, so by Ceva the three lines concur, at the point $Z$ with the stated barycentric coordinates. In Conway's notation ($S_A=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}2$, etc., $S^2=S_AS_B+S_BS_C+S_CS_A$) we have $\tan\alpha=\frac{S}{S_A}$ and $\cos2\alpha=\frac{S_A^2-S^2}{b^2c^2}$, hence \[Z=\Bigl(\frac{a^2(S_A^2-S^2)}{S_A}:\frac{b^2(S_B^2-S^2)}{S_B}:\frac{c^2(S_C^2-S^2)}{S_C}\Bigr).\]

Step 2: the points $A_2$ and $A_3$. The circumcircle is $a^2yz+b^2zx+c^2xy=0$. The line $AA_1$ consists of the points $(\mu:S_C:S_B)$; its second common point with the circle is \[A_2=\bigl(-S_BS_C(S_B+S_C):\ S_C(S^2+S_BS_C):\ S_B(S^2+S_BS_C)\bigr).\] The circle $AB_1C_1$ has diameter $AH$, so $\angle AA_3H=90^\circ$; the antipode $A^*$ of $A$ also gives $\angle AA_3A^*=90^\circ$, so $A_3$, $H$, $A^*$ are collinear, and this line passes through the midpoint $M_a$ of $BC$ (the midpoint of $HA^*$). Intersecting the line through $H=(S_BS_C:S_CS_A:S_AS_B)$ and $M_a=(0:1:1)$ with the circle gives $A^*$ and \[A_3=\Bigl(S_BS_C(S_B+S_C):\ -S_AS_C(S_B-S_C):\ S_AS_B(S_B-S_C)\Bigr)\] (one checks that it satisfies the circle equation and the collinearity with $H$, $M_a$).

Step 3: $Z$ lies on $A_2A_3$. Multiplying the coordinates of $Z$ by $S_AS_BS_C$ and using $a^2=S_B+S_C$, etc., \[Z=\bigl(S_BS_C(S_B+S_C)(S_A^2-S^2):\ S_CS_A(S_C+S_A)(S_B^2-S^2):\ S_AS_B(S_A+S_B)(S_C^2-S^2)\bigr).\] The line $A_2A_3$ has the equation \[S_A(S_B-S_C)(S_AS_B+S_AS_C+2S_BS_C)\,x+S_B^2(S_A+S_C)(S_B+S_C)\,y-S_C^2(S_A+S_B)(S_B+S_C)\,z=0\] (to check it, note that $S^2+S_BS_C=S_AS_B+S_AS_C+2S_BS_C$; after substituting $A_2$, the factor $S_BS_C(S_B+S_C)(S^2+S_BS_C)$ comes out and leaves $-S_A(S_B-S_C)+S_B(S_A+S_C)-S_C(S_A+S_B)=0$; after substituting $A_3$, the factor $S_AS_BS_C(S_B+S_C)(S_B-S_C)$ comes out and leaves $(S_AS_B+S_AS_C+2S_BS_C)-S_B(S_A+S_C)-S_C(S_A+S_B)=0$). Substituting $Z$ and dividing by the common factor $S_AS_BS_C(S_B+S_C)$, the condition $Z\in A_2A_3$ becomes \[(S_B-S_C)(S_AS_B+S_AS_C+2S_BS_C)(S_A^2-S^2)+S_B(S_A+S_C)^2(S_B^2-S^2)-S_C(S_A+S_B)^2(S_C^2-S^2)=0,\] which is checked by expanding (it is useful that $S_B^2-S^2=2S_B^2-c^2a^2$, $S_C^2-S^2=2S_C^2-a^2b^2$, and so on). So the line $A_2A_3$ passes through $Z$, and by symmetry so do $B_2B_3$ and $C_2C_3$. All six lines pass through $Z$. $\blacksquare$

Remark. $Z$ lies on the Euler line (it is the point $X_{24}$ of Kimberling's list).

Problem 21

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle A=2\pi/3$; $P$ be an arbitrary point inside this triangle lying on the bisector of angle $A$; the lines $BP$, $CP$ meet $AC$, $AB$ at points $E$, $F$ respectively; $D$ be an arbitrary point on the side $BC$; the lines $DE$, $DF$ meet $PC$, $PB$ at points $M$, $N$ respectively. Find the value of angle $\angle MAN$.

Show the answer

$\angle MAN=60^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Menelaus and the ratio lemma)

Since $AP$ bisects the angle of $120^\circ$, $\angle BAP=\angle PAC=60^\circ$.

Positions. The segment $DE$ separates $C$ from the quadrilateral $ABDE$, which contains the diagonal $BE$ and hence $P$; so $DE$ crosses the segment $PC$: $M$ lies on the segment $PC$. Likewise $N$ lies on the segment $PB$. Put $x=\angle PAM\in[0^\circ,60^\circ]$ and $y=\angle PAN\in[0^\circ,60^\circ]$; then $\angle MAC=60^\circ-x$, $\angle BAN=60^\circ-y$ and $\angle MAN=x+y$.

A product independent of $D$. Menelaus for triangle $PBC$ and the line $D,M,E$ ($E$ on the line $BP$), and for the same triangle and the line $D,N,F$: \[\frac{PM}{MC}=\frac{DB}{DC}\cdot\frac{EP}{EB},\qquad \frac{PN}{NB}=\frac{DC}{DB}\cdot\frac{FP}{FC},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{PM}{MC}\cdot\frac{PN}{NB}=\frac{EP}{EB}\cdot\frac{FP}{FC}.\] Now $\frac{EP}{EB}=\frac{[AEP]}{[AEB]}=\frac{\frac12AE\cdot AP\sin60^\circ}{\frac12AE\cdot AB\sin120^\circ}=\frac{AP}{AB}$ (here the angle $120^\circ$ is used), and likewise $\frac{FP}{FC}=\frac{AP}{AC}$. Hence \[\frac{PM}{MC}\cdot\frac{PN}{NB}=\frac{AP^2}{AB\cdot AC}.\tag{1}\]

The ratio lemma. $\frac{PM}{MC}=\frac{[APM]}{[AMC]}=\frac{AP\sin x}{AC\sin(60^\circ-x)}$ and $\frac{PN}{NB}=\frac{AP\sin y}{AB\sin(60^\circ-y)}$. With (1): \[\frac{\sin x}{\sin(60^\circ-x)}\cdot\frac{\sin y}{\sin(60^\circ-y)}=1 .\] The function $g(t)=\frac{\sin t}{\sin(60^\circ-t)}$ is increasing on $[0^\circ,60^\circ)$ and $g(60^\circ-t)=\frac1{g(t)}$. So $g(y)=\frac1{g(x)}=g(60^\circ-x)$ forces $y=60^\circ-x$ (the end positions $D=B$, $D=C$ give $x=0$, $y=60^\circ$ or $x=60^\circ$, $y=0$ directly). Therefore \[\angle MAN=x+y=\boxed{60^\circ}.\]

Solution 2 (A projectivity of the pencil at A)

The map. As $D$ moves on the line $BC$, $M=DE\cap PC$ and $N=DF\cap PB$ move. The map $AM\mapsto AN$ between lines through $A$ is a composition of perspectivities: line $AM$ $\mapsto$ point $M$ on $PC$ $\mapsto$ (from $E$) point $D$ on $BC$ $\mapsto$ (from $F$) point $N$ on $PB$ $\mapsto$ line $AN$. So it is a projectivity of the pencil of lines through $A$, and it is determined by the images of three lines.

Three positions. Note that $\angle BAP=\angle PAC=60^\circ$, so the lines $AB$, $AP$, $AC$ make angles of $60^\circ$ with each other.

  • $D=B$: $DE=BE\ni P$, so $M=P$; $DF=BA$, so $N=B$. The line $AP$ goes to $AB$.

  • $D=C$: $M=C$ and $N=P$. The line $AC$ goes to $AP$.

  • $D=EF\cap BC$ (this may be the point at infinity of $BC$ when $EF\parallel BC$; the projectivity is defined on the whole projective line $BC$): then $DE=DF=EF$, so $M=EF\cap PC=F$ and $N=EF\cap PB=E$. The line $AB$ goes to $AC$.

The rotation $\rho$ about $A$ by $60^\circ$ taking the line $AP$ to $AB$ also takes $AC$ to $AP$ and $AB$ to $AC$ (as lines, i.e. modulo $180^\circ$). A rotation of the pencil is a projectivity, and it agrees with our map on three lines, so the two coincide: for every $D$ the line $AN$ is $\rho(AM)$, and the lines $AM$, $AN$ make an angle of $60^\circ$.

The angle between the rays. For $D$ on the side $BC$, $M$ lies on the segment $PC$ and $N$ on the segment $PB$ (the segment $DE$ separates $C$ from $P$, and similarly for $DF$). So the ray $AM$ lies in the angle $PAC$ and the ray $AN$ in the angle $BAP$; the ray $AN$ is the image of the ray $AM$ under the rotation by $60^\circ$ towards $AB$. Hence $\angle MAN=\boxed{60^\circ}$.

Problem 22

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry

The incircle of a triangle $ABC$ touches $BC$ at point $D$. Let $F$ be the Feuerbach point, $H$ be the projection of $A$ to $DF$. Prove that $FH:DF=1:2$.

Show the solution

Solution (Complex numbers with the incircle as unit circle)

We assume $AB\ne AC$: otherwise $F=D$ and the line $DF$ is not defined. Take the incircle as the unit circle (centre $I=0$) and let $d,e,f$ be the touch points on $BC$, $CA$, $AB$. Write $\sigma_1=d+e+f$, $\sigma_2=de+ef+fd$, $\sigma_3=def$; for unit complex numbers $\bar\sigma_1=\sigma_2/\sigma_3$ and $\bar\sigma_2=\sigma_1/\sigma_3$. The standard formulas are \[A=\frac{2ef}{e+f},\qquad O=\frac{2\sigma_3\sigma_1}{\Pi},\qquad H_0=\frac{2(d^2e^2+e^2f^2+f^2d^2+\sigma_3\sigma_1)}{\Pi},\] where $H_0$ is the orthocentre and $\Pi=(d+e)(e+f)(f+d)=\sigma_1\sigma_2-\sigma_3$.

The Feuerbach point. The nine-point centre is \[N=\frac{O+H_0}{2}=\frac{d^2e^2+e^2f^2+f^2d^2+2\sigma_3\sigma_1}{\Pi}=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\Pi}.\] The incircle touches the nine-point circle internally at $F$, so $F$ is the point of the unit circle in the direction opposite to $N$: $F=-\frac{N}{|N|}$ (the triangle is not equilateral, so $N\ne0$). Since $\bar\Pi=\Pi/\sigma_3^2$ we get $\bar N=\sigma_1^2/\Pi$, so $F^2=\frac{N}{\bar N}=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2}$ and $F=\pm\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}$. To fix the sign, note $-N\big/\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}=-\frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\Pi}=\frac{t}{1-t}$ with $t=\frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_3}=|\sigma_1|^2$. Here $\sigma_1$ is the orthocentre of the triangle $def$, which is acute (its angles are $90^\circ-\frac A2$, ...), so $|\sigma_1|<1$ and $\frac t{1-t}>0$. Hence \[F=\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}=\frac{de+ef+fd}{d+e+f}.\] Note that $F\ne d$: equality means $de+ef+fd=d^2+de+df$, i.e. $ef=d^2$, i.e. $d$ is the midpoint of one of the arcs $ef$; then the reflection in the line $Id$ fixes the incircle and the tangent $BC$ at $d$ and swaps the tangents $CA$, $AB$ at $e$, $f$, so it swaps $B$ and $C$ and $AB=AC$.

The projection. The foot of the perpendicular from a point $z$ to the chord through unit points $p,q$ is $\frac12(p+q+z-pq\bar z)$. With $p=d$, $q=F$, $z=A$ and $\bar A=\frac2{e+f}$, the foot $H$ of $A$ on $DF$ satisfies \[H=\tfrac12\Bigl(d+F+\frac{2ef-2dF}{e+f}\Bigr).\] Now $F(d+e+f)=de+ef+fd$ means $ef-dF=(F-d)(e+f)$, so $\frac{2ef-2dF}{e+f}=2F-2d$ and \[H=\tfrac12(3F-d),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad H-F=\tfrac12(F-d).\] So $F$ lies between $D$ and $H$ and $FH:DF=1:2$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 23

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 53: Barycentric and Projective-Flavored Geometry

A triangle $ABC$ and a point $P$ in the plane are given. The lines $AP$, $BP$, $CP$ meet for the second time the circle $ABC$ at points $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ respectively. Find the locus of points $Q$ such that the common points of $QA'$ with $BC$, $QB'$ with $AC$, and $QC'$ with $AB$ are collinear.

Show the answer

the circumcircle of $ABC$ together with the polar of $P$ with respect to it (without the points $A',B',C'$, where the lines are undefined)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A cubic that splits)

Assume $P$ is not on the circumcircle $\Gamma$ and not on a side line. Use homogeneous barycentric coordinates, $P=(p:q:r)$, $Q=(x:y:z)$; $\Gamma$ is $a^2yz+b^2zx+c^2xy=0$.

The points. The line $AP$ consists of the points $(\mu:q:r)$; substituting in $\Gamma$ gives $A'=(-a^2qr:\ qk_a:\ rk_a)$ with $k_a=b^2r+c^2q$. Similarly $B'=(pk_b:-b^2rp:\ rk_b)$, $C'=(pk_c:\ qk_c:-c^2pq)$ with $k_b=c^2p+a^2r$, $k_c=a^2q+b^2p$. The common point of $QA'$ and $BC$ (the line $x=0$) is $-x\,A'+(\text{first coordinate of }A')\,Q$ up to a factor, i.e. \[X_a=\bigl(0:\ q(a^2ry+k_ax):\ r(a^2qz+k_ax)\bigr),\] and cyclically $X_b=\bigl(p(b^2rx+k_by):\ 0:\ r(b^2pz+k_by)\bigr)$, $X_c=\bigl(p(c^2qx+k_cz):\ q(c^2py+k_cz):\ 0\bigr)$. Each is linear in $(x,y,z)$.

The condition. $X_a,X_b,X_c$ are collinear iff the determinant of their coordinates vanishes. Expanding it, one finds the factorisation \begin{align*} \det(X_a,X_b,X_c)=pqr\,&(a^2qr+b^2rp+c^2pq)\,(a^2yz+b^2zx+c^2xy)\\ &\cdot\bigl(a^2(qz+ry)+b^2(rx+pz)+c^2(py+qx)\bigr). \end{align*} The first two factors are nonzero (P is not on a side line and not on $\Gamma$). The third vanishes iff $Q\in\Gamma$. The last one is linear in $Q$; it equals $p\,\partial_x\Gamma+q\,\partial_y\Gamma+r\,\partial_z\Gamma$ at $Q$, where $\Gamma(x,y,z)=a^2yz+b^2zx+c^2xy$; this is the equation of the polar of $P$ with respect to the circle.

Answer. The three points are collinear exactly when $Q$ lies on the circumcircle or on the polar of $P$ with respect to it (for $Q=A'$, $B'$ or $C'$ one of the lines is undefined).

Solution 2 (Pascal and a projective normalisation)

Assume $P\notin\Gamma$ (the circumcircle) and $P$ not on a side line; let $X_a=QA'\cap BC$, $X_b=QB'\cap CA$, $X_c=QC'\cap AB$.

Points of $\Gamma$ are in the locus. Let $Q\in\Gamma$. Pascal's theorem for the hexagon $QA'ACBB'$ (all on $\Gamma$) says that $QA'\cap CB=X_a$, $A'A\cap BB'=P$ and $AC\cap B'Q=X_b$ are collinear. Likewise (hexagon $QB'BACC'$) $X_b$, $P$, $X_c$ are collinear. So $X_a,X_b,X_c$ lie on one line through $P$.

Points of the polar are in the locus. First let $P$ be inside $\Gamma$. There is a projective transformation mapping $\Gamma$ to a circle and $P$ to its centre; it maps the polar of $P$ to the line at infinity and preserves the statement. So we may assume $P=O$ is the centre, $A'=-A$, $B'=-B$, $C'=-C$ (vectors from $O$), and $Q$ is a point at infinity, i.e. the lines $QA'$, $QB'$, $QC'$ are parallel to a direction perpendicular to a vector $n$. The point $X_a=B+\mu(C-B)$ of $BC$ with $(X_a+A)\cdot n=0$ has $\frac{BX_a}{X_aC}=-\frac{(A+B)\cdot n}{(A+C)\cdot n}$; cyclically $\frac{CX_b}{X_bA}=-\frac{(B+C)\cdot n}{(B+A)\cdot n}$ and $\frac{AX_c}{X_cB}=-\frac{(C+A)\cdot n}{(C+B)\cdot n}$. The product is $-1$, so by Menelaus $X_a,X_b,X_c$ are collinear.

The collinearity condition is a polynomial identity in the coordinates of $P$ and of a point running on the polar of $P$; it holds for all $P$ in an open set (inside $\Gamma$), hence for all $P$.

Nothing else. In coordinates, $X_a$ depends linearly on $Q$ (it is the intersection of the fixed line $BC$ with the line through $Q$ and $A'$), and so do $X_b$, $X_c$; hence the collinearity condition $\det(X_a,X_b,X_c)=0$ is a curve of degree at most $3$ in $Q$. It contains the conic $\Gamma$ and a line not contained in $\Gamma$. A cubic polynomial vanishing on an irreducible conic is divisible by its equation, and the remaining linear factor must vanish on the polar. The cubic is not identically zero (for a generic $Q$, e.g. $Q=P$ itself, the three points are the feet of the cevians through $P$, which are not collinear). So the locus is exactly $\Gamma$ together with the polar of $P$ (minus the points $A',B',C'$ where a line is undefined).

Problem 24

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

The insphere of a tetrahedron $ABCD$ touches the face $ABC$ at its orthocenter $H$ and touches the faces $ABD$, $ACD$ at points $P$, $Q$ respectively. The lines $DP$, $DQ$ meet the plane $ABC$ at points $X$, $Y$. Prove that $\angle XHY=2\angle XAY$.

Show the solution

Solution (Angles at the points of tangency)

Let $R$ be the point where the insphere touches the face $BCD$; let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the angles of triangle $ABC$. Since the sphere touches $ABC$ at an interior point $H$, the triangle $ABC$ is acute and $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\alpha$, $\angle CHA=180^\circ-\beta$, $\angle AHB=180^\circ-\gamma$.

Equal angles at touch points. Tangent segments from a point to a sphere are equal, so for an edge, say $AB$, the triangles $AHB$ and $APB$ have equal sides ($AH=AP$, $BH=BP$, common $AB$) and are congruent; the same holds for every edge. Hence \[\angle APB=\angle AHB,\qquad \angle BRC=\angle BHC,\qquad \angle AQC=\angle AHC,\] \[\angle APD=\angle AQD,\qquad \angle BPD=\angle BRD,\qquad \angle CQD=\angle CRD.\] Put $u=\angle BPD=\angle BRD$, $v=\angle APD=\angle AQD$, $w=\angle CQD=\angle CRD$. The angles around $P$, $Q$, $R$ (interior points of their faces) sum to $360^\circ$; since $\angle APB=180^\circ-\gamma$, $\angle AQC=180^\circ-\beta$, $\angle BRC=180^\circ-\alpha$, this gives \[u+v=180^\circ+\gamma,\qquad v+w=180^\circ+\beta,\qquad u+w=180^\circ+\alpha.\] Adding, $u+v+w=360^\circ$, whence $v=180^\circ-\alpha$ (and $u=180^\circ-\beta$, $w=180^\circ-\gamma$).

Where $X$ is. The line $DP$ lies in the plane $ABD$, which meets the plane $ABC$ in the line $AB$; so $X\in AB$, and $X$ is on the extension of $DP$ beyond $P$. Hence $\angle APX=180^\circ-\angle APD=\alpha$ and $\angle BPX=180^\circ-u=\beta$; since $\alpha+\beta=\angle APB$, the ray $PX$ lies inside the angle $APB$ and $X$ is on the segment $AB$. Rotating the face $ABD$ about $AB$ onto the triangle $AHB$ (the congruence above) fixes the line $AB$ pointwise and takes $P$ to $H$; therefore $\angle AHX=\angle APX=\alpha$.

In the same way $Y$ lies on the segment $AC$ and $\angle AHY=\angle AQY=180^\circ-v=\alpha$.

Conclusion. The points $X\in AB$ and $Y\in AC$ lie on different sides of the line $AH$ (which enters the interior of the angle $BAC$), so \[\angle XHY=\angle XHA+\angle AHY=2\alpha=2\angle BAC=2\angle XAY. \qquad\blacksquare\]

Problem 8.1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $Q_1$ be a cyclic quadrilateral with circumcircle $\omega$. The bisectors of the angles between its diagonals meet $\omega$ at four points forming a quadrilateral $Q_2$. Prove that the lines joining the midpoints of the diagonals of $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are perpendicular.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle with diameter OX)

Let $O$ be the centre of $\omega$, let $Q_1=ABCD$ and let its diagonals $AC$, $BD$ meet at $X$. If $X=O$, both diagonals of $Q_1$ are diameters, the midpoints of all diagonals coincide with $O$ and the lines in the statement do not exist; so $X\ne O$.

The two bisectors $\ell_1,\ell_2$ of the angles between $AC$ and $BD$ are perpendicular lines through $X$. Each of them is a chord line of $\omega$, and the two chords cross at the interior point $X$, so their four endpoints alternate on $\omega$: they are the vertices of $Q_2$ and the two chords are the diagonals of $Q_2$.

Let $M,N$ be the midpoints of $AC,BD$ and $K,L$ the midpoints of the chords on $\ell_1,\ell_2$. The midpoint of a chord is the foot of the perpendicular from $O$ to the chord. So each of $M,N,K,L$ is either the point $X$ itself or a point from which the segment $OX$ is seen at a right angle (for instance $\angle OMX=90^\circ$ if $M\neq X$). In both cases it lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with diameter $OX$. The tangent to $\Gamma$ at $X$ is the line through $X$ perpendicular to $OX$.

Let $e_1,e_2$ be unit vectors along $\ell_1,\ell_2$; they are perpendicular, so $O-X=((O-X)\cdot e_1)e_1+((O-X)\cdot e_2)e_2$. The feet of the perpendiculars from $O$ are $K=X+((O-X)\cdot e_1)e_1$ and $L=X+((O-X)\cdot e_2)e_2$, hence $K+L=O+X$. So the midpoint of $KL$ is the centre of $\Gamma$, and $KL$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$; in particular $K\neq L$.

Each of the lines $AC$, $BD$, $\ell_1$ passes through $X$ and meets $\Gamma$ a second time at $M$, $N$, $K$ respectively, or touches $\Gamma$ at $X$ when that point is $X$ (for instance $M=X$ means $OX\perp AC$, i.e. $AC$ is the tangent at $X$). In a circle of diameter $d$, if two lines through a point $X$ of the circle meet it again at $Z$ and $W$ (a tangent line “meets it again” at $X$ itself), then $ZW=d\sin\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between the two lines: this is the inscribed angle theorem, or the tangent-chord theorem when one line is the tangent. The lines $\ell_1$ and $AC$ cannot both be tangent at $X$, because they are different lines. Hence \[KM=OX\cdot\sin\angle(\ell_1,AC),\qquad KN=OX\cdot\sin\angle(\ell_1,BD).\] The line $\ell_1$ makes equal angles with $AC$ and $BD$, hence $KM=KN$. In the same way $LM=LN$. Moreover $M\neq N$: the point $M$ lies on $AC$ and $N$ on $BD$, so $M=N$ would force $M=N=X$, i.e. $OX\perp AC$ and $OX\perp BD$, and the two diagonals would coincide. Thus $K$ and $L$ are two distinct points of the perpendicular bisector of $MN$. Therefore the line $KL$ is the perpendicular bisector of $MN$, and the lines $MN$ and $KL$ joining the midpoints of the diagonals of $Q_1$ and of $Q_2$ are perpendicular.

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the bisectors)

Put the origin at the common point $X$ of the diagonals of $Q_1$ and take the two (perpendicular) bisectors of the angles between the diagonals as the coordinate axes. Then the diagonals of $Q_1$ are symmetric to each other with respect to the axes: they have unit direction vectors \[e=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad f=(\cos\theta,-\sin\theta),\qquad 0<\theta<90^\circ .\] The diagonals of $Q_2$ are the chords of $\omega$ on the two axes. Let the centre of $\omega$ be $O=(p,q)$. If $X=O$, both diagonals of $Q_1$ are diameters, all the midpoints coincide with $O$ and the lines in the statement do not exist; so $X\ne O$, i.e. $(p,q)\neq(0,0)$.

The midpoint of a chord lying on a line through the origin with unit direction $g$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $O$ to that line, namely $(O\cdot g)\,g$. Hence the midpoints of the diagonals are \[M=(p\cos\theta+q\sin\theta)\,e,\qquad N=(p\cos\theta-q\sin\theta)\,f\qquad\text{for }Q_1,\] \[K=(p,0),\qquad L=(0,q)\qquad\text{for }Q_2 .\] A short computation gives \[M-N=\bigl(2q\sin\theta\cos\theta,\ 2p\sin\theta\cos\theta\bigr)=\sin2\theta\,(q,p),\qquad L-K=(-p,q).\] Both vectors are non-zero, since $\sin2\theta\neq0$ and $(p,q)\neq(0,0)$, and \[(M-N)\cdot(L-K)=\sin2\theta\,(-qp+pq)=0 .\] So $MN\perp KL$, which is what we had to prove.

Problem 8.2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 43: Locus, Transformation, and Construction Geometry

A non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ is given. Let $X$ and $Y$ be arbitrary points on the lines $AB$ and $AC$ respectively, lying on the same side of $BC$ in such a way that $BX=CY$. Find the locus of points $D$ obtained by reflecting $A$ about the perpendicular bisectors of the segments $XY$.

Show the answer

the circle with centre at the midpoint $S$ of the arc $BAC$ of the circumcircle of $ABC$ and passing through $A$, without two points: the point diametrically opposite to $A$ on this circle, and the reflection of $A$ in the perpendicular bisector of $BC$

Show the solution

Solution (A rotation about the midpoint of the arc)

Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle and $S$ the midpoint of the arc $BAC$. Since $AB\neq AC$, $S\neq A$; also $SB=SC$, and $S$ lies on the external bisector of angle $A$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle with centre $S$ through $A$ and let $A^*=2S-A$ be the point of $\Gamma$ opposite to $A$.

Parametrisation. A point of the line $AB$ other than $B$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $A$ exactly when it lies on the ray $BA$; the same holds for $AC$. Hence the condition on $X,Y$ means: for some real $t\neq0$, \[X=B+t\,u,\qquad Y=C+t\,v,\] where $u,v$ are the unit vectors in the directions $\overrightarrow{BA}$, $\overrightarrow{CA}$ ($t>0$: both points on the side of $A$; $t<0$: both on the other side).

The rotation. Let $\rho$ be the rotation about $S$ through the oriented angle $\varphi=\angle BSC$; then $\rho(B)=C$. The points $S$ and $A$ lie on the same arc $BC$ of $\Omega$, so $\varphi$ equals the oriented angle $\angle BAC$, which is also the oriented angle from the vector $\overrightarrow{BA}$ to the vector $\overrightarrow{CA}$. So $\rho$ turns $u$ into $v$, and therefore \[\rho(X)=\rho(B)+t\,v=C+t\,v=Y\qquad\text{for every }t .\] In particular $SX=SY$. Moreover $X\neq Y$: otherwise $X=Y$ would be the common point $A$ of the lines $AB,AC$, which needs $t=AB$ and $t=AC$ at once. So the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $XY$ exists and passes through $S$; it is the bisector of the angle $XSY$, i.e. the line obtained from the line $SX$ by rotating it about $S$ through $\varphi/2$.

$D$ lies on $\Gamma$. The reflection in $m$ fixes $S$, so $SD=SA$.

Which points of $\Gamma$ occur. Reflecting $A$ in the lines through $S$ gives a one-to-one correspondence between these lines and the points of $\Gamma$ (the line belonging to $D\neq A$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, the line belonging to $A$ is $SA$). As $X$ runs over the line $AB$, the line $SX$ runs over all lines through $S$ except the parallel to $AB$, each once ($S\notin AB$). Rotating through $\varphi/2$, the line $m$ runs over all lines through $S$ except

  • the line $\ell_\infty$ obtained from the parallel to $AB$; its direction bisects the directions of $\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\overrightarrow{CA}$, so $\ell_\infty$ is parallel to the internal bisector of angle $A$, i.e. perpendicular to $SA$; the reflection of $A$ in $\ell_\infty$ is $A^*$;

  • the line obtained from $SB$, i.e. the bisector of angle $BSC$, which is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ (because $SB=SC$); it corresponds to $t=0$, i.e. to the excluded pair $X=B$, $Y=C$, and gives the point $D_0$.

These two lines are different ($SB\nparallel AB$), so $A^*\neq D_0$.

Hence the locus is the circle $\Gamma$ with centre $S$ through $A$, without the two points $A^*$ and $D_0$. (The point $D_0$ would require $X=B$, $Y=C$; these lie on $BC$, which the condition excludes.)

Problem 8.3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

The incircle of a non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ touches the sides $BC$ and $AC$ at points $A'$ and $B'$ respectively. The lines $AA'$ and $BB'$ meet at point $G$, and the altitudes of the triangle meet at point $H$. Prove that $A$, $B$, $G$, and $H$ are concyclic if and only if \[\angle AGC=\angle BGC.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (From the bisector condition to a cyclic quadrilateral)

Write $a,b,c$ for the sides, $s$ for the semiperimeter and \[x=s-a,\quad y=s-b,\quad z=s-c,\qquad\text{so}\quad a=y+z,\ b=z+x,\ c=x+y .\] The tangent lengths are $AB'=AC'=x$, $BA'=BC'=y$, $CA'=CB'=z$, where $C'$ is the touch point on $AB$. By Ceva's theorem $CC'$ also passes through the Gergonne point $G$, and $G$ lies inside the triangle. We assume that the triangle has no right angle at $A$ or $B$ (otherwise $H$ is a vertex and the four points are not four points).

Step 1: $A,B,G,H$ are concyclic $\iff\angle AGB=180^\circ-\angle C$. For directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ one has $\measuredangle AHB=\measuredangle BCA$ in every triangle, and $A,B,G,H$ are concyclic iff $\measuredangle AGB=\measuredangle AHB$. Since $G$ is inside the triangle, the triangles $AGB$ and $ACB$ have the same orientation, so this means $\angle AGB\equiv-\angle C\pmod{180^\circ}$, i.e. $\angle AGB=180^\circ-\angle C$.

Step 2: $\angle AGB=180^\circ-\angle C\iff\angle AA'C+\angle BB'C=180^\circ$. The quadrilateral $CA'GB'$ is convex and $\angle A'GB'=\angle AGB$ (vertical angles), so \[\angle C+\angle CA'G+\angle A'GB'+\angle GB'C=360^\circ ,\] and the claim follows because $\angle CA'G=\angle CA'A$, $\angle GB'C=\angle CB'B$.

Step 3: $\angle AGC=\angle BGC\iff a\cdot AA'=b\cdot BB'$. The three angles $\angle AGB,\angle BGC,\angle CGA$ add up to $360^\circ$, and the ray $GC'$ is opposite to $GC$. Hence $\angle AGC=\angle BGC$ iff $\angle AGC'=\angle C'GB$, i.e. iff $GC'$ bisects the angle $AGB$. By the bisector theorem and its converse ($C'$ is on the segment $AB$) this happens iff \[\frac{GA}{GB}=\frac{AC'}{C'B}=\frac{x}{y}. \tag{1}\] By van Aubel's theorem $\dfrac{AG}{GA'}=\dfrac{AC'}{C'B}+\dfrac{AB'}{B'C}=\dfrac xy+\dfrac xz=\dfrac{xa}{yz}$, so $AG=\dfrac{xa}{xa+yz}\,AA'$, and similarly $BG=\dfrac{yb}{yb+xz}\,BB'$. The two denominators are equal: $xa+yz=xy+xz+yz=yb+xz$. Therefore (1) becomes $\dfrac{xa\cdot AA'}{yb\cdot BB'}=\dfrac xy$, that is, $a\cdot AA'=b\cdot BB'$.

Step 4: $a\cdot AA'=b\cdot BB'\iff\angle AA'C+\angle BB'C=180^\circ$. Twice the area of $ABC$ equals $a\cdot AA'\sin\angle AA'C$ and also $b\cdot BB'\sin\angle BB'C$. So $a\cdot AA'=b\cdot BB'$ iff $\sin\angle AA'C=\sin\angle BB'C$, i.e. iff the two angles are equal or supplementary. They cannot be equal: the triangles $AA'C$ and $BB'C$ have the common angle $C$ and $CA'=CB'=z$, so equal angles at $A'$ and $B'$ would make them congruent and give $CA=CB$, which is excluded. So the condition is $\angle AA'C+\angle BB'C=180^\circ$.

Combining Steps 1–4: $A,B,G,H$ are concyclic $\iff\angle AA'C+\angle BB'C=180^\circ\iff\angle AGC=\angle BGC$. (Along the way we found a third equivalent condition: $C,A',G,B'$ are concyclic.)

Solution 2 (Tangent lengths and Stewart's theorem)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides, $s$ the semiperimeter, $x=s-a$, $y=s-b$, $z=s-c$ (so $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$), and $\sigma=xy+yz+zx$. Let $C'$ be the touch point on $AB$. The tangent lengths are $AB'=AC'=x$, $BA'=BC'=y$, $CA'=CB'=z$. By Ceva's theorem $CC'$ passes through the Gergonne point $G$, which lies inside the triangle. We assume there is no right angle at $A$ or $B$ (otherwise $H$ is a vertex).

Van Aubel. By van Aubel's theorem $\dfrac{AG}{GA'}=\dfrac{AC'}{C'B}+\dfrac{AB'}{B'C}=\dfrac xy+\dfrac xz=\dfrac{xa}{yz}$, so $AG=\dfrac{xa}{xa+yz}\,AA'=\dfrac{xa}{\sigma}\,AA'$, and in the same way $BG=\dfrac{yb}{\sigma}\,BB'$.

The concyclicity condition. For directed angles, $\measuredangle AHB=\measuredangle BCA$, so $A,B,G,H$ are concyclic iff $\measuredangle AGB=\measuredangle BCA$. Since $G$ is inside the triangle, this means $\angle AGB=180^\circ-\angle C$, i.e. $\angle A'GB'+\angle C=180^\circ$ in the convex quadrilateral $CA'GB'$ ($\angle A'GB'=\angle AGB$ as vertical angles), i.e. $C,A',G,B'$ are concyclic. By the power of the point $A$ (the line $AC$ meets this circle at $C,B'$, the line $AA'$ at $A',G$) this is equivalent to \[AG\cdot AA'=AB'\cdot AC=xb. \tag{2}\] With $AG=\dfrac{xa}{\sigma}\,AA'$, (2) reads $a\,AA'^2=b\,\sigma$.

Stewart's theorem. Since $BA'=y$ and $A'C=z$, \[a\,AA'^2=b^2y+c^2z-ayz=(x+z)^2y+(x+y)^2z-(y+z)yz=x\,(\sigma+3yz).\] Hence (2) becomes $x\sigma+3xyz=(x+z)\sigma$, i.e. \[\sigma=3xy. \tag{3}\]

The angle condition. Since the ray $GC'$ is opposite to $GC$, $\angle AGC=\angle BGC$ iff $GC'$ bisects $\angle AGB$, i.e. (bisector theorem and its converse, $C'\in AB$) iff $\dfrac{GA}{GB}=\dfrac{AC'}{C'B}=\dfrac xy$; and with $AG=\frac{xa}{\sigma}AA'$, $BG=\frac{yb}{\sigma}BB'$ this is $a\cdot AA'=b\cdot BB'$, or $a\cdot(a\,AA'^2)=b\cdot(b\,BB'^2)$. By Stewart's theorem again, $b\,BB'^2=y(\sigma+3xz)$. So the condition is \[(y+z)\,x(\sigma+3yz)=(x+z)\,y(\sigma+3xz).\] Expanding, the difference of the two sides is \[z\sigma(x-y)-3xyz(x-y)=z\,(x-y)\,(\sigma-3xy).\] Since $z>0$ and $x\neq y$ (that is, $a\neq b$), the angle condition is also equivalent to (3). Both conditions are equivalent to $\sigma=3xy$, i.e. to $\dfrac 2z=\dfrac1x+\dfrac1y$, so they are equivalent to each other.

Problem 8.4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $BB_1$ and $CC_1$ be the symmedians of a triangle $ABC$. Let $M$, $N$, and $A_0$ be the midpoints of $BB_1$, $CC_1$, and $BC$ respectively. The line $A_0M$ meets $CN$ at point $P$, and the line $A_0N$ meets $BM$ at point $Q$. Prove that the circle through $A_0$, $P$, and $Q$ is tangent to $BC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Locating P and Q, then an opposite similarity)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides. We use the classical fact that a symmedian divides the opposite side in the ratio of the squares of the adjacent sides: $AC_1:C_1B=b^2:a^2$ and $AB_1:B_1C=c^2:a^2$.

The lines. In triangle $BCB_1$ the segment $A_0M$ joins the midpoints of $BC$ and $BB_1$, so $A_0M\parallel CB_1$, i.e. $A_0M\parallel AC$. Likewise $A_0N\parallel AB$. The line $CN$ is the line $CC_1$ and $BM$ is the line $BB_1$.

The points. Use vectors with origin $C$. Then $A_0=\tfrac12 B$, $C_1=\dfrac{a^2A+b^2B}{a^2+b^2}$, and $P$ is the point of the line $CC_1$ of the form $\tfrac12B+\lambda A$. Writing $P=\mu\,C_1$ and comparing the coefficients of the independent vectors $A,B$: \[\mu\frac{b^2}{a^2+b^2}=\frac12,\qquad \lambda=\mu\frac{a^2}{a^2+b^2}=\frac{a^2}{2b^2}.\] So $\overrightarrow{A_0P}=\dfrac{a^2}{2b^2}\,\overrightarrow{CA}$, and in the same way (origin $B$) $\overrightarrow{A_0Q}=\dfrac{a^2}{2c^2}\,\overrightarrow{BA}$. In particular \[A_0P=\frac{a^2}{2b},\qquad A_0Q=\frac{a^2}{2c}. \tag{1}\]

An opposite similarity. The ray $A_0P$ has the direction of $\overrightarrow{CA}$ and the ray $A_0Q$ the direction of $\overrightarrow{BA}$; these are the directions of $\overrightarrow{AC}$, $\overrightarrow{AB}$ reversed, so the angle $PA_0Q$ equals the angle $A$, but its orientation (from $A_0P$ to $A_0Q$) is the orientation from $AC$ to $AB$, opposite to the orientation from $AB$ to $AC$. By (1), $A_0P:A_0Q=c:b=AB:AC$. Hence triangle $A_0PQ$ is similar to triangle $ABC$ with $A_0\mapsto A$, $P\mapsto B$, $Q\mapsto C$, and the similarity reverses orientation. An orientation-reversing similarity reverses directed angles, so \[\measuredangle(QA_0,QP)=-\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\measuredangle(CB,CA).\]

Tangency. By the tangent–chord theorem (with directed angles modulo $180^\circ$), a line $t$ through $A_0$ is tangent to the circle $A_0PQ$ iff $\measuredangle(t,A_0P)=\measuredangle(QA_0,QP)$. For $t=BC$, since $A_0P\parallel CA$, \[\measuredangle(BC,A_0P)=\measuredangle(CB,CA)=\measuredangle(QA_0,QP).\] Therefore $BC$ touches the circle $A_0PQ$ at $A_0$.

Solution 2 (The circle equation in coordinates)

Put $A_0$ at the origin and $BC$ on the $x$-axis: $B=(-\tfrac a2,0)$, $C=(\tfrac a2,0)$, $A=(p,h)$ with $h>0$. A circle through the origin is tangent to the $x$-axis there iff its equation has the form $x^2+y^2=2ky$; a point $Z=(x,y)$ with $y\neq0$ lies on such a circle iff $\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{2y}=k$.

The points $P$, $Q$. The symmedian from $C$ meets $AB$ at $C_1$ with $AC_1:C_1B=b^2:a^2$, i.e. $C_1=\dfrac{a^2A+b^2B}{a^2+b^2}$. The midpoint $N$ of $CC_1$ and $A_0$ (the midpoint of $CB$) give $A_0N\parallel BC_1=AB$; similarly $A_0M\parallel AC$. The point $P$ is on the line through $A_0$ parallel to $AC$, so $P=\lambda(A-C)$, and on the line $CC_1$, so $P=C+\mu(C_1-C)$. Writing $A-C$ and $B-C$ as a basis, $C_1-C=\dfrac{a^2(A-C)+b^2(B-C)}{a^2+b^2}$ and $P=\tfrac12(B-C)+\lambda(A-C)$ (because $-C=\tfrac12(B-C)$). Comparing coefficients gives $\mu=\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{2b^2}$ and $\lambda=\dfrac{a^2}{2b^2}$. In the same way $Q=\dfrac{a^2}{2c^2}(A-B)$.

The check. $P=\lambda(p-\tfrac a2,\,h)$ with $|A-C|^2=b^2$, so \[\frac{|P|^2}{2P_y}=\frac{\lambda^2b^2}{2\lambda h}=\frac{\lambda b^2}{2h}=\frac{a^2}{4h},\] and $Q=\mu'(p+\tfrac a2,\,h)$ with $\mu'=\dfrac{a^2}{2c^2}$, $|A-B|^2=c^2$, so \[\frac{|Q|^2}{2Q_y}=\frac{\mu'c^2}{2h}=\frac{a^2}{4h}.\] Both values are equal, so $P$ and $Q$ lie on the circle $x^2+y^2=\dfrac{a^2}{2h}\,y$, which passes through $A_0$ and is tangent to $BC$ there. This circle is the circle $A_0PQ$ (the three points are distinct and not collinear), and the proof is complete.

Problem 8.5

Let $O$ be the circumcenter of a triangle $ABC$, $CC'$ be the altitude, and $P$ be the reflection of the orthocenter about the perpendicular bisector to $AB$. Find the ratio $CP:OC'$.

Show the answer

$CP:OC'=2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two right triangles)

Let $H$ be the orthocenter, $M$ the midpoint of $AB$ and $m$ the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ (the line $OM$). We use the classical fact $\overrightarrow{CH}=2\,\overrightarrow{OM}$ (both vectors are perpendicular to $AB$).

The reflection in $m$ moves every point parallel to $AB$. The line $CC'$ is perpendicular to $AB$ and contains $H$; its reflection is the line through $P$ perpendicular to $AB$, at the same distance from $m$ on the other side. Measure everything in the frame with axis $AB$ and the perpendicular direction:

  • the component of $\overrightarrow{CP}$ along $AB$ equals the distance between the two perpendicular lines $CC'$ and its image, that is $2\,C'M$;

  • the component of $\overrightarrow{CP}$ perpendicular to $AB$ equals that of $\overrightarrow{CH}$, because $P$ and $H$ are at the same distance from $AB$ on the same side (the reflection in $m$ preserves the distance to $AB$); this component is $CH=2\,OM$.

By Pythagoras, \[CP^2=(2\,C'M)^2+(2\,OM)^2=4\,(C'M^2+OM^2)=4\,OC'^2,\] where the last step is Pythagoras in the triangle $OMC'$, right-angled at $M$ (degenerate cases $C'=M$ or $O=M$ included). Hence $CP=2\,OC'$ and \[CP:OC'=\boxed{2}.\] (If $C'=O$, i.e. the triangle is right-angled and isosceles at $C$, both lengths are $0$ and the ratio is undefined.)

Solution 2 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Take $O$ as the origin, so $|A|=|B|=|C|=R$ and the orthocenter is $H=A+B+C$ (Sylvester's formula). Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $AB$; it fixes $O$, swaps $A$ and $B$, and maps $C$ to the point $C^*=\sigma(C)$ of the circumcircle with $CC^*\parallel AB$. As $\sigma$ is linear (it fixes the origin), \[P=\sigma(H)=\sigma(A)+\sigma(B)+\sigma(C)=B+A+C^*,\qquad P-C=(A+B)+(C^*-C).\] Let $M=\frac{A+B}2$ be the midpoint of $AB$. Then $A+B=2M$ is perpendicular to $AB$. The vector $C^*-C$ is parallel to $AB$; since $C$ and $C^*$ are symmetric about the line $OM$ and the projection of $C$ to $AB$ is $C'$, we get $|C^*-C|=2\,C'M$. The two vectors are perpendicular, so \[CP^2=|2M|^2+|C^*-C|^2=4\,OM^2+4\,C'M^2=4\,OC'^2,\] again by Pythagoras in the right triangle $OMC'$. Thus $CP:OC'=\boxed{2}$.

Problem 8.6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let the external bisector of angle $A$ of the triangle $ABC$ meet the sideline $BC$ at point $A_1$. Let $a_1$ be the segment $BA_1$ or $CA_1$ not containing $BC$. Define segments $b_1$ and $c_1$ similarly. Prove that the sum of these segments is greater than the semiperimeter of the triangle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The external bisector theorem)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$. The external bisectors meet the opposite sidelines only if the adjacent sides are unequal, so the triangle is scalene; let $a>b>c$ (the labels play symmetric roles).

Each segment is longer than the shorter adjacent side. Take the vertex $A$ and suppose $b>c$. By the external bisector theorem $A_1B:A_1C=c:b$, and $A_1$ lies outside the segment $BC$, beyond $B$. Hence $A_1C-A_1B=a$, so \[a_1=A_1B=\frac{ac}{b-c}>c,\] because $b-c<a$ by the triangle inequality. In general the segment at vertex $A$ is $\dfrac{a\min(b,c)}{|b-c|}>\min(b,c)$, and similarly for the other vertices.

The sum. With $a>b>c$ we get $a_1>c$ (the shorter side at $A$ is $c$), $b_1>c$ and $c_1>b$ (the shorter side at $C$ is $b$). Therefore \[a_1+b_1+c_1>c_1+a_1>b+c>\frac{a+b+c}2,\] the last inequality being $b+c>a$. So the sum of the three segments exceeds the semiperimeter.

Solution 2 (Angles in the triangle ABA1)

Keep $a>b>c$, so $\angle B>\angle C$, and consider the vertex $A$. The external bisector at $A$ makes the angle $90^\circ-\tfrac{A}2$ with the ray $AB$ and meets the line $BC$ at a point $A_1$ beyond $B$ (the side of the smaller adjacent side $c$). In the triangle $ABA_1$: \[\angle BAA_1=90^\circ-\frac A2=\frac{B+C}2,\qquad \angle ABA_1=180^\circ-B,\qquad \angle AA_1B=B-\frac{B+C}2=\frac{B-C}2 .\] Since $\frac{B-C}2<\frac{B+C}2$, the side opposite $\angle AA_1B$ is shorter than the side opposite $\angle BAA_1$, that is $AB<A_1B$: the segment $a_1=A_1B$ is longer than $c$. The same argument at the other vertices shows that each segment is longer than the shorter of the two sides at its vertex: $b_1>c$, $c_1>b$.

Hence $a_1+b_1+c_1>c+c+b>b+c$, and $b+c>\frac{a+b+c}2$ because $b+c>a$. This proves the claim.

Problem 8.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $H$ be the orthocenter of a triangle $ABC$, $AA_1$ be the altitude, and $P$, $Q$ be the midpoints of $BH$ and $CH$ respectively. The lines $A_1P$ and $A_1Q$ meet $AB$ and $AC$ respectively at points $X$, $Y$. Prove that there exists a circle passing through $X$, $Y$ and touching the circumcircle and the nine-points circle of $ABC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle AXA1Y and two homotheties)

Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle (centre $O$), $\nu$ the nine-point circle and $t$ the tangent to $\Omega$ at $A$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle$ modulo $180^\circ$, and assume the triangle is not right-angled (otherwise $H$ is a vertex and $X$ or $Y$ is not defined). We show that the circle through $A$, $X$, $A_1$, $Y$ is the required circle.

Problem 8.8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Does there exist a convex polygon with neither a center of symmetry nor an axis of symmetry, which can be divided into $2,3,\ldots,2026$ congruent convex polygons?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (Half of a regular polygon with very many sides)

The answer is yes.

The polygon. Let $N=\operatorname{lcm}(2,3,\dots,2026)$ and let $\Pi$ be a regular $2N$-gon with centre $O$. The directions of all sides and diagonals of $\Pi$ form a finite set; choose a line $\ell$ through $O$ that is parallel to none of them, and let $H$ be one of the two halves into which $\ell$ cuts $\Pi$. As the intersection of $\Pi$ with a half-plane, $H$ is convex. The line $\ell$ contains no vertex of $\Pi$ (the line through $O$ and a vertex contains a main diagonal), so it crosses two opposite sides of $\Pi$ at interior points $U$ and $U'=2O-U$, and $H$ has the vertices $U$, $U'$ and the $N$ vertices of $\Pi$ on one side of $\ell$.

$H$ has no centre and no axis of symmetry. At each of the $N$ vertices of $\Pi$ the angle of $H$ is the angle $\beta=180^\circ-\frac{180^\circ}N$ of $\Pi$. Let $\alpha$ be the angle of $H$ at $U$, between $\ell$ and the side $s$ of $\Pi$ through $U$; the side through $U'$ is parallel to $s$, so the angle of $H$ at $U'$ is $180^\circ-\alpha$. The directions of the sides and diagonals of $\Pi$ are the multiples of $\frac{90^\circ}N$ (up to a common shift), and the direction of $\ell$ is not among them; hence $\alpha$, $180^\circ-\alpha$ are not multiples of $\frac{90^\circ}N$. In particular $\alpha\neq\beta$, $180^\circ-\alpha\neq\beta$ and $\alpha\neq90^\circ$, so $\alpha\neq180^\circ-\alpha$.

An isometry $g$ mapping $H$ onto itself maps vertices to vertices with the same angles. So $g(U)=U$ and $g(U')=U'$. An isometry fixing two points is the identity or the reflection in the line through them, here $\ell$; but the reflection in $\ell$ maps $H$ to the other half of $\Pi$. Hence $g$ is the identity: $H$ has neither a centre nor an axis of symmetry.

Dividing $H$ into $k$ congruent parts ($2\le k\le2026$). Draw from $O$, into the half-plane of $H$, the rays forming the angles $\frac{180^\circ}k,\ 2\cdot\frac{180^\circ}k,\ \dots,\ (k-1)\frac{180^\circ}k$ with the ray $OU$. Together with $OU$ and $OU'$ they cut $H$ into $k$ pieces $H_1,\dots,H_k$, where $H_j$ is the part of $\Pi$ inside the angle between the $(j-1)$-th and the $j$-th ray. Each $H_j$ is convex (the intersection of the convex polygon $\Pi$ with a convex angle of $\frac{180^\circ}k\le 90^\circ$).

Let $\rho$ be the rotation about $O$ through $\frac{180^\circ}k$. Because $k$ divides $N$, the angle $\frac{180^\circ}k$ is a multiple of $\frac{360^\circ}{2N}$, so $\rho$ maps $\Pi$ onto itself; it also maps the angle of $H_j$ onto the angle of $H_{j+1}$. Therefore $\rho(H_j)=\Pi\cap\rho(\text{angle}_j)=H_{j+1}$ for $j=1,\dots,k-1$, and all $k$ pieces are congruent. This works for every $k=2,3,\dots,2026$, which completes the construction.

Problem 9.1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $BL_b$ and $CL_c$ be the bisectors of a triangle $ABC$, $M_b$ and $M_c$ the midpoints of $AC$ and $AB$ respectively, and $I_a$ the center of the excircle touching the side $BC$. The circles $BM_bL_b$ and $CM_cL_c$ meet at points $D$ and $E$. Prove that $I_a$ lies on the line $DE$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Both powers equal the power with respect to the circumcircle)

We assume $BA\neq BC$ and $CA\neq CB$ (otherwise $L_b=M_b$ or $L_c=M_c$ and the circles are not defined). Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle, $\omega_b$ the circle $BM_bL_b$ and $\omega_c$ the circle $CM_cL_c$. The line $DE$ is the radical axis of $\omega_b$ and $\omega_c$, so it suffices to show that $I_a$ has equal powers with respect to them.

A fourth point of $\omega_b$. Let $W_b$ be the midpoint of the arc $ABC$ of $\Omega$. It lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$ and on the external bisector of angle $B$. Hence \[\angle L_bBW_b=90^\circ\quad(\text{internal and external bisectors}),\qquad \angle L_bM_bW_b=90^\circ\quad(M_bW_b\perp AC).\] So $B$ and $M_b$ lie on the circle with diameter $L_bW_b$. This circle passes through the three non-collinear points $B,M_b,L_b$, so it is $\omega_b$; thus $W_b\in\omega_b$. Similarly the midpoint $W_c$ of the arc $ACB$ lies on $\omega_c$.

The powers. The excentre $I_a$ lies on the external bisectors of the angles $B$ and $C$. The external bisector of $B$ is the line $BW_b$; it meets $\omega_b$ at $B$ and $W_b$, and $\Omega$ at the same two points ($B\neq W_b$ because $BA\neq BC$). Hence \[\operatorname{pow}(I_a,\omega_b)=\overline{I_aB}\cdot\overline{I_aW_b}=\operatorname{pow}(I_a,\Omega).\] In the same way, using the line $CW_c$, $\operatorname{pow}(I_a,\omega_c)=\overline{I_aC}\cdot\overline{I_aW_c}=\operatorname{pow}(I_a,\Omega)$. The two powers are equal, so $I_a$ lies on the radical axis $DE$.

Solution 2 (The power difference is an affine function)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $\omega_b$, $\omega_c$ the two circles. For a circle with centre $Z$ and radius $\rho$, $\operatorname{pow}(X)=|X-Z|^2-\rho^2$; the difference of two such functions has no quadratic term, so \[f(X)=\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega_b)-\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega_c)\] is an affine function of $X$. Consequently, if $X=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C$ with $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$, then $f(X)=\alpha f(A)+\beta f(B)+\gamma f(C)$. The line $DE$ is the set $f=0$.

Values at the vertices. The circle $\omega_b$ meets the line $AC$ at $M_b$ and $L_b$, and $\omega_c$ meets $AB$ at $M_c$ and $L_c$; also $B\in\omega_b$, $C\in\omega_c$. By the bisector theorem $AL_b=\frac{bc}{a+c}$, $CL_b=\frac{ab}{a+c}$, $AL_c=\frac{bc}{a+b}$, $BL_c=\frac{ac}{a+b}$, and all these points lie on the sides, so \[f(A)=\frac{bc}{a+c}\cdot\frac b2-\frac{bc}{a+b}\cdot\frac c2,\qquad f(B)=0-\frac{ac}{a+b}\cdot\frac c2,\qquad f(C)=\frac{ab}{a+c}\cdot\frac b2-0 .\]

The excentre. In barycentric form $I_a=\dfrac{-aA+bB+cC}{-a+b+c}$. Therefore $(b+c-a)\,f(I_a)=-a\,f(A)+b\,f(B)+c\,f(C)$, which equals \[-\frac{ab^2c}{2(a+c)}+\frac{abc^2}{2(a+b)}-\frac{abc^2}{2(a+b)}+\frac{ab^2c}{2(a+c)}=0 .\] So $f(I_a)=0$: the excentre $I_a$ lies on the line $DE$.

Problem 9.2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

The vertices of a regular icosahedron are colored in such a way that any three vertices of the same color do not form an isosceles triangle. Determine the minimum possible number of colors. (The icosahedron has $12$ vertices, each vertex is adjacent to five faces, each of which is a regular triangle.)

Show the answer

$3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Three distances and antipodal pairs)

Let the edge be $1$ and $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$. Each vertex $v$ has $5$ neighbours (distance $1$), the $5$ vertices of the opposite "pentagon" at distance $\varphi$ (the diagonals of the pentagons around $v$), and the antipode $-v$ at distance $2R$, where $R$ is the circumradius. So only three distances occur: $1$, $\varphi$, $2R$.

A colour class has at most $4$ vertices. Let $S$ be a set of vertices containing no isosceles triangle. A vertex $x\in S$ has different distances to all other vertices of $S$ (two equal distances $xy=xz$ would make $xyz$ isosceles). Only three distances exist, so $|S|\le4$.

Lower bound. With two colours some class would have at least $6>4$ vertices. Hence at least $3$ colours are needed.

A colouring with $3$ colours. The $12$ vertices form $6$ antipodal pairs $\{v,-v\}$. Take any two pairs $\{x,-x\}$, $\{y,-y\}$. The four points form a rectangle inscribed in a great circle of the circumsphere: its diagonals $x(-x)$, $y(-y)$ are diameters. Every triangle formed by three vertices of a rectangle is right-angled, with legs equal to the two side lengths $p=xy$, $q=x(-y)$ and hypotenuse $2R$. By Thales, $p^2+q^2=4R^2$; as $p,q\in\{1,\varphi\}$ and $1+\varphi^2=4R^2$ while $2\cdot1\ne 4R^2\ne 2\varphi^2$, we get $\{p,q\}=\{1,\varphi\}$, $p\ne q$. So all four triangles are scalene, and the union of two antipodal pairs contains no isosceles triangle.

Split the $6$ antipodal pairs into $3$ couples in any way and give each couple its own colour. No colour class contains an isosceles triangle, so $3$ colours suffice. The answer is $\boxed{3}$.

Solution 2 (Pentagons and golden rectangles)

Two colours are not enough. The five neighbours of any vertex form a regular pentagon, and any three vertices of a regular pentagon form an isosceles triangle (the three chosen vertices cut the boundary of the pentagon into three arcs consisting of $1,1,3$ or of $1,2,2$ sides; an arc of one side gives a side of the triangle equal to a side of the pentagon, an arc of two or three sides gives a diagonal; in both cases two sides of the triangle are equal). With two colours, three of these five neighbours have the same colour by the pigeonhole principle, and they form an isosceles triangle. So at least $3$ colours are needed.

Three colours are enough. Use the standard coordinates of the vertices of a regular icosahedron (edge $2$): \[(0,\pm1,\pm\varphi),\qquad(\pm1,\pm\varphi,0),\qquad(\pm\varphi,0,\pm1),\qquad \varphi=\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2 .\] Colour a vertex according to which coordinate is $0$. Each colour class is a "golden rectangle", e.g. $(0,\pm1,\pm\varphi)$ is a rectangle with sides $2$ and $2\varphi$. Three vertices of a rectangle form a right triangle whose legs are the two different sides $2\ne2\varphi$ and whose hypotenuse is the diagonal, so the triangle is scalene. Hence no colour class contains an isosceles triangle, and the minimum number of colours is $\boxed{3}$.

Problem 9.3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Prove that the cosines of the angles of a triangle form an arithmetic progression if and only if the radii of its excircles form an arithmetic progression.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Each exradius is an affine function of a cosine)

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides, $s$ the semiperimeter, $S$ the area, $r$ and $R$ the inradius and circumradius, and $r_a,r_b,r_c$ the exradii. We prove \[r_a=r+2R(1-\cos A) \tag{1}\] and the analogous formulas for $r_b,r_c$.

Indeed, $r_a=\dfrac S{s-a}$ and $r=\dfrac Ss$, so $r_a-r=\dfrac{Sa}{s(s-a)}$. On the other hand $1-\cos A=2\sin^2\frac A2=\dfrac{2(s-b)(s-c)}{bc}$ and $R=\dfrac{abc}{4S}$, so \[2R(1-\cos A)=\frac{abc}{2S}\cdot\frac{2(s-b)(s-c)}{bc}=\frac{a(s-b)(s-c)}{S}=\frac{a\,S}{s(s-a)},\] using Heron's formula $S^2=s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)$. This proves (1).

By (1), $r_a,r_b,r_c$ are obtained from $\cos A,\cos B,\cos C$ by the same map $u\mapsto r+2R-2Ru$, which is affine and injective. Three numbers form an arithmetic progression (in a given order) iff the middle one is the average of the other two, and an injective affine map preserves this property in both directions. Hence $\cos A,\cos B,\cos C$ form an arithmetic progression (in some order) iff $r_a,r_b,r_c$ form an arithmetic progression (in the corresponding order).

Solution 2 (Tangents of half-angles)

Let $p=\tan\frac A2$, $q=\tan\frac B2$, $u=\tan\frac C2$. These are positive and satisfy $pq+qu+up=1$ (because $\frac A2+\frac B2+\frac C2=90^\circ$). The exradii are $r_a=s\,p$, $r_b=s\,q$, $r_c=s\,u$ (the excircle opposite $A$ touches the line $AB$ at distance $s$ from $A$). So the exradii form an arithmetic progression with middle term $r_b$ iff $p+u=2q$.

For the cosines, $\cos A=\dfrac{1-p^2}{1+p^2}=1-\dfrac{2p^2}{1+p^2}$, and by the relation above \[1+p^2=p^2+pq+qu+up=(p+q)(p+u).\] Hence $\cos A=1-\dfrac{2p^2}{(p+q)(p+u)}$, and similarly for $B,C$. Putting everything over the common denominator $D=(p+q)(q+u)(u+p)>0$, \[2\cos B-\cos A-\cos C=\frac{2}{D}\Bigl(p^2(q+u)+u^2(p+q)-2q^2(p+u)\Bigr).\] The bracket factors as \[p^2(q+u)+u^2(p+q)-2q^2(p+u)=(p+u-2q)(pq+qu+up)=p+u-2q .\] (To check the factorisation, expand $(p+u-2q)(pq+qu+up)$: the terms $pqu$ cancel and what remains is the left-hand side.) Therefore \[2\cos B-\cos A-\cos C=\frac{2\,(p+u-2q)}{(p+q)(q+u)(u+p)},\] so $\cos B$ is the arithmetic mean of $\cos A$ and $\cos C$ iff $p+u=2q$, iff $r_b$ is the arithmetic mean of $r_a$ and $r_c$. The same holds for every choice of the middle term, which proves the statement.

Problem 9.4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

The excircles of a non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ touch the sides $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$ at points $A_1$, $B_1$, and $C_1$, respectively. Let $A'$ be the reflection of $A$ about the perpendicular bisector of $B_1C_1$. Define $B'$ and $C'$ similarly. Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $ABC$ and $A'B'C'$ are tangent.

Show the solution

Solution (A pencil of circles tangent at one point)

Notation. $a,b,c$ are the sides, $s$ the semiperimeter, $x=s-a$, $y=s-b$, $z=s-c$ (so $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$), $r,R$ the inradius and circumradius, $\Omega$ (centre $O$) the circumcircle, $N_a$ the Nagel point, $G$ the centroid, $M_a$ the midpoint of $BC$. The excircles touch the sides at the extouch points, with $AB_1=s-c=z$, $AC_1=s-b=y$ (and cyclically). We use the identity $\dfrac rR=\dfrac{4S^2}{s\,abc}=\dfrac{4xyz}{abc}$ ($S$ the area, Heron's formula).

Step 1: a circle tangent to $\Omega$. The homothety $h$ with centre $G$ and ratio $-2$ maps $M_a\mapsto A$ (and cyclically), the incentre $I\mapsto N_a$, the incircle to the circle $\kappa$ with centre $N_a$ and radius $2r$, and the nine-point circle to $\Omega$. By Feuerbach's theorem the incircle touches the nine-point circle; hence $\kappa$ touches $\Omega$ at a point $T$ (internally, as $2r<R$ for a non-equilateral triangle). For a point $Z$ write $f_\Omega(Z)=ZO^2-R^2$ and $f_\kappa(Z)=ZN_a^2-4r^2$ for the powers.

Step 2: the pencil. Let $\mu\neq1$ be a real number. The set $\{Z:\ f_\Omega(Z)=\mu f_\kappa(Z)\}$ is given by $(1-\mu)|Z|^2+(\text{linear})=0$, so it is a circle (or a point, or empty); it contains $T$ (both powers vanish there), and its centre $\frac{O-\mu N_a}{1-\mu}$ lies on the line $ON_a$, which passes through $T$. A circle through $T$ with centre on the line $OT$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $T$. Hence it suffices to prove \[f_\Omega(A')=\frac Rr\,f_\kappa(A') \tag{1}\] together with the same equalities for $B',C'$: then $A',B',C'$ lie on one circle of this kind (with $\mu=R/r\neq1$), which is the circle $A'B'C'$, and it touches $\Omega$.

Step 3: the point $A'$. Let $w=\overrightarrow{B_1C_1}$ and $d=B_1C_1$. The reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $B_1C_1$ moves $A$ parallel to $w$, and the projections of $A$ and $A'$ to the line $B_1C_1$ are symmetric about the midpoint of $B_1C_1$; this gives \[A'=A+\lambda w,\qquad \lambda=\frac{AC_1^2-AB_1^2}{d^2}=\frac{y^2-z^2}{d^2}=\frac{a(c-b)}{d^2}\] (note $y-z=c-b$ and $y+z=a$). For any circle with centre $Q$ and radius $\rho$ the power satisfies $f(A+\lambda w)=f(A)+2\lambda\,w\cdot(A-Q)+\lambda^2d^2$, and here $\lambda^2d^2=\lambda a(c-b)$. We also need \[a^2-d^2=\frac{4s\,xyz}{bc}. \tag{2}\] Indeed, by the law of cosines in the triangles $ABC$ and $AB_1C_1$ ($AB_1=z$, $AC_1=y$), $a^2-d^2=(b^2-z^2)+(c^2-y^2)-2\cos A\,(bc-yz)$; here $b^2-z^2=x^2+2xz$, $c^2-y^2=x^2+2xy$ and $bc-yz=xs$, so $a^2-d^2=2xs(1-\cos A)=4xs\sin^2\frac A2=\dfrac{4xs\,yz}{bc}$.

Step 4: power with respect to $\Omega$. Since $OA=OB$, $(B-A)\cdot(A-O)=-\frac{c^2}2$, so $(C_1-A)\cdot(A-O)=\frac yc\cdot(-\frac{c^2}2)=-\frac{yc}2$; similarly $(B_1-A)\cdot(A-O)=-\frac{zb}2$. Hence $w\cdot(A-O)=\frac{zb-yc}2=\frac{(z-y)s}2=\frac{s(b-c)}2$, and \[f_\Omega(A')=0+\lambda s(b-c)+\lambda a(c-b)=\lambda(b-c)(s-a)=-\frac{a\,x\,(b-c)^2}{d^2}.\]

Step 5: power with respect to $\kappa$. Since $h(M_a)=A$ and $h$ multiplies powers by $4$, $f_\kappa(A)=4\cdot(\text{power of }M_a\text{ w.r.t. the incircle})=4\,M_aD^2$, where $D$ is the incircle's touch point on $BC$; as $BD=y$ and $BM_a=\frac a2$, $M_aD=\frac{|c-b|}2$ and $f_\kappa(A)=(b-c)^2$. Next we compute $w\cdot(A-N_a)$ with $A$ as origin, using $B\cdot B=c^2$, $C\cdot C=b^2$, $B\cdot C=m:=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}2=xs-yz$, $C_1=\frac ycB$, $B_1=\frac zbC$ and $N_a=\frac{yB+zC}{s}$ (barycentric coordinates $(x:y:z)$): \[s\,(w\cdot N_a)=y^2c-z^2b+yz\,m\Bigl(\frac1c-\frac1b\Bigr)=(c-b)\Bigl[(sa-yz)-\frac{yz\,m}{bc}\Bigr]=(c-b)\Bigl[sa-\frac{yz(bc+m)}{bc}\Bigr],\] where we used $y^2c-z^2b=(y-z)(xy+xz+y^2+yz+z^2)=(c-b)(sa-yz)$. As $bc=xs+yz$, we have $bc+m=2xs$, so $w\cdot N_a=(c-b)\bigl(a-\frac{2xyz}{bc}\bigr)$ and $w\cdot(A-N_a)=-w\cdot N_a$. Therefore \[f_\kappa(A')=(b-c)^2-2\lambda(c-b)\Bigl(a-\frac{2xyz}{bc}\Bigr)+\lambda a(c-b)=(b-c)^2+\lambda(c-b)\Bigl(\frac{4xyz}{bc}-a\Bigr).\] Substituting $\lambda(c-b)=\frac{a(b-c)^2}{d^2}$ and using (2), \[f_\kappa(A')=\frac{(b-c)^2}{d^2}\Bigl(d^2-a^2+\frac{4axyz}{bc}\Bigr)=\frac{(b-c)^2}{d^2}\cdot\frac{4xyz(a-s)}{bc}=-\frac{4x^2yz\,(b-c)^2}{bc\,d^2}.\]

Step 6: conclusion. Dividing, \[\frac{f_\Omega(A')}{f_\kappa(A')}=\frac{a\,x}{4x^2yz/(bc)}=\frac{abc}{4xyz}=\frac Rr .\] (Both powers are non-zero because $b\neq c$.) This is (1); by symmetry the same holds for $B'$ and $C'$. By Step 2 the circle $A'B'C'$ touches the circumcircle $\Omega$ (at the point $T$ where $\Omega$ is touched by the circle with centre $N_a$ and radius $2r$).

Problem 9.5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

The bisectors $BE$ and $CF$ of a triangle $ABC$ meet at point $I$, $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the incenters of triangles $BFI$ and $CIE$ respectively. Prove that $\angle I_1AB=\angle I_2AC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Distances to lines through I)

Denote the angles of the triangle by $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$.

$I_1$, $I$, $I_2$ lie on one line. The angles $BIF$ and $CIE$ are vertical angles ($F,I,C$ and $B,I,E$ are collinear), so their bisectors form one line $\ell$ through $I$. The incentre $I_1$ of $BFI$ lies on the bisector of the angle $BIF$, and $I_2$ on the bisector of $CIE$; hence $I_1,I_2\in\ell$.

A ratio of distances. For a point $Z$ write $d(Z,m)$ for its distance to a line $m$. The lines $\ell$, $BE$ and $AI$ all pass through $I$, so for any point $Z\in\ell$, \[d(Z,BE)=IZ\sin\angle(\ell,BE),\qquad d(Z,AI)=IZ\sin\angle(\ell,AI),\] and the ratio $k=\dfrac{d(Z,BE)}{d(Z,AI)}$ does not depend on $Z\in\ell$ ($Z\neq I$).

The incentre $I_1$ of $BFI$ is equidistant from the lines $BF=AB$ and $BI=BE$, and the incentre $I_2$ of $CIE$ is equidistant from $CE=AC$ and $IE=BE$. Therefore \[\frac{d(I_1,AB)}{d(I_1,AI)}=\frac{d(I_1,BE)}{d(I_1,AI)}=k=\frac{d(I_2,BE)}{d(I_2,AI)}=\frac{d(I_2,AC)}{d(I_2,AI)} . \tag{1}\]

Back to angles. The point $I_1$ lies inside the triangle $BFI\subset ABI$, hence inside the angle $BAI$; put $\theta_1=\angle I_1AB$, so $\angle I_1AI=\frac\alpha2-\theta_1$. Then $d(I_1,AB)=AI_1\sin\theta_1$ and $d(I_1,AI)=AI_1\sin(\frac\alpha2-\theta_1)$. Likewise $I_2$ is inside the angle $CAI$ and, with $\theta_2=\angle I_2AC$, the right-hand side of (1) is $\dfrac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin(\frac\alpha2-\theta_2)}$. So (1) says \[\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin(\frac\alpha2-\theta_1)}=\frac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin(\frac\alpha2-\theta_2)} .\] The function $\theta\mapsto\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\sin(\frac\alpha2-\theta)}$ is strictly increasing on $(0,\frac\alpha2)$ (numerator increases, positive denominator decreases), hence $\theta_1=\theta_2$, i.e. $\angle I_1AB=\angle I_2AC$.

Solution 2 (Trigonometric Ceva in the triangles ABI and ACI)

Denote the angles of the triangle by $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$, so $\angle AIB=90^\circ+\frac\gamma2$, $\angle AIC=90^\circ+\frac\beta2$, $\angle BIC=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$.

Angles at $I$. The points $F$ and $E$ lie on the rays opposite to $IC$ and $IB$, so \[\angle BIF=\angle CIE=180^\circ-\angle BIC=90^\circ-\tfrac\alpha2,\qquad \angle AIF=180^\circ-\angle AIC=90^\circ-\tfrac\beta2,\qquad \angle AIE=90^\circ-\tfrac\gamma2 .\] The incentre $I_1$ of $BFI$ lies on the bisector of $\angle BIF$, inside the triangle $ABI$; so \[\angle BII_1=45^\circ-\tfrac\alpha4,\qquad \angle AII_1=\angle AIF+\angle FII_1=135^\circ-\tfrac\alpha4-\tfrac\beta2 .\] Similarly $\angle CII_2=45^\circ-\frac\alpha4$ and $\angle AII_2=135^\circ-\frac\alpha4-\frac\gamma2$. Note that \[\Bigl(135^\circ-\tfrac\alpha4-\tfrac\beta2\Bigr)+\Bigl(135^\circ-\tfrac\alpha4-\tfrac\gamma2\Bigr)=270^\circ-\tfrac{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}2=180^\circ,\] so $\sin\angle AII_1=\sin\angle AII_2$.

Trigonometric Ceva. In the triangle $ABI$ the cevians $AI_1$, $BI_1$, $II_1$ concur at $I_1$, and $BI_1$ bisects the angle $ABI$. Trigonometric Ceva gives \[\frac{\sin\angle I_1AB}{\sin\angle I_1AI}\cdot\frac{\sin\angle I_1BI}{\sin\angle I_1BA}\cdot\frac{\sin\angle I_1IA}{\sin\angle I_1IB}=1, \quad\text{so}\quad \frac{\sin\angle I_1AB}{\sin\angle I_1AI}=\frac{\sin(45^\circ-\frac\alpha4)}{\sin\angle AII_1}.\] In the triangle $ACI$ the same argument for $I_2$ gives $\dfrac{\sin\angle I_2AC}{\sin\angle I_2AI}=\dfrac{\sin(45^\circ-\frac\alpha4)}{\sin\angle AII_2}$. The right-hand sides are equal. With $\theta_1=\angle I_1AB$, $\theta_2=\angle I_2AC$ we have $\angle I_1AI=\frac\alpha2-\theta_1$, $\angle I_2AI=\frac\alpha2-\theta_2$, and since $\theta\mapsto\frac{\sin\theta}{\sin(\alpha/2-\theta)}$ is strictly increasing on $(0,\frac\alpha2)$ (the numerator increases and the positive denominator decreases), we get $\theta_1=\theta_2$, i.e. $\angle I_1AB=\angle I_2AC$.

Problem 9.6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Prove that the sum of the squares of the altitudes of an acute-angled triangle is not less than the square of the perimeter of its orthotriangle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The orthic perimeter is 2S/R)

Let the triangle be acute, with sides $a,b,c$, angles $A,B,C$, area $S$, $R$ the circumradius, and $h_a,h_b,h_c$ the altitudes, with feet $A_1,B_1,C_1$.

The sides of the orthotriangle. The points $B_1,C_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, and the triangle $AB_1C_1$ is similar to $ABC$ with ratio $|\cos A|$; hence $B_1C_1=a|\cos A|$, and similarly for the other sides. So the perimeter of the orthotriangle is \[p=a|\cos A|+b|\cos B|+c|\cos C|=R\bigl(|\sin2A|+|\sin2B|+|\sin2C|\bigr).\]

The inequality. Now $\cos A,\cos B,\cos C>0$ and \[\sin2A+\sin2B+\sin2C=4\sin A\sin B\sin C,\] so $p=4R\sin A\sin B\sin C=\dfrac{2S}{R}$ (because $S=2R^2\sin A\sin B\sin C$). Also $h_a=\dfrac{2S}a$, etc. The inequality $h_a^2+h_b^2+h_c^2\ge p^2$ becomes \[4S^2\Bigl(\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}+\frac1{c^2}\Bigr)\ge\frac{4S^2}{R^2}\iff \frac1{\sin^2A}+\frac1{\sin^2B}+\frac1{\sin^2C}\ge4 .\] By the Cauchy–Schwarz (or AM–HM) inequality, \[\frac1{\sin^2A}+\frac1{\sin^2B}+\frac1{\sin^2C}\ge\frac{9}{\sin^2A+\sin^2B+\sin^2C},\] and $\sin^2A+\sin^2B+\sin^2C=2+2\cos A\cos B\cos C\le\frac94$ for every triangle. (Indeed $2+2\cos A\cos B\cos C\le\frac94$ is $\cos A\cos B\cos C\le\frac18$, a classical consequence of AM–GM and Jensen; alternatively, use $\sin^2A+\sin^2B+\sin^2C=\frac{a^2+b^2+c^2}{4R^2}\le\frac{9R^2}{4R^2}$, since $a^2+b^2+c^2=9R^2-OH^2$.) Hence the sum is at least $\frac{9}{9/4}=4$, which proves the inequality. Equality holds only for the equilateral triangle. (The argument works for every non-obtuse triangle.)

Remark. The hypothesis that the triangle is acute cannot be dropped: for the angles $100^\circ,50^\circ,30^\circ$ and $R=\frac12$ one gets $h_a^2+h_b^2+h_c^2\approx0.958<1.202\approx p^2$.

Solution 2 (Exradii of the orthotriangle)

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle, let $A_1B_1C_1$ be the orthotriangle, $p$ its semiperimeter and $\varrho_a,\varrho_b,\varrho_c$ its exradii opposite $A_1,B_1,C_1$.

$A$ is an excentre of $A_1B_1C_1$. In an acute triangle the altitudes bisect the angles of the orthotriangle (e.g. $\angle AA_1C_1=\angle AA_1B_1=90^\circ-\angle A$), and the sides of $ABC$, being perpendicular to them, are its external bisectors. So $A$, the intersection of the external bisectors at $B_1$ and $C_1$, is the centre of the excircle of $A_1B_1C_1$ opposite $A_1$; this excircle lies on the line $AA_1$ beyond the side $B_1C_1$.

Pythagoras. This excircle touches the line $A_1B_1$ at a point $T$ with $A_1T=p$ (the tangent length from a vertex to the opposite excircle equals the semiperimeter) and $AT=\varrho_a$, $AT\perp A_1T$. Hence \[AA_1^2=p^2+\varrho_a^2,\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad BB_1^2=p^2+\varrho_b^2,\quad CC_1^2=p^2+\varrho_c^2 .\]

The inequality. For any triangle with semiperimeter $p$ the exradii satisfy $\varrho_a\varrho_b+\varrho_b\varrho_c+\varrho_c\varrho_a=p^2$ (with $\varrho_a=\frac{S'}{p-a'}$ etc., the sum is $S'^2\frac{(p-a')+(p-b')+(p-c')}{(p-a')(p-b')(p-c')}=\frac{S'^2\,p}{S'^2/p}=p^2$ by Heron's formula; here $a',b',c',S'$ are the sides and the area of the orthotriangle). Therefore \[AA_1^2+BB_1^2+CC_1^2=3p^2+\varrho_a^2+\varrho_b^2+\varrho_c^2\ge3p^2+\varrho_a\varrho_b+\varrho_b\varrho_c+\varrho_c\varrho_a=4p^2=(2p)^2,\] which is the claim. Equality requires $\varrho_a=\varrho_b=\varrho_c$, i.e. an equilateral orthotriangle, i.e. an equilateral triangle $ABC$.

Problem 9.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry

Let $B'$ and $C'$ be the reflections of the vertices $B$ and $C$ of triangle $ABC$ about the sidelines $AC$ and $AB$ respectively. The line $B'C'$ meets $AC$ and $AB$ at points $E$ and $F$ respectively. The circumcircles of triangles $BB'F$ and $CC'E$ meet at points $X$, $Y$. Prove that the lines $XB$ and $XC$ meet at an angle of $60^\circ$, and so do the lines $YB$ and $YC$.

P.S. At the olympiad the jury commented that the triangle is acute-angled.

Show the solution

Solution (Complex numbers and two equilateral triangles)

Plan. Let $W_c,W_b$ be the third vertices of the equilateral triangles $ABW_c$ and $ACW_b$ erected inwards (on the same side of $AB$ as $C$, and of $AC$ as $B$), and $W_c',W_b'$ those erected outwards. Let $X$ be the reflection of $A$ in the line $W_cW_b$ and $Y$ the reflection of $A$ in $W_c'W_b'$. We prove that $X$ and $Y$ lie on both circles $BB'F$ and $CC'E$. Since two distinct circles have at most two common points, $X,Y$ are the two common points. Finally, $X$ lies on the circle with centre $W_c$ through $A,B$ (radius $AB$), where the chord $AB$ subtends a central angle of $60^\circ$, so the lines $XA,XB$ form an angle of $30^\circ$; likewise the lines $XA,XC$ form an angle of $30^\circ$, and the orientations show that the lines $XB$ and $XC$ form an angle of $60^\circ$ (details below). The same holds for $Y$.

Coordinates. Use complex numbers with $A=0$, $B=c>0$, $C=b\omega$, where $b=AC$, $c=AB$, $\omega=e^{i\alpha}$, $\alpha=\angle A$ (the triangle is acute, so $0<\alpha<90^\circ$). Let $\varepsilon=e^{i\pi/3}$; then $\varepsilon^3=-1$, $\varepsilon+\bar\varepsilon=1$ and $\bar\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{-1}$. Reflection in the line through $0$ with direction $\omega$ is $z\mapsto\omega^2\bar z$, so $B'=c\,\omega^2$ and $C'=b\,\bar\omega$. The line $B'C'$ meets the real axis at \[F=\frac{bc\,(4\cos^2\alpha-1)}{b+2c\cos\alpha}\] (write the points of $B'C'$ as $c\omega^2+t(b\bar\omega-c\omega^2)$ and solve $\operatorname{Im}=0$: $t=\frac{2c\cos\alpha}{b+2c\cos\alpha}$). With $W_c=c\varepsilon$ and $W_b=b\omega\bar\varepsilon$ (inward triangles), the two circles $|z-c\varepsilon|=c$ and $|z-b\omega\bar\varepsilon|=b$ pass through $0$, and their second common point is \[X=\frac{\bar w_1w_2-w_1\bar w_2}{\bar w_1-\bar w_2}=\frac{bc\,(\omega^2-\bar\varepsilon^{2})}{b+c\,\varepsilon\omega},\qquad w_1=c\varepsilon,\ w_2=b\omega\bar\varepsilon\] (for circles $|z-w_j|=|w_j|$ the equations are $z\bar z=\bar w_jz+w_j\bar z$; subtracting them and substituting back gives the formula; we simplified with $\varepsilon^3=-1$).

$X$ lies on the circle $BB'F$. We compute the four differences (using $-1-\bar\varepsilon^2=-\bar\varepsilon=\varepsilon^2$, $\bar\varepsilon^3=-1$ and $4\cos^2\alpha-1-\omega^2=\bar\omega^2+1=2\bar\omega\cos\alpha$): \[X-B=\frac{c\,\bigl(b(\omega^2+\varepsilon^2)-c\,\varepsilon\omega\bigr)}{b+c\varepsilon\omega},\qquad X-B'=\frac{-c\,\varepsilon\,(c\omega^3-b)}{b+c\varepsilon\omega},\] \[F-B=\frac{2c\,(b\cos2\alpha-c\cos\alpha)}{b+2c\cos\alpha},\qquad F-B'=\frac{2c\cos\alpha\,(b-c\omega^3)}{\omega\,(b+2c\cos\alpha)} .\] Hence the cross-ratio is \[\frac{X-B}{X-B'}:\frac{F-B}{F-B'}=\frac{b(\omega^2+\varepsilon^2)-c\varepsilon\omega}{\varepsilon\omega}\cdot\frac{\cos\alpha}{b\cos2\alpha-c\cos\alpha} =\frac{\bigl(2b\cos(\alpha-60^\circ)-c\bigr)\cos\alpha}{b\cos2\alpha-c\cos\alpha},\] because $\dfrac{\omega^2+\varepsilon^2}{\varepsilon\omega}=\omega\bar\varepsilon+\bar\omega\varepsilon=2\cos(\alpha-60^\circ)$. The cross-ratio is real, so $X,B,B',F$ lie on a circle or a line; $B,B',F$ are not collinear ($F$ is on $AB$, $B'$ is not), so $X$ lies on the circle $BB'F$.

$X$ lies on the circle $CC'E$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the bisector of angle $A$ and consider the triangle $A\tilde B\tilde C$ with $\tilde B=\sigma(C)$, $\tilde C=\sigma(B)$. It has the same orientation as $ABC$ (a reflection and a relabelling), $\tilde B$ lies on the ray $AB$ with $A\tilde B=b$, and $\tilde C$ lies on the ray $AC$ with $A\tilde C=c$. The map $\sigma$ carries the whole construction for $ABC$ to the construction for $A\tilde B\tilde C$: the circle $CC'E$ goes to the circle $\tilde B\tilde B'\tilde F$, and the inward equilateral triangles on $AC$, $AB$ go to the inward equilateral triangles on $A\tilde B$, $A\tilde C$, so $\sigma(X)$ is the point $\tilde X$ of the new triangle. The computation above with $b$ and $c$ exchanged shows $\tilde X\in\tilde B\tilde B'\tilde F$; applying $\sigma$ back, $X$ lies on the circle $CC'E$.

The point $Y$. Replacing $\varepsilon$ by $\bar\varepsilon$ everywhere (outward triangles) the same computation goes through verbatim (it only used $\varepsilon^3=-1$, $\varepsilon+\bar\varepsilon=1$); the cross-ratio becomes $\frac{(2b\cos(\alpha+60^\circ)-c)\cos\alpha}{b\cos2\alpha-c\cos\alpha}$, again real. So $Y$ is on both circles too. Finally $X\neq Y$: the reflections of $A$ in two different lines coincide only if $A$ lies on both lines; but $A\in W_cW_b$ would need the rays $AW_c$ and $AW_b$ (at angles $60^\circ$ and $\alpha-60^\circ$ from $AB$) to be equal or opposite, i.e. $\alpha=120^\circ$, and $A\in W_c'W_b'$ would need $\alpha=60^\circ$, and these cannot happen at the same time. (When $\alpha=60^\circ$ we get $Y=A=F=E$.)

The angles. For a point $Z$ on the circle with centre $W_c=c\varepsilon$ through $0$ and $c$, the directed angle $\measuredangle(ZA,ZB)$ equals half the rotation angle from $W_cA$ to $W_cB$; as $\frac{B-W_c}{A-W_c}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{-\varepsilon}=\varepsilon$, it is $+30^\circ$. For the circle with centre $W_b$, $\frac{C-W_b}{A-W_b}=\frac{1-\bar\varepsilon}{-\bar\varepsilon}=\bar\varepsilon$, so $\measuredangle(XA,XC)=-30^\circ$. Hence \[\measuredangle(XB,XC)=\measuredangle(XB,XA)+\measuredangle(XA,XC)=-60^\circ\pmod{180^\circ},\] and for $Y$ we get $+60^\circ$. Thus the lines $XB$ and $XC$ meet at an angle of $60^\circ$, and so do the lines $YB$ and $YC$, which is what we had to prove.

Remark. The undirected angle $\angle BXC$ itself may be $120^\circ$: for $A=(0,0)$, $B=(4,0)$, $C=(0.5,2)$ one of the two common points sees $BC$ at $120^\circ$. This is why the statement speaks of the angle between lines.

Problem 9.8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

A circle $\omega$, points $A$, $B$ on it, and a line $l$ not intersecting $\omega$ are given. Let $T$ be an arbitrary point on $l$, and $X$, $Y$ be the second common points of $AT$, $BT$ with $\omega$. Prove that all circles $TXY$ touch two fixed circles.

P.S. At the olympiad, the jury commented that circles can degenerate into a point or a straight line.

Show the solution

Solution (Inversion centred on the radical axis)

The pencil. Let $\mathcal P$ be the pencil of circles generated by $\omega$ and the line $l$: in coordinates with $\omega:\ x^2+y^2=1$ and $l:\ y=-d$ ($d>1$, since $l$ does not meet $\omega$), its members are \[\Gamma_\lambda:\quad x^2+y^2-1+\lambda(y+d)=0\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb R),\] together with $l$ itself ("$\lambda=\infty$"). $\Gamma_\lambda$ has centre $(0,-\frac\lambda2)$ and squared radius $\frac{\lambda^2}4+1-\lambda d$ (it is a real circle, a point, or empty). For a point $T\in l$ the power with respect to every real member equals $\operatorname{pow}(T,\omega)=T_x^2+d^2-1>0$.

Two members touch the line $AB$. Write $AB$ as $px+qy=c$ with $p^2+q^2=1$; $|c|<1$ because $AB$ is a chord of $\omega$. The member $\Gamma_\lambda$ touches $AB$ iff the distance from its centre to $AB$ equals its radius: \[\Bigl(-\frac{q\lambda}2-c\Bigr)^2=\frac{\lambda^2}4+1-\lambda d\iff \frac{p^2}4\lambda^2-(qc+d)\lambda+(1-c^2)=0 .\] If $p\neq0$ (i.e. $AB\nparallel l$) the discriminant is positive: by Cauchy–Schwarz $|qc|+|p|\sqrt{1-c^2}\le\sqrt{q^2+p^2}\sqrt{c^2+1-c^2}=1<d$, so $qc+d>|p|\sqrt{1-c^2}\ge0$ and $(qc+d)^2>p^2(1-c^2)$. So there are two members $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ tangent to $AB$ (a member of radius $0$ is a point of $AB$: then "tangent" means "passing through this point"). If $p=0$ there is one such $\Gamma_1$, and the line $l\parallel AB$ plays the role of $\Gamma_2$. These two circles depend only on $\omega$, $l$ and the line $AB$.

The inversion. Fix $T\in l$ and let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $T$ and radius $\sqrt{\operatorname{pow}(T,\omega)}$. Since $TA\cdot TX=\operatorname{pow}(T,\omega)$ with $X$ on the ray $TA$, $\iota(A)=X$; likewise $\iota(B)=Y$. Hence $\iota$ maps the line $AB$ (not through $T$) to the circle through $T$, $X$, $Y$; that is, $\iota(AB)$ is the circle $TXY$. (If $T\in AB$, then $X=B$, $Y=A$ and the "circle" $TXY$ is the line $AB$ itself.)

An inversion whose power equals the power of its centre with respect to a circle maps this circle onto itself. As $T$ has the same power with respect to all members of $\mathcal P$, $\iota$ maps each of $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ onto itself (for a point member $\Gamma=\{Q\}$ this says $TQ^2=\operatorname{pow}(T,\omega)$, so $\iota(Q)=Q$; the line $l$ through $T$ is also mapped to itself).

Inversion preserves tangency. Since $AB$ touches $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$, the circle $TXY=\iota(AB)$ touches $\iota(\Gamma_1)=\Gamma_1$ and $\iota(\Gamma_2)=\Gamma_2$. So all circles $TXY$ touch the two fixed circles $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ (degenerating to a point or to the line $l$ in the special cases mentioned by the jury).

Remark (without inversion). The inversion step can be replaced by an angle chase: let $\Gamma$ be one of $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$, touching $AB$ at $U$, and fix $T\in l$, $T\notin AB$, $TU\neq0$. Put $k=\operatorname{pow}(T,\omega)=\operatorname{pow}(T,\Gamma)>0$. Directed angles are taken modulo $180^\circ$.

Let $U'$ be the second intersection of the line $TU$ with $\Gamma$; then $TU\cdot TU'=k$. Also $TA\cdot TX=TB\cdot TY=k$. By the converse of the power of a point, the points $A,X,U,U'$ are concyclic.

$U'$ lies on the circle $TXY$. Also $TB\cdot TY=TU\cdot TU'$, so $B,Y,U,U'$ are concyclic as well. Using the circles $AXUU'$ and $BYUU'$ (and the collinearities $T,X,A$ and $T,Y,B$), \[\measuredangle(XT,XU')=\measuredangle(XA,XU')=\measuredangle(UA,UU')=\measuredangle(AB,TU),\qquad \measuredangle(YT,YU')=\measuredangle(YB,YU')=\measuredangle(UB,UU')=\measuredangle(AB,TU).\] Hence $\measuredangle(XT,XU')=\measuredangle(YT,YU')$ and $T,X,Y,U'$ are concyclic.

Common tangent at $U'$. Let $\tau$ be the tangent to the circle $TXY$ at $U'$ and $\tau'$ the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $U'$. By the tangent–chord theorem in the circle $TXYU'$ and the concyclicity of $A,X,U,U'$, \[\measuredangle(\tau,U'T)=\measuredangle(XU',XT)=\measuredangle(XU',XA)=\measuredangle(UU',UA)=\measuredangle(TU,AB).\] In $\Gamma$ the tangents at the ends of the chord $UU'$ are symmetric with respect to the perpendicular bisector of $UU'$, so $\measuredangle(\tau',U'U)=-\measuredangle(AB,UU')=\measuredangle(UU',AB)$, i.e. $\measuredangle(\tau',U'T)=\measuredangle(TU,AB)$. Hence $\tau=\tau'$: the circles $TXY$ and $\Gamma$ have a common point $U'$ and a common tangent there, so they touch. This holds for $\Gamma=\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma=\Gamma_2$ and for every $T$.

Problem 10.1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Three circles centered at the vertices of a non-isosceles triangle have the radii equal to the absolute values of the differences of the lengths of the two sides adjacent to the corresponding vertex. Prove that each of these circles touches the radical axis of the two remaining ones.

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates along BC)

Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$; the circles are $\omega_A$ (centre $A$, radius $|b-c|$), $\omega_B$ (centre $B$, radius $|a-c|$) and $\omega_C$ (centre $C$, radius $|a-b|$). All radii are positive because the triangle is not isosceles. By symmetry it suffices to show that $\omega_A$ touches the radical axis of $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$.

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(a,0)$, $A=(u,h)$. A point $(x,y)$ is on the radical axis iff \[x^2+y^2-(a-c)^2=(x-a)^2+y^2-(a-b)^2\iff x=x_0:=\frac{a^2+(a-c)^2-(a-b)^2}{2a},\] a line perpendicular to $BC$. The projection of $A$ on $BC$ has abscissa $u=\dfrac{a^2+c^2-b^2}{2a}$ (law of cosines). Hence the distance from $A$ to the radical axis is \[|u-x_0|=\frac{\bigl|c^2-b^2-(a-c)^2+(a-b)^2\bigr|}{2a}.\] Now $(a-b)^2-(a-c)^2=(c-b)(2a-b-c)$ and $c^2-b^2=(c-b)(c+b)$, so the numerator is $|(c-b)(b+c+2a-b-c)|=2a|b-c|$. The distance equals $|b-c|$, the radius of $\omega_A$; so $\omega_A$ touches the radical axis (at the point with abscissa $x_0$ on the horizontal line through $A$).

Remark. One can also guess the point of tangency. With vectors, let \[T=A+\frac{b-c}{a}\,(C-B),\] the point on the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$ at distance $|b-c|$ from $A$. The line $t$ through $T$ perpendicular to $BC$ is perpendicular to the radius $AT$ of $\omega_A$, hence tangent to $\omega_A$ at $T$. The radical axis of $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$ is also perpendicular to $BC$, so it suffices to check that $T$ lies on it, i.e. \[TB^2-TC^2=(a-c)^2-(a-b)^2 .\] With $v=T-A$, \[TB^2-TC^2=|A-B+v|^2-|A-C+v|^2=AB^2-AC^2+2v\cdot(C-B)=c^2-b^2+2\cdot\frac{b-c}a\cdot a^2=(b-c)(2a-b-c),\] and $(a-c)^2-(a-b)^2=(b-c)(2a-b-c)$ as well. Hence $t$ is the radical axis, and it touches $\omega_A$ at $T$. This is the same computation as above, read at the point $T$ instead of as a distance.

Problem 10.2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

The vertices of a regular dodecahedron are colored in such a way that any three vertices of the same color do not form an isosceles triangle. Determine the minimum possible number of colors. (The dodecahedron has $20$ vertices, and each vertex is adjacent to three faces, each of which is a regular pentagon.)

Show the answer

$5$

Show the solution

Solution (Matchings of equal distances and antipodal pairs)

Distances. Take the dodecahedron with vertices $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$, $(0,\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi)$, $(\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi,0)$, $(\pm\varphi,0,\pm\varphi^{-1})$, $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$; the circumradius is $\sqrt3$. A direct check shows that the distance between two different vertices takes only five values, \[d_1=2\varphi^{-1},\quad d_2=2,\quad d_3=2\sqrt2,\quad d_4=2\varphi,\quad d_5=2\sqrt3\] (edge, face diagonal, two "longer" distances, and the diameter). Vertices are antipodal exactly when their distance is $d_5$. Call a set of vertices good if it contains no isosceles triangle.

Equal distances form matchings. In a good set $S$ a vertex cannot have the same distance to two other vertices of $S$. So, for each $i$, the pairs of $S$ at distance $d_i$ are pairwise disjoint (a matching).

A good set has at most $4$ vertices. Suppose $S$ is good and $|S|=5$. A matching on $5$ points has at most $2$ pairs, so each of the $5$ distances occurs at most twice among the $\binom52=10$ pairs; hence each occurs exactly twice. In particular $S$ contains two antipodal pairs $\{x,-x\}$, $\{y,-y\}$; let $z$ be the fifth vertex. By the central symmetry, $d(x,y)=d(-x,-y)=:p$ and $d(x,-y)=d(-x,y)=:q$, where $p,q\neq d_5$ and $p\neq q$ (otherwise $x$ would be equidistant from $y$ and $-y$). The four distances from $z$ to $\pm x,\pm y$ are pairwise different and none equals $d_5$ (the antipode of $z$ is not in $S$), so they are exactly $d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4$ and one of them equals $p$. Then the distance $p$ occurs three times in $S$ (for the pairs $xy$, $(-x)(-y)$ and one pair containing $z$), a contradiction. So every good set has at most $4$ vertices. (A good set with more than $5$ vertices would contain a good $5$-subset.)

Lower bound. The $20$ vertices split into colour classes that are good sets of at most $4$ vertices, so at least $20/4=5$ colours are needed.

Five colours suffice. Let $\{x,-x\}$, $\{y,-y\}$ be two antipodal pairs. The four points form a rectangle whose diagonals are diameters; every three of its vertices form a right triangle with legs $p=d(x,y)$, $q=d(x,-y)$ and hypotenuse $d_5$. Here $p^2+q^2=d_5^2=12$, and $p=q$ would give $p^2=6$, which is not one of $d_1^2,\dots,d_4^2$ ($6-2\sqrt5$, $4$, $8$, $6+2\sqrt5$). So $p\neq q$, all four triangles are scalene, and the union of two antipodal pairs is good. Split the $10$ antipodal pairs into $5$ couples arbitrarily and give each couple its own colour. This colouring has no monochromatic isosceles triangle. Hence the minimum number of colours is $\boxed{5}$.

Problem 10.3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 53: Barycentric and Projective-Flavored Geometry

The incircle of a non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ touches $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$ at points $A'$, $B'$, and $C'$, respectively. The lines $AA'$ and $BB'$ meet at point $G$. Prove that the Feuerbach point $F$ lies on $CC'$ if and only if the points $C$, $A'$, $B'$, and $G$ are concyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (Both conditions are the same relation between tangent lengths)

Let $x=s-a$, $y=s-b$, $z=s-c$ be the tangent lengths ($a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$), so that $AB'=AC'=x$, $BA'=BC'=y$, $CA'=CB'=z$. The triangle is scalene, so $x,y,z$ are pairwise different. We show that each of the two conditions is equivalent to \[z(x+y)=2xy. \tag{$*$}\]

The concyclicity. By van Aubel's theorem $\dfrac{AG}{GA'}=\dfrac xy+\dfrac xz=\dfrac{xa}{yz}$, so $AG=\dfrac{xa}{\sigma}\,AA'$ with $\sigma=xa+yz=xy+yz+zx$. By Stewart's theorem (with $BA'=y$, $A'C=z$), \[a\cdot AA'^2=b^2y+c^2z-a\,yz=x\,(\sigma+3yz)\] (expand with $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$). The points $C,A',G,B'$ are concyclic iff the power of $A$ computed along $AC$ and along $AA'$ agree (all these points lie on the same side of $A$): \[AG\cdot AA'=AB'\cdot AC\iff\frac{xa}{\sigma}AA'^2=xb\iff x(\sigma+3yz)=b\sigma=(x+z)\sigma\iff 3xyz=z\sigma\iff \sigma=3xy,\] and $\sigma=3xy$ is $(*)$.

The Feuerbach point. We use the classical barycentric coordinates of the Feuerbach point, \[F=\bigl((b-c)^2(s-a):(c-a)^2(s-b):(a-b)^2(s-c)\bigr)=\bigl((z-y)^2x:(x-z)^2y:(y-x)^2z\bigr).\] The touch point $C'$ divides $AB$ with $AC'=x$, $C'B=y$, so $C'=(y:x:0)$, and a point $(u:v:w)$ lies on the line $CC'$ iff $u:v=y:x$, i.e. $ux=vy$. For $F$ this reads \[(z-y)^2x^2=(x-z)^2y^2\iff |z-y|\,x=|x-z|\,y .\] If $(z-y)x=(x-z)y$, we get $zx-xy=xy-zy$, which is $(*)$. If $(z-y)x=-(x-z)y$, we get $zx-xy=zy-xy$, i.e. $z(x-y)=0$, impossible since $x\neq y$. Conversely $(*)$ gives $(z-y)x=(x-z)y$ and hence $F\in CC'$.

So $F\in CC'\iff(*)\iff C,A',B',G$ are concyclic.

Problem 10.4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $\Omega$ and $\gamma$ be two circles meeting at points $P$ and $Q$. For an arbitrary point $A$ on $\Omega$, the tangents from $A$ to $\gamma$ meet the line $PQ$ at points $B$ and $C$. Prove that the circumcircles of all triangles $ABC$ are tangent to a fixed circle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An inversion at A and Poncelet's theorem)

Let $\mathcal P$ be the pencil of circles through $P$ and $Q$; it contains $\Omega$ and $\gamma$. Let $O$ be the centre of $\Omega$. Fix $A\in\Omega$, $A\ne P,Q$, outside $\gamma$ (so that the two tangents exist), and assume neither tangent is parallel to $PQ$. Then $A\notin PQ$, so $B\neq C$ and $A,B,C$ are not collinear. Let $B_0$, $C_0$ be the second intersections of the lines $AB$, $AC$ with $\Omega$ ($B_0=A$ if $AB$ touches $\Omega$). We use directed angles $\measuredangle$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

The map $J$. Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\sqrt{AP\cdot AQ}$, let $\rho$ be the reflection in the bisector of the angle $PAQ$, and let $s$ be the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$. This line passes through $O$, so $s(\Omega)=\Omega$, and $s$ maps the line $PQ$ onto itself. Put $J=s\circ\rho\circ\iota$; it is a bijection of the plane completed by one point at infinity, it maps circles and lines to circles and lines, and it preserves tangency.

(a) $\iota(P)$ lies on the ray $AP$ at distance $AP\cdot AQ/AP=AQ$ from $A$, and $\rho$ moves it to the ray $AQ$; so $\rho\iota(P)=Q$, and likewise $\rho\iota(Q)=P$. Hence $J(P)=s(Q)=P$ and $J(Q)=Q$.

(b) $\rho$ and $\iota$ commute (an inversion commutes with every isometry fixing its centre) and are involutions, so $\rho\iota$ is an involution. The circle $\Omega$ passes through the centre $A$ of $\iota$, so $\rho\iota(\Omega)$ is a line; it contains $\rho\iota(P)=Q$ and $\rho\iota(Q)=P$, so it is the line $PQ$. As $\rho\iota$ is an involution, it maps the line $PQ$ onto $\Omega$. Therefore $J(\Omega)=s(PQ)=PQ$ and $J(PQ)=s(\Omega)=\Omega$.

(c) $J(B)=B_0$ and $J(C)=C_0$. The point $B^\circ=\rho\iota(B)$ lies on $\Omega$ by (b), since $B\in PQ$, and $B^\circ\ne A$. As $\iota(B)$ lies on the line $AB$, the point $B^\circ$ lies on the reflection of the line $AB$ in the bisector of $\angle PAQ$. This reflection reverses directed angles and swaps the lines $AP$, $AQ$, so $\measuredangle(AP,AB_0)=\measuredangle(AB^\circ,AQ)$; call this angle $\varphi$. By the inscribed angle theorem (with the tangent–chord theorem if $B_0=A$), the rotation about $O$ through $\theta=2\varphi$ carries $P$ to $B_0$, and it also carries $B^\circ$ to $Q$. The reflection $s$ swaps $P$ and $Q$ and satisfies $s\circ(\text{rotation through }\theta)=(\text{rotation through }-\theta)\circ s$. Applying $s$ to “$B^\circ\mapsto Q$” shows that the rotation through $-\theta$ carries $s(B^\circ)$ to $P$, i.e. the rotation through $\theta$ carries $P$ to $s(B^\circ)$. Hence $s(B^\circ)=B_0$, i.e. $J(B)=B_0$. In the same way $J(C)=C_0$.

(d) The circle $ABC$ passes through the centre $A$ of $\iota$, so $J$ maps it onto a line. By (c) this is the line $B_0C_0$ (note $B_0\ne C_0$: two different lines through $A$ cannot both meet $\Omega$ again at the same point, and they cannot both be tangent at $A$).

Poncelet's theorem. The triangle $AB_0C_0$ is inscribed in $\Omega$, and its sides $AB_0$, $AC_0$ touch $\gamma$. We use Poncelet's closure theorem in its general form for a pencil of circles: if a triangle varies with its vertices on a circle $\Omega$ of a pencil and two of its sides touch circles of the same pencil, then the third side touches a circle $\kappa$ of the pencil, and $\kappa$ is the same for all such triangles. So the line $B_0C_0$ touches a circle $\kappa\in\mathcal P$ that does not depend on $A$.

Back to the circles $ABC$. By (d), the circle $ABC=J^{-1}(B_0C_0)$ touches $K_A=J^{-1}(\kappa)$, which passes through $J^{-1}(P)=P$ and $J^{-1}(Q)=Q$. $K_A$ is not the line $PQ$, because that line meets the circle $ABC$ at the two points $B\ne C$; so $K_A$ is a circle through $P$ and $Q$. It remains to show that $K_A$ does not depend on $A$. Each of $\iota,\rho,s$ reverses oriented angles between curves, so $J$ does too. At the fixed point $P$ this gives $\measuredangle(J(c_1),J(c_2))=-\measuredangle(c_1,c_2)$ for curves $c_1,c_2$ through $P$ (angles between their tangents at $P$). With $c_1=\Omega$, $c_2=K_A$ and (b): \[\measuredangle(\Omega,K_A)=-\measuredangle(PQ,\kappa)=\measuredangle(\kappa,PQ)\qquad\text{at }P .\] The right-hand side does not involve $A$, and a circle through $P$ and $Q$ is determined by its tangent at $P$. Hence $K_A=K$ is the same circle for every $A$: all circles $ABC$ touch the fixed circle $K$, which belongs to the pencil of $\Omega$ and $\gamma$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates with the radical axis as the y-axis)

Set-up. Take the line $PQ$ as the $y$-axis. Every circle through $P$ and $Q$ has an equation $x^2+y^2-2tx-k=0$ with the same $k>0$ ($P,Q=(0,\pm\sqrt k)$); write \[\Omega:\ x^2+y^2-2\omega x-k=0,\qquad \gamma:\ x^2+y^2-2gx-k=0\qquad(\omega\ne g),\] so $\gamma$ has centre $G=(g,0)$ and radius $\rho$, $\rho^2=g^2+k$. We prove that every circle $ABC$ touches the circle \[K:\ x^2+y^2-2\tau x-k=0,\qquad \tau=\omega+\frac{(g^2-2g\omega-k)^2}{4(g-\omega)(g^2+k)},\] which passes through $P,Q$ and depends only on $\Omega$ and $\gamma$. (Geometrically: $g^2-2g\omega-k=\operatorname{pow}(G,\Omega)$, so the centre of $K$ is $O+\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{pow}(G,\Omega)}{2\rho\cdot OG}\bigr)^2\,\overrightarrow{OG}$, where $O$ is the centre of $\Omega$.)

The points $B,C$. Let $A=(x_0,y_0)\in\Omega$, $A\ne P,Q$, so $x_0\ne0$ and $x_0^2+y_0^2=2\omega x_0+k$. The line through $A$ and $(0,\beta)$ is $(y_0-\beta)x-x_0y+x_0\beta=0$; it touches $\gamma$ iff the distance from $G$ to it is $\rho$, i.e. \[\bigl(gy_0+\beta(x_0-g)\bigr)^2=\rho^2\bigl((y_0-\beta)^2+x_0^2\bigr).\] This is a quadratic equation in $\beta$ whose roots are the ordinates $b_1,c_1$ of $B,C$. Using $\rho^2=g^2+k$ and the equation of $\Omega$, its leading coefficient is $D=(x_0-g)^2-\rho^2=2(\omega-g)x_0-y_0^2$ (we assume $D\ne0$, i.e. no tangent is parallel to $PQ$), and \[b_1+c_1=-\frac{2y_0(gx_0+k)}{D},\qquad b_1c_1=\frac{g^2y_0^2-(g^2+k)(2\omega x_0+k)}{D}.\]

The circle $ABC$. Write it as $\Sigma:\ x^2+y^2-2ux-2vy+w=0$. On the $y$-axis it gives $y^2-2vy+w=0$, with roots $b_1,c_1$; hence $2v=b_1+c_1$ and $w=b_1c_1$. Substituting $A$ gives $2ux_0=x_0^2+y_0^2-2vy_0+w$, and a short simplification (using $x_0^2+y_0^2=2\omega x_0+k$) gives \[u=-\frac{x_0\bigl(g^2+k+2(g-\omega)x_0\bigr)}{2D},\qquad v=-\frac{y_0(gx_0+k)}{D},\qquad w=\frac{g^2y_0^2-(g^2+k)(2\omega x_0+k)}{D}.\]

Tangency. Two circles $x^2+y^2-2u_ix-2v_iy+w_i=0$ ($i=1,2$) with radii $r_i$ ($r_i^2=u_i^2+v_i^2-w_i$) are tangent iff the distance $d$ of their centres equals $r_1+r_2$ or $|r_1-r_2|$, i.e. iff $(d^2-r_1^2-r_2^2)^2=4r_1^2r_2^2$; here $d^2-r_1^2-r_2^2=w_1+w_2-2u_1u_2-2v_1v_2$. For $\Sigma$ and $K$ ($u_2=\tau$, $v_2=0$, $w_2=-k$) the condition reads \[\bigl(w-k-2u\tau\bigr)^2=4\,(u^2+v^2-w)\,(\tau^2+k). \tag{1}\] Substituting the expressions for $u,v,w,\tau$, multiplying by the denominators and reducing $y_0^2$ by $y_0^2=2\omega x_0+k-x_0^2$, the difference of the two sides of (1) becomes a polynomial in $x_0,y_0,\omega,g,k$ which vanishes identically. This expansion is long but mechanical, and we did not do it by hand: the final identity was verified by computer algebra (see the check field). So this solution is complete only together with that computer check; Solution 1 needs no computer. Hence $\Sigma$ touches $K$ for every position of $A$.

Problem 10.5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Does there exist a convex hexagon $A_1B_2A_3B_1A_2B_3$ such that \[A_1A_2=2B_1B_2,\qquad A_1A_3=2B_1B_3,\qquad A_2A_3=2B_2B_3?\]

Show the answer

No

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The B-triangle is congruent to the medial triangle)

The answer is no.

Suppose such a convex hexagon $A_1B_2A_3B_1A_2B_3$ exists. Let $M_1,M_2,M_3$ be the midpoints of $A_2A_3$, $A_3A_1$, $A_1A_2$. By the midline theorem $M_1M_2=\frac12A_1A_2=B_1B_2$, $M_1M_3=\frac12A_1A_3=B_1B_3$, $M_2M_3=\frac12A_2A_3=B_2B_3$. So the triangles $M_1M_2M_3$ and $B_1B_2B_3$ are congruent (SSS) with $M_i\leftrightarrow B_i$: there is an isometry $g$ of the plane with $g(M_i)=B_i$.

What convexity gives. In the convex hexagon $A_1B_2A_3B_1A_2B_3$ the diagonal $A_3A_2$ cuts off the vertex $B_1$; so $B_1$ lies strictly on the other side of the line $A_2A_3$ than $A_1$. Similarly $B_2$ is strictly beyond $A_1A_3$ and $B_3$ strictly beyond $A_1A_2$. Let the vertices $A_1,A_2,A_3$ be in counterclockwise order and let $N_1$ be the outward normal of the side $A_2A_3$ of length $A_2A_3$ (the vector $\overrightarrow{A_2A_3}$ turned by $90^\circ$ clockwise); define $N_2,N_3$ likewise. Since $M_i$ lies on the side opposite $A_i$, \[N_i\cdot(B_i-M_i)>0\quad(i=1,2,3),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad \Sigma:=\sum_{i=1}^3N_i\cdot(B_i-M_i)>0. \tag{1}\] (In fact $\Sigma$ is twice the total area of the three triangles $A_2B_1A_3$, $A_3B_2A_1$, $A_1B_3A_2$.)

Computing $\Sigma$. Use complex numbers with the origin at the centroid of $A_1A_2A_3$, so $A_1+A_2+A_3=0$ and $M_i=-\frac12A_i$. For complex numbers $u\cdot v=\operatorname{Re}(\bar uv)$, and $N_i=-i(A_k-A_j)$ for $(i,j,k)=(1,2,3),(2,3,1),(3,1,2)$. A direct expansion gives \[\sum_{(i,j,k)}(\bar A_k-\bar A_j)A_i=2i\operatorname{Im}(\bar A_1A_2+\bar A_2A_3+\bar A_3A_1)=4i\,S,\qquad \sum_{(i,j,k)}(\bar A_k-\bar A_j)\bar A_i=0,\] where $S>0$ is the area of $A_1A_2A_3$. The isometry is $g(z)=e^{i\theta}z+t$ (direct) or $g(z)=e^{i\theta}\bar z+t$ (opposite). In both cases the translation part contributes $\sum N_i\cdot t=0$, because $N_1+N_2+N_3=0$.

  • Direct: $B_i-M_i=(e^{i\theta}-1)M_i+t$, and \[\Sigma=\operatorname{Re}\Bigl(\sum i(\bar A_k-\bar A_j)(e^{i\theta}-1)\bigl(-\tfrac12A_i\bigr)\Bigr)=-\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(i(e^{i\theta}-1)\cdot4iS\bigr)=2S(\cos\theta-1)\le0 .\]

  • Opposite: $B_i-M_i=e^{i\theta}\bar M_i-M_i+t$, and \[\Sigma=\operatorname{Re}\Bigl(\sum i(\bar A_k-\bar A_j)\bigl(-\tfrac12e^{i\theta}\bar A_i+\tfrac12A_i\bigr)\Bigr)=0+\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(i\cdot4iS)=-2S<0 .\]

Both contradict (1). Hence no such convex hexagon exists. (The bound is sharp: $B_i=M_i$ gives $\Sigma=0$ and a degenerate hexagon with three straight angles.)

Solution 2 (The trace of the similarity in barycentric coordinates)

The answer is no.

Suppose such a convex hexagon $A_1B_2A_3B_1A_2B_3$ exists.

The similarity. The triangle with vertices $2B_1,2B_2,2B_3$ (vectors from any fixed origin) has the same side lengths as $A_1A_2A_3$, so by SSS there is an isometry $\sigma$ of the plane with $\sigma(A_i)=2B_i$. Then $g=\frac12\sigma$ maps $A_i$ to $B_i$, and $g(Z)=L(Z)+t$ for a vector $t$ and a linear map $L$ which is $\frac12$ times a rotation or $\frac12$ times a reflection in a line through the origin. A rotation through $\theta$ has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, of trace $2\cos\theta$; a reflection has the eigenvalues $1,-1$, so its trace is $0$. Hence \[\operatorname{tr}L=\cos\theta\ \text{ or }\ 0,\qquad\text{in both cases}\qquad 1+\operatorname{tr}L\ge0. \tag{1}\]

Barycentric coordinates. Since $A_1,A_2,A_3$ are not collinear, every point $Z$ can be written uniquely as $Z=f_1(Z)A_1+f_2(Z)A_2+f_3(Z)A_3$ with $f_1(Z)+f_2(Z)+f_3(Z)=1$. The functions $f_i$ are affine, $f_i(A_j)$ is $1$ for $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise, and $f_1$ vanishes on the line $A_2A_3$ and is positive exactly on the side of this line containing $A_1$ (similarly for $f_2,f_3$).

What convexity gives. In the convex hexagon $A_1B_2A_3B_1A_2B_3$ the diagonal $A_3A_2$ cuts off the vertex $B_1$, so $B_1$ lies strictly on the other side of the line $A_2A_3$ than $A_1$, and $f_1(B_1)<0$. Similarly $f_2(B_2)<0$ and $f_3(B_3)<0$, so \[f_1(B_1)+f_2(B_2)+f_3(B_3)<0. \tag{2}\]

A matrix for $g$. Write $\varphi(Z)=(f_1(Z),f_2(Z),f_3(Z))^{T}\in\mathbb R^3$ and let $M$ be the $3\times3$ matrix whose $i$-th column is $\varphi(B_i)$. Its diagonal entries are $f_i(B_i)$, so by (2), $\operatorname{tr}M<0$. Since $\varphi$ is affine, $\varphi(Z)-\varphi(W)$ depends only on the vector $Z-W$; write $\psi(Z-W)=\varphi(Z)-\varphi(W)$. Then $\psi$ is a linear map from plane vectors to $\mathbb R^3$, and with $e_1,e_2,e_3$ the standard basis, \[u_1:=\psi(A_2-A_1)=e_2-e_1,\qquad u_2:=\psi(A_3-A_1)=e_3-e_1,\qquad u_3:=\varphi(A_1)=e_1\] form a basis of $\mathbb R^3$. Let $\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}\\ p_{21}&p_{22}\end{pmatrix}$ be the matrix of $L$ in the basis $A_2-A_1$, $A_3-A_1$ of the plane, i.e. $L(A_2-A_1)=p_{11}(A_2-A_1)+p_{21}(A_3-A_1)$ and $L(A_3-A_1)=p_{12}(A_2-A_1)+p_{22}(A_3-A_1)$; its trace is $p_{11}+p_{22}=\operatorname{tr}L$. Now \[Mu_1=Me_2-Me_1=\varphi(B_2)-\varphi(B_1)=\psi(B_2-B_1)=\psi\bigl(L(A_2-A_1)\bigr)=p_{11}u_1+p_{21}u_2,\] because $B_2-B_1=g(A_2)-g(A_1)=L(A_2-A_1)$. In the same way $Mu_2=p_{12}u_1+p_{22}u_2$. Finally $Mu_3=\varphi(B_1)=u_3+\psi(B_1-A_1)$, and $\psi(B_1-A_1)$ is a combination of $u_1,u_2$. So in the basis $u_1,u_2,u_3$ the matrix of $M$ is \[\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}&*\\ p_{21}&p_{22}&*\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\] whose trace is $p_{11}+p_{22}+1$. The trace of a matrix does not change under a change of basis, hence \[\operatorname{tr}M=1+\operatorname{tr}L\ge0\] by (1). This contradicts $\operatorname{tr}M<0$. Hence no such convex hexagon exists.

Remark. The bound is sharp: if $B_i$ is the midpoint of the side of $A_1A_2A_3$ opposite $A_i$, then $L=-\frac12\,\mathrm{id}$, $\operatorname{tr}L=-1$ and $f_i(B_i)=0$; the "hexagon" is the triangle $A_1A_2A_3$ with its three side midpoints, which has three straight angles.

Problem 10.6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

An angle and a point $P$ inside it are given. An arbitrary line passing through $P$ meets the sides of the angle at points $X$ and $Y$. Prove that there exists a pencil of circles such that for each pair $X$, $Y$ there exists a circle of this pencil passing through these points.

(The pencil is the set of circles having a common radical axis.)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Apollonius circles with foci P and its reflection)

Let $O$ be the vertex of the angle and let $K$, $L$ be the points on the two sides such that $OKPL$ is a parallelogram ($PK\parallel OL$, $PL\parallel OK$). Let $P'$ be the reflection of $P$ in the line $KL$; $P'\neq P$ because $P$ is not on the diagonal $KL$. We prove that for every line through $P$ meeting the sides at $X$ and $Y$, \[\frac{XP}{XP'}=\frac{YP}{YP'} . \tag{1}\] This suffices: the Apollonius circles $\{Z:\ ZP/ZP'=k\}$, $k>0$, $k\neq1$, have the common radical axis $KL$ (the perpendicular bisector of $PP'$): they form the pencil of circles with limit points $P,P'$. By (1), $X$ and $Y$ lie on the same circle of this pencil (and $k\neq1$, because $XP=XP'$ would put $X$ on $KL$, i.e. $X=K$, which is impossible as shown below).

Where $X,Y$ are. Write $OK=\alpha$, $OL=\beta$, $KX=\xi$, $LY=\eta$ (signed along the sides). The line $KP\parallel OY$ cuts the triangle $XOY$, so by Thales $\dfrac{XP}{PY}=\dfrac{XK}{KO}$, and in the same way $\dfrac{XP}{PY}=\dfrac{OL}{LY}$; hence $\xi\eta=\alpha\beta>0$ and $\dfrac{XP}{PY}=\dfrac{\xi}{\alpha}$. Moreover $X$ is beyond $K$ and $Y$ beyond $L$ ($\xi,\eta>0$): if both were negative, $|\xi|<\alpha$ and $|\eta|<\beta$ would give $\xi\eta<\alpha\beta$.

An isosceles trapezoid. The triangles $KPL$ and $LOK$ are congruent (parallelogram), and $P'$ is the reflection of $P$ in $KL$; so $P'$ is the reflection of $O$ in the perpendicular bisector of $KL$, i.e. $OKP'L$ is an isosceles trapezoid with $OP'\parallel KL$. In particular $KP'=KP=\beta$, $LP'=LP=\alpha$, and by the symmetry of the trapezoid $\angle OKP'=\angle OLP'$.

Similar triangles. Since $X$ is beyond $K$ and $Y$ beyond $L$, \[\angle XKP'=180^\circ-\angle OKP'=180^\circ-\angle OLP'=\angle P'LY,\qquad \frac{KX}{KP'}=\frac{\xi}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha}{\eta}=\frac{LP'}{LY}.\] So the triangles $XKP'$ and $P'LY$ are similar (SAS), whence $\dfrac{XP'}{P'Y}=\dfrac{KX}{LP'}=\dfrac{\xi}{\alpha}$. Together with $\dfrac{XP}{PY}=\dfrac\xi\alpha$ this gives $\dfrac{XP}{XP'}=\dfrac{YP}{YP'}$, which is (1). Hence every pair $X,Y$ lies on a circle of the pencil of Apollonius circles with limit points $P$ and $P'$.

Solution 2 (One explicit pencil in oblique coordinates)

Let $O$ be the vertex, $u,v$ unit vectors along the sides, and write $P=\alpha u+\beta v$ ($\alpha,\beta>0$); put $K=\alpha u$, $L=\beta v$. Let $\ell$ be the affine function with $\ell(xu+yv)=\beta x+\alpha y-\alpha\beta$; it vanishes exactly on the line $KL$. For each real $\lambda$ consider \[\mathcal C_\lambda=\{Z:\ |Z-P|^2=\lambda\,\ell(Z)\}.\] The equation has the form $|Z|^2+(\text{linear})=0$, so each $\mathcal C_\lambda$ is a circle (possibly a point or empty), and for $\lambda\neq\mu$ the radical axis of $\mathcal C_\lambda$ and $\mathcal C_\mu$ is $\{(\lambda-\mu)\ell=0\}=KL$. So the $\mathcal C_\lambda$ form a pencil. We show that $X$ and $Y$ lie on the same $\mathcal C_\lambda$, i.e. \[\frac{|X-P|^2}{\ell(X)}=\frac{|Y-P|^2}{\ell(Y)} .\] Write $X=(\alpha+\xi)u$, $Y=(\beta+\eta)v$. The points $X,P,Y$ are collinear iff $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\xi}+\frac{\beta}{\beta+\eta}=1$, i.e. iff $\xi\eta=\alpha\beta$; in particular $\xi,\eta\ne0$. Let $\varphi$ be the angle of the given angle, $u\cdot v=\cos\varphi$. Then \[|X-P|^2=|\xi u-\beta v|^2=\xi^2-2\beta\xi\cos\varphi+\beta^2,\qquad \ell(X)=\beta\xi,\] \[|Y-P|^2=|\eta v-\alpha u|^2=\eta^2-2\alpha\eta\cos\varphi+\alpha^2,\qquad \ell(Y)=\alpha\eta .\] Hence \[\frac{|X-P|^2}{\ell(X)}=\frac\xi\beta-2\cos\varphi+\frac\beta\xi,\qquad \frac{|Y-P|^2}{\ell(Y)}=\frac\eta\alpha-2\cos\varphi+\frac\alpha\eta,\] and with $\eta=\frac{\alpha\beta}\xi$ we have $\frac\eta\alpha=\frac\beta\xi$ and $\frac\alpha\eta=\frac\xi\beta$: the two values coincide. So $X$ and $Y$ lie on the same circle $\mathcal C_\lambda$ of the pencil (with radical axis $KL$).

Problem 10.7

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ be the bisectors of a triangle $ABC$, $I$ be its incenter, and $X_b$ be the reflection of the circumcenter of the triangle $FBD$ about $FD$. $X_a$, $X_c$ are defined similarly. Prove that $X_a$, $X_b$, $X_c$, $I$ are concyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (Concurrent reflected circles and an Apollonius circle)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle$ between lines modulo $180^\circ$, and $\delta(m)$ for the direction of a line $m$ (so $\measuredangle(m,n)=\delta(n)-\delta(m)$). Here $D\in BC$, $E\in CA$, $F\in AB$; the point $X_a$ is built from the circle $AEF$ and the line $EF$, $X_b$ from $FBD$ and $FD$, and $X_c$ from $DCE$ and $DE$.

Step 1: three concurrent circles. Let $\omega_a,\omega_b,\omega_c$ be the reflections of the circles $AEF$, $FBD$, $DCE$ in the lines $EF$, $FD$, $DE$. Their centres are $X_a,X_b,X_c$. The circle $\omega_b$ passes through $F$, $D$ and the reflection $B^*$ of $B$ in $FD$; similarly $\omega_a$ passes through $E,F,A^*$ and $\omega_c$ through $D,E,C^*$. Let $K$ be the second common point of $\omega_b$ and $\omega_c$ (other than $D$). A reflection reverses directed angles, so \[\measuredangle(KF,KD)=\measuredangle(B^*F,B^*D)=-\measuredangle(BF,BD)=\measuredangle(BC,BA),\qquad \measuredangle(KD,KE)=\measuredangle(C^*D,C^*E)=-\measuredangle(CD,CE)=\measuredangle(CA,CB).\] Since $\measuredangle(AB,AC)+\measuredangle(CA,CB)+\measuredangle(BC,BA)=0$, adding gives \[\measuredangle(KF,KE)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\measuredangle(AC,AB)=\measuredangle(AE,AF)=-\measuredangle(A^*E,A^*F)=\measuredangle(A^*F,A^*E).\] Hence $K\in\omega_a$: the three circles pass through $K$.

Step 2: the angle at $X_b$. The circles $\omega_a,\omega_b$ have the common chord $FK$, so $X_aX_b\perp FK$. Likewise $X_bX_c\perp DK$. Turning both lines through $90^\circ$ does not change the angle between them, so \[\measuredangle(X_bX_a,X_bX_c)=\measuredangle(KF,KD)=\measuredangle(BC,BA). \tag{1}\]

Step 3 (Lemma): the line $IX_b$ is the reflection of the line $IB$ in the bisector of the angle $FID$. The ray $IF$ is opposite to $IC$ and the ray $ID$ is opposite to $IA$, so $\angle FID=\angle AIC=90^\circ+\frac{\angle B}2$. Since $D$ lies between $B$ and $C$, the points $B$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the line $FD$; the open segment $FC$ lies on the side of $C$, and $I$ lies on it. So $I$ and $B$ are on opposite sides of $FD$.

If $\angle B=60^\circ$, then $\angle FID=120^\circ=180^\circ-\angle FBD$, so $I$ lies on the circle $FBD$, on the arc not containing $B$; being on the bisector of $\angle FBD$, it is the midpoint $W$ of that arc considered in (i) below, and (ii) below gives $X_b=I$ (the inverse of a point of $\Gamma$ is the point itself). Then the four points are only three, and they are trivially concyclic. If $\angle A=\angle C$, the figure is symmetric in the line $BI$, which is then the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $FD$; $X_b$ lies on $m$, so $IX_b=IB$ and the lemma is trivial. We now assume $\angle B\neq60^\circ$ and $\angle A\neq\angle C$.

(i) Let $\Gamma$ be the circle $FID$, with centre $Z$ and radius $\rho_0$, and let $N$ be the midpoint of $FD$. As $\angle FID>90^\circ$, $Z$ lies on the side of $FD$ opposite to $I$ (the side of $B$), and $\angle FZD=2(180^\circ-\angle FID)=180^\circ-\angle B$. Let $W$ be the reflection of $Z$ in $FD$. Then $\angle FWD=180^\circ-\angle B$ and $W$ is on the side of $FD$ opposite to $B$, so $FBDW$ is cyclic. As $WF=WD$, $W$ is the midpoint of the arc $FD$ of the circle $FBD$ not containing $B$, so it lies on the bisector $BI$ of the angle $FBD$; and $W\neq I$ because $\angle B\neq60^\circ$.

(ii) The reflection of the circle $FBD$ in $FD$ is $\omega_b$; it passes through the reflection $Z$ of $W$. So $X_b$, the circumcentre of $FDZ$, lies on the perpendicular bisector $m$ of $FD$, which also contains $Z$, $N$, $W$. Let $\rho_1$ be the radius of $\omega_b$ and $Z'$ the point of $\omega_b$ diametrically opposite to $Z$. The diameter $ZZ'$ lies on $m$, and the foot of the perpendicular from $F$ to $m$ is $N$. In the right triangle $ZFZ'$ (right angle at $F$) this gives $ZF^2=ZN\cdot ZZ'=2\rho_1\cdot ZN$, with $N$ between $Z$ and $Z'$; so $X_b$, the midpoint of $ZZ'$, lies on the ray $ZN$. Since $ZW=2ZN$ along the same ray, \[ZW\cdot ZX_b=2ZN\cdot\rho_1=ZF^2=\rho_0^2 ,\] so $W$ and $X_b$ are inverse with respect to $\Gamma$.

(iii) Two points that are inverse with respect to a circle have that circle as an Apollonius circle: $YW/YX_b$ is the same for all $Y\in\Gamma$. The line $m$ passes through the centre $Z$ and meets $\Gamma$ at two points $S,S'$, which divide $WX_b$ internally and externally in this ratio. By the bisector theorem and its converse, for every point $Y\in\Gamma$ other than $S,S'$ the lines $YS$, $YS'$ bisect the angles between $YW$ and $YX_b$. Take $Y=I$ (allowed: $I\notin m$ because $\angle A\neq\angle C$). The points $S,S'$ are also the midpoints of the two arcs $FD$ of $\Gamma$, so $IS$, $IS'$ are the bisectors of the angles between $IF$ and $ID$. The reflection in $IS$ (which gives the same image of any line through $I$ as the reflection in $IS'$) therefore swaps the lines $IF$, $ID$ and also the lines $IW=IB$, $IX_b$. This proves the lemma.

Step 4: the angle at $I$. The reflection in a bisector of the angle between lines $\ell_1,\ell_2$ through $I$ maps a line through $I$ of direction $\delta$ to the line of direction $\delta(\ell_1)+\delta(\ell_2)-\delta$. By the lemma and its analogues for $X_a$ (angle $EIF$) and $X_c$ (angle $DIE$), and since $D\in IA$, $E\in IB$, $F\in IC$, \[\delta(IX_a)=\delta(IB)+\delta(IC)-\delta(IA),\qquad \delta(IX_c)=\delta(IA)+\delta(IB)-\delta(IC),\] so $\measuredangle(IX_a,IX_c)=2\measuredangle(IC,IA)$. Now $\measuredangle(IC,IA)=\measuredangle(CI,CB)+\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(AB,AI)$. Because $CI$ and $AI$ are angle bisectors, $2\measuredangle(CI,CB)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)$ and $2\measuredangle(AB,AI)=\measuredangle(AB,AC)$. Hence \[\measuredangle(IX_a,IX_c)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)+\measuredangle(AB,AC)+2\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(BA,BC)+2\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA). \tag{2}\]

Conclusion. By (1) and (2), $\measuredangle(X_bX_a,X_bX_c)=\measuredangle(IX_a,IX_c)$, so $X_a,X_b,X_c,I$ are concyclic. (If $\angle A$ or $\angle C$ equals $60^\circ$, then $X_a=I$ or $X_c=I$ by the remark in Step 3, and again only three distinct points remain.)

Problem 10.8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

In an acute triangle $ABC$, $M$ is the midpoint of side $BC$. Points $B_1$ and $C_1$ are taken on sides $AC$ and $AB$, respectively, such that $\angle MB_1C=\angle MC_1B=60^\circ$. Prove that the Euler lines of the triangles $AB_1C_1$, $BMC_1$, and $CMB_1$ are concurrent.

Show the solution

Solution (The pedal triangle of the centre of an equilateral triangle)

Let $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ be the apexes of the equilateral triangles erected inwards on $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ (on the same side as the opposite vertex), and let $P$ be the centre of $BCA'$. Then $PM\perp BC$ and $\angle PBC=\angle PCB=30^\circ$. We assume $\angle A,\angle B,\angle C\ne60^\circ$: if $\angle B=60^\circ$ or $\angle C=60^\circ$, one of the triangles $BMC_1$, $CMB_1$ is equilateral and has no Euler line, and if $\angle A=60^\circ$ the three Euler lines are parallel (see the note).

Step 1: $B_1$, $C_1$ are the feet of the perpendiculars from $P$. In the right triangle $PMC$, $\angle MPC=60^\circ=\angle MB_1C$. The points $P$ and $B_1$ lie on the same side of $BC$ (the side of $A$), so $M,C,P,B_1$ are concyclic. This circle has diameter $PC$ because $\angle PMC=90^\circ$; hence $\angle PB_1C=90^\circ$. Likewise $\angle PC_1B=90^\circ$. So $MB_1C_1$ is the pedal triangle of $P$, and the circles $AB_1C_1$, $BMC_1$, $CMB_1$ have diameters $AP$, $BP$, $CP$. Their centres $O_1,O_2,O_3$ are the midpoints of $AP,BP,CP$. Let $h$ be the homothety with centre $P$ and ratio $2$; then $h(O_1)=A$, $h(O_2)=B$, $h(O_3)=C$, and $h$ maps each Euler line to the parallel line through the image of the centre.

Step 2: a triangle with a $60^\circ$ angle. Let $UVW$ be a non-equilateral triangle with $\angle V=60^\circ$, circumcentre $O'$ and orthocentre $H'$. We show that $O'$ and $H'$ are symmetric in the bisector of $\angle V$, so the Euler line $O'H'$ is perpendicular to this bisector. Use complex numbers with $V=0$ and the bisector as the real axis: $U=u\,e^{-i\pi/6}$, $W=w\,e^{i\pi/6}$ with $u,w>0$, and $O'=x+iy$. The condition $|O'-U|=|O'|$ reads $u^2=2\operatorname{Re}(O'\bar U)$, i.e. $u=2\operatorname{Re}(O'e^{i\pi/6})=\sqrt3x-y$; similarly $w=\sqrt3x+y$. Hence \[U+W=(u+w)\cos30^\circ+i(w-u)\sin30^\circ=3x+iy .\] By the classical formula $H'=U+V+W-2O'$ (vectors), $H'=3x+iy-2(x+iy)=x-iy=\overline{O'}$. Finally $O'\neq H'$, since $y=0$ would give $u=w$ and an equilateral triangle.

Step 3: two of the images. In $BMC_1$ the angle at $C_1$ is $60^\circ$, so its Euler line $e_2$ passes through $O_2$ and is perpendicular to the bisector of $\angle BC_1M$. Then $h(e_2)$ is the line through $B$ perpendicular to this bisector. The reflection in the bisector maps this line onto itself and the ray $C_1B$ onto the ray $C_1M$, so $h(e_2)$ meets the ray $C_1M$ at a point $T$ with $C_1T=C_1B$. The triangle $BC_1T$ is isosceles with apex angle $60^\circ$, hence equilateral, and $\angle C_1BT=60^\circ$. Moreover $T$ lies on the same side of $AB$ as $M$, i.e. as $C$. Since the ray $BC_1$ is the ray $BA$, the line $BT$ makes the angle $60^\circ$ with $BA$ on the side of $C$: $h(e_2)=BC'$. In the same way, the image $h(e_3)$ of the Euler line $e_3$ of $CMB_1$ is the line $CB'$.

Step 4: the third image. For $AB_1C_1$ we have $2O_1=A+P$, so by the same formula its orthocentre is $H_1=A+B_1+C_1-2O_1=B_1+C_1-P$, and \[h(H_1)=2H_1-P=(2B_1-P)+(2C_1-P)-P=P_b+P_c-P=:R,\] where $P_b=2B_1-P$ and $P_c=2C_1-P$ are the reflections of $P$ in $AC$ and $AB$ (by Step 1). So $h(e_1)$ is the line $AR$.

Step 5: $AR$ is the reflection of $AA'$ in the bisector of angle $A$. Use complex numbers with $A=0$, the bisector of angle $A$ as the real axis and $ABC$ counterclockwise: $B=c\,e^{-i\theta}$, $C=b\,e^{i\theta}$, $\theta=\frac{\angle A}2$, $k=\cos2\theta=\cos A$, $\varepsilon=e^{i\pi/3}$. The inner apex is $A'=\frac{B+C}2+\frac{\sqrt3}2i(C-B)=B\bar\varepsilon+C\varepsilon$, and $P=\frac{B+C+A'}3=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\bigl(Be^{-i\pi/6}+Ce^{i\pi/6}\bigr)$, because $1+\varepsilon=\sqrt3e^{i\pi/6}$. With $\varphi=\theta+\frac\pi6$ and $\psi=\theta+\frac\pi3$, \[A'=c\,e^{-i\psi}+b\,e^{i\psi},\qquad \sqrt3P=c\,e^{-i\varphi}+b\,e^{i\varphi}.\] Reflection in the line through $0$ with direction $e^{\pm i\theta}$ is $z\mapsto e^{\pm2i\theta}\bar z$, so $R=(e^{2i\theta}+e^{-2i\theta})\bar P-P=2k\bar P-P$ and \[\sqrt3R=(2kc-b)\,e^{i\varphi}+(2kb-c)\,e^{-i\varphi}.\] Multiply by $A'$, using $\varphi-\psi=-\frac\pi6$ and $\sin(\varphi+\psi)=\sin(2\theta+\frac\pi2)=k$: \[\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt3RA')=-\tfrac12(2kc-b)c+\tfrac12(2kb-c)b+k\bigl[(2kc-b)b-(2kb-c)c\bigr]=k(b^2-c^2)+k(c^2-b^2)=0 .\] So $RA'$ is real, i.e. $\arg R\equiv-\arg A'\pmod\pi$: the line $AR$ is the mirror image of $AA'$ in the real axis, the bisector of angle $A$.

Step 6: concurrence. At $B$ the lines $BA'$ and $BC'$ make $60^\circ$ with $BC$ and with $BA$ respectively, on the inner side, so they are isogonal. Similarly $CA'$ and $CB'$ are isogonal at $C$. The point $A'$ is not on a sideline (because $\angle B,\angle C\ne60^\circ$) and not on the circumcircle (because $\angle A\neq60^\circ$), so it has an isogonal conjugate, which is the finite point $Q=BC'\cap CB'$. Hence the reflection of $AA'$ in the bisector of angle $A$, which by Step 5 is $AR$, also passes through $Q$. Thus $h(e_1)=AR$, $h(e_2)=BC'$ and $h(e_3)=CB'$ all pass through $Q$, and the Euler lines $e_1,e_2,e_3$ all pass through $Z=h^{-1}(Q)$, the midpoint of $PQ$.
