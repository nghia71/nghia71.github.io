Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 29: Exponents, Powers, and Growth · Laws of Exponents

If $x$ is a positive real number satisfying $x^x=3$, what is $x^{x^{x+1}}$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Add parentheses to show which power in the exponent tower is evaluated first. Identify the part of the tower that already resembles the given equation.
Hint 2: key idea
Split the exponent $x+1$ in the innermost power using the rule for adding exponents. One factor of the result is known from the condition on $x$. (LPS VI, Chapter 29: Exponents, Powers, and Growth)
Hint 3: approach
Replace the known factor and simplify the exponent of the outer power. Use the power-of-a-power rule to regroup the outer expression with $x^x$ as its base.
Common trap
An exponent tower is evaluated from the top down, so grouping the bottom two powers first changes the expression.
Show the answer

$27$

Show solution 1 – Rewrite the top exponent

The top exponent is $x^{x+1}=x^x\cdot x=3x$. Hence \[x^{x^{x+1}}=x^{3x}=\left(x^x\right)^3=3^3=27.\] The answer is $\boxed{27}$.

Show solution 2 – Write $x$ as a power of $3$

Raise both sides of $x^x=3$ to the power $\frac1x$: since $\left(x^x\right)^{1/x}=x$, we get $x=3^{1/x}$. Then \[x^{x^{x+1}}=\left(3^{1/x}\right)^{x^{x+1}}=3^{\,x^{x+1}/x}=3^{\,x^{x}}=3^3=27.\] The answer is $\boxed{27}$.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Tangent and External Angle Relations

The diagram shows a large circle of radius $10$ and nine smaller circles, each tangential to two other small circles and also tangential to the large circle. The sum of the distances between the centres of the smaller circles and the centre of the large circle is equal to $d$.

What is the perimeter of the shaded polygon in terms of $d$?

(A) $90-2d$ (B) $90-d$ (C) $180-d$ (D) $180-2d$ (E) $180+2d$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Label the small-circle radii in order around the ring. Compare the centre segments that make up the shaded polygon with the segments whose total length is $d$.
Hint 2: key idea
Join the centres of two tangent circles through their point of contact. Adjacent small circles touch externally, so their radii add along this line, while the large radius includes the centre distance and the small radius at an internal tangency. (LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Sum the expressions for the polygon edges, keeping track of how many times each radius occurs. Separately use the distances from the large centre to express the sum of the small radii in terms of $d$.
Common trap
Include the edge that joins the last small-circle centre back to the first.
Show the answer

(D) $180-2d$

Show the solution – Two tangency facts

Let $O$ be the centre of the large circle, and let the small circles have centres $O_1,O_2,\dots,O_9$ (in order around the ring) and radii $r_1,r_2,\dots,r_9$.

When two circles touch, the point of contact lies on the line through their centres. Hence:

  • small circle $i$ touches the large circle from inside, so $OO_i=10-r_i$;

  • neighbouring small circles touch from outside, so $O_iO_{i+1}=r_i+r_{i+1}$ (with $O_{10}=O_1$, $r_{10}=r_1$).

The perimeter of the shaded polygon is \[(r_1+r_2)+(r_2+r_3)+\dots+(r_9+r_1)=2(r_1+r_2+\dots+r_9),\] since every radius appears in exactly two sides. Also \[d=(10-r_1)+(10-r_2)+\dots+(10-r_9)=90-(r_1+\dots+r_9),\] so $r_1+\dots+r_9=90-d$ and the perimeter is $2(90-d)=\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 180-2d}$.

Problem 3

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition · Additive and Subtractive Area

Two regular hexagons with side-lengths of $60$ are shown in the diagram which is not drawn to scale. The right-hand hexagon was created by shifting the left-hand hexagon horizontally by the length of the segment $AB$. This creates three regions of equal area. What is the length of $AB$?

(A) $30$ (B) $39$ (C) $40$ (D) $45$ (E) $52$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the overlap and the two regions belonging to just one hexagon. Which of the equal-area regions make up the left hexagon?
Hint 2: key idea
Join the leftmost vertices of the two hexagons with a horizontal segment. This cuts the left-only region into an upper and a lower parallelogram, with horizontal bases equal to the shift. (LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Use equilateral triangles to find the area of one regular hexagon and the height of each parallelogram. Express the left-only area both as a share of the hexagon and as the sum of the parallelogram areas, then set the two expressions equal.
Common trap
The three equal regions are in the union, and one original hexagon contains only two of them.
Show the answer

(D) $45$

Show solution 1 – Two parallelograms

Let $x=AB$. A regular hexagon of side $60$ is made of six equilateral triangles of side $60$, each of height $30\sqrt3$ and area $\frac12\cdot60\cdot30\sqrt3=900\sqrt3$. So each hexagon has area $5400\sqrt3$ and height $60\sqrt3$.

In the diagram $B$ lies on the top side of the left hexagon, so $0<x<60$ and the two hexagons overlap. Each hexagon consists of the shaded overlap and one white region. The three regions have equal areas, so the white region on the left has area $\frac12\cdot5400\sqrt3=2700\sqrt3$.

Cut the left white region (blue in the figure) by the horizontal segment joining the left corners of the two hexagons. That segment has length $x$, because the second hexagon is the first one shifted by $x$. The upper piece has two horizontal sides of length $x$ (the segment $AB$ and the cut), and its two slanted sides are parallel, since one is the shift of the other. So it is a parallelogram with base $x$ and height $30\sqrt3$. The lower piece is its mirror image. Therefore the white region has area $2\cdot x\cdot30\sqrt3=60\sqrt3\,x$, and \[60\sqrt3\,x=2700\sqrt3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=45.\] The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 45}$.

Show solution 2 – Area of the overlap

Let $x=AB$; in the diagram $x<60$, since $B$ lies on the top side of the left hexagon. A regular hexagon of side $60$ is made of six equilateral triangles of side $60$ and height $30\sqrt3$, so its area is $6\cdot\frac12\cdot60\cdot30\sqrt3=5400\sqrt3$.

The overlap is a hexagon. Its top side runs from $B$ to the top right corner of the left hexagon, so it has length $60-x$; the same holds for its bottom side. Its horizontal middle line runs from the left corner of the right hexagon to the right corner of the left hexagon, so it has length $120-x$. This middle line cuts the overlap into two trapezoids with parallel sides $60-x$ and $120-x$ and height $30\sqrt3$. Hence \[\text{overlap}=2\cdot\frac{(60-x)+(120-x)}2\cdot30\sqrt3=30\sqrt3\,(180-2x).\] The left hexagon is the overlap plus one white region, and these two have equal area, so the overlap is half of a hexagon: \[30\sqrt3\,(180-2x)=2700\sqrt3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad180-2x=90\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=45.\] The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}\ 45}$.

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Diameter and Semicircle Geometry

Square $ABCD$ with side length $4$ inches is inscribed in a circle. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of minor arcs $AB$ and $CD$, respectively. What is the value of $AM\times AN$? Express your answer in simplest radical form.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the two arc midpoints on a sketch and join them to $A$. Which triangle has both of the lengths you need?
Hint 2: key idea
Find the line of symmetry through the center that cuts both arcs in half. Use it to identify $MN$ and determine the angle it subtends at $A$. (LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Drop the perpendicular from $A$ to $MN$ and locate its foot on the square. Express the area of $AMN$ once using the two sides from $A$ and once using $MN$ as the base, then set the two expressions equal.
Common trap
Do not take $MN$ to be a side or a diagonal of the square: the two arc midpoints lie on the symmetry line perpendicular to $AB$.
Show the answer

$8\sqrt2$

Show solution 1 – Area of a right triangle

Let $O$ be the centre. The diagonal of the square is $4\sqrt2$ and is a diameter, so the radius is $2\sqrt2$.

The line through $O$ perpendicular to $AB$ is a line of symmetry of the figure; it cuts both arcs $AB$ and $CD$ in their midpoints. So $M$ and $N$ lie on it and $MN$ is a diameter, $MN=4\sqrt2$. Hence $\angle MAN=90^\circ$ (angle in a semicircle).

Let $P$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $MN$; it is the midpoint of $AB$, so $AP=2$. Computing the area of the right triangle $AMN$ in two ways, \[\tfrac12\,AM\cdot AN=\tfrac12\,MN\cdot AP\quad\Longrightarrow\quad AM\cdot AN=4\sqrt2\cdot2=8\sqrt2.\] The answer is $\boxed{8\sqrt2}$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put the centre at the origin with the sides of the square parallel to the axes. Then $A=(-2,2)$, and the radius is the half-diagonal $2\sqrt2$. The arc midpoints lie on the vertical axis of symmetry: $M=(0,2\sqrt2)$ and $N=(0,-2\sqrt2)$. Then \[AM^2=2^2+(2\sqrt2-2)^2=16-8\sqrt2,\qquad AN^2=2^2+(2\sqrt2+2)^2=16+8\sqrt2,\] so $AM^2\cdot AN^2=16^2-(8\sqrt2)^2=256-128=128$ and $AM\cdot AN=\sqrt{128}=\boxed{8\sqrt2}$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Place-Value Decomposition

Anni places the digits $1,2,\dots,9$ on a circle in some order. She reads three adjacent digits clockwise to form a three-digit number, such as $\blacktriangle\,\blacksquare\,\bigstar$ as in the diagram, and writes down all nine such numbers. One of these numbers is $a$, which is a factor of the sum of the remaining $8$ numbers. How many possible values of $a$ are there?

(A) $1$ (B) $2$ (C) $3$ (D) $4$ (E) $5$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a few readings from a sample circle, including one that wraps past the starting point. Follow a single digit and mark where it appears in each reading.
Hint 2: key idea
Track the contribution of each digit in the hundreds, tens and units places. This gives a total for all nine readings that is independent of the order of the digits. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Find the total and see why adding back $a$ preserves divisibility by $a$. Factor the total and screen its three-digit divisors using the digit restrictions. For any candidate that survives, check whether its digits can appear as consecutive entries in the circle.
Common trap
The three digits in any reading must be distinct and all come from $1$ through $9$.
Show the answer

(B) $2$

Show the solution – The total is always 4995

Each of the nine numbers uses three neighbouring digits. Going round the circle, every digit is the hundreds digit of exactly one number, the tens digit of exactly one number and the units digit of exactly one number. So the total of all nine numbers is always \[T=100(1+2+\dots+9)+10(1+2+\dots+9)+(1+2+\dots+9)=111\cdot45=4995.\] The number $a$ divides the sum of the other eight, $T-a$, exactly when $a$ divides $T$ (because $a$ divides $a$).

Now $4995=3^3\cdot5\cdot37$, and its divisors are \[1,3,5,9,15,27,37,45,111,135,185,333,555,999,1665,4995.\] The three-digit ones are $111$, $135$, $185$, $333$, $555$, $999$. The number $a$ is made of three different digits, so $111$, $333$, $555$, $999$ are impossible. Both $135$ and $185$ do occur: put $1,3,5$ (or $1,8,5$) next to each other clockwise and the other digits anywhere.

So there are $\boxed{\textbf{(B)}\ 2}$ possible values of $a$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Complementary Counting

Zoe has an unusual method of choosing when to go swimming. At the beginning of the year she randomly chooses three different days of the week, then always has a swim on those days only. Using this method, what is the probability her schedule will include at least three days in a row without a swim?

(A) $\frac25$ (B) $\frac12$ (C) $\frac35$ (D) $\frac7{10}$ (E) $\frac45$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a week with Sunday followed by Monday and mark three swim days. Record the lengths of the stretches between those days.
Hint 2: key idea
Count the schedules with no stretch of three days off. Going around the week gives three gaps between swim days, and each gap in a schedule you want to exclude has length at most two. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
List the possible gap lengths for the excluded schedules, allowing zero. For each pattern, use its unique adjacent pair of swim days or of days off to count where it fits in the week. Count all three-day choices separately to prepare the complement calculation.
Common trap
A stretch of days off can cross from one week into the next.
Show the answer

(C) $\frac35$

Show solution 1 – Count the bad schedules

The schedule repeats every week, so the days form a cycle: Sunday is followed by Monday. There are $\binom73=35$ equally likely choices of the three swim days.

Going round the week, the four days without a swim fall into three gaps between consecutive swim days. The gap sizes $g_1,g_2,g_3\ge0$ satisfy $g_1+g_2+g_3=4$. The schedule is bad (no three non-swim days in a row) exactly when every gap is at most $2$. With sum $4$ and each gap at most $2$, the gaps are $2,2,0$ or $2,1,1$ in some order.

  • Gaps $2,2,0$: two swim days are neighbours. Choose this pair of consecutive days ($7$ ways); then the rest is forced: two days off, a swim, two days off. That gives $7$ schedules.

  • Gaps $2,1,1$: choose the two consecutive days off ($7$ ways); then the rest is forced: swim, day off, swim, day off, swim. That gives $7$ schedules.

So $14$ schedules are bad and $35-14=21$ are good. The probability is $\frac{21}{35}=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ \frac35}$.

(If Sunday and Monday were not counted as neighbours, the same kind of count would give $\frac{16}{35}$, which is not among the options; so the cyclic reading is the intended one.)

Show solution 2 – Count the good schedules directly

Treat the week as a cycle (Sunday is followed by Monday), since the schedule repeats every week. There are $\binom73=35$ equally likely choices.

There are only four days without a swim, so a schedule has at most one run of three or more such days. Count by the length of that run.

  • A run of $4$ days off: then the three swim days are consecutive. There are $7$ such schedules (one for each starting day).

  • A run of exactly $3$ days off: choose the run ($7$ ways, by its first day). The day just before and the day just after the run must be swim days. Of the two remaining days, one is a swim day and one is not: $2$ ways. That gives $14$ schedules.

So $7+14=21$ schedules are good, and the probability is $\frac{21}{35}=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ \frac35}$.

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Diameter and Semicircle Geometry

  • (a) Triangle $PQR$ has a right angle at $Q$ and $W$ is the midpoint of the side $PR$. Given that $PQ=6$ and $QR=8$, use the diagram below to find the length of $QW$.

  • (b) Triangle $ABC$ has a right angle at $B$ and $AB<BC$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AC$. Let $D$ be the reflection of $C$ in the line $BM$ (extended beyond $M$).

    Given that angle $DAC$ is $56^\circ$ find the size of the angle

    • (i) $ACD$,

    • (ii) $BAC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the hypotenuse and its midpoint in each triangle. In part (b), mark the line of reflection and note which points on it stay fixed.
Hint 2: key idea
Complete the right triangle in part (a) to a rectangle and compare its diagonals. Use the same midpoint property and the reflection in part (b) to place $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ on one circle. (LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
In (a), use the hypotenuse length and the rectangle’s common diagonal midpoint. In (b)(i), use diameter $AC$ to set up the angle sum in $ACD$. In (b)(ii), express $\angle BMC$ using the base angles of $MBC$, then use the mirror line to relate its supplementary angle to the vertex angle of $MCD$.
Common trap
Use $AB<BC$ to check which ray of the mirror line from $M$ bisects $\angle CMD$.
Show the answer

(a) $QW=5$; (b)(i) $\angle ACD=34^\circ$, (ii) $\angle BAC=62^\circ$

Show solution 1 – The circle on the hypotenuse

(a) Complete the rectangle $PQRS$ as in the diagram. The diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other, so the midpoint $W$ of $PR$ is also the midpoint of $QS$, and \[QW=\tfrac12QS=\tfrac12PR=\tfrac12\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\boxed{5}.\]

(b) By the same fact, in the right triangle $ABC$ the midpoint $M$ of the hypotenuse satisfies $MA=MB=MC$. The reflection in line $BM$ fixes $M$, so $MD=MC$. Hence $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ all lie on the circle with centre $M$ and diameter $AC$.

(i) Since $AC$ is a diameter, $\angle ADC=90^\circ$. In triangle $ADC$, $\angle ACD=180^\circ-90^\circ-56^\circ=\boxed{34^\circ}$.

(ii) Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$. In a triangle the longer side is opposite the larger angle; $AB<BC$ gives $\angle ACB<\angle BAC$, and since these two angles add up to $90^\circ$, $\alpha>45^\circ$.

Triangle $MBC$ is isosceles ($MB=MC$) with base angles $\angle MCB=90^\circ-\alpha$, so $\angle BMC=180^\circ-2(90^\circ-\alpha)=2\alpha>90^\circ$.

Triangle $MCD$ is isosceles ($MC=MD$). As $M$ lies on segment $AC$, its base angle is $\angle MCD=\angle ACD=34^\circ$, so $\angle CMD=180^\circ-2\cdot34^\circ=112^\circ$. The reflection swaps $C$ and $D$ and fixes the line $BM$, so this line is the axis of symmetry of triangle $MCD$: one of the two rays from $M$ along the line $BM$ bisects $\angle CMD$ and makes an angle of $56^\circ$ with $MC$. The ray $MB$ makes the angle $2\alpha>90^\circ$ with $MC$, so it is the other ray, the extension of $BM$ beyond $M$. That ray makes the angle $180^\circ-2\alpha$ with $MC$. Hence $180^\circ-2\alpha=56^\circ$, so $\alpha=\boxed{62^\circ}$.

Show solution 2 – Isosceles triangles and inscribed angles

(a) Put $Q=(0,0)$, $P=(0,6)$, $R=(8,0)$. The midpoint of $PR$ is $W=(4,3)$, so $QW=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=\boxed{5}$. (This is the diagram's rectangle $PQRS$ in coordinates: $S=(8,6)$ and $W$ is the common midpoint of both diagonals.)

(b) The midpoint $M$ of the hypotenuse of a right triangle is equally far from the three vertices (as in (a)): $MA=MB=MC$. The reflection in line $BM$ fixes $M$ and $B$, so $MD=MC$ and $BD=BC$.

(i) Let $y=\angle ACD$. Triangle $MAD$ is isosceles ($MA=MD$), so $\angle MDA=\angle MAD=56^\circ$. Triangle $MDC$ is isosceles ($MD=MC$), so $\angle MDC=\angle MCD=y$. Since $M$ lies inside the segment $AC$, the ray $DM$ runs inside the angle $ADC$, so $\angle ADC=56^\circ+y$. The angles of triangle $ADC$ give $56^\circ+(56^\circ+y)+y=180^\circ$, so $y=\boxed{34^\circ}$.

(ii) Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$; then $\angle BCA=90^\circ-\alpha$. As $AB<BC$, the angle opposite $AB$ is smaller than the angle opposite $BC$: $90^\circ-\alpha<\alpha$, so $\alpha>45^\circ$. In the isosceles triangle $MBC$, $\angle MBC=\angle MCB=90^\circ-\alpha$. The line $BM$ is the mirror, so it bisects the angle $CBD$; the half-angle is $\angle CBM=90^\circ-\alpha$ (the bisecting ray is $BM$, because the opposite ray makes an obtuse angle with $BC$, and half of an angle is never obtuse). Thus $\angle CBD=180^\circ-2\alpha$.

The four points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ lie on the circle with centre $M$. The inscribed angles $\angle CAD=56^\circ$ and $\angle CBD$ stand on the same chord $CD$, so they are equal if $A$ and $B$ are on the same side of $CD$, and they add up to $180^\circ$ otherwise. In the second case $180^\circ-2\alpha=124^\circ$ would give $\alpha=28^\circ<45^\circ$, which is impossible. So $180^\circ-2\alpha=56^\circ$ and $\alpha=\boxed{62^\circ}$.

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · Regular Polygons

Aadi draws a regular hexagon and two squares inside the hexagon, as shown. What is the ratio of the area of the striped triangle to the area of the shaded triangle?

(A) $1:4$ (B) $\sqrt3:6$ (C) $1:3$ (D) $2:5$ (E) $1:2$

Hint 1: understand the problem
Find the side shared by the striped and shaded triangles. Mark the square corners near the triangles and the hexagon angle where the squares meet.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the regular hexagon’s interior angle and the two right angles at $U$ to find the angle between $UP$ and $UR$. Compare the lengths of those sides using the squares. (LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Classify triangle $PUR$ and use its angles together with the square corners to find the base angles of $PQR$. Drop altitudes to the shared base $PR$ and use the resulting right triangles to set up the area comparison.
Common trap
Keep the order of the requested ratio as striped area to shaded area.
Show the answer

(C) $1:3$

Show solution 1 – Angle chase

Take the side of the hexagon as $1$ and use the labels of the figure: the first square stands on the bottom side $OU$ and has top right corner $R$ (above $U$); the second square stands on the side $UV$ and $P$ is its corner next to $U$; its side from $P$ meets the top side of the first square at $Q$. The shaded triangle is $PUR$ and the striped triangle is $PQR$.

The angle of the hexagon at $U$ is $120^\circ$ and $\angle OUR=90^\circ$, so $\angle RUV=30^\circ$. As $\angle VUP=90^\circ$, we get $\angle RUP=90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ$. Also $UR=UP=1$ (sides of the two squares). So triangle $PUR$ is isosceles with a $60^\circ$ angle between the equal sides: it is equilateral with side $1$, and its area is $\frac{\sqrt3}4$.

In the striped triangle: $\angle PRQ=90^\circ-\angle PRU=90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ$, and $\angle RPQ=90^\circ-\angle UPR=90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ$ (the angle of the second square at $P$ is $90^\circ$). So triangle $PQR$ is isosceles with $QP=QR$ and base $PR=1$. Its height from $Q$ falls on the midpoint of $PR$ and equals $\frac12\tan30^\circ=\frac1{2\sqrt3}$. Hence its area is $\frac12\cdot1\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{12}$.

The ratio of the areas is $\frac{\sqrt3}{12}:\frac{\sqrt3}{4}=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 1:3}$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Take the side of the hexagon as $1$, with bottom side from $O=(0,0)$ to $U=(1,0)$. The first square is $O$, $U$, $R=(1,1)$, $(0,1)$. The next vertex of the hexagon is $V=\left(\frac32,\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)$, so $\overrightarrow{UV}=\left(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)$. Turning it through $90^\circ$ towards the inside of the hexagon gives $\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}2,\frac12\right)$, so the second square has the corner \[P=U+\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\tfrac12\right)=\left(1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\ \tfrac12\right),\] and its side from $P$ goes in the direction $\left(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)$.

Shaded triangle $PUR$: its side $UR$ (length $1$) lies on the line $x=1$, and $P$ is at distance $\frac{\sqrt3}2$ from that line. Its area is $\frac12\cdot1\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2=\frac{\sqrt3}4$.

Striped triangle $PQR$: the side of the second square from $P$ reaches the line $y=1$ after rising $\frac12$, that is after moving $\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ to the right. So $Q=\left(1-\frac{\sqrt3}2+\frac{\sqrt3}6,\,1\right)=\left(1-\frac{\sqrt3}3,\,1\right)$ and $QR=\frac{\sqrt3}3$. The height from $P$ to the line $y=1$ is $\frac12$, so the area is $\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}3\cdot\frac12=\frac{\sqrt3}{12}$.

The ratio is $\frac{\sqrt3}{12}:\frac{\sqrt3}4=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 1:3}$.

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Difference of Squares and Related Forms

What is the sum of all prime factors of $2^{49}-2$ that can be written in the form $2^n+1$ for some integer $n$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pull out any factor that is visible in both terms. Separately, note how you could test whether a prime is one more than a power of two.
Hint 2: key idea
After removing the common factor, repeatedly use the difference-of-squares identity while the exponent in the remaining difference is even. Look for a sum of cubes among the factors you obtain. (LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition)
Hint 3: approach
Factor the resulting integers and verify any remaining candidate primes by trial division. Subtract one from each distinct prime factor and keep track of which results are powers of two.
Common trap
Allow $n=0$ when checking the required form.
Show the answer

$284$

Show solution 1 – Factor completely

Use $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ again and again: \[2^{49}-2=2\left(2^{48}-1\right)=2\left(2^{3}-1\right)\left(2^{3}+1\right)\left(2^{6}+1\right)\left(2^{12}+1\right)\left(2^{24}+1\right)=2\cdot7\cdot9\cdot65\cdot4097\cdot\left(2^{24}+1\right).\] Here $9=3^2$, $65=5\cdot13$, $4097=17\cdot241$, and with $a^3+1=(a+1)(a^2-a+1)$ for $a=2^8$: \[2^{24}+1=\left(2^8+1\right)\left(2^{16}-2^8+1\right)=257\cdot65281=257\cdot97\cdot673.\] (Each of $13$, $17$, $97$, $241$, $257$, $673$ is prime: none has a prime factor up to its square root.) Step 1: $n$ is a power of $2$. If $n$ had an odd factor $m>1$, say $n=mk$, then $2^k+1$ would divide $\left(2^k\right)^m+1=2^n+1$ (for odd $m$, $x+1$ divides $x^m+1$), and $1<2^k+1<2^n+1$, so $2^n+1$ would not be prime.

Show the answer

proof ($r+s=-\frac32$ always)

Show solution 1 – Sum of the roots

(a) Here $a$, $b$ are real and the roots are real. A quadratic $x^2+2px+q=0$ with real $p,q$ has two distinct real roots exactly when $p^2-q>0$ (its discriminant is $4(p^2-q)$). So \[a^2-3b>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad b^2-3a>0.\] Adding, $a^2-3a+b^2-3b>0$. Completing the squares, \[\left(a-\tfrac32\right)^2+\left(b-\tfrac32\right)^2=a^2-3a+b^2-3b+\tfrac92>\tfrac92 .\]

(b) Let $x_0$ be the common root. Subtracting the two equations at $x=x_0$ gives \[2(a-b)x_0+3(b-a)=0,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad (a-b)(2x_0-3)=0.\] If $a=b$, the two equations are the same equation, so they have both roots in common, not exactly one. Hence $a\ne b$ and $x_0=\frac32$. Putting $x_0=\frac32$ into the first equation: $\frac94+3a+3b=0$, so $a+b=-\frac34$.

By Vieta's formulas (sum of the roots), $x_0+r=-2a$ and $x_0+s=-2b$. Adding, \[r+s=-2(a+b)-2x_0=\tfrac32-3=-\tfrac32,\] which does not depend on $a$ and $b$.

(Such $a,b$ exist, for example $a=0$, $b=-\frac34$: the equations $x^2-\frac94=0$ and $x^2-\frac32x=0$ have roots $\pm\frac32$ and $0,\frac32$.)

Show solution 2 – Product of the roots

(a) Here $a$, $b$ are real and each equation has two distinct real roots, so the discriminants are positive: $4a^2-12b>0$ and $4b^2-12a>0$, that is $a^2-3b>0$ and $b^2-3a>0$. Expanding, \[\left(a-\tfrac32\right)^2+\left(b-\tfrac32\right)^2-\tfrac92=\left(a^2-3b\right)+\left(b^2-3a\right)>0.\]

(b) Let $x_0$ be the common root. Then $x_0^2+2ax_0+3b=0=x_0^2+2bx_0+3a$, so $2(a-b)x_0=3(a-b)$. If $a=b$ the two equations coincide and share both roots, which is excluded; so $a\ne b$ and $x_0=\frac32$.

By Vieta's formulas (product of the roots), $x_0r=3b$ and $x_0s=3a$, so $r=2b$ and $s=2a$, and $r+s=2(a+b)$. Finally, $x_0=\frac32$ satisfies the second equation: $\frac94+3b+3a=0$, so $a+b=-\frac34$ and \[r+s=2\cdot\left(-\tfrac34\right)=-\tfrac32,\] a constant.

Pick a prime and record its exponent in $30$, $a$ and $b$. What exponent would appear on the product side of the given equation?

Hint 3: approach

Show solution 1 – Prime by prime

Fix a prime $p$ and let $x$, $y$, $z$ be the exponents of $p$ in $30$, $a$, $b$. The exponent of $p$ in the gcd is the smallest of $x,y,z$ and in the lcm it is the largest.   • the $24$ pairs with $a=b$ (then $\gcd(a,b)=a>1$);

Show solution 2 – Peel off one number at a time

For two numbers, $\gcd(m,n)\cdot\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)=mn$. Let $L=\mathrm{lcm}(30,a)=\frac{30a}{\gcd(30,a)}$. The only numbers in the list with a common prime factor are $49,77,91$ (all multiples of $7$), $11$ and $77$, and $13$ and $91$: $5$ unordered pairs, $10$ ordered. The answer is $576-24-10=\boxed{542}$.

  • (a) You have three red and four blue cubes. The cubes of the same colour are identical.

    This part of the question is about the number of ways to distribute all seven cubes among some boxes, following these rules:

  • (b) The factorial of a positive integer $n$ is defined as $n!=1\times2\times3\times\dots\times n$. For example, $3!=1\times2\times3=6$.

    Reorderings of $a$, $b$, $c$ count as different solutions. For example, $120\times54\times560$ is not allowed because all three numbers share a common factor of $2$, while $640\times162\times35$ is allowed and counts as a different solution from $162\times640\times35$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Label the boxes and try recording a placement by writing the number of cubes of each colour in each box. Look at how the record changes when you swap two boxes.
Hint 2: key idea
Count the placements of each colour independently by distributing identical objects among labelled boxes. With three boxes, use stars and bars and remove the placements in which every box gets at least one object of that colour. (LPS VI, Chapter 34: Distributions and Stars-and-Bars)
Hint 3: approach
With two boxes, choose each colour's count in the first box and let the second take what remains. Common trap

Show the answer

(a)(i) $20$, (ii) $108$; (b) $5184$

Show solution 1 – Complements, colour by colour and prime by prime

We use the stars-and-bars count: a whole number $e$ can be written as an ordered sum of three non-negative whole numbers in $\binom{e+2}2$ ways, and as an ordered sum of three positive whole numbers in $\binom{e-1}2$ ways (this is $0$ when $e<3$).

(a)(i) The red cubes and the blue cubes can be distributed independently. The first box gets $0$, $1$, $2$ or $3$ red cubes ($4$ ways) and $0$ to $4$ blue cubes ($5$ ways); the second box gets the rest. So there are $4\cdot5=\boxed{20}$ ways.

(ii) Red cubes into three boxes: $\binom52=10$ ways, of which $\binom22=1$ puts red in all three boxes (one cube in each). So $9$ ways leave some box without red. Blue: $\binom62=15$ ways, of which $\binom32=3$ put blue in all three boxes; $12$ ways remain. The colours are independent, so there are $9\cdot12=\boxed{108}$ ways.

(b) $10!=2^8\cdot3^4\cdot5^2\cdot7$. Choosing $a,b,c$ with $abc=10!$ is the same as splitting, for each prime, its exponent into three parts (the exponents of that prime in $a$, $b$, $c$). The three numbers have no common factor greater than $1$ exactly when no prime divides all three, i.e. for each prime the three parts are not all positive. For a prime with exponent $e$ this leaves $\binom{e+2}2-\binom{e-1}2$ splits: \[e=8:\ 45-21=24,\qquad e=4:\ 15-3=12,\qquad e=2:\ 6-0=6,\qquad e=1:\ 3-0=3.\] The primes are independent, so the number of ways is $24\cdot12\cdot6\cdot3=\boxed{5184}$.

Show solution 2 – Inclusion-exclusion

(a)(i) List by the number of red cubes in the first box ($0,1,2,3$); for each, the number of blue cubes in the first box is $0,1,2,3$ or $4$; the second box takes what is left. That is $4\times5=\boxed{20}$ ways.

(ii) “Red does not appear in all three boxes” means some box has no red cube. Let $E_k$ be the set of ways (for the red cubes alone) in which box $k$ has no red cube. If box $1$ is red-free, the $3$ red cubes go into two boxes: $4$ ways; if boxes $1$ and $2$ are both red-free, there is $1$ way; all three boxes cannot be red-free. By inclusion-exclusion, \[|E_1\cup E_2\cup E_3|=3\cdot4-3\cdot1+0=9.\] For blue in the same way: $3\cdot5-3\cdot1+0=12$. The colours are independent: $9\cdot12=\boxed{108}$ ways.

(b) Write $10!=2^8\cdot3^4\cdot5^2\cdot7$. A factorisation $abc$ is the same as choosing, for each prime $p$ with exponent $e$, the exponents $(x,y,z)$ of $p$ in $a,b,c$ with $x+y+z=e$. The condition “no common factor greater than $1$” says: for every prime, at least one of $x,y,z$ is $0$.

For one prime with exponent $e\ge1$, count the triples with at least one zero by inclusion-exclusion: $x=0$ leaves $y+z=e$, which has $e+1$ solutions; two zeros leave $1$ solution; three zeros are impossible. So the count is $3(e+1)-3\cdot1=3e$.

This gives $24$, $12$, $6$, $3$ for the exponents $8,4,2,1$, and the total is $24\cdot12\cdot6\cdot3=\boxed{5184}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a dot for each player and join two dots when those players meet. What would a group in which nobody has played anyone else in the group look like?
Hint 2: key idea

Hint 3: approach
Examine equality in the game count and ask when two opponents of the same member could replace that member in $S$. If those opponents all met one another, check whether their group could be paired entirely within itself in each round. For the opposite bound, look for a schedule that splits the players into small groups in which everyone has met everyone else, so that a set of strangers can contain at most one player per group.
Common trap
A proposed schedule must split all the players into disjoint pairs in each individual round.
Show the answer

$k=5$

Show solution 1 – A largest set of strangers

Call two players strangers if they have not played each other. After $4$ rounds every player has played exactly $4$ different opponents.

$k\le5$. Split the players into five groups of four. In rounds $1$, $2$, $3$ pair the players inside each group so that each two players of a group meet once (in a group $\{w,x,y,z\}$: $wx,yz$; then $wy,xz$; then $wz,xy$). In round $4$ pair players from different groups: number the groups $1,\dots,5$ and their members $1,\dots,4$, and pair member $1$ of group $j$ with member $2$ of group $j+1$, and member $3$ of group $j$ with member $4$ of group $j+1$ (group $6$ means group $1$). Every player gets exactly one partner, and no pair meets twice. Now any two players of the same group have played, so a set of strangers contains at most one player from each group: at most $5$ players. So $k\le5$.

$k\ge5$. Take a set $S$ of strangers that is as large as possible, $|S|=s$. Every player outside $S$ has played someone in $S$ (otherwise we could add him to $S$). Count the games between a player in $S$ and a player outside: there are at most $4s$ of them (each member of $S$ plays $4$ games) and at least $20-s$ of them. So $20-s\le4s$ and $s\ge4$.

Suppose $s=4$. Then $20-s=16=4s$, so both counts are exact: each player outside $S$ has played exactly one member of $S$. Take $v\in S$ and its four opponents $u_1,u_2,u_3,u_4$ (all outside $S$, since $S$ consists of strangers). Each $u_i$ has played no member of $S$ other than $v$. If two of them, $u_i$ and $u_j$, were strangers, then removing $v$ from $S$ and adding $u_i$, $u_j$ would give a set of $5$ strangers, contradicting the choice of $S$. So $u_1,\dots,u_4$ have all played each other. Then each $u_i$ has played $v$ and the three other $u$'s, which are all its $4$ games, and $v$ has played only the $u$'s. So these five players have played only among themselves. But in round $1$ each of them was paired with an opponent from the same five players, which splits five players into pairs: impossible, since $5$ is odd.

Hence $s\ge5$: one can always choose $5$ strangers. The answer is $\boxed{k=5}$.

Show solution 2 – Greedy choice with a good start

Call two players strangers if they have not played each other. After $4$ rounds every player has exactly $4$ opponents.

$k\le5$. Divide the players into five groups of four. In rounds $1$–$3$ every group plays its own round robin (in a group $\{w,x,y,z\}$: $wx,yz$; $wy,xz$; $wz,xy$). In round $4$, with groups numbered $1,\dots,5$ cyclically and members numbered $1,\dots,4$, pair member $1$ of group $j$ with member $2$ of group $j+1$, and member $3$ of group $j$ with member $4$ of group $j+1$; this is a valid round with only new pairs. Two players of the same group are never strangers, so at most one player per group can be chosen: at most $5$.

$k\ge5$. Step 1: some player $v$ has two opponents $u$, $w$ who are strangers. If not, take any player $v$: its four opponents have all played each other, so each of them has played $v$ and three others among them, which is all of its $4$ games. Then $v$ and its opponents form a group of five who have played only among themselves; in round $1$ these five players would be split into pairs among themselves, impossible as $5$ is odd.

Step 2. Choose $u$ and $w$, and cross out $u$, $w$ and all their opponents. Since $v$ is an opponent of both, at most $2+4+4-1=9$ players are crossed out, so at least $11$ players remain, none of whom has played $u$ or $w$.

Step 3. Now repeat: choose any player who is not crossed out, then cross out him and his opponents (at most $5$ players each time). After two such choices at most $9+10=19$ players are crossed out, so a third choice is still possible.

Every chosen player was not crossed out when chosen, so he has not played any earlier chosen player. Thus the chosen players $u,w$ and the three others are $5$ strangers. Together with the example, the answer is $\boxed{k=5}$.

If such an $f$ exists, then as $n$ runs over $\mathbb{N}$, $f(n)$ runs over all of $\mathbb{N}$, so $2f(n)$ runs over all even numbers; the equation says that $f$ maps the even numbers exactly onto the numbers $2n+3$, the odd numbers from $3$ on. So $f$ must map the odd numbers onto the remaining numbers \[V=\{0,1,2,4,6,8,\dots\}\quad(\text{$0$, $1$ and the even numbers}).\] We build $f$ in this way. Write $T=\{3,5,7,\dots\}$ for the odd numbers from $3$ on, and pair the odd numbers with $V$ by \[h(1)=0,\qquad h(3)=1,\qquad h(2j+1)=2j-2\ \ (j\ge2),\] that is $1\mapsto0$, $3\mapsto1$, $5\mapsto2$, $7\mapsto4$, $9\mapsto6$, …; this is a one-to-one correspondence between the odd numbers and $V$.

We define two functions $f$ and $g$ on $\mathbb{N}$ ($g$ will be the inverse of $f$) by four rules:

  • (R1) for odd $n$: $f(n)=h(n)$;

  • (R2) for even $n$: $f(n)=2g\!\left(\tfrac n2\right)+3$;

  • (R3) for $y\in V$: $g(y)$ is the odd number $m$ with $h(m)=y$;

  • (R4) for $y\in T$: $g(y)=2f\!\left(\tfrac{y-3}2\right)$.

These rules really define all values. Go through $N=0,1,2,\dots$ in order and compute $f(N)$ and $g(N)$: rules (R1) and (R3) are direct; (R2) needs $g$ at $\frac N2$, which is smaller than $N$ when $N\ge2$, and for $N=0$ it needs $g(0)=1$, given by (R3); (R4) needs $f$ at $\frac{N-3}2<N$. So every value only uses values computed earlier. (For example $f(0)=2g(0)+3=5$, $f(2)=2g(1)+3=9$, $g(5)=2f(1)=0$, $f(10)=2g(5)+3=3$.)

Claim: for every $N\in\mathbb{N}$, $g(f(N))=N$ and $f(g(N))=N$. We prove both statements together by strong induction on $N$.

  • $g(f(N))=N$. If $N$ is odd, $f(N)=h(N)\in V$ and $g(h(N))=N$ by (R3). If $N=0$: $f(0)=5\in T$ and $g(5)=2f(1)=2h(1)=0$. If $N\ge2$ is even, $f(N)=2g(\frac N2)+3\in T$, so by (R4) $g(f(N))=2f\!\left(g(\tfrac N2)\right)=2\cdot\tfrac N2=N$, using the induction hypothesis for $\frac N2<N$.

  • $f(g(N))=N$. If $N\in V$, $g(N)$ is the odd $m$ with $h(m)=N$, and $f(m)=h(m)=N$. If $N\in T$, $g(N)=2f(\frac{N-3}2)$ is even, so by (R2) $f(g(N))=2g\!\left(f(\tfrac{N-3}2)\right)+3=2\cdot\tfrac{N-3}2+3=N$, using the induction hypothesis for $\frac{N-3}2<N$.

So $f$ and $g$ undo each other, and $f$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{N}$. Finally, $2f(n)$ is even, so by (R2) and the claim, \[f(2f(n))=2g(f(n))+3=2n+3\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.\]

Remark. If $\mathbb{N}$ is read as $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, the answer is still yes: the same construction works with $T=\{5,7,9,\dots\}$ (the values $2n+3$, $n\ge1$), $V=\{1,2,3,4,6,8,\dots\}$ and $h(1)=1$, $h(3)=2$, $h(5)=3$, $h(2j+1)=2j-2$ for $j\ge3$; rule (R2) is then used only for $n\ge2$, where $\frac n2\ge1$.

Show solution 2 – A shift, and a formula by remainders modulo 4

(a) Yes. Let $f(n)=n+1$. It is a bijection of $\mathbb{Z}$ (its inverse is $n\mapsto n-1$), and \[f(2f(n))=f(2n+2)=2n+3 .\]

(b) Yes. Define $f$ on $\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ according to the remainder of $n$ modulo $4$: \[ f(n)=\begin{cases} 1, & n=1,\\ n-3, & n\ \text{odd},\ n\ge3,\\ n+9, & n\equiv0\pmod 4,\\ 5, & n=2,\\ 4f(t)+3, & n=4t+6\ \ (t\ge0). \end{cases} \] Every $n$ falls under exactly one line (the numbers leaving remainder $2$ on division by $4$ are $2$ and $6,10,14,\dots$). The last line uses the value at $t<n$, so going through $n=0,1,2,\dots$ in order defines every value, and all values are in $\mathbb{N}$. The first values are \[f(0),f(1),\dots,f(11)=9,\ 1,\ 5,\ 0,\ 13,\ 2,\ 39,\ 4,\ 17,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8 .\]

$f$ is a bijection. We show by strong induction on $y$ that every $y\in\mathbb{N}$ is $f(n)$ for exactly one $n$. Look at the values of each line:

  • the odd $n\ge3$ give $0,2,4,\dots$, every even number once;

  • $n=1$ and $n=2$ give $1$ and $5$, and the multiples of $4$ give $9,13,17,\dots$; together, every number leaving remainder $1$ on division by $4$ exactly once;

  • the last line gives numbers leaving remainder $3$.

So an even $y$, or a $y$ leaving remainder $1$, has exactly one $n$ with $f(n)=y$. If $y=4z+3$, then $f(n)=y$ happens only on the last line, that is for $n=4t+6$ with $4f(t)+3=4z+3$, i.e. $f(t)=z$. Since $z<y$, by the induction hypothesis there is exactly one such $t$, hence exactly one such $n$.

The equation. We check $f(2f(n))=2n+3$ by strong induction on $n$.

  • $n$ odd, $n\ge3$: $2f(n)=2n-6$ is a multiple of $4$ (as $n-3$ is even), so $f(2f(n))=2n-6+9=2n+3$.

  • $n=1$: $f(2f(1))=f(2)=5$.  $n=2$: $f(2f(2))=f(10)=f(4\cdot1+6)=4f(1)+3=7$.

  • $n=4t$: $2f(n)=8t+18=4(2t+3)+6$, so $f(2f(n))=4f(2t+3)+3=4\cdot2t+3=2n+3$, since $2t+3$ is odd and at least $3$. (This includes $n=0$.)

  • $n=4t+6$: $2f(n)=8f(t)+6=4\cdot2f(t)+6$, so \[f(2f(n))=4f(2f(t))+3=4(2t+3)+3=8t+15=2n+3,\] using the induction hypothesis for $t<n$.

So $f$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{N}$ with $f(2f(n))=2n+3$ for all $n$, and the answer to (b) is yes.

Remark. If $\mathbb{N}$ is read as $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, the answer is still yes, with the same idea: $f(1)=1$, $f(3)=3$, $f(n)=n-3$ for odd $n\ge5$, $f(n)=n+9$ for $n\equiv0\pmod4$, $f(2)=5$, $f(6)=9$ and $f(4t+6)=4f(t)+3$ for $t\ge1$; the checks are the same, with the new cases $f(2f(3))=f(6)=9$ and $f(2f(6))=f(18)=4f(3)+3=15$.
