Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $ABCD$ be a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle of radius $1$. If lines $AD$ and $BC$ are perpendicular, determine all possible values of $AB^2+CD^2$.

Show the answer

$4$ (the only possible value)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Extended law of sines)

Let $E$ be the intersection of the perpendicular lines $AD$ and $BC$. Note $E\ne B$ (otherwise $B$ would lie on line $AD$, which meets the circle only at $A$ and $D$) and similarly $E\ne D$.

By the extended law of sines in the circle of radius $1$, \[AB=2\sin\angle ADB,\qquad CD=2\sin\angle DBC .\] Now $\angle ADB$ is either $\angle EDB$ or its supplement (depending on whether $A$ lies on ray $DE$ or on the opposite ray), so $\sin\angle ADB=\sin\angle EDB$; likewise $\sin\angle DBC=\sin\angle DBE$. In triangle $EBD$ the angle at $E$ is $90^\circ$, so $\angle EDB+\angle DBE=90^\circ$ and $\sin\angle DBE=\cos\angle EDB$. Therefore \[AB^2+CD^2=4\bigl(\sin^2\angle EDB+\cos^2\angle EDB\bigr)=4 .\] Such quadrilaterals exist (for example the one in the figure of Solution 2, or any convex cyclic quadrilateral whose vertices are at the polar angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ with $\alpha+\delta-\beta-\gamma\equiv180^\circ\pmod{360^\circ}$). Hence the only possible value is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the two perpendicular lines)

Let $E=AD\cap BC$ and take coordinates with $E$ at the origin, line $AD$ as the $x$-axis and line $BC$ as the $y$-axis. Let the centre be $O=(p,q)$; the circle is $(x-p)^2+(y-q)^2=1$.

The $x$-coordinates $x_A,x_D$ of $A$ and $D$ are the roots of $(x-p)^2+q^2=1$, i.e. of $x^2-2px+(p^2+q^2-1)=0$, so by Vieta \[x_A^2+x_D^2=(x_A+x_D)^2-2x_Ax_D=4p^2-2(p^2+q^2-1)=2p^2-2q^2+2 .\] In the same way the $y$-coordinates of $B$ and $C$ satisfy $y_B^2+y_C^2=2q^2-2p^2+2$. Since $A=(x_A,0)$, $B=(0,y_B)$, $C=(0,y_C)$, $D=(x_D,0)$, Pythagoras gives \[AB^2+CD^2=(x_A^2+y_B^2)+(y_C^2+x_D^2)=(2p^2-2q^2+2)+(2q^2-2p^2+2)=\boxed{4}.\] (The figure also shows the feet $M_1,M_2$ of the perpendiculars from $O$: $OM_1EM_2$ is a rectangle, which is the geometric meaning of the computation.)

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n\ge 3$ be a positive integer. Let $S=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}$ be a set of positive real numbers $x_1<x_2<\cdots<x_n$ satisfying the following condition: \[\text{for all distinct elements } a \text{ and } b \text{ of } S,\ |a^2-b^2| \text{ is also an element of } S.\] Prove that $x_2-x_1<\frac{1}{2}$.

Show the solution

Solution (Largest element and the two smallest)

Write $a=x_1$, $b=x_2$ and $M=x_n$ (the largest element). For distinct $u>v$ in $S$ the number $u^2-v^2$ is a positive element of $S$, hence at most $M$.

Step 1: every element of $S$ is less than $a+1$. Since $M^2-a^2\in S$, we have $M^2-a^2\le M$, so $M^2-M-a^2\le0$ and \[M\le\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4a^2}}2<\frac{1+(1+2a)}2=a+1 .\] Suppose now, for contradiction, that $b-a\ge\frac12$.

Step 2. Then $b^2-a^2=(b-a)(b+a)\ge\frac{a+b}2>a$; as $b^2-a^2\in S$ and it exceeds the smallest element, $b^2-a^2\ge b$. Also $b\ge a+\frac12>\frac12$.

Step 3: $n=3$. For each index $i$ with $2\le i\le n-1$ we have $x_i\ge b$, so $x_i^2-a^2\ge b^2-a^2\ge b$, and therefore \[M^2-x_i^2=(M^2-a^2)-(x_i^2-a^2)\ \le\ M-b\ <\ (a+1)-\bigl(a+\tfrac12\bigr)=\tfrac12<b .\] But $M^2-x_i^2$ is an element of $S$, and the only element of $S$ below $b$ is $a$; so $M^2-x_i^2=a$ for every $2\le i\le n-1$. If $n\ge4$, this gives $M^2-x_2^2=M^2-x_3^2$, i.e. $x_2=x_3$, which is false. Hence $n=3$ and $S=\{a,b,M\}$ with $M^2-b^2=a$.

Step 4: the case $S=\{a,b,M\}$. By Step 2, $M^2-a^2=(M^2-b^2)+(b^2-a^2)\ge a+b>b$, so $M^2-a^2=M$. Then $b^2-a^2=(M^2-a^2)-(M^2-b^2)=M-a$, which is an element of $S$ smaller than $M$ and at least $b$; so $b^2-a^2=b$ and $M=a+b$. Substituting into $M^2-a^2=M$: \[(a+b)^2-a^2=a+b\iff 2ab+b^2=a+b\iff 2ab+(a^2+b)=a+b\iff a(a+2b-1)=0,\] using $b^2=a^2+b$. As $a>0$, $a+2b=1$, so $b<\frac12$, contradicting Step 2.

Hence $b-a<\frac12$, that is, $x_2-x_1<\frac12$.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

Let $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ be positive real numbers such that $a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=4$. Prove that \[\frac{a^2}{2+b^3}+\frac{b^2}{2+c^3}+\frac{c^2}{2+d^3}+\frac{d^2}{2+a^3}\ge\frac{4}{3}.\]

Show the solution

Solution (AM-GM in the denominator)

For each term, \[\frac{a^2}{2+b^3}=\frac{a^2}2-\frac{a^2b^3}{2(2+b^3)} .\] By AM-GM, $2+b^3=1+1+b^3\ge 3b$, so $\frac{a^2b^3}{2+b^3}\le\frac{a^2b^3}{3b}=\frac{a^2b^2}3$ and \[\frac{a^2}{2+b^3}\ \ge\ \frac{a^2}2-\frac{a^2b^2}6 .\] Adding the four cyclic inequalities and using $a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=4$: \[\sum\frac{a^2}{2+b^3}\ \ge\ 2-\frac{a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2d^2+d^2a^2}6=2-\frac{(a^2+c^2)(b^2+d^2)}6 .\] By AM-GM, $(a^2+c^2)(b^2+d^2)\le\bigl(\frac{a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2}2\bigr)^2=4$, so the sum is at least $2-\frac46=\frac43$. Equality holds for $a=b=c=d=1$.

Remark. Divided by $a^2$, the per-term bound reads $\frac1{2+t^3}\ge\frac{3-t^2}6$ with $t=b$: its right side is the tangent line of $\frac1{2+t^3}$, viewed as a function of $t^2$, at $t^2=1$, and the bound is equivalent to $t^3-3t+2=(t-1)^2(t+2)\ge0$, the factored form of the AM-GM step $1+1+t^3\ge3t$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Initially, the numbers $1,2,2^2,\dots,2^n$ are arranged around a circle in some order. Every minute, Alyssa chooses two consecutive numbers, say $a$ and $b$, on the circle, write the nonnegative difference $|a-b|$ between them, and then erase the original two numbers. She keeps doing this until there is only one number remaining on the circle. Prove that for all odd positive integers $m<2^n$, no matter what the initial configuration is, Alyssa can perform the operations so that the remaining number is $m$.

Show the solution

Solution (Induction by doubling: insert the 1 at the right moment)

Language. At every moment each number on the circle is the result of combining the original numbers of an arc; call such an arc with its current value a block. A procedure is a sequence of operations, each combining two adjacent blocks with values $x,y$ into one block of value $|x-y|$.

A fact about values. If the original numbers are distinct powers of $2$, every block value is of the form $\sum\pm2^{j}$ over the distinct powers in the block (because $|x-y|=\pm(x-y)$). Such a signed sum of distinct powers of $2$ is never $0$: if $2^{s}$ is the smallest power occurring, the sum is $\equiv2^{s}\pmod{2^{s+1}}$. In particular two blocks that are combined always have different values.

We prove by induction on $n\ge1$:

$S(n)$: for every circular arrangement of $1,2,\dots,2^n$ and every odd $m$ with $1\le m<2^n$ there is a procedure ending with $m$.

Base $n=1$. The circle is $1,2$ and $|1-2|=1$ is the only odd number below $2$.

Step. Let $n\ge2$ and assume $S(n-1)$. Fix an arrangement of $1,2,\dots,2^n$ and an odd $m<2^n$. Write $m=2k+\varepsilon$ with $k$ odd and $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$, namely \[k=\tfrac{m+1}2,\ \varepsilon=-1\ \text{ if } m\equiv1\!\!\pmod4;\qquad k=\tfrac{m-1}2,\ \varepsilon=1\ \text{ if } m\equiv3\!\!\pmod4 .\] In both cases $1\le k\le 2^{n-1}-1$ (if $m\equiv1\pmod 4$ then $m\le2^n-3$).

Remove the number $1$ from the circle. The numbers $2,4,\dots,2^n$ remain in a circular order, and halving them gives an arrangement of $1,2,\dots,2^{n-1}$. By $S(n-1)$ there is a procedure $\Pi$ for this reduced circle (with the doubled numbers) ending with $2k$; all its block values are even.

In the full circle, $1$ sits between two numbers $L$ and $R$, which are neighbours in the reduced circle. Every operation of $\Pi$ joins two blocks across one gap of the reduced circle (the very last one, joining the last two blocks, across two gaps), and every gap is joined at some point. Let $\mu$ be the first operation of $\Pi$ that joins across the gap $L|R$: it combines a block $P$ ending with $L$ and a block $Q$ starting with $R$.

Perform $\Pi$ in the full circle. Operations before $\mu$ never use the gap $L|R$, so they can be carried out unchanged, and $1$ is still untouched; just before $\mu$, the circle reads $\dots,P,1,Q,\dots$. Let $p\ne q$ be the (even, nonzero) values of $P,Q$, say $p>q$ (otherwise exchange the names). Replace $\mu$ by two operations:

  • either combine $1$ with $Q$ first ($q-1\ge1$), then with $P$: value $p-(q-1)=(p-q)+1$;

  • or combine $1$ with $P$ first, then with $Q$: value $(p-1)-q=(p-q)-1$ (note $p-1>q$ since $p-q\ge2$).

So the new block has value $(p-q)+\delta$ with $\delta=+1$ or $\delta=-1$, as we choose. Then carry out the rest of $\Pi$ unchanged.

Tracking the change. In $\Pi$, each later operation combines two blocks of different even values $x\ne y$, so $|x-y|\ge2$. If one of them is replaced by $x+\delta'$ with $\delta'=\pm1$, the order of the two values is unchanged and $|(x+\delta')-y|=|x-y|+\delta''$ with $\delta''=\delta'$ if $x>y$ and $\delta''=-\delta'$ if $x<y$; the new value is still positive. Hence, step by step, the block containing $1$ always has value (old value)$\,+\,(\pm\delta)$, where the sign $\pm$ depends only on the comparisons made in $\Pi$, not on our choice of $\delta$. So the final number is $2k+\tau\delta$ for a fixed $\tau\in\{1,-1\}$. Choosing $\delta=\tau\varepsilon$ gives the final number $2k+\varepsilon=m$.

This proves $S(n)$, and by induction the statement holds for all $n$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Determine all positive integers $c$ for which the infinite sequence of positive integers $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ such that $a_{n+1}=c\cdot\varphi(a_n)$ for all positive integers $n$ is eventually periodic for all choices of $a_1$.

Note. $\varphi(n)$ is the number of integers in $\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}$ which are relatively prime to $n$. A sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ is eventually periodic if there exist positive integers $N$ and $m$ such that $a_{n+m}=a_n$ for all integers $n>N$.

Show the answer

$c\in\{1,2,3\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Monotonicity for small c, a growing valuation for c at least 4)

Reduction. A sequence of positive integers with $a_{n+1}=F(a_n)$ is eventually periodic iff it is bounded: a bounded such sequence takes finitely many values, so some value repeats and from then on the sequence repeats; an eventually periodic sequence takes finitely many values. We use: $\varphi(m)\le m$; $\varphi(m)$ is even for $m\ge3$; and $\varphi(m)=m\prod_{p\mid m}\bigl(1-\frac1p\bigr)$.

$c=1$. $a_{n+1}=\varphi(a_n)\le a_n$, so the sequence is bounded.

$c=2$. Every $a_n$ with $n\ge2$ is even, and for even $m$, $\varphi(m)\le\frac m2$. So $a_{n+1}=2\varphi(a_n)\le a_n$ for $n\ge2$.

$c=3$. For $n\ge2$, $a_n=3\varphi(a_{n-1})$ is a multiple of $3$, so $a_n\ge3$ and $\varphi(a_n)$ is even; hence $6\mid a_n$ for $n\ge3$. For $6\mid m$, $\varphi(m)\le m\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac23=\frac m3$, so $a_{n+1}=3\varphi(a_n)\le a_n$ for $n\ge3$.

So for $c\le3$ every sequence is eventually periodic.

$c\ge4$: every sequence tends to infinity. For $m=\prod p^{e_p}$ and a prime $q$, \[v_q(\varphi(m))=\max(e_q-1,0)+\sum_{p\mid m,\ p\ne q}v_q(p-1).\tag{1}\] For $n\ge2$, $c\mid a_n$, so $a_n\ge4$; hence for $n\ge3$, $a_n=c\,\varphi(a_{n-1})$ is even (as $\varphi(a_{n-1})$ is). For $n\ge3$, (1) gives \[v_2(a_{n+1})=v_2(c)+v_2(a_n)-1+\sum_{p\mid a_n,\ p\text{ odd}}v_2(p-1).\tag{2}\] We show that from some index on, $v_2(a_n)$ or $v_3(a_n)$ increases by at least $1$ at every step; then $a_n\to\infty$.

  • $4\mid c$: by (2), $v_2(a_{n+1})\ge v_2(a_n)+1$.

  • $2\,\|\,c$: then $c=2c'$ with $c'$ odd and $c'\ge 3$ (since $c\ge4$); an odd prime $p\mid c'$ divides $a_n$ and $v_2(p-1)\ge1$, so again $v_2$ increases.

  • $c$ odd with a prime factor $q\equiv1\pmod 4$: $q\mid a_n$ and $v_2(q-1)\ge2$, so the sum in (2) is $\ge2$ and $v_2$ increases.

  • $c$ odd with a prime factor $q\equiv3\pmod4$, $q\ge7$: then $\frac{q-1}2$ is odd and greater than $1$; let $r$ be a prime factor of it ($r$ odd, $r<q$). For $n\ge3$, $q\mid a_n$ gives $q-1\mid\varphi(a_n)$, so $r\mid a_{n+1}$. Thus for $n\ge4$ both $q$ and $r$ divide $a_n$, the sum in (2) is $\ge v_2(q-1)+v_2(r-1)\ge2$, and $v_2$ increases.

  • The remaining odd $c\ge4$ have no prime factor other than $3$, i.e. $c=3^e$ with $e\ge2$. For $n\ge2$, $3\mid a_n$, and (1) with $q=3$ gives $v_3(a_{n+1})=e+v_3(a_n)-1+\sum_{p\ne3}v_3(p-1)\ge v_3(a_n)+1$.

In every case $a_n\to\infty$, so the sequence is not eventually periodic (for any $a_1$).

Therefore the answer is $\boxed{c\in\{1,2,3\}}$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 16: Diophantine Equations: Linear and Structural Foundations

Charlotte plays a game on a sheet of paper. Charlotte first chooses an ordered pair of positive integers $(C,D)$. She initially writes $0$ on the paper. Then, on each subsequent move, if $N$ is the last number she wrote, then Charlotte may write one of $N+C$, $N-C$, or $D-N$ as the next number, where repetition is allowed.

Determine all possible ordered pairs $(C,D)$ such that Charlotte can write both $1$ and $-1$ after some finite number of moves.

Show the answer

$C=1$ with any $D$, or $C=2$ with $D$ odd

Show the solution

Solution (Describing all reachable numbers)

The reachable numbers. Let $R=\{kC:k\in\mathbb Z\}\cup\{D+kC:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. Starting from $0\in R$, every move keeps us in $R$: from $kC$ we can go to $(k\pm1)C$ or to $D-kC=D+(-k)C$; from $D+kC$ we can go to $D+(k\pm1)C$ or to $D-(D+kC)=(-k)C$. Conversely every element of $R$ is reachable: $kC$ by $|k|$ moves of $\pm C$, and $D+kC$ by first reaching $-kC$ and then writing $D-(-kC)$. Moreover each move can be undone ($N\to N+C$ by $N\to N-C$ and vice versa; $N\to D-N$ by itself), so after writing one element of $R$ Charlotte can still reach any other. Hence Charlotte can write both $1$ and $-1$ iff $1\in R$ and $-1\in R$.

When is that true? $1\in R$ iff $C\mid 1$ or $C\mid D-1$; and $-1\in R$ iff $C\mid1$ or $C\mid D+1$.

  • If $C=1$, both hold for every $D$.

  • If $C\ge2$, we need $C\mid D-1$ and $C\mid D+1$, hence $C\mid 2$, so $C=2$, and then $D$ must be odd; conversely $C=2$ with $D$ odd works.

So the pairs are $\boxed{(C,D)=(1,D)\ \text{for any } D\ge1,\ \text{and}\ (2,D)\ \text{with } D \text{ odd}}$.

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Determine all positive integers $n\ge 3$ for which there exists a permutation $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ of $(1,2,\dots,n)$ such that \[\gcd(a_i+a_{i+1},a_j+a_{j+1})=1\] for all $1\le i<j\le n-1$.

Show the answer

$n\in\{3,4,5,6,7\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Smallest prime factors and a prime count)

Examples. The following permutations work (the adjacent sums are listed and are pairwise coprime):

  • $n=3$: $(1,2,3)$, sums $3,5$; $n=4$: $(1,2,3,4)$, sums $3,5,7$;

  • $n=5$: $(1,3,2,5,4)$, sums $4,5,7,9$;

  • $n=6$: $(2,1,4,3,5,6)$, sums $3,5,7,8,11$;

  • $n=7$: $(1,3,2,5,4,7,6)$, sums $4,5,7,9,11,13$.

No permutation for $n\ge8$. The $n-1$ adjacent sums $a_i+a_{i+1}$ lie in $[3,\,2n-1]$ and are pairwise coprime, hence pairwise distinct and all greater than $1$. Assign to each sum its smallest prime factor; pairwise coprime numbers have no common prime factor, so these primes are distinct, and all are at most $2n-1$. Hence \[n-1\ \le\ \pi(2n-1),\] the number of primes up to $2n-1$. But for $n\ge8$ the primes up to $2n-1$ are $2$ and some of the $n-1$ odd numbers $3,5,\dots,2n-1$, among which $9$ and $15$ are composite; so $\pi(2n-1)\le 1+(n-3)=n-2$, a contradiction.

So the answer is $\boxed{n\in\{3,4,5,6,7\}}$.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle, and let $D\ne B,C$ be a point on the side $BC$. Let $\Omega_B$ be the circle passing through $B$ and $D$ and tangent to $AB$ at $B$. Let $\Omega_C$ be the circle passing through $C$ and $D$ and tangent to $AC$ at $C$. Let $\gamma$ be the circle passing through $A$ and $D$ and tangent to $BC$ at $D$. Suppose $\gamma$ intersects $\Omega_B$ and $\Omega_C$ again at points $X$ and $Y$, respectively ($X,Y\ne D$). Lines $BX$ and $CY$ intersect at $Z$.

Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $XYZ$ and $ABC$ are tangent, and the tangency point lies on both $\Omega_B$ and $\Omega_C$.

Show the solution

Solution (Directed angle chase)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, taken modulo $180^\circ$; recall the tangent-chord theorem in the form: if $t$ is the tangent at $U$ to a circle through $U,V,W$, then $\measuredangle(t,UV)=\measuredangle(WU,WV)$.

Step 0: three tangent-chord facts. For $X$ on $\Omega_B$ (tangent $BA$ at $B$) and on $\gamma$ (tangent $BC$ at $D$): \[\measuredangle(XB,XD)=\measuredangle(BA,BD),\qquad \measuredangle(XD,XA)=\measuredangle(BC,DA),\qquad \measuredangle(AD,AX)=\measuredangle(BC,DX).\tag{1}\] Similarly for $Y$ on $\Omega_C$ and $\gamma$: $\measuredangle(YC,YD)=\measuredangle(CA,CD)$ and $\measuredangle(AD,AY)=\measuredangle(BC,DY)$.

Step 1: the second common point $M$ of $\Omega_B,\Omega_C$ lies on the circle $(ABC)$. (The two circles are not tangent at $D$: their tangents at $D$ are the reflections of $BA$ and $CA$ in the perpendiculars to $BC$ through the midpoints of $BD$ and $CD$; since $BA\nparallel CA$, these reflections are not parallel, so they are different lines through $D$.) By the tangent-chord theorem in $\Omega_B$ and in $\Omega_C$, \[\measuredangle(MB,MC)=\measuredangle(MB,MD)+\measuredangle(MD,MC)=\measuredangle(BA,BD)+\measuredangle(CD,CA)=\measuredangle(BA,CA),\] so $M$ lies on $(ABC)$.

Step 2: $M$ lies on $(XYZ)$. Using the circles $\Omega_B\ni M,X,B,D$ and $\Omega_C\ni M,D,C,Y$, \[\measuredangle(MX,MY)=\measuredangle(MX,MD)+\measuredangle(MD,MY)=\measuredangle(BX,BD)+\measuredangle(CD,CY)=\measuredangle(BX,CY)=\measuredangle(ZX,ZY).\] Step 3: $A$, $Z$, $M$ are collinear. On the one hand, by $(ABC)$ and $\Omega_B$, \[\measuredangle(MA,MX)=\measuredangle(MA,MB)+\measuredangle(MB,MX)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)+\measuredangle(DB,DX)=\measuredangle(CA,DX).\] On the other hand, by Step 2, then $\Omega_C$ and $\gamma\ni A,D,X,Y$, then (1), \begin{align*}\measuredangle(MZ,MX)&=\measuredangle(YZ,YX)=\measuredangle(YC,YD)+\measuredangle(YD,YX)=\measuredangle(CA,CD)+\measuredangle(AD,AX)\\ &=\measuredangle(CA,BC)+\measuredangle(BC,DX)=\measuredangle(CA,DX).\end{align*} So the lines $MA$ and $MZ$ make the same angle with $MX$; they coincide, and $Z$ lies on line $AM$.

Step 4: the circle $(ABX)$ is tangent to $AD$. Adding the first two facts in (1), $\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(BA,BC)+\measuredangle(BC,DA) =\measuredangle(BA,DA)$. Since the directed angles of triangle $ABX$ add up to $0$, \[\measuredangle(BA,BX)=-\measuredangle(AX,AB)-\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(AB,AX)-\measuredangle(AB,AD)=\measuredangle(AD,AX).\tag{2}\] Step 5: tangency at $M$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $(ABC)$ at $M$. By the tangent-chord theorem in $(ABC)$, the circle $\Omega_B$, then (1) and (2), \begin{align*}\measuredangle(t,MX)&=\measuredangle(t,MB)+\measuredangle(MB,MX)=\measuredangle(AM,AB)+\measuredangle(DB,DX)\\ &=\measuredangle(AM,AB)+\measuredangle(AD,AX)=\measuredangle(AM,AB)+\measuredangle(BA,BX)=\measuredangle(AM,BX).\end{align*} As $Z$ lies on $AM$ (Step 3) and on $BX$, this equals $\measuredangle(ZM,ZX)$. By the converse of the tangent-chord theorem, $t$ is also tangent at $M$ to the circle through $M,X,Z$, which is $(XYZ)$ by Step 2. Hence $(XYZ)$ and $(ABC)$ are tangent at $M$, and $M$ lies on both $\Omega_B$ and $\Omega_C$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 9

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Let $\mathbb{R}$ be the set of real numbers. Determine all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that, for all real numbers $x$ and $y$, \[f(f(x)-y)+2yf(x)=f(x^2)+f(y).\]

Show the answer

$f(x)=0$ for all $x$, and $f(x)=x^2$ for all $x$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Periodicity of f(x) minus x squared)

Both $f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=x^2$ work: for the second, $(x^2-y)^2+2yx^2=x^4+y^2$. Let $f$ be any solution and write $P(x,y)$ for the equation.

Basic facts. $P(x,0)$: $f(f(x))=f(x^2)+f(0)$. $P(x,f(x))$: $f(0)+2f(x)^2=f(x^2)+f(f(x))=2f(x^2)+f(0)$, so \[f(x^2)=f(x)^2\quad\text{for all }x .\] With $x=0$ and $x=1$: $f(0),f(1)\in\{0,1\}$. If $f(0)=1$, then $P(0,y)$ reads $f(1-y)+2y=1+f(y)$, and $y=0$ gives $f(1)=2$, impossible. So $f(0)=0$. Now $P(0,y)$ gives $f(-y)=f(y)$, and $f(f(x))=f(x^2)=f(x)^2$. Hence $P(x,y)$ becomes, for every value $t=f(x)$ of $f$, \[f(t-y)=t^2-2ty+f(y)\qquad(t\in f(\mathbb R),\ y\in\mathbb R).\tag{1}\] Replacing $y$ by $-y$ and using that $f$ is even: \[f(t+y)=t^2+2ty+f(y)\qquad(t\in f(\mathbb R),\ y\in\mathbb R).\tag{2}\] Periods. Let $g(y)=f(y)-y^2$. Then (2) says $g(y+t)=g(y)$: every value $t$ of $f$ is a period of $g$. Differences of periods are periods. If $f\not\equiv0$, choose $t_0\ne0$ in $f(\mathbb R)$. For every real $y$, both $f(y)$ and $f(y+t_0)$ are periods, hence so is \[f(y+t_0)-f(y)=t_0^2+2t_0y\qquad\text{(by (2))}.\] As $y$ runs over $\mathbb R$, $t_0^2+2t_0y$ takes every real value. So every real number is a period of $g$, $g$ is constant, and $g\equiv g(0)=f(0)=0$. Thus $f(y)=y^2$.

The solutions are $\boxed{f\equiv0}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=x^2}$.

Solution 2 (Every real number is a difference of two values)

Both $f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=x^2$ satisfy the equation. Let $f$ be a solution and write $P(x,y)$ for the equation.

First facts. $P(x,0)$ gives $f(f(x))=f(x^2)+f(0)$. $P(x,f(x))$ gives $f(0)+2f(x)^2=f(x^2)+f(f(x))=2f(x^2)+f(0)$, so $f(x^2)=f(x)^2$ for all $x$. With $x=0$ and $x=1$: $f(0),f(1)\in\{0,1\}$, and $f(0)=1$ is impossible because then $P(0,0)$ reads $f(1)=2f(0)=2$. So $f(0)=0$, $f$ is even (from $P(0,y)$), and $f(f(x))=f(x)^2$. For every value $t$ of $f$ and every $y$: \[f(t-y)=t^2-2ty+f(y),\qquad f(t+y)=t^2+2ty+f(y).\tag{1}\] Every real is a difference of two values. Suppose $f\not\equiv0$ and take $t_0\ne0$ in the range. Given any real $z$, let $y=\frac{z-t_0^2}{2t_0}$. By the second equation in (1), $f(y+t_0)-f(y)=t_0^2+2t_0y=z$. So $z=f(a)-f(b)$ with $a=y+t_0$, $b=y$.

Conclusion. Apply the first equation in (1) with $t=f(a)$ and $y=f(b)$, and use $f(f(b))=f(b)^2$: \[f(z)=f\bigl(f(a)-f(b)\bigr)=f(a)^2-2f(a)f(b)+f(f(b))=\bigl(f(a)-f(b)\bigr)^2=z^2 .\] Hence $f(z)=z^2$ for all $z$, and the solutions are $\boxed{f\equiv0}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=x^2}$.

Problem 10

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle with circumcircle $\Omega$. Let $\Omega_B$ be the circle centered at $B$ which is tangent to $AC$, and $\Omega_C$ be the circle centered at $C$ which is tangent to $AB$. Let $\ell_B$ be the common chord of $\Omega_B$ and $\Omega$, and $\ell_C$ be the common chord of $\Omega_C$ and $\Omega$. The tangent lines to $\Omega$ at $B$ and $C$ intersect at $T$. Prove that lines $AT$, $\ell_B$, and $\ell_C$ are concurrent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power as a linear function along AT)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths, $R$ the circumradius, and $h_b=c\sin A$, $h_c=b\sin A$ the radii of $\Omega_B$, $\Omega_C$ (the distances from $B$ to $AC$ and from $C$ to $AB$). Each of $\Omega_B,\Omega_C$ has its centre on $\Omega$ and radius smaller than the diameter, so it meets $\Omega$ in two points; the common chords lie on the radical axes.

For a point $X$ let $F(X)=\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(X)-\operatorname{pow}_{\Omega_B}(X)$ and $G(X)=\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(X)-\operatorname{pow}_{\Omega_C}(X)$. Since $\operatorname{pow}(X)=|X-\text{centre}|^2-\text{radius}^2$, the quadratic terms cancel and $F$, $G$ are affine functions of $X$; their zero sets are the lines $\ell_B$ and $\ell_C$.

Values at $A$ and $T$. $\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(A)=0$ and $\operatorname{pow}_{\Omega_B}(A)=AB^2-h_b^2=c^2-c^2\sin^2A$, so $F(A)=-c^2\cos^2A$. As $TB$ is tangent to $\Omega$, $\operatorname{pow}_\Omega(T)=TB^2$, while $\operatorname{pow}_{\Omega_B}(T)=TB^2-h_b^2$; so $F(T)=h_b^2=c^2\sin^2A$. In the same way $G(A)=-b^2\cos^2A$ and, using $TC=TB$, $G(T)=h_c^2=b^2\sin^2A$.

The common point. Points of line $AT$ are $X_\lambda=(1-\lambda)A+\lambda T$, and by affinity $F(X_\lambda)=(1-\lambda)F(A)+\lambda F(T)$. This vanishes exactly for \[\lambda=\frac{F(A)}{F(A)-F(T)}=\frac{c^2\cos^2A}{c^2\cos^2A+c^2\sin^2A}=\cos^2A,\] and the same computation for $G$ gives $\lambda=\frac{b^2\cos^2A}{b^2\cos^2A+b^2\sin^2A}=\cos^2A$. Hence the point $X=A+\cos^2\!A\,(T-A)$ of segment $AT$ lies on $\ell_B$ and on $\ell_C$: the three lines are concurrent.

Solution 2 (Distances to the two tangents)

Lemma. If $P,Q$ lie on a circle of radius $R$, the distance from $P$ to the tangent at $Q$ equals $\frac{PQ^2}{2R}$. Proof. The angle $\theta$ between the chord $QP$ and the tangent at $Q$ equals an inscribed angle on the chord $QP$, so $PQ=2R\sin\theta$ and the distance is $PQ\sin\theta=\frac{PQ^2}{2R}$. $\square$

The line $\ell_B$. Let $U,V$ be the endpoints of the common chord of $\Omega$ and $\Omega_B$; then $BU=BV=h_b=c\sin A$. As $B$ is equidistant from $U$ and $V$, $OB\perp UV$, so $\ell_B=UV$ is parallel to the tangent $TB$. By the lemma, $U$ (and so all of $\ell_B$) is at distance $\frac{h_b^2}{2R}$ from $TB$, on the same side as $\Omega$. Likewise $\ell_C\parallel TC$ at distance $\frac{h_c^2}{2R}$ from $TC$, on the side of $\Omega$.

Comparison. The lines $TB$, $TC$ are not parallel ($A\ne90^\circ$), so $\ell_B$ and $\ell_C$ meet at a point $X$ lying inside the angle $BTC$ (the side of both tangents that contains $\Omega$), with \[\frac{\operatorname{dist}(X,TB)}{\operatorname{dist}(X,TC)}=\frac{h_b^2}{h_c^2}=\frac{c^2\sin^2A}{b^2\sin^2A}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}.\] The point $A$ is also inside angle $BTC$, and by the lemma $\frac{\operatorname{dist}(A,TB)}{\operatorname{dist}(A,TC)}=\frac{AB^2/2R}{AC^2/2R}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}$.

For a point $P$ inside an angle with vertex $T$, if the ray $TP$ makes angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ with the two sides ($\alpha+\beta=\angle BTC$ fixed), then $\frac{\operatorname{dist}(P,TB)}{\operatorname{dist}(P,TC)}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\beta}$, which is strictly increasing in $\alpha$. So the ratio determines the ray $TP$. Hence $X$ lies on ray $TA$, i.e. $AT$, $\ell_B$, $\ell_C$ are concurrent.
