Problem 1

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Power of a Point · also Silk Road MO 2026, Problem 1

The line joining the midpoints of the diagonals of a cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ intersects segment $AB$ at point $X$, and the extension of line $BC$ beyond point $C$ at point $Y$. Prove that if the points $A,C,X,Y$ lie on the same circle, then the segments $AB$, $BC$, and $BD$ can form a right triangle.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the line through the two diagonal midpoints and mark where its intersections lie on the rays $BA$ and $BC$. From $B$, identify the two secants of the circle through $A,C,X,Y$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use $B$ as the vector origin, and write $X,Y$ as multiples of $A,C$ and $D$ as their linear combination. Use the fact that both midpoints lie on $XY$ to relate the coefficients, and use power at $B$ to replace the two ray scalars by expressions in $AB^2$ and $BC^2$. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Write the equation of the circle through $B$ in the form relating the squared norm of a point to its dot product with the center vector. Apply it to $A,C,D$ and use your coefficient relations to compare the squared lengths of the three segments.
Common trap
The right triangle is to be formed from the three lengths, so the original rays $BA$ and $BC$ need not be perpendicular.
Show solution 1 – Vectors from $B$

Let $K$ and $L$ be the midpoints of $AC$ and $BD$ (they are distinct, otherwise $ABCD$ would be a parallelogram and the line $KL$ would not be defined). Put the origin at $B$ and write points as vectors; $A$ and $C$ are linearly independent. Since $X$ lies on segment $AB$ and $Y$ on ray $BC$ beyond $C$, \[ X=pA,\quad Y=qC\qquad(0<p<1<q), \] and we write $D=\alpha A+\beta C$.

The Newton line. $K=\tfrac12A+\tfrac12C$ lies on line $XY$, so $K=(1-s)pA+sqC$ for some $s$, i.e. $(1-s)p=\tfrac12$ and $sq=\tfrac12$. Adding $\tfrac1{2p}=1-s$ and $\tfrac1{2q}=s$: \[ \frac1p+\frac1q=2 . \] Similarly $L=\tfrac12D$ lies on $XY$: $\tfrac\alpha2=(1-r)p$, $\tfrac\beta2=rq$ for some $r$, hence \[ \frac\alpha p+\frac\beta q=2 . \]

The concyclicity. $X$ and $A$ lie on one ray from $B$, $Y$ and $C$ on another, so $A,C,X,Y$ are concyclic iff the powers agree: $BX\cdot BA=BY\cdot BC$, i.e. $p|A|^2=q|C|^2=:t$. Then $\tfrac1p=|A|^2/t$ and $\tfrac1q=|C|^2/t$, and the two relations become \[ |A|^2+|C|^2=2t,\qquad \alpha|A|^2+\beta|C|^2=2t . \]

The circle $ABCD$. Let $O$ be its centre. Since $B$ (the origin) is on the circle, $|Z-O|^2=|O|^2$, i.e. $|Z|^2=2\,Z\cdot O$, for every point $Z$ of the circle. Applying this to $A$, $C$, $D$: \[ BD^2=|D|^2=2D\cdot O=\alpha\,(2A\cdot O)+\beta\,(2C\cdot O)=\alpha|A|^2+\beta|C|^2=2t=AB^2+BC^2 . \]

Thus $BD^2=AB^2+BC^2$. In particular $BD<AB+BC$ and $BD$ is the longest of the three segments, so they form a triangle, and by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem it is right-angled (with hypotenuse $BD$).

Show solution 2 – An inversion centred at $B$

Let $\Gamma$ be the circle $ABCD$ and $K$, $L$ the midpoints of $AC$, $BD$ (distinct: if $K=L$ the diagonals bisect each other, $ABCD$ is a parallelogram and the line $KL$ is not defined). As $X$ lies on ray $BA$ and $Y$ on ray $BC$, the concyclicity of $A,C,X,Y$ means \[ t:=BX\cdot BA=BY\cdot BC>0 . \] Let $\iota$ be the inversion with centre $B$ and power $t$. It exchanges $A\leftrightarrow X$ and $C\leftrightarrow Y$. The circle $\Gamma$ passes through $B$, so $\iota(\Gamma)$ is a line, through $\iota(A)=X$ and $\iota(C)=Y$: the line $XY$, which contains $K$ and $L$. Hence $\iota(K)$ and $\iota(L)$ lie on $\Gamma$.

From $L$. $\iota(L)$ lies on $\Gamma$ and on ray $BL=$ ray $BD$, and is different from $B$, so $\iota(L)=D$. Thus $BL\cdot BD=t$, and as $BL=\tfrac12BD$, \[ BD^2=2t . \]

From $K$. $K$ is the midpoint of the chord $AC$, so it is inside $\Gamma$, and ray $BK$ meets $\Gamma$ again at a point $K^*$ beyond $K$; as above $\iota(K)=K^*$, so $t=BK\cdot BK^*$. The power of $K$ gives $KB\cdot KK^*=KA\cdot KC=\tfrac14AC^2$, and the median formula gives $BK^2=\tfrac14(2AB^2+2BC^2-AC^2)$. Hence \[ t=BK\,(BK+KK^*)=BK^2+\tfrac14AC^2=\tfrac12\bigl(AB^2+BC^2\bigr). \]

Therefore $BD^2=2t=AB^2+BC^2$, so $BD$ is the longest of the three segments and $BD<AB+BC$; they form a triangle, right-angled by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem, with hypotenuse $BD$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences · also Silk Road MO 2026, Problem 2

In an infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ consisting of positive integers, for each $n\ge 2$, the term $a_{n+1}$ is determined as follows: the fraction \[\frac{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_{n-1}}{a_n}\] is reduced to its lowest terms (irreducible form), and then $a_{n+1}$ is set equal to the numerator of this reduced fraction. Prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that for all $n\ge N$, the following equality holds: \[a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the reduced numerator using a prefix sum and the gcd that is canceled. In a few iterations, record those canceled factors as well as the new terms.
Hint 2: key idea
Let $g_n=\gcd(a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1},a_n)$ and use the recurrence to show that the next canceled factor divides $g_n$. This restricts how the factors can change indefinitely. (LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics)
Hint 3: approach
Once the factors stabilize, subtract the formulas for two successive reduced numerators to relate $a_n$ to the difference of two later terms. Use that relation to test whether powers of the stabilized factor must divide every term of the tail.
Common trap
Eventual constancy of the canceled factors alone does not show that the stable factor is one.
Show the solution – The cancelled factor shrinks and then disappears

Let $S_n=a_1+\dots+a_n$ and, for $n\ge2$, $g_n=\gcd(S_{n-1},a_n)$. The rule says \[ a_{n+1}=\frac{S_{n-1}}{g_n}\qquad(n\ge2). \]

Step 1: $g_{n+1}\mid g_n$. Let $d=g_{n+1}=\gcd(S_n,a_{n+1})$. Then $d\mid a_{n+1}$, and $a_{n+1}\mid S_{n-1}$, so $d\mid S_{n-1}$; also $d\mid S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n$, so $d\mid a_n$. Hence $d\mid\gcd(S_{n-1},a_n)=g_n$.

Step 2. The positive integers $g_2\ge g_3\ge\cdots$ are non-increasing (by Step 1), so they are constant from some index $N$ on: $g_n=g$ for all $n\ge N$.

Step 3: $g=1$. For $n\ge N$ we have $g\mid a_n$ (as $g=g_n\mid a_n$) and \[ a_{n+2}=\frac{S_n}{g}=\frac{S_{n-1}+a_n}{g}=a_{n+1}+\frac{a_n}{g},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a_n=g\,(a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}). \] We show by induction on $k$ that $g^k\mid a_n$ for all $n\ge N$. For $k=0$ this is clear; if it holds for $k$, then $g^k$ divides $a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}$, so $g^{k+1}\mid g(a_{n+2}-a_{n+1})=a_n$. If $g\ge2$, the positive integer $a_N$ would be divisible by arbitrarily large powers of $g$, which is impossible. So $g=1$.

Step 4. For $n\ge N$ we now have $a_{n+1}=S_{n-1}$ and $a_{n+2}=S_n$, hence \[ a_{n+2}=S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n=a_{n+1}+a_n\qquad\text{for all }n\ge N . \]

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity · also Silk Road MO 2026, Problem 3

On a horizontal segment of length $2025$, its endpoints and all points dividing it into unit segments are marked. Some pairs of marked points are connected by one-way teleporters transporting from the left point to the right point. It is known that several people, using only the teleporters, can travel from the left endpoint to the right endpoint in such a way that each teleporter is used exactly once. Prove that this can be done so that, for every $k=1,2,\dots,2025$, the teleporters transporting $k$ units to the right are used by at most $1013$ people.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick an interior marked point and compare how many teleporters enter and leave it in an arrangement that uses each once. Consider how a person's incoming teleporter determines the available choices to continue.
Hint 2: key idea
Specify the routes by pairing arriving and departing teleporters at each interior point. Whenever an arrival and a departure of the same length are both present, pair those first and then complete the local pairing. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
For a fixed length, group its teleporters into maximal consecutive runs under these pairings and see how many people one run can involve. Check that two different runs of that length cannot share a marked point, then count how many marked points a run requires.
Common trap
The bound is on the number of people using a given length, and one person can use several teleporters of that length.
Show the solution – Let equal jumps follow each other

Label the marked points $0,1,\dots,2025$. Each pair of points carries at most one teleporter (this is what “some pairs are connected” means; with parallel teleporters the claim would fail, e.g. for $1014$ copies of the teleporter $0\to2025$), and a teleporter from $i$ to $j>i$ has length $j-i$.

Plans as matchings. In a valid plan every passage of a person through an inner point $v$ uses one teleporter arriving at $v$ and one leaving $v$, and every teleporter is used exactly once; so at every inner point the numbers of arriving and leaving teleporters are equal. Conversely, suppose that at every inner point $v$ we fix a bijection $\pi_v$ from the teleporters arriving at $v$ to those leaving $v$. Send one person along each teleporter leaving $0$, and let a person arriving at $v$ by $e$ continue with $\pi_v(e)$. Teleporters go to the right, so every person reaches $2025$. Every teleporter $f$ is used exactly once: going backwards ($f=\pi_v(e)$ determines $e$, and so on) we reach a teleporter leaving $0$, whose person uses $f$; two persons cannot share a teleporter because their routes are determined forwards. So every such choice of bijections is a valid plan.

The choice. At each inner point $v$ and for each $k$ such that both teleporters $v-k\to v$ and $v\to v+k$ exist, let $\pi_v$ send the first to the second. Different $k$ involve different teleporters, so this is a partial bijection; complete it arbitrarily to a bijection (possible since the numbers of arriving and leaving teleporters are equal).

Counting. Fix $k$. Call a $k$-chain a maximal run $v_0\to v_0+k\to v_0+2k\to\dots\to v_0+mk$ ($m\ge1$) of existing teleporters of length $k$. By our rule, whoever uses the first teleporter of a $k$-chain uses all of them. Each length-$k$ teleporter lies in exactly one $k$-chain, so the number of people using teleporters of length $k$ is at most the number of $k$-chains.

Two $k$-chains share no marked point: a point $v$ has at most one arriving length-$k$ teleporter (from $v-k$) and at most one leaving one (to $v+k$), and if both exist they are consecutive in the same chain. Every $k$-chain contains at least $2$ marked points, and there are $2026$ marked points, so there are at most $1013$ $k$-chains.

Hence, with this plan, for every $k=1,\dots,2025$ at most $1013$ people use teleporters of length $k$.

Problem 4

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities · Symmetric and Cyclic Polynomial Forms · also Silk Road MO 2026, Problem 4

Let $P(x,y,z)$ be a polynomial with real coefficients satisfying \[P(x,y,z)=P(y+z-x,y,z)=P(x,z+x-y,z)=P(x,y,x+y-z).\]

  • (1) Prove that there exists a polynomial $Q(x,y,z)$ with real coefficients such that \[P(x,y,z)=Q(x-y,y-z,z-x).\]

  • (2) Prove that there exists a polynomial $R(x,y)$ with real coefficients such that \[P(x,y,z)=R\big(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx,\ (x-y)(y-z)(z-x)\big).\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Name the three substitutions in the hypothesis and apply a few of them in succession. Record what happens both to the three coordinates and to their pairwise differences.
Hint 2: key idea
Apply the first, second, third, and second substitutions in that order. Check whether the net change is the same in all three coordinates and whether that change stays fixed under repetition. (LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities)
Hint 3: approach
Use the repeated shifts to get infinitely many roots of a one-variable polynomial, then extend the resulting identity to triples where the shift vanishes and choose a common shift that sets one coordinate to zero. On the plane of difference triples, compute how the original substitutions permute the three entries. Average over permutations before applying the theorem on symmetric polynomials and restricting back to that plane.
Common trap
The polynomial in the differences is initially known to be symmetric only on their zero-sum plane.
Show the solution – Composing the three symmetries

Let $T_1(x,y,z)=(y+z-x,y,z)$, $T_2(x,y,z)=(x,z+x-y,z)$, $T_3(x,y,z)=(x,y,x+y-z)$. The hypothesis says $P\circ T_i=P$ for $i=1,2,3$, hence $P$ is invariant under every composition of these maps.

(1) Apply $T_1$, then $T_2$, then $T_3$, then $T_2$: \[ (x,y,z)\mapsto(y+z-x,\,y,\,z)\mapsto(y+z-x,\,2z-x,\,z)\mapsto(y+z-x,\,2z-x,\,y+2z-2x)\mapsto(y+z-x,\,2y+z-2x,\,y+2z-2x). \] The result is $(x,y,z)+d\,(1,1,1)$ with $d=y+z-2x$. Hence \[ P(x+d,\,y+d,\,z+d)=P(x,y,z),\qquad d=y+z-2x . \] Adding the same number to $x,y,z$ does not change $y+z-2x$, so we may iterate: $P(x+jd,y+jd,z+jd)=P(x,y,z)$ for every integer $j\ge0$. Fix $(x,y,z)$ with $d\ne0$. The polynomial $s\mapsto P(x+s,y+s,z+s)-P(x,y,z)$ vanishes at the infinitely many points $s=jd$, so it is zero. Thus the polynomial \[ G(x,y,z,s)=P(x+s,y+s,z+s)-P(x,y,z) \] vanishes whenever $y+z\ne2x$; then $(y+z-2x)\,G$ vanishes everywhere, so it is the zero polynomial, and so is $G$. Taking $s=-z$: \[ P(x,y,z)=P(x-z,\,y-z,\,0)=Q(x-y,\,y-z,\,z-x)\qquad\text{with}\qquad Q(u,v,w)=P(-w,\,v,\,0). \]

(2) Put $u=x-y$, $v=y-z$, $w=z-x$. A direct check shows how the maps act on the differences: \[ T_1:\ (u,v,w)\mapsto(w,v,u),\qquad T_2:\ (u,v,w)\mapsto(v,u,w),\qquad T_3:\ (u,v,w)\mapsto(u,w,v). \] (For example, for $T_1$: $(y+z-x)-y=z-x$, $y-z$, $z-(y+z-x)=x-y$.) The triples $(x-y,y-z,z-x)$ fill the whole plane $\Pi=\{u+v+w=0\}$ (take $x=u+v$, $y=v$, $z=0$). So the invariance of $P$ gives $Q(w,v,u)=Q(v,u,w)=Q(u,w,v)=Q(u,v,w)$ on $\Pi$. These three transpositions generate all permutations, so $Q\circ\tau=Q$ on $\Pi$ for every permutation $\tau$ of the variables.

Let $\widetilde Q=\frac16\sum_\tau Q\circ\tau$. It is a symmetric polynomial that coincides with $Q$ on $\Pi$. By the fundamental theorem on symmetric polynomials, $\widetilde Q=F(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ for a real polynomial $F$, where $e_1=u+v+w$, $e_2=uv+vw+wu$, $e_3=uvw$. On $\Pi$ we have $e_1=0$ and $e_2=-\tfrac12(u^2+v^2+w^2)$, and \[ u^2+v^2+w^2=(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2=2\,(x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx). \] Hence, writing $\sigma=x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx$ and $\pi=(x-y)(y-z)(z-x)$, \[ P(x,y,z)=\widetilde Q(u,v,w)=F(0,-\sigma,\pi)=R(\sigma,\pi),\qquad R(s,p)=F(0,-s,p). \]

Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis · also APMO 2026, Problem 1

Find all quadruples of positive integers $(a,p,m,o)$ with $o\ge2$ such that \[a!+p!=m^o+26.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
The two factorials play the same role, so put their inputs in order before trying examples. For a few small pairs, check whether the amount left after subtracting $26$ is positive and could be a perfect power.
Hint 2: key idea
If both factorial inputs are at least $4$, compare the remainder forced for $m^o$ modulo $4$ with the possibilities for an odd or even base when $o\ge 2$. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
When the smaller input is $1$, use modulo $4$ to restrict the exponent, then use powers of $5$ to narrow the range and inspect the remaining residues. When it is $2$, factor the first positive candidate and use modulo $9$ for the later ones. When it is $3$, use modulo $8$ to determine the exponent and modulo $11$ to reduce the problem to a short list of factorials.
Common trap
In the case of smaller input $1$, justify the parity restriction on $o$ before requiring three factors of $5$.
Show the answer

$(a,p,m,o)\in\{(3,4,2,2),\,(4,3,2,2),\,(3,5,10,2),\,(5,3,10,2)\}$

Show the solution – Reduce to $\min(a,p)\le3$, then three cases

By symmetry let $a\le p$. Put $M=m^o=a!+p!-26$; recall $o\ge2$.

$a\le3$. If $a\ge4$, then $4\mid a!+p!$ and $M\equiv-26\equiv2\pmod4$. But a perfect power with exponent $o\ge2$ is odd or divisible by $4$. So $a\in\{1,2,3\}$.

$a=1$: $M=p!-25$. We need $p!>25$, so $p\ge4$; $p=4$ gives $M=-1$. Let $p\ge5$. Then $4\mid p!$, so $M\equiv3\pmod 4$; a square is $0$ or $1$ mod $4$, and an even power is a square, so $o$ is odd and $o\ge3$. Also $5\mid p!-25=m^o$, so $5\mid m$ and $125\mid M$, hence $25\mid p!$, so $p\ge10$. If $p\ge15$ then $125\mid p!$ and $M\equiv-25\pmod{125}$, a contradiction. For $10\le p\le14$, $v_5(p!)=2$ and $M=25\bigl(\tfrac{p!}{25}-1\bigr)$; the values $\frac{p!}{25}\bmod5$ for $p=10,11,12,13,14$ are $2,2,4,2,3$, never $1$, so $v_5(M)=2<3$, a contradiction. No solutions.

$a=2$: $M=p!-24$. We need $p\ge5$. For $p=5$, $M=96=2^5\cdot3$ is not a perfect power ($v_3=1$). For $p\ge6$: $3\mid M$, so $3\mid m$ and $9\mid M$; but $9\mid p!$ and $9\nmid24$. No solutions.

$a=3$: $M=p!-20$. We need $p\ge4$. For $p\ge4$ we have $8\mid p!$ and $M\equiv-20\equiv4\pmod8$, so $v_2(M)=2$; since $v_2(M)=o\,v_2(m)$ and $o\ge2$, we get $o=2$. So $M$ is a square. For $p\ge11$, $M\equiv-20\equiv2\pmod{11}$, but $2$ is not a square modulo $11$ (the squares are $0,1,3,4,5,9$). For $4\le p\le10$, $M=4,\,100,\,700,\,5020,\,40300,\,362860,\,3628780$; only $4=2^2$ and $100=10^2$ are squares ($700=7\cdot10^2$, $70^2<5020<71^2$, $200^2<40300<201^2$, $602^2<362860<603^2$, $1904^2<3628780<1905^2$).

So $(a,p,m,o)=(3,4,2,2)$ or $(3,5,10,2)$, and by symmetry the answer is \[\boxed{(a,p,m,o)\in\{(3,4,2,2),(4,3,2,2),(3,5,10,2),(5,3,10,2)\}}.\] Check: $3!+4!=30=2^2+26$ and $3!+5!=126=10^2+26$.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting · also APMO 2026, Problem 2

Let $n\ge2$ be an integer. Let $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_{2^n}$ be the $2^n$ subsets of an $n$-element set in some order. Prove that \[|A_1\cap A_2|+|A_2\cap A_3|+\dots+|A_{2^n-1}\cap A_{2^n}|+|A_{2^n}\cap A_1|\ge2^{n-2}.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
For $n=2$, place all four subsets in a circle and record the contribution of each neighboring pair. Change the order and see how the contributions change.
Hint 2: key idea
Use inclusion–exclusion to compare the number of elements in both sets of a neighboring pair with the number in neither. When you sum around the circle, count how many subsets contain each ground-set element. (LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities)
Hint 3: approach
Use the comparison of the two totals to rewrite twice the sum as a count of membership agreements between neighboring subsets. Characterize the pairs with no agreements and use the uniqueness of each subset's complement to bound how many such pairs can occur around the circle.
Common trap
The pair formed by the last and first subsets also contributes to the cyclic sum.
Show solution 1 – Common elements equal common non-elements; complements

Let the ground set be $U$, $|U|=n$, indices modulo $2^n$, and $S=\sum_i|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|$. Put $O_i=U\setminus(A_i\cup A_{i+1})$, the elements in neither set.

Step 1: $\sum_i|O_i|=S$. By inclusion-exclusion, $|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|-|O_i|=|A_i|+|A_{i+1}|-n$. Summing over $i$, every set is counted twice: \[S-\sum_i|O_i|=2\sum_i|A_i|-n2^n=2\cdot n2^{n-1}-n2^n=0,\] since each element lies in exactly $2^{n-1}$ subsets.

Step 2. Hence $2S=\sum_i\bigl(|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|+|O_i|\bigr)$, and the $i$-th term is the number of elements on which $A_i$ and $A_{i+1}$ agree (in both or in neither). This term is $0$ only if $A_{i+1}=U\setminus A_i$. Each subset has exactly one complement, so the pairs $\{A,U\setminus A\}$ form a perfect matching of the $2^n$ subsets; a cycle through all $2^n$ subsets can use at most $2^{n-1}$ of these pairs as consecutive pairs (no two used pairs share a set). So at least $2^n-2^{n-1}=2^{n-1}$ of the $2^n$ consecutive pairs agree somewhere, and $2S\ge2^{n-1}$, i.e. $S\ge2^{n-2}$.

Show solution 2 – Counting runs element by element

Let $U$ be the ground set. For an element $x$, write around the cycle the word $w_x$ whose $i$-th letter is $1$ if $x\in A_i$ and $0$ otherwise. It is a cyclic word with $2^{n-1}$ ones and $2^{n-1}$ zeros ($n\ge1$). Let $r_x\ge1$ be the number of maximal blocks of ones; the blocks of zeros alternate with them, so there are also $r_x$ of those.

The number of indices $i$ with $x\in A_i\cap A_{i+1}$ is the number of adjacent pairs $11$ in $w_x$, which is $2^{n-1}-r_x$ (a block of length $\ell$ contains $\ell-1$ such pairs). Likewise there are $2^{n-1}-r_x$ adjacent pairs $00$. Summing over $x$: \[S=\sum_i|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|=\sum_x(2^{n-1}-r_x),\qquad T:=\sum_i\#\{x:\ x\notin A_i\cup A_{i+1}\}=\sum_x(2^{n-1}-r_x)=S .\] So $2S=S+T=\sum_i a_i$, where $a_i$ is the number of elements on which $A_i$ and $A_{i+1}$ agree.

Now $a_i=0$ exactly when $A_{i+1}$ is the complement of $A_i$. If this happened for two consecutive indices, $A_{i+2}$ would be the complement of the complement of $A_i$, i.e. $A_{i+2}=A_i$, impossible since $2^n\ge4$ sets are distinct. So among the $2^n$ indices, no two cyclically consecutive ones have $a_i=0$; hence at most $2^{n-1}$ of them have $a_i=0$, at least $2^{n-1}$ have $a_i\ge1$, and $2S\ge2^{n-1}$. Thus $S\ge2^{n-2}$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup · also APMO 2026, Problem 4

Let $ABCD$ be a quadrilateral with an incircle $\omega$ of centre $I$. The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at $E$. Let $J$ be the incentre of triangle $ABD$. The extension of the ray $EJ$ intersects $\omega$ at $P$. Prove that $PI\perp BD$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the four points where $\omega$ touches the sides, and sketch the incircle of $ABD$ as well. Mark the direction of the ray from $E$ through $J$.
Hint 2: key idea
Give the two tangent segments from each vertex a common length label and use $BD$ as the horizontal axis. Intersect $BD$ with a chord joining opposite points of tangency and look for triangles in which the sine rule relates the tangent lengths to distances along $BD$. (LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation)
Hint 3: approach
Use both chords joining opposite points of tangency along each diagonal to locate $E$ and check that it is inside $\omega$. Find $J$ from the triangle's tangent lengths and inradius, then find the coordinates of $I$ using its squared distances to $B$ and $D$ and a signed-area decomposition of $ABD$. Set up a collinearity test for $E,J$ and the point of $\omega$ vertically above $I$, using the half-angle at $A$ to relate the two inradii and the heights to check the ray direction.
Common trap
The area contribution from $IBD$ depends on which side of $BD$ contains $I$.
Show solution 1 – Tangent lengths and coordinates along $BD$

Let $r$ be the radius of $\omega$ and $x_A,x_B,x_C,x_D$ the tangent lengths from the vertices, so $AB=x_A+x_B$, $AD=x_A+x_D$, etc. Let $Q$ be the point of $\omega$ with $IQ\perp BD$ on the same side of $BD$ as $A$. We show that $E,J,Q$ are collinear with $J,Q$ on the same side of $BD$; since $E$ lies inside $\omega$ (see the lemma), the ray $EJ$ meets $\omega$ exactly once, so $P=Q$ and $PI\perp BD$.

Lemma: $E$ divides $BD$ in the ratio $BE:ED=x_B:x_D$, and $E$ is inside $\omega$. Let $\omega$ touch $AB,BC,CD,DA$ at $W,X,V,Z$. The segment $WV$ joins points on opposite sides of $BD$, so it meets segment $BD$ at a point $E_1$ inside $\omega$. The tangents at $W$ and $V$ make equal angles with the chord $WV$, so $\sin\angle BWE_1=\sin\angle DVE_1$, and the angles at $E_1$ are vertical. The law of sines in triangles $BWE_1$, $DVE_1$ gives $BE_1:E_1D=BW:DV=x_B:x_D$. The same argument for the chord $XZ$ gives a point of $BD$ with the same ratio, hence the same point $E_1$; so $E_1=WV\cap XZ$. Repeating everything with the diagonal $AC$ shows that $WV\cap XZ$ also lies on $AC$. Hence $E=E_1$.

Coordinates. Put $L=BD$ and coordinates with $B=(0,0)$, $D=(L,0)$, $A$ above the $x$-axis. Let $S$ be the area and $s$ the semiperimeter of triangle $ABD$, so $2s=2x_A+x_B+x_D+L$, and let $r_1=S/s$ be its inradius.

  • $E=(e,0)$ with $e=\dfrac{Lx_B}{x_B+x_D}$.

  • The incircle of $ABD$ touches $BD$ at $T=(t,0)$ with $t=\frac{AB+BD-AD}{2}=\frac{L+x_B-x_D}{2}$, and $J=(t,r_1)$.

  • Let $I=(i,k)$. From $IB^2=r^2+x_B^2$ and $ID^2=r^2+x_D^2$ we get $i=\dfrac{L^2+x_B^2-x_D^2}{2L}$. Splitting $ABD$ by $I$ (which lies on the same side of $AB$ as $D$ and on the same side of $AD$ as $B$) and using signed area for $IBD$: $S=\frac r2(AB+AD)+\frac{Lk}{2}$, so $k=\dfrac{2S-r(2s-L)}{L}$.

  • $Q=(i,k+r)$.

Now \[t-e=\frac{(x_D-x_B)(L-x_B-x_D)}{2(x_B+x_D)},\qquad i-e=\frac{(x_D-x_B)(L-x_B-x_D)(L+x_B+x_D)}{2L(x_B+x_D)} .\] The points $E$, $J$, $Q$ are collinear iff $r_1(i-e)=(k+r)(t-e)$, and by the two formulas this holds as soon as \[r_1\cdot\frac{L+x_B+x_D}{L}=k+r .\] Since $L+x_B+x_D=2s-2x_A$, the left side is $\frac{2S-2x_Ar_1}{L}$, while $k+r=\frac{2S-2r(s-L)}{L}$. So we need $x_Ar_1=r(s-L)$, i.e. $\frac{r}{x_A}=\frac{r_1}{s-L}$. Both equal $\tan\frac{\angle BAD}2$: $\omega$ touches $AB$ at distance $x_A$ from $A$, and the incircle of $ABD$ touches $AB$ at distance $s-BD$ from $A$. So $E,J,Q$ are collinear.

Finally $k+r=r_1\frac{L+x_B+x_D}{L}>0$ and $r_1>0$, so $Q$ and $J$ are both above $BD$, i.e. on the same ray from $E\in BD$. Hence $P=Q$ and $PI\perp BD$.

Show solution 2 – Tangent parameters on the unit circle

We take $ABCD$ convex (it has an incircle). Choose the unit of length and axes so that $\omega$ is the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$, $I$ is the origin, $BD$ is the horizontal line $y=h$, and $A$ lies above $BD$. Since $D$ lies inside the angle $ABC$, whose sides are the two tangents from $B$, the line $BD$ passes through the interior of $\omega$; hence $|h|<1$. Let $Q=(0,1)$. We show that $J$ lies strictly between $E$ and $Q$ on a line, and that $E$ is inside $\omega$; then the ray $EJ$ meets $\omega$ only at $Q$, so $P=Q$ and $PI$ is vertical, i.e. $PI\perp BD$.

Parameters. Every point of $\omega$ except $(0,-1)$ can be written as $\bigl(\frac{2u}{1+u^2},\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2}\bigr)$ for a unique real $u$ (the parameter; $u=0$ gives $Q$), and the tangent there is \[2u\,x+(1-u^2)\,y=1+u^2,\] because this line contains the point and is perpendicular to the radius. Two tangents with parameters $u,v$ (with $uv\ne-1$) meet at \[\Bigl(\frac{u+v}{1+uv},\ \frac{1-uv}{1+uv}\Bigr),\] as one checks by substituting into both equations.

A tangent passes through a point $(b,h)$ of $BD$ exactly when its parameter satisfies $(1+h)u^2-2bu+(1-h)=0$. (The tangent at $(0,-1)$ is $y=-1$, which misses $BD$ because $h\ne-1$.) So the two tangents from $B$ have parameters whose product is $k=\frac{1-h}{1+h}>0$, and the same holds for the two tangents from $D$. Let $s$ be the parameter of the point where $\omega$ touches $AB$ and $t$ that of the point where it touches $AD$; then the touching points on $BC$ and $CD$ have parameters $k/s$ and $k/t$. Since $A$ and $C$ are the intersections of these pairs of tangents, \[A=\Bigl(\frac{s+t}{1+st},\frac{1-st}{1+st}\Bigr),\qquad C=\Bigl(\frac{k/s+k/t}{1+k^2/(st)},\frac{1-k^2/(st)}{1+k^2/(st)}\Bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{k(s+t)}{st+k^2},\frac{st-k^2}{st+k^2}\Bigr).\]

Signs. The touching point on segment $AB$ lies strictly above $BD$ (as $B$ is on $BD$ and $A$ above it), so $\frac{1-s^2}{1+s^2}>h$, which rearranges to $s^2<k$; likewise $t^2<k$. Hence $|st|<k$ and $k+st>0$. Also $1+st>0$: if $1+st<0$, then $1-st>2$ and $A_y=\frac{1-st}{1+st}<-1<h$, contradicting that $A$ is above $BD$. A direct computation, using $h=\frac{1-k}{1+k}$ and $1+h=\frac{2}{1+k}$, gives \[1-hA_y=\frac{(1+h)(k+st)}{1+st}>0 .\]

The point $E$. Substituting the coordinates and $h=\frac{1-k}{1+k}$ and simplifying gives the identity \[A_x\,(hC_y-1)=C_x\,(hA_y-1).\] (Geometrically: $A$, $C$ and the pole $(0,1/h)$ of $BD$ are collinear.) The expression $g(X)=X_x(hA_y-1)-A_x(hX_y-1)$ is a linear function of the point $X$ that vanishes at $A$ and, by the identity, at $C$; so it vanishes on the whole line $AC$, in particular at $E=(E_x,h)$: \[E_x=\frac{A_x(h^2-1)}{hA_y-1}=\frac{(1-h^2)A_x}{1-hA_y}.\] $E$ is inside $\omega$. We need $E_x^2+h^2<1$, i.e. $(1-h^2)A_x^2<(1-hA_y)^2$. Another direct computation gives \[(1-hA_y)^2-(1-h^2)A_x^2=\frac{(1+h)^2}{(1+st)^2}\,(k-s^2)(k-t^2),\] which is positive by the signs above.

The point $J$. Both $I$ and $J$ lie on the bisector of $\angle BAD$, on the ray from $A$ through $I$. On this ray the distance to line $AB$ is proportional to the distance from $A$; it equals $1$ at $I$ and $r$ at $J$, where $r$ is the inradius of $ABD$. Hence $J=A+r(I-A)=(1-r)A$. As $J$ lies above $BD$ at distance $r$, $J_y=h+r$, so $(1-r)A_y=h+r$ and, since $A_y>h>-1$, \[r=\frac{A_y-h}{A_y+1}>0,\qquad J_x=\frac{(1+h)A_x}{A_y+1},\qquad J_y-1=\frac{hA_y-1}{A_y+1}<0 .\]

Collinearity. We have \[\frac{J_x}{J_y-1}=\frac{(1+h)A_x}{hA_y-1}=\frac{E_x}{h-1},\] using the formula for $E_x$. So the vectors $J-Q$ and $E-Q$ are parallel, i.e. $E$, $J$, $Q$ are collinear. Moreover $h<J_y<1$, so $J$ lies strictly between $E$ (height $h$) and $Q$ (height $1$). The ray from $E$ through $J$ therefore passes through $Q$, and since $E$ is inside $\omega$ this ray meets $\omega$ exactly once. Hence $P=Q=(0,1)$, the line $PI$ is the $y$-axis, and $PI\perp BD$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight · also APMO 2026, Problem 5

Let $n$ be a positive integer. There exist $n(n+1)$ rooms arranged in a $(n+1)\times n$ grid. For each two adjacent rooms, there is a door between them. Find the number of ways to choose a subset of doors and lock them such that there are two rooms $S$ and $G$ satisfying the following properties:

  • (i) $S$ is in the first row and $G$ is in the $(n+1)$-th row.

  • (ii) One can reach $G$ from $S$ only through unlocked doors.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a board with $n=2$ and trace an unlocked route from a top-row room to a bottom-row room. If the route returns to the top row before reaching the bottom, ask where the useful part of the route can start.
Hint 2: key idea
Draw a locked door as a wall segment and look for a chain of such segments joining the left side to the right side. Think about where that chain and an unlocked top-to-bottom route would meet if both existed. (LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Trim a crossing to its first bottom-row room and the last top-row room before it to identify the doors you can ignore. If no crossing exists, use the parity of degrees along the boundary between reachable and unreachable rooms to look for a component meeting both sides. After merging each side in the wall graph and each end row in the passage graph, compare the graphs by a quarter-turn and set up a pairing that reverses the relevant door states.
Common trap
Consecutive wall segments meet at grid corners, while a route travels from room to room.
Show the answer

$\boxed{2^{2n^2-2}}$

Show the solution – Planar duality: crossings versus walls

Draw the rooms as unit squares $[j-1,j]\times[i-1,i]$ ($1\le j\le n$ columns, $1\le i\le n+1$ rows, row $1$ at the top). A door is a unit wall segment shared by two rooms; there are $(n+1)(n-1)$ doors between horizontally adjacent rooms and $n\cdot n$ between vertically adjacent ones, $2n^2-1$ in all. A locking is good if some room of the top row is joined to some room of the bottom row by a path through unlocked doors.

Irrelevant doors. The $n-1$ doors between two rooms of the top row and the $n-1$ doors between two rooms of the bottom row do not matter: a good path can be cut to start at its last top-row room and to end at its first bottom-row room. Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of the other $m=2n^2-2n+1$ doors.

Walls. A locked door is a wall segment. A blocking wall is a chain of locked doors of $\mathcal D$, consecutive ones sharing an endpoint, running from the left side of the rectangle to the right side.

Duality lemma: a locking is good if and only if there is no blocking wall.

Not both. Suppose a good path and a blocking wall both exist; cut the path as above, so it uses doors of $\mathcal D$ only. Draw the path through room centres, crossing each used door at its midpoint, and extend it by vertical segments from its first room centre up to the top side and from its last room centre down to the bottom side. These two extensions lie inside the top and bottom rows, which contain no wall segment of $\mathcal D$ (those lie on the lines at heights $1,\dots,n$ and, for vertical ones, between heights $1$ and $n$). In a rectangle, a curve joining the left and right sides meets every curve joining the top and bottom sides (a standard consequence of the Jordan curve theorem). A segment joining the centres of two adjacent rooms meets the wall segments only at the midpoint of the door between those two rooms. So the meeting point is the midpoint of a door that is both unlocked (used by the path) and locked (on the wall), which is impossible.

No good path gives a blocking wall. Suppose the locking is not good, and let $R$ be the set of rooms that can be reached from some top-row room through unlocked doors of $\mathcal D$. Then $R$ contains the whole top row and no bottom-row room. Let $B$ be the set of doors between a room of $R$ and a room not in $R$; each of them is locked (otherwise the outer room would be reachable), and $B\subseteq\mathcal D$, because the two rooms of a top-row door are both in $R$ and those of a bottom-row door are both outside $R$. Consider the graph $W$ whose vertices are the lattice points $(j,i)$ with $0\le j\le n$, $1\le i\le n$ and whose edges are the doors of $B$, each door being the unit segment joining two such points. We count degrees in $W$.

  • An inner point $(j,i)$, $1\le j\le n-1$, is a corner of four rooms (in rows $i,i+1$ and columns $j,j+1$), and the four grid segments at it separate cyclically consecutive rooms among these four. A segment is in $B$ exactly when its two rooms differ in membership of $R$; going once around the point, the membership changes an even number of times. So the degree of $(j,i)$ is even.

  • A left-side point $(0,i)$ lies on only one door, the one between the rooms of column $1$ in rows $i$ and $i+1$; its degree is $1$ if exactly one of these rooms is in $R$ and $0$ otherwise. Going down column $1$ from row $1$ (in $R$) to row $n+1$ (not in $R$), membership changes an odd number of times, so an odd number of left-side points have degree $1$. In the same way an odd number of right-side points $(n,i)$ have degree $1$.

Every connected component of a graph has an even number of vertices of odd degree. The odd vertices of $W$ are exactly the side points of degree $1$, and the left ones are odd in number, so some component of $W$ contains an odd number of left-side odd vertices; having an even total, it also contains a right-side point. A path in this component from a left-side point to a right-side point is a chain of locked doors of $\mathcal D$, consecutive ones sharing an endpoint, from the left side to the right side: a blocking wall.

The dual board. The doors of $\mathcal D$ join lattice points $(j,i)$ with $0\le j\le n$ and $1\le i\le n$ (horizontal walls at heights $1,\dots,n$, vertical walls between heights $i$ and $i+1$ for $1\le i\le n-1$); merging all points of the left side into one terminal $\lambda$ and all points of the right side into one terminal $\rho$, the doors of $\mathcal D$ become the edges of a graph $H$: $n$ rows of $n-1$ interior points, each row joined to $\lambda$ and $\rho$, consecutive rows joined vertically. Likewise, merging the top row of rooms into a terminal $\tau$ and the bottom row into $\beta$, the doors of $\mathcal D$ form a graph $G$: $n-1$ rows of $n$ rooms, joined to $\tau$ and $\beta$. Turning the picture by $90^\circ$ gives an isomorphism $\psi$ from $H$ to $G$ taking $\lambda,\rho$ to $\tau,\beta$; each door $e\in\mathcal D$ is an edge of $H$ (as a wall) and of $G$ (as a passage), so $\sigma(e)=\psi(e)$ is a permutation of $\mathcal D$.

Pairing. For a set $K\subseteq\mathcal D$ of locked doors let $\Phi(K)=\mathcal D\setminus\sigma(K)$. Then: $K$ has a blocking wall $\iff$ the edges $K$ contain a $\lambda$-$\rho$ path in $H$ $\iff$ the edges $\sigma(K)$ contain a $\tau$-$\beta$ path in $G$ $\iff$ the locking $\Phi(K)$ (whose unlocked doors are $\sigma(K)$) is good. With the lemma: $K$ is good $\iff$ $\Phi(K)$ is not good. Since $\Phi$ is a bijection on the subsets of $\mathcal D$, it maps the good lockings injectively into the bad ones and vice versa, so exactly half of the $2^m$ lockings of $\mathcal D$ are good.

Counting the $2n-2$ irrelevant doors freely, the number of good lockings is \[2^{m-1}\cdot2^{2n-2}=2^{2n^2-2}=\boxed{2^{2n^2-2}}.\] (For $n=1$ there is one door and it must be unlocked: $2^0=1$.)

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

Some permutation $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{502}$ of numbers $3,8,13,\dots,2508$ satisfies the following inequality for every $k=1,2,\dots,502$ \[a_k-a_{k-1}+a_{k-2}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}a_1>0.\] Find all possible values of $a_1$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Call the alternating expression through $a_k$ by the name $S_k$. Write out its first few values and compare $S_k$ with the previous expression.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the recurrence $S_k=a_k-S_{k-1}$ and the common residue of the permitted entries modulo $5$. Determine how the residue of $S_k$ alternates. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Use the positivity requirement to find a lower bound for either residue of $S_k$. Ask which entries could not be placed after the first position without violating that bound. For attainability, look for an ordering in which every partial expression stays positive.
Common trap
A residue of zero modulo $5$ does not allow $S_k=0$, because the required inequality is strict.
Show the answer

$a_1=3$

Show the solution – Alternating sums modulo 5

Write $S_0=0$ and $S_k=a_k-a_{k-1}+a_{k-2}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}a_1$ for $1\le k\le 502$. Then \[S_k=a_k-S_{k-1}\qquad(1\le k\le 502),\] and the condition says $S_k>0$ for every $k$.

Residues. Every given number is $\equiv 3\pmod 5$. Hence $S_1=a_1\equiv3$, and by induction $S_k\equiv 3-3=0$ for even $k$ and $S_k\equiv 3-0=3$ for odd $k$ (mod $5$). A positive integer that is $\equiv0$ or $\equiv3\pmod5$ is at least $3$. So $S_k\ge3$ for every $k\ge1$.

Where is $3$? Suppose $3=a_j$ with $j\ge2$. Then $S_j=a_j-S_{j-1}=3-S_{j-1}\le 3-3=0$, a contradiction. Therefore $a_1=3$.

$a_1=3$ is possible. Take the increasing order $a_k=5k-2$. Then $S_{2i}=5i$ and $S_{2i+1}=5i+3$ for all $i\ge0$: indeed $S_{2i+1}=(10i+3)-5i=5i+3$ and $S_{2i+2}=(10i+8)-(5i+3)=5(i+1)$. All these are positive.

Hence the only possible value is $\boxed{a_1=3}$.

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting

Find the largest value of $d$ (in terms of $n$) such that there exist $k=2^n$ subsets $S_1,S_2,\dots,S_k$ of the set $\{1,2,3,\dots,2^n\}$ such that for any $1\le i<j\le k$ at least $d$ elements lie in exactly one of $S_i$ and $S_j$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a chosen element, mark which of the sets contain it. Think about how many unordered pairs of sets disagree on that element.
Hint 2: key idea
Count disagreements over all pairs of sets by fixing the element first, and bound the contribution using the number of sets containing it. For a construction, note that equality forces every element to lie in exactly half of the sets. (LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the total number of disagreements with the lower requirement on each pair and use integrality to sharpen the upper bound. For the construction, look for a highly symmetric family in which every two distinct sets differ in exactly the bound. Check the smallest case separately if the rounding in the bound behaves differently there.
Common trap
The upper bound from averaging may round differently when $n=1$.
Show the answer

$d=2^{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$ (and $d=2$ for $n=1$)

Show solution 1 – Double counting and a linear construction

Let $N=2^n$ and write $d(S,T)=|S\triangle T|$ for the number of elements lying in exactly one of $S,T$.

Bound. Suppose $S_1,\dots,S_N$ satisfy $d(S_i,S_j)\ge d$ for all $i<j$. Count the pairs $(\{i,j\},x)$ with $x$ in exactly one of $S_i,S_j$. If $x$ lies in $c_x$ of the sets, it contributes $c_x(N-c_x)\le N^2/4$ pairs $\{i,j\}$. Hence \[\binom N2 d\le\sum_{i<j}d(S_i,S_j)=\sum_{x=1}^{N}c_x(N-c_x)\le N\cdot\frac{N^2}{4},\qquad\text{so}\qquad d\le\frac{N^2}{2(N-1)}=\frac N2+\frac{N}{2(N-1)} .\] For $n=1$ this gives $d\le2$. For $n\ge2$ we have $N\ge4$, so $0<\frac{N}{2(N-1)}<1$; as $d$ and $\frac N2$ are integers, $d\le \frac N2=2^{n-1}$.

Construction for $n\ge2$. Label the elements of $\{1,\dots,2^n\}$ by the $2^n$ vectors $v\in\{0,1\}^n$, and for each vector $u$ let \[S_u=\{v:\ u\cdot v \text{ is odd}\},\qquad u\cdot v=u_1v_1+\dots+u_nv_n .\] This gives $2^n$ sets. For $u\ne u'$, an element $v$ lies in exactly one of $S_u,S_{u'}$ exactly when $u\cdot v+u'\cdot v$ is odd, that is, when $w\cdot v$ is odd for $w=u+u'\pmod 2$, a nonzero vector. If $w_t=1$, then changing the $t$-th coordinate of $v$ changes the parity of $w\cdot v$; this pairs the vectors $v$ into pairs with one odd and one even value, so exactly $2^{n-1}$ vectors have $w\cdot v$ odd. Thus $d(S_u,S_{u'})=2^{n-1}$ for all $u\ne u'$.

Construction for $n=1$. The sets $\varnothing$ and $\{1,2\}$ give $d=2$.

So the largest value is $\boxed{d=2^{n-1}}$ for $n\ge2$, and $d=2$ for $n=1$.

Show solution 2 – Sign vectors and a doubling construction

Let $N=2^n$. Represent a set $S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ by the vector $\mathbf s\in\{1,-1\}^N$ whose $x$-th entry is $-1$ if $x\in S$ and $1$ otherwise. For two sets, the entries agree on the elements lying in both or in neither, and differ on the elements of $S\triangle T$, so \[\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t=N-2|S\triangle T| .\]

Bound. Let $\mathbf s_1,\dots,\mathbf s_N$ represent the sets and let every symmetric difference have at least $d$ elements. Then \[0\le\Bigl|\sum_i\mathbf s_i\Bigr|^2=\sum_i|\mathbf s_i|^2+2\sum_{i<j}\mathbf s_i\cdot\mathbf s_j\le N\cdot N+N(N-1)(N-2d),\] so $N-2d\ge-\frac{N}{N-1}$, i.e. $d\le\frac N2+\frac{N}{2(N-1)}$. For $n\ge2$ the last fraction is less than $1$, and since $d$ and $N/2$ are integers, $d\le N/2=2^{n-1}$; for $n=1$ we get $d\le2$.

Construction. We build, for every $n\ge1$, a family $\mathcal F_n$ of $2^n$ sign vectors of length $2^n$, with first vector $(1,\dots,1)$, any two of which have dot product $0$ (then any two sets differ in exactly $2^{n-1}$ elements). For $n=1$ take $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. If $\mathcal F_n$ is built, let $\mathcal F_{n+1}$ consist of the vectors $(\mathbf s,\mathbf s)$ and $(\mathbf s,-\mathbf s)$ for $\mathbf s\in\mathcal F_n$ (length $2^{n+1}$, $2^{n+1}$ vectors). The dot products are \[(\mathbf s,\mathbf s)\cdot(\mathbf t,\pm\mathbf t)=\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t\pm\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t,\qquad (\mathbf s,-\mathbf s)\cdot(\mathbf t,-\mathbf t)=2\,\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t,\] which is $0$ when $\mathbf s\ne\mathbf t$; and $(\mathbf s,\mathbf s)\cdot(\mathbf s,-\mathbf s)=0$. So for $n\ge2$ the bound $2^{n-1}$ is attained. For $n=1$ the sets $\varnothing,\{1,2\}$ differ in $2$ elements.

Answer: $\boxed{d=2^{n-1}}$ for $n\ge2$, and $d=2$ for $n=1$.

Mark the equal lengths around $D$ and $E$. Look for a turn about $D$ taking $B$ to $A$ and a turn about $E$ taking $A$ to $C$.

Hint 3: approach

Before matching the angle at $Q$ with the rotation angles, check whether $Q$ lies on the rays $DC$ and $EB$ or on their extensions: the comparison needs both or neither.
Show the solution – Composition of two rotations

Let $d=\angle ADB$ and $e=\angle AEC$, so $d+e=180^\circ$, and label $ABC$ counterclockwise. The point $C$ is to the left of the directed line $AB$ and $D$ to its right, so the triangle $DBA$ is counterclockwise; similarly $B$ is to the right of the directed line $AC$ and $E$ to its left, so $EAC$ is counterclockwise. Hence the rotation $\rho_D$ about $D$ through $d$ counterclockwise maps $B$ to $A$, and the rotation $\rho_E$ about $E$ through $e$ counterclockwise maps $A$ to $C$.

Step 1: the point $F$. The composition $\rho_E\circ\rho_D$ is a rotation through $d+e=180^\circ$, i.e. a half-turn, and it maps $B\mapsto A\mapsto C$; a half-turn taking $B$ to $C$ is the half-turn $\sigma_M$ about the midpoint $M$ of $BC$. Put $F=\rho_D(C)$. Then $DF=DC$, and $\rho_E(F)=\sigma_M(C)=B$, so $EF=EB$. The points $P$ with $DP=DC$ and $EP=EB$ are the common points of two circles centred at $D$ and $E$; they are $F$ and its mirror image $F'$ in the line $DE$.

Step 2: oriented angles. For nonzero vectors $u,v$ let $\angle(u,v)$ be the counterclockwise angle from $u$ to $v$, taken modulo $360^\circ$; we have $\angle(-u,-v)=\angle(u,v)$ and $\angle(u,w)=\angle(u,v)+\angle(v,w)$. By construction $\angle(\vec{DC},\vec{DF})=d$ and $\angle(\vec{EF},\vec{EB})=e$. Therefore \[\angle(\vec{FD},\vec{FE})=\angle(\vec{DF},\vec{EF})=\angle(\vec{DF},\vec{DC})+\angle(\vec{DC},\vec{EB})+\angle(\vec{EB},\vec{EF})=\angle(\vec{DC},\vec{EB})-180^\circ .\]

Step 3: where $Q$ is. The points $Q$, $D$, $E$ are distinct, as vertices of the quadrilateral $PDQE$. Suppose that $Q$ lies on both rays $DC$ and $EB$ (the rays starting at $D$ and at $E$), or on neither of them; this holds for every acute triangle, see below. If $Q$ is on the ray $DC$, the vector $\vec{QD}$ has the direction of $\vec{CD}$, otherwise that of $\vec{DC}$; likewise $\vec{QE}$ has the direction of $\vec{BE}$ or of $\vec{EB}$. By our assumption both directions are reversed or neither is, and since $\angle(-u,-v)=\angle(u,v)$ we get $\angle(\vec{QD},\vec{QE})=\angle(\vec{DC},\vec{EB})$. Comparing with Step 2, \[\angle(\vec{FD},\vec{FE})=\angle(\vec{QD},\vec{QE})+180^\circ .\] For points $Z$ off the line $DE$, the value of $\angle(\vec{ZD},\vec{ZE})$ is constant on each of the two arcs of a circle through $D$ and $E$, and the values on the two arcs differ by $180^\circ$ (on one arc it is the inscribed angle $\varphi$, on the other $-(180^\circ-\varphi)$). Hence $F$ lies on the circle $DQE$, on the arc not containing $Q$; so $F$ and $Q$ are on opposite sides of $DE$, and $FDQE$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral.

Step 4: conclusion. If $P=F$, then $PDQE$ is cyclic. Otherwise $P=F'\ne F$ lies on the same side of $DE$ as $Q$; but in a convex quadrilateral $PDQE$ the diagonal $DE$ separates $P$ from $Q$. So if $PDQE$ is convex, then $P=F$ and $PDQE$ is cyclic.

Why Step 3 holds for an acute triangle. The points $D$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of $AB$, so the segment $DC$ meets the line $AB$ at a point $U$. The orthogonal projection onto $AB$ maps the segment $DC$ onto the segment between the midpoint of $AB$ (the projection of $D$) and the foot of the altitude from $C$; both lie inside the segment $AB$ when the angles at $A$ and $B$ are acute, hence so does $U$ (which is its own projection). In the same way the segment $EB$ meets the side $AC$ at an interior point $V$. The cevians $CU$ and $BV$ meet at a point inside the triangle; this point is $Q$, and it lies on the segments $UC\subset DC$ and $VB\subset EB$, so on both rays $DC$ and $EB$.

Remark: the assumption of Step 3 is needed. If $Q$ lies on exactly one of the rays $DC$, $EB$, then exactly one direction is reversed, and Step 2 gives $\angle(\vec{FD},\vec{FE})=\angle(\vec{QD},\vec{QE})$: the point $F$ is on the circle $DQE$ but on the arc through $Q$, so $FDQE$ is not convex. The only candidate is then $P=F'$, which is on the circle $DQE$ only if $DE$ is a diameter of it. This case occurs for some obtuse triangles, and there $F'DQE$ can be convex without being cyclic: for $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(1.8,\,0.13)$ and $\angle ADB\approx137.5^\circ$ it is. So the statement is proved whenever $Q$ lies on both rays or on neither (in particular for every acute triangle), and it can fail otherwise.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

A positive integer $n$ has the following property: for any representation $n=abc$ as a product of three positive integer, two of $a,b,c$ sum up to a perfect square. Prove that $n$ is squarefree (i.e. there is no perfect square greater than $1$ that divides $n$).

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try the factorization with two factors equal to one. Determine which of its pairwise sums would have to be a square.
Hint 2: key idea
Write $n$ as a difference of squares using the first hint. If an odd prime square divides $n$, it must divide just one of the two factors of that difference. (LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods)
Hint 3: approach
For an odd prime $p$ whose square divides $n$, use the triple $(p,p,n/p^2)$ and compare its sums with nearby consecutive squares, keeping both signs in the expression for the square root of $n+1$. For the prime $2$, write that root as $2r+1$ and try the triple $(1,4,r(r+1))$. If a sum can be a square for an exceptional value of $r$, test a different factorization there.
Common trap
The hypothesis applies to factorizations with equal factors as well as to factorizations with distinct factors.
Show the solution – Squeezing between consecutive squares

Step 1: $n+1$ is a square. Use $n=1\cdot1\cdot n$. The pairwise sums are $2$, $n+1$, $n+1$, and $2$ is not a square, so $n+1=t^2$ for an integer $t\ge2$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $p^2\mid n$ for a prime $p$. We exhibit a factorization $n=abc$ in which no two of $a,b,c$ sum to a square. Recall that there is no square strictly between $q^2$ and $(q+1)^2$.

Step 2: $p$ odd. Since $p^2\mid(t-1)(t+1)$ and $\gcd(t-1,t+1)\mid2$, the odd prime $p$ divides only one of the factors, so $p^2\mid t-1$ or $p^2\mid t+1$: $t=p^2q\pm1$ with $q\ge1$. Then \[n=t^2-1=p^2q(p^2q\pm2),\qquad m:=\frac n{p^2}=p^2q^2\pm2q .\] Take $(a,b,c)=(p,p,m)$. Here $a+b=2p$ is not a square ($p$ odd). For $a+c=b+c=p+m$:

  • sign $+$: $(pq)^2<p^2q^2+2q+p<(pq+1)^2$, because $2q+p<2pq+1\iff p-1<2q(p-1)$;

  • sign $-$: $p+m=p^2q^2-2q+p$. If $p>2q$, it lies strictly between $(pq)^2$ and $(pq)^2+2pq+1$ (as $0<p-2q<2pq+1$); if $p<2q$ it lies strictly between $(pq-1)^2=p^2q^2-2pq+1$ and $(pq)^2$ (as $-2pq+1<p-2q<0$, the left inequality being $1-p<2q(p-1)$); $p=2q$ is impossible since $p$ is odd.

So $p+m$ is never a square, and $(p,p,m)$ violates the property.

Step 3: $p=2$. Then $t$ is odd, $t=2r+1$ with $r\ge1$, and $n=4r(r+1)$. If $r\ne3$, take $(1,4,r(r+1))$: the sums are $5$, $r^2+r+1$ and $r^2+r+4$. The second lies strictly between $r^2$ and $(r+1)^2$. The third lies strictly between $r^2$ and $(r+2)^2$, and it equals $(r+1)^2$ only if $r+4=2r+1$, i.e. $r=3$. So no sum is a square. If $r=3$, then $n=48=3\cdot4\cdot4$ and the sums $7,7,8$ are not squares.

In all cases we found a bad factorization, a contradiction. Hence $n$ is squarefree.

Problem 3

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Given positive integers $n>1$ and $m$. Cells of a $2m\times 2m$ board are filled with positive integers not exceeding $n-1$. We call a rectangular region made of cells kind if the sum of numbers in it is divisible by $n$. For all values of $n$ and $m$, find the maximum possible number of kind rectangles in the board.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Fix a column interval and record the running sums as you add one row at a time. Express the sum of a rectangle with those columns as a difference of two recorded values.
Hint 2: key idea
Group the prefix positions by residue and compare the number of unequal adjacent pairs with the number of all unequal pairs. The largest residue group helps bound both quantities. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Prove the comparison by separating the cases where the largest group contains at least half the positions and where it does not. Apply it first to each row and then to each fixed column interval to bound the number of rectangles with nonzero residue. For sharpness, look for a filling that attains the bound for every column interval at once, and decide which rectangle shapes must then be kind.
Common trap
The prefix at index zero must be included to count rectangles that start at the first row or column.
Show the answer

$m^3(3m+2)$ (for every $n$)

Show the solution – Prefix sums and a counting lemma

Let $N=2m+1$. The board has $\binom N2^2=m^2(2m+1)^2$ rectangles. The answer is $\boxed{m^3(3m+2)}$, for every $n$.

Example. Write $1$ in the cells $(i,j)$ with $i+j$ even and $n-1$ in the others (for $n=2$ all entries are $1$). A rectangle with an even number of cells has an even side, so it splits into dominoes, each with sum $1+(n-1)=n$: it is kind. A rectangle with both sides odd has one more cell of one kind than of the other, so its sum is $\equiv\pm1\not\equiv0\pmod n$. In a line of $2m$ cells there are $2m+(2m-2)+\dots+2=m(m+1)$ intervals of odd length, so the number of kind rectangles is \[m^2(2m+1)^2-m^2(m+1)^2=m^2\bigl((2m+1)^2-(m+1)^2\bigr)=m^3(3m+2).\]

Lemma. Let $x_0,x_1,\dots,x_{2m}$ be residues mod $n$, and let $e$ be the number of $i\in\{1,\dots,2m\}$ with $x_{i-1}\ne x_i$. Then the number $U$ of pairs $i<j$ with $x_i\ne x_j$ is at least $\frac{(m+1)e}{2}$.

Proof. Group the indices by their residue; let the largest group have $s$ indices and put $u=N-s$. Each of the $e$ "unequal neighbours" has an index outside the largest group, and each index belongs to at most two neighbouring pairs, so $e\le 2u$. If $u\le m$, then $s\ge m+1$ and the pairs joining the largest group to the others give $U\ge su\ge(m+1)u\ge\frac{(m+1)e}2$. If $u\ge m+1$, use $U=\frac12\bigl(N^2-\sum s_i^2\bigr)$, where $s_i$ are the group sizes; since $\sum s_i^2\le s\sum s_i=sN$, we get $U\ge\frac{N(N-s)}2=\frac{Nu}{2}\ge\frac{(2m+1)(m+1)}2\ge\frac{(m+1)e}{2}$, as $e\le2m$. $\square$

Bound. Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,2m$. For a column interval $J=\{j_1+1,\dots,j_2\}$ ($0\le j_1<j_2\le2m$) let $x_i$ be the sum of the entries in rows $1,\dots,i$ and columns of $J$ ($x_0=0$). The rectangle with rows $i_1+1,\dots,i_2$ and columns $J$ has sum $x_{i_2}-x_{i_1}$, so it is kind iff $x_{i_1}\equiv x_{i_2}$. Also $x_i-x_{i-1}$ is the sum of the segment of row $i$ over $J$. Let $e_J$ be the number of rows whose segment over $J$ is not kind. By the lemma, at most $\binom N2-\frac{(m+1)e_J}{2}$ kind rectangles have column set $J$, so the total number of kind rectangles is at most \[\binom N2^2-\frac{m+1}{2}\sum_J e_J .\] Now $\sum_J e_J$ counts the pairs (row, non-kind segment of that row). In a fixed row, apply the lemma to the prefix sums $y_0=0,y_1,\dots,y_{2m}$ of the row: all entries lie in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, so consecutive prefix sums differ mod $n$ and $e=2m$; hence at least $m(m+1)$ segments of the row are not kind. Thus $\sum_Je_J\ge 2m\cdot m(m+1)$, and the number of kind rectangles is at most \[m^2(2m+1)^2-\frac{m+1}{2}\cdot2m^2(m+1)=m^2(2m+1)^2-m^2(m+1)^2=m^3(3m+2).\]

Concurrency of lines, or their being all parallel, means that they pass through one point of the projective plane, and this is preserved by projective transformations, as are incidences and tangency. Since $A$ is outside $\omega$, some line through $A$ misses $\omega$; a projective transformation sending this line to the line at infinity and $\omega$ to a circle exists. So we may assume that $\omega$ is the unit circle and $A$ is the point at infinity in the vertical direction. Then:

  • the tangents from $A$ are the vertical tangents $x=1$ and $x=-1$, touching at $F=(1,0)$ and $E=(-1,0)$; the chord $EF$ is the $x$-axis;

  • lines through $A$ are vertical lines.

Let $D=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, $K=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$, $\sigma=\frac{\alpha+\beta}2$, $\delta=\frac{\alpha-\beta}2$. Then $H=(\cos\alpha,0)$ and $L=(\cos\beta,0)$ (vertical lines through $D$ and $K$). The line $DK$ is $x\cos\sigma+y\sin\sigma=\cos\delta$ (both points satisfy it, as $\cos(\alpha-\sigma)=\cos(\beta-\sigma)=\cos\delta$).

If $\sin\sigma=0$, then $DK$ is vertical, i.e. it passes through $A$ in the original figure; then $M=N=A$, $H=L$, and the four lines are $AE$, $AF$ and twice the line $DK$, all through $A$. So let $\sin\sigma\ne0$; then $\cos\alpha\ne\cos\beta$. Now \[M=\Bigl(1,\ m\Bigr),\quad N=\Bigl(-1,\ n\Bigr),\qquad m=\frac{\cos\delta-\cos\sigma}{\sin\sigma},\quad n=\frac{\cos\delta+\cos\sigma}{\sin\sigma}.\]

Lines $ME$ and $FN$: $y=\frac m2(x+1)$ and $y=\frac n2(1-x)$. Since $m+n=\frac{2\cos\delta}{\sin\sigma}$, if $\cos\delta\ne0$ they meet at \[x=\frac{n-m}{n+m}=\frac{\cos\sigma}{\cos\delta},\qquad y=\frac{mn}{m+n}=\frac{\cos^2\delta-\cos^2\sigma}{2\sin\sigma\cos\delta}.\]

Lines $DL$ and $KH$: $y=\frac{\sin\alpha\,(x-\cos\beta)}{\cos\alpha-\cos\beta}$ and $y=\frac{\sin\beta\,(x-\cos\alpha)}{\cos\beta-\cos\alpha}$. Equating, $x(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta)$, and then $y=\frac{\sin\alpha\sin\beta}{\sin\alpha+\sin\beta}$. With $\sin\alpha+\sin\beta=2\sin\sigma\cos\delta\ne0$, $\sin(\alpha+\beta)=2\sin\sigma\cos\sigma$ and $\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\frac{\cos2\delta-\cos2\sigma}2=\cos^2\delta-\cos^2\sigma$, \[x=\frac{\cos\sigma}{\cos\delta},\qquad y=\frac{\cos^2\delta-\cos^2\sigma}{2\sin\sigma\cos\delta}.\] This is the same point, so the four lines are concurrent.

The case $\cos\delta=0$, i.e. $K=-D$. Then $m=-n=-\cot\sigma=\tan\alpha$ (as $\sigma=\alpha+90^\circ$ up to a multiple of $180^\circ$). The four lines have directions $\vec{EM}=(2,\tan\alpha)$, $\vec{NF}=(2,-n)=(2,\tan\alpha)$, $\vec{LD}=(2\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and $\vec{KH}=(2\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, all proportional ($\cos\alpha\ne0$ because $\sin\sigma\ne0$). So the four lines are parallel.

Going back by the projective transformation, the four lines of the original figure have a common point or are parallel.

Show solution 2 – Homogeneous coordinates on the conic

We work in the real projective plane. Four lines have a common point there exactly when they are concurrent or all parallel (a common point at infinity). Projective transformations preserve lines, incidence, conics and tangency, so it suffices to prove that the four lines have a common projective point, and we may choose homogeneous coordinates $(x:y:z)$ freely.

Naming. If $D$ or $K$ equals $E$, exchange the names $E$ and $F$. This exchanges $M$ and $N$ and turns the four lines $ME,DL,KH,FN$ into $NF,DL,KH,EM$: the same four lines. Since the chords $DK$ and $EF$ are different, after this neither $D$ nor $K$ is $E$.

Coordinates. The points $E$, $A$, $F$ are not collinear ($AE$ and $AF$ are different tangents). Choose coordinates with \[E=(1:0:0),\qquad A=(0:1:0),\qquad F=(0:0:1).\] Then $AE$ is the line $z=0$, $AF$ is $x=0$ and $EF$ is $y=0$. Write $\omega$ as $q(x,y,z)=\alpha x^2+\beta y^2+\gamma z^2+2pxy+2syz+2rxz=0$. Since $E,F\in\omega$, $\alpha=\gamma=0$. The tangent at a point $(x_0:y_0:z_0)$ of $\omega$ is the line with coefficients $\bigl(\frac{\partial q}{\partial x},\frac{\partial q}{\partial y},\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}\bigr)$ taken at that point; at $E$ these are proportional to $(0,p,r)$, and this line must be $z=0$, so $p=0$; at $F$ they are proportional to $(r,s,0)$, and this line must be $x=0$, so $s=0$. Thus $\omega$ is $\beta y^2+2rxz=0$, where $\beta\ne0$ and $r\ne0$ because $\omega$ is not a pair of lines. Replacing $x$ by $-x$ if necessary and then $y$ by a suitable multiple of $y$, we get \[\omega:\ xz=y^2.\] Every point of $\omega$ other than $E$ has $z\neq0$, so it can be written $(e^2:e:1)$ for a unique real $e$.

Since neither $D$ nor $K$ is $E$, \[D=(d^2:d:1),\qquad K=(k^2:k:1),\qquad d\ne k.\]

The points. The line $DK$ is $x-(d+k)y+dkz=0$ (both points satisfy it, e.g. $d^2-(d+k)d+dk=0$). Hence \[M=DK\cap AF=(0:dk:d+k),\qquad N=DK\cap AE=(d+k:1:0);\] $M$ is a genuine point because $dk=d+k=0$ would force $d=k=0$. The line $AD$ consists of the points with $x=d^2z$, so it meets $EF$ ($y=0$) at $H=(d^2:0:1)$; likewise $L=(k^2:0:1)$.

The case $dk\ne0$. Put \[T=\bigl((d+k)dk:\ dk:\ d+k\bigr),\] a genuine point since $dk\ne0$. In coordinates $T=(d+k)dk\cdot E+M=dk\cdot N+(d+k)\cdot F$, so $T$ lies on the line $ME$ and on the line $FN$ (note $M\ne E$ and $N\ne F$). The line $DL$ is \[d\,x-(d^2-k^2)\,y-dk^2\,z=0:\] at $D$ the left side is $d^3-(d^2-k^2)d-dk^2=0$, at $L$ it is $dk^2-dk^2=0$, and its coefficients are not all zero since $d\ne0$. At $T$ it equals \[d(d+k)dk-(d^2-k^2)dk-dk^2(d+k)=dk(d+k)\bigl(d-(d-k)-k\bigr)=0.\] Exchanging the roles of $d$ and $k$ (which exchanges $D,H$ with $K,L$ and fixes $T$) shows that $T$ also lies on $KH$. So the four lines pass through $T$.

The case $dk=0$. Say $d=0$ (the case $k=0$ is the same with $D,K$ exchanged); then $k\ne0$, $D=F$, $M=(0:0:k)=F$ and $H=(0:0:1)=F$. The four lines are $ME=FE$, $DL=FL$, $KH=KF$ and $FN$, and all of them pass through $F$.

In every case the four lines have a common point of the projective plane; returning to the original figure, they have a common point or are parallel.

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes · Recursive Reduction of Solutions

Find the largest positive integer $n$ for which there exist positive integers $m$ and $k$ such that the number \[\frac{n^{2k-1}m^2-2m}{n+2}\] is a perfect square.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Name the integer square root of the fraction and clear the denominator. Look for a way to complete the square in a multiple of $m$.
Hint 2: key idea
Multiply by $n^{2k-1}$ to obtain a Pell equation with coefficient $(n+1)^2-1$. The solution associated with $n+1$ gives a descent that preserves the first coordinate modulo $n$. (LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes)
Hint 3: approach
For a positive second coordinate $Z$, use $X'=(n+1)X-n(n+2)Z$ and $Z'=(n+1)Z-X$ and check that they solve the same equation. Verify that the second coordinate decreases to a nonnegative integer and track the first coordinate's residue down to the base case. Compare that residue with the original expression for $X$ and test the largest remaining candidate directly.
Common trap
A Pell descent must keep the new coordinates in the required nonnegative range.
Show the answer

$2$

Show solution 1 – Pell equation and descent

$n=2$ works: $m=2$, $k=1$ give $\frac{2\cdot4-4}{4}=1=1^2$.

Now let $n,m,k$ be positive integers and $\frac{n^{2k-1}m^2-2m}{n+2}=s^2$ with $s\ge0$ an integer. Put $N=n^{2k-1}$. Multiplying by $N$, \[N^2m^2-2Nm=N(n+2)s^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (Nm-1)^2-n(n+2)\bigl(n^{k-1}s\bigr)^2=1 .\] So $X=Nm-1$ and $Z=n^{k-1}s$ are non-negative integers with $X^2-DZ^2=1$, where $D=n(n+2)=a^2-1$, $a=n+1$.

Lemma. Every solution of $X^2-DZ^2=1$ in non-negative integers has $X\equiv1\pmod n$.

Proof. Note $X\ge1$. If $Z=0$ then $X=1$. If $Z\ge1$, put $X'=aX-DZ$, $Z'=aZ-X$. Using $a^2-D=1$, \[X'^2-DZ'^2=(a^2-D)(X^2-DZ^2)=1,\] and

  • $X'>0$, since $(aX)^2-(DZ)^2=a^2X^2-D(X^2-1)=X^2+D>0$;

  • $Z'\ge0$, since $(aZ)^2-X^2=a^2Z^2-DZ^2-1=Z^2-1\ge0$;

  • $Z'<Z$, i.e. $X>(a-1)Z$, since $X^2=(a^2-1)Z^2+1>(a-1)^2Z^2$;

  • $X'\equiv X\pmod n$, since $a\equiv1$ and $D\equiv0\pmod n$.

So $(X',Z')$ is a solution of the same kind with smaller $Z$ and the same residue of $X$ mod $n$. Repeating, we reach $Z=0$, $X=1$; hence $X\equiv1\pmod n$. $\square$

Apply the lemma: $Nm-1\equiv1\pmod n$. Since $2k-1\ge1$, $n\mid N$, so $-1\equiv1$, i.e. $n\mid2$. Hence $n\le2$, and the largest $n$ is $\boxed{2}$.

Show solution 2 – All solutions of the Pell equation

$n=2$ works: $m=2$, $k=1$ give the square $\frac{2\cdot4-4}{4}=1$.

Suppose $\frac{n^{2k-1}m^2-2m}{n+2}=s^2$ with an integer $s\ge0$, and let $N=n^{2k-1}$, $a=n+1$, $D=a^2-1=n(n+2)$. Multiplying by $N$ and completing the square, \[(Nm-1)^2-D\,(n^{k-1}s)^2=1 ,\] so $(X,Z)=(Nm-1,\,n^{k-1}s)$ solves the Pell equation $X^2-DZ^2=1$ with $X\ge 1$, $Z\ge0$. As $D$ lies strictly between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$, it is not a square, and the smallest solution with $Z\ge1$ is $(a,1)$ (for $Z=1$, $X^2=a^2$). By the theory of the Pell equation, all solutions with $X,Z\ge0$ are given by $X_j+Z_j\sqrt D=(a+\sqrt D)^j$, $j\ge0$. From $(a+\sqrt D)^{j+1}+(a+\sqrt D)^{j-1}=2a(a+\sqrt D)^j$ (as $(a+\sqrt D)^2=2a(a+\sqrt D)-1$) we get \[X_0=1,\quad X_1=a,\quad X_{j+1}=2aX_j-X_{j-1}.\] Modulo $n$, $a\equiv1$, so $X_{j+1}\equiv2X_j-X_{j-1}$ with $X_0\equiv X_1\equiv1$; by induction $X_j\equiv1\pmod n$ for all $j$.

On the other hand $X=Nm-1\equiv-1\pmod n$ because $n\mid N$. Thus $1\equiv-1\pmod n$, so $n\le2$. The largest $n$ is $\boxed{2}$.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Sequences with Arithmetic Constraints

An infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ of positive integers is such that for all positive integers $n>2026$ the following equality holds \[\left\{\frac{a_1}{a_n}\right\}+\left\{\frac{a_2}{a_n}\right\}+\dots+\left\{\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n}\right\}=2,\] where $\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the fractional part of $x$. Let $b_k$ be the number of times $a_k$ appears in $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$. Prove that there exists a constant $M$ such that for any positive integer $k$ the following inequality holds \[\frac{1}{a_1\cdot 2^{b_1}}+\frac{1}{a_2\cdot 2^{b_2}}+\dots+\frac{1}{a_k\cdot 2^{b_k}}<M.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Follow the successive occurrences of one value and record its weights in the target sum. Separately, write a fractional part in the hypothesis using an ordinary remainder.
Hint 2: key idea
Sum the weights of repeated occurrences geometrically, leaving a reciprocal sum over distinct values. To control how many distinct values lie between $X$ and $2X$, apply the remainder equation at the one whose first occurrence is latest. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Split the values of this interval into those below and above the last new value. Bound the contributions of the smaller ones individually, and bound the sum from the larger ones using the distinct positive differences they contribute. Apply the resulting count bound on successive dyadic intervals to estimate the reciprocal sum by a convergent series.
Common trap
Start the interval argument above every value in the first $2026$ positions so that the remainder equation applies at the chosen first occurrence.
Show the solution – Few distinct values in each dyadic window

Write $r(x,z)$ for the remainder of $x$ modulo $z$; then $\{x/z\}=r(x,z)/z$, and the condition reads \[\sum_{i<n} r(a_i,a_n)=2a_n\qquad(n>2026). \tag{1}\]

Reduction. Let $V$ be the set of values of the sequence. If a value $v$ occurs at indices $k_1<k_2<\dots$, then $b_{k_j}=j$. Hence for every $K$, \[\sum_{k\le K}\frac{1}{a_k2^{b_k}}\le\sum_{v\in V}\frac1v\Bigl(\frac12+\frac14+\cdots\Bigr)\le\sum_{v\in V}\frac1v ,\] and it suffices to show that $\sum_{v\in V}\frac1v$ is finite; then $M=1+\sum_{v\in V}\frac1v$ works.

Lemma. Let $X_0=1+\max(a_1,\dots,a_{2026})$. For every $X\ge X_0$, at most $4+\sqrt{8X}$ values of $V$ lie in $[X,2X)$.

Proof. Let $W=V\cap[X,2X)$, assume it is non-empty, and let $z\in W$ be the value whose first occurrence, at index $n$, comes last among the first occurrences of the elements of $W$. As $z\ge X_0$, $n>2026$, and every other element of $W$ occurs among $a_1,\dots,a_{n-1}$. In (1) all terms are non-negative, so the elements of $W$ alone contribute at most $2z$:

  • an element $y\in W$ with $y<z$ contributes $r(y,z)=y\ge X>z/2$;

  • the elements $w\in W$ with $w>z$ satisfy $z<w<2X\le2z$, so they contribute $w-z$; these are distinct positive integers, so if there are $q$ of them they contribute at least $1+2+\dots+q=\frac{q(q+1)}2$.

If $p$ elements of $W$ are smaller than $z$, then $p\cdot\frac z2+\frac{q(q+1)}2\le 2z$ with strict inequality when $p\ge1$. Hence $p\le3$, and $q^2<q(q+1)\le4z<8X$, so $q<\sqrt{8X}$. Therefore $|W|=1+p+q<4+\sqrt{8X}$. $\square$

Conclusion. Split $[X_0,\infty)$ into the windows $[2^jX_0,2^{j+1}X_0)$, $j\ge0$. Each value $v$ in the $j$-th window has $\frac1v\le\frac1{2^jX_0}$, so \[\sum_{v\in V,\ v\ge X_0}\frac1v\le\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{4+\sqrt{8\cdot2^jX_0}}{2^jX_0}=\frac{8}{X_0}+\sqrt{\frac{8}{X_0}}\sum_{j\ge0}\Bigl(\frac1{\sqrt2}\Bigr)^j<\infty .\] Only finitely many values are smaller than $X_0$. So $\sum_{v\in V}\frac1v<\infty$, and the required constant $M$ exists.

Problem 1

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Extremal Graph Structure

Given a positive integer $n$ and a $n\times n$ board each cell of which is either dark or contains a lantern, which is directed towards one of the board's sides. If a lantern is directed towards board's side $B$, then it illuminates the cell $c$ in which it is located and all cells on the shortest path from $c$ to $B$. Find the minimal value of $k$ such that for any arrangement of $k$ lanterns in a table, each of them can be directed such that every cell of the board is illuminated.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick a dark cell and mark every cell from which a lantern could illuminate it. How could an arrangement with many lanterns still leave some cell unlit?
Hint 2: key idea
To guarantee illumination, try assigning a distinct lantern to each dark cell. Apply Hall's theorem using the rows and columns through any set of dark cells to describe its possible lanterns. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Use such an arrangement to set a candidate for the threshold. For a group of dark cells occupying $r$ rows and $c$ columns, count the union of those rows and columns, then subtract the maximum possible number of dark cells. Compare the remaining capacity with the group size by splitting according to whether $rc$ exceeds the dark-cell budget.
Common trap
For Hall's condition, count each lantern only once even if it can reach several cells in the chosen group.
Show the answer

$k=(n-1)^2+1=n^2-2n+2$

Show solution 1 – Hall's marriage theorem

The answer is $\boxed{k=(n-1)^2+1}$.

$(n-1)^2$ lanterns are not enough. Put $(n-1)^2$ lanterns in the cells of the first $n-1$ rows and first $n-1$ columns. The corner cell in row $n$ and column $n$ is dark, and only a lantern in its row or column can illuminate it; there is none. (For $k<(n-1)^2$ lanterns, remove some lanterns from this arrangement.)

$(n-1)^2+1$ lanterns are enough. For $n=1$ the single cell holds the lantern. Let $n\ge2$; then at most $n^2-(n-1)^2-1=2n-2$ cells are dark. A dark cell $c$ is illuminated if some lantern in its row or column is directed towards $c$ (it then lights all cells from itself to the border, including $c$); lantern cells are always illuminated. So it suffices to assign to every dark cell its own lantern in the same row or column (different dark cells getting different lanterns) and direct each assigned lantern towards its dark cell. By Hall's theorem such an assignment exists if every set $S$ of dark cells has at least $|S|$ lanterns in the union of the rows and columns that meet $S$.

Let $S$ meet $r$ rows and $c$ columns ($1\le r,c\le n$). Their union has $rn+cn-rc$ cells, of which at most $2n-2$ are dark; also $|S|\le\min(rc,\,2n-2)$. So it is enough to prove \[rn+cn-rc\ \ge\ (2n-2)+\min(rc,\,2n-2). \tag{1}\]

  • If $rc\le2n-2$: (1) reads $n(r+c-2)\ge 2rc-2$. Since $2n\ge rc+2$ and $r+c\ge2$, it suffices that $(rc+2)(r+c-2)\ge4rc-4$. For $r=1$ this is $(c+2)(c-1)-4c+4=(c-1)(c-2)\ge0$; for $r=2$ it is $2c^2-6c+4=2(c-1)(c-2)\ge0$; by symmetry the same holds for $c\le2$; and for $r,c\ge3$, $(rc+2)(r+c-2)\ge rc(r+c-2)\ge 4rc$.

  • If $rc>2n-2$: then $r,c\ge2$ (if $r=1$ then $c>2n-2\ge n$, impossible). (1) reads $rn+cn-rc\ge4n-4$, i.e. $(n-r)(n-c)\le(n-2)^2$, true since $n-r,n-c\le n-2$.

So Hall's condition holds, the assignment exists, and every arrangement of $(n-1)^2+1$ lanterns can be directed to illuminate the whole board. (Adding lanterns only helps: extra lanterns can be directed arbitrarily.)

Show solution 2 – Leftmost and rightmost lanterns

The answer is $\boxed{k=(n-1)^2+1}$.

$(n-1)^2$ lanterns are not enough. Put $(n-1)^2$ lanterns in the cells of the first $n-1$ rows and first $n-1$ columns. The cell in row $n$ and column $n$ is dark, and only a lantern in its row or column could illuminate it; there is none. (For fewer lanterns, remove some from this arrangement.)

$(n-1)^2+1$ lanterns are enough. Then at most $2n-2$ cells are dark; we show that any such arrangement can be directed to light the whole board. A lantern directed towards a side lights its own cell and every cell of its row or column between it and that side. Two simple observations:

  • (a) If a row contains at least two lanterns, direct its leftmost lantern to the right and its rightmost lantern to the left: together they light the whole row. The same holds for columns.

  • (b) If each dark cell can be given its own lantern in the same row or column (different dark cells getting different lanterns), directing each such lantern towards its dark cell lights every dark cell, hence the whole board.

Step 1. If every row has at least two lanterns, (a) applied to all rows finishes; the same if every column has at least two lanterns. From now on there is a row $r$ and a column $c$ each containing at most one lantern. Then each of them has at least $n-1$ dark cells, and they share only the cell $(r,c)$, so $r\cup c$ contains at least $2n-3$ dark cells. Hence the core, the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ set of cells outside row $r$ and column $c$, contains at most one dark cell, which we call the hole. For $n=1$ the single lantern lights the board, so let $n\ge2$.

Step 2: $n\ge5$, or $n=4$ and the core has no hole. Number the core rows $i_1,\dots,i_{n-1}$ and the core columns $j_1,\dots,j_{n-1}$, and consider the cells $(i_s,j_s)$. If one of them is the hole, say $(i_1,j_1)$, replace $(i_1,j_1),(i_2,j_2)$ by $(i_1,j_2),(i_2,j_1)$, which are not the hole. We get $n-1$ lanterns, one in each core row and one in each core column; direct each of them vertically towards row $r$, so that it lights the cell of row $r$ in its column. In each core row at least $(n-1)-1-1=n-3\ge2$ lanterns are still unused if $n\ge5$, and $n-2\ge2$ if there is no hole; by (a) applied to the unused ones, every core row is lit entirely (including its cell in column $c$). It remains to light row $r$; its cells outside column $c$ are already lit. If $(r,c)$ is dark, then $r\cup c$ contains a lantern (otherwise its $2n-1$ cells would all be dark, more than $2n-2$); that lantern is outside the core and unused, so we direct it towards $(r,c)$.

Step 3: $n=4$ and the core has a hole. Then $r\cup c$ contains at most $2n-2-1=5=2n-3$ dark cells, hence exactly $5$, and exactly $2$ of its $7$ cells are lanterns. If $(r,c)$ were a lantern, the other lantern would give row $r$ or column $c$ two lanterns; so $(r,c)$ is dark, row $r$ has exactly one lantern, at $(r,j_0)$, and column $c$ exactly one, at $(i_0,c)$. Direct $(i_0,c)$ towards row $r$; it lights $(r,c)$. The two core rows without the hole are full of lanterns in the core; match them with the two core columns other than $j_0$, and direct these two lanterns towards row $r$. Now row $r$ is lit. Each full core row has $3-1=2$ unused core lanterns, and the core row with the hole has its $2$ core lanterns unused; by (a), every core row is lit. So the whole board is lit.

Step 4: $n=2$. There are $2$ lanterns. If they are in the same row, direct both vertically; each lights its whole column. Otherwise they are in different rows; direct each horizontally towards the other cell of its row, which lights its whole row.

Step 5: $n=3$. There are at most $4$ dark cells; we use (b). By Step 1, $r\cup c$ (five cells) has at least $3$ dark cells, so it has $3$ or $4$. Let $i_1,i_2$ be the core rows and $j_1,j_2$ the core columns.

  • $r\cup c$ has $4$ dark cells and one lantern $\ell$; then the core is full. Give $(r,j_1)$ the lantern $(i_1,j_1)$, $(r,j_2)$ the lantern $(i_2,j_2)$, $(i_1,c)$ the lantern $(i_1,j_2)$, $(i_2,c)$ the lantern $(i_2,j_1)$, and $(r,c)$, if it is dark, the lantern $\ell$ (which lies in row $r$ or column $c$). Unneeded assignments of lantern cells are simply dropped.

  • $r\cup c$ has $3$ dark cells and two lanterns. As in Step 3, $(r,c)$ is dark and the lanterns are $(r,j_0)$ and $(i_0,c)$ with $j_0\in\{j_1,j_2\}$, $i_0\in\{i_1,i_2\}$; let $j'$ be the other core column and $i'$ the other core row. The dark cells are $(r,c)$, $(r,j')$, $(i',c)$ and possibly one hole $h$ in the core. Assign:

    • no hole, $h=(i_0,j_0)$ or $h=(i',j_0)$: $(r,c)\leftarrow(i_0,c)$, $(r,j')\leftarrow(i_0,j')$, $(i',c)\leftarrow(i',j')$ if $h=(i',j_0)$ and $(i',c)\leftarrow(i',j_0)$ otherwise, and (if there is a hole) $h\leftarrow(r,j_0)$;

    • $h=(i_0,j')$: $(r,c)\leftarrow(i_0,c)$, $(r,j')\leftarrow(i',j')$, $(i',c)\leftarrow(i',j_0)$, $h\leftarrow(i_0,j_0)$;

    • $h=(i',j')$: $(r,c)\leftarrow(r,j_0)$, $(r,j')\leftarrow(i_0,j')$, $(i',c)\leftarrow(i_0,c)$, $h\leftarrow(i',j_0)$.

    In each line the lanterns are distinct and each lies in the row or column of its dark cell.

By (b) the board is lit.

So $(n-1)^2+1$ lanterns always suffice (with more lanterns there are even fewer dark cells), and $(n-1)^2$ do not.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Explicit Bijections

Does there exist a polynomial $P(x,y,z)$ with rational coefficients such that for any positive integer $n$ there exists a unique triple of positive integers $(x,y,z)$ such that $P(x,y,z)=n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write out the first few pairs of positive integers in order of their coordinate sum. Choose an order for the pairs that have the same sum.
Hint 2: key idea
Assign each pair its position in this list. The count of pairs on all earlier diagonals is a triangular number, which gives a polynomial expression for the position. (LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence)
Hint 3: approach
Add the earlier-diagonal count to the position within the pair's own diagonal and check that each positive index is used exactly once. Then ask how a polynomial bijection for pairs could help with triples.
Common trap
The pair's index must start at one so it can be used again as a positive integer input.
Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – Composing a pairing polynomial

Yes. First we pair positive integers. For $x,y\ge1$ put $s=x+y$ and \[f(x,y)=\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}2+y=\frac{(x+y-1)(x+y-2)}2+y .\] The pairs with sum $t$ ($t\ge2$) are $(t-1,1),\dots,(1,t-1)$, i.e. $t-1$ pairs, and there are $\sum_{t=2}^{s-1}(t-1)=\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}2$ pairs with sum less than $s$. List all pairs by increasing sum, and pairs with equal sum by increasing $y$; then $f(x,y)$ is exactly the position of $(x,y)$ in this list. Hence $f$ is a bijection from pairs of positive integers onto the positive integers.

Now let \[P(x,y,z)=f\bigl(f(x,y),\,z\bigr),\] a polynomial in $x,y,z$ with rational coefficients. For any positive integer $n$ there is exactly one pair $(u,z)$ with $f(u,z)=n$, and exactly one pair $(x,y)$ with $f(x,y)=u$; so exactly one triple of positive integers gives $P(x,y,z)=n$.

Show solution 2 – Tetrahedral numbering of triples

Yes. For positive integers $x,y,z$ let $s=x+y+z$ and $u=y+z$, and put \[P(x,y,z)=\binom{s-1}{3}+\binom{u-1}{2}+z ,\] a polynomial with rational coefficients (e.g. $\binom{s-1}3=\frac{(s-1)(s-2)(s-3)}6$).

List all triples of positive integers ordered by $s$, then by $u$, then by $z$. There are $\binom{t-1}{2}$ triples with sum $t$ (stars and bars), so $\sum_{t=3}^{s-1}\binom{t-1}2=\binom{s-1}3$ triples come before all triples with sum $s$ (hockey-stick identity). Among the triples with sum $s$, those with $y+z=w$ number $w-1$ (choices of $z$), so $\sum_{w=2}^{u-1}(w-1)=\binom{u-1}2$ of them have $y+z<u$. Finally, among the triples with given $s$ and $u$, the one with third coordinate $z$ is the $z$-th. Hence $P(x,y,z)$ is the position of $(x,y,z)$ in the list, and every positive integer is the position of exactly one triple.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Euler Totient and Related Functions

Given positive integers $a$ and $b$.

  • a) Prove that the following limit exists: \[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi(ak)}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi(bk)}\]

  • b) Find it.

(Here $\phi(n)$ denotes the Euler totient function.)

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a small fixed $a$, write the summands $\phi(ak)$ using the prime factors of $ak$. Look for the contribution of primes dividing $a$ in every term.
Hint 2: key idea
Expand $\phi(ak)$ with the Möbius divisor formula and swap the order of summation. For each divisor index $d$, use $\gcd(a,d)$ to rewrite the condition $d\mid ak$ as a divisibility condition on $k$ alone. (LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Sum the allowed multiples of $d/\gcd(a,d)$ using a triangular sum, separating its quadratic term from the floor error. Bound the total error and justify extending the main coefficient to an absolutely convergent series. Express that series as an Euler product and compare the prime factors for the two parameters.
Common trap
A floor error for one divisor index still has to be summed over all contributing indices.
Show the answer

$\displaystyle\lim=\frac{a\prod_{p\mid a}\frac{p}{p+1}}{b\prod_{p\mid b}\frac{p}{p+1}}$ ($p$ prime)

Show the solution – An asymptotic formula through Moebius inversion

We show that for every positive integer $a$ \[\Phi_a(n):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varphi(ak)=\frac{a\,c_a}{2}\,n^2+O(n\log n),\qquad c_a=\prod_{p\mid a}\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)\prod_{p\nmid a}\Bigl(1-\frac1{p^2}\Bigr)>0 , \tag{1}\] where the products run over primes. Then the limit exists (part a) and equals $\frac{a c_a}{b c_b}$; since $\prod_{p\nmid a}(1-p^{-2})=\prod_{p}(1-p^{-2})\big/\prod_{p\mid a}(1-p^{-2})$ and $\frac{1-1/p}{1-1/p^2}=\frac{p}{p+1}$, \[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi_a(n)}{\Phi_b(n)}=\boxed{\frac{a\prod_{p\mid a}\frac{p}{p+1}}{b\prod_{p\mid b}\frac{p}{p+1}}}\] (part b).

Proof of (1). Use $\varphi(N)=\sum_{d\mid N}\mu(d)\frac Nd$ ($\mu$ the Möbius function). Then \[\Phi_a(n)=\sum_{k\le n}\sum_{d\mid ak}\mu(d)\frac{ak}{d}=a\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\sum_{\substack{k\le n\\ d\mid ak}}k .\] For a given $d$ let $g=\gcd(a,d)$ and $d'=d/g$. Since $\gcd(d',a/g)=1$, $d\mid ak\iff d'\mid k$. Writing $k=d'j$, the inner sum is $d'\,T(\lfloor n/d'\rfloor)$ with $T(q)=\frac{q(q+1)}2$; it is empty unless $d'\le n$, which needs $d\le an$. For $x\ge0$ we have $\frac{x^2}2-\frac x2<T(\lfloor x\rfloor)\le\frac{x^2}2+\frac x2$, so $d'T(\lfloor n/d'\rfloor)=\frac{n^2}{2d'}+E_d$ with $|E_d|\le\frac n2$. Hence \[\Phi_a(n)=\frac{a n^2}{2}\sum_{d\le an}\frac{\mu(d)}{d\,d'}+R,\qquad |R|\le a\sum_{d\le an}\frac{n}{2d}\le an\,(1+\ln(an)) .\] Since $dd'=d^2/g\ge d^2/a$, the series $\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{dd'}=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)\gcd(a,d)}{d^2}$ converges absolutely, and its tail beyond $an$ is at most $a\sum_{d>an}\frac1{d^2}\le\frac{a}{an}=\frac1n$, which changes $\Phi_a(n)$ by at most $\frac{an}{2}$. The function $h(d)=\frac{\mu(d)\gcd(a,d)}{d^2}$ is multiplicative, vanishes on non-squarefree $d$, and $h(p)=-\frac1p$ if $p\mid a$, $h(p)=-\frac1{p^2}$ otherwise; by the Euler product for absolutely convergent series of multiplicative functions, \[\sum_{d\ge1}h(d)=\prod_p\bigl(1+h(p)\bigr)=c_a ,\] and $c_a>0$ because $\prod_p(1-p^{-2})$ converges to a positive number and the finitely many factors $1-\frac1p$ are positive. This proves (1).
