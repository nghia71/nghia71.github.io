Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $k\geqslant 2$ and $n$ be positive integers. Find the largest integer $m$ for which there exists a grid with $m$ rows and $n$ columns satisfying the following conditions:

  • Each cell contains a non-negative integer;

  • The sum of numbers in each row is at most $kn$;

  • The numbers in each column are pairwise distinct.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a single column of height $m$, write down the smallest possible entries from top to bottom after sorting. Compare that constraint with what the problem says about the total in a row.
Hint 2: key idea
Bound the sum of all entries below using the columns and above using the rows. For a sharp example, try arranging columns as permutations of the smallest possible entries, with blocks of columns designed to have equal row sums. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Treat one column separately, and for two columns use the two orderings of the smallest possible entries so the row sums are constant. For an odd number of columns, design one block of three columns with constant row sums, letting the required common sum determine the third column. Check that each column of the block uses the needed entries exactly once.
Common trap
Reversed pairs alone do not handle an odd number of columns.
Show the answer

$m=2k+1$ for $n\ge2$; $m=k+1$ for $n=1$

Show the solution – Column sums against row sums

Upper bound. The $m$ numbers in a column are distinct non-negative integers, so the column sum is at least $0+1+\dots+(m-1)=\frac{m(m-1)}2$. Summing over the columns and comparing with the rows, \[n\cdot\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\le(\text{sum of all entries})\le m\cdot kn,\] so $m-1\le 2k$, i.e. $m\le 2k+1$. If $n=1$, every entry is at most $k\cdot1=k$, so the single column holds distinct numbers from $\{0,\dots,k\}$ and $m\le k+1$; the column $0,1,\dots,k$ shows that $m=k+1$ is attained.

Construction for $n\ge2$, $m=2k+1$. Number the rows $0,1,\dots,2k$. Every column will be a permutation of $\{0,1,\dots,2k\}$.

  • A pair of columns: $x_i=i$ and $x_i'=2k-i$ in row $i$. Each row of the pair sums to $2k$.

  • A triple of columns $(a_i,b_i,c_i)$: $a_i=i$; for $0\le i\le k$ put $b_i=2k-2i$, $c_i=k+i$; for $i=k+j$ with $1\le j\le k$ put $b_i=2k+1-2j$, $c_i=j-1$. The $b$'s run over all even and all odd numbers in $\{0,\dots,2k\}$ once, the $c$'s over $\{k,\dots,2k\}$ and $\{0,\dots,k-1\}$, and every row sums to $i+(2k-2i)+(k+i)=3k$ or $(k+j)+(2k+1-2j)+(j-1)=3k$.

For even $n$ use $n/2$ pairs; for odd $n\ge3$ use one triple and $(n-3)/2$ pairs. Every row then sums to exactly $kn$, and the columns have distinct entries. Hence the answer is $\boxed{m=2k+1}$ for $n\ge 2$ (and $m=k+1$ for $n=1$).

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Metric Geometry via Trigonometric Reformulation

Points $B,A,C,D,E$ lie on a circle in that order such that \[AB\times CD=AC\times BD\quad\text{and}\quad AB+CE=AC+BE.\] Show that the line through $E$ parallel to $BC$ and the line through $D$ perpendicular to $AD$ intersect on the angle bisector of $\angle BAC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the midpoint $M$ of the arc $BC$ that avoids $A$. Draw the line through $E$ parallel to $BC$ and identify the segment on which its intersection with the angle bisector is expected to lie.
Hint 2: key idea
Put $M$ at the bottom of the unit circle, take the angular coordinates of $B,C$ to be opposites, and express chord lengths using sines of half-differences. Turn the product condition into a comparison of chord ratios, and the sum condition into a comparison of chord differences. (LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion)
Hint 3: approach
Use sum-to-product formulas to extract the half-angle relations for $D$ and $E$, then use the height of $E$ to find the division ratio on $MA$. The perpendicular through $D$ passes through the antipode of $A$, so verify where that chord crosses $MA$ and obtain its division ratio using similar triangles. Substitute the half-angle relations into the two ratios to set up a comparison.
Common trap
Chord lengths are positive even when the signed angular coordinates of $D$ and $E$ are negative.
Show the solution – Arc coordinates and the intersecting-chord ratio

Scale so that the circle is the unit circle centred at $O$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$; it lies on the bisector of $\angle BAC$. Put $M=(0,-1)$ and write $P(\varphi)=(\sin\varphi,-\cos\varphi)$, so that $P(0)=M$ and $|P(\varphi)P(\psi)|=2\left|\sin\frac{\varphi-\psi}2\right|$. We may take $B=P(-\beta)$, $C=P(\beta)$ with $0<\beta<\pi$, so $\angle BAC=\beta$, and $A=P(2g)$ with $\frac\beta2<g<\pi-\frac\beta2$. By the order $B,A,C,D,E$, the points $D=P(d)$ and $E=P(\delta)$ lie on arc $BMC$: $-\beta<d,\delta<\beta$. Let $T=\tan\frac\beta2$ and $k=\cot g$.

The point $D$. $AB=2\sin(g+\frac\beta2)$, $AC=2\sin(g-\frac\beta2)$, $DB=2\sin\frac{\beta+d}2$, $DC=2\sin\frac{\beta-d}2$. The condition $\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{DB}{DC}$, rewritten as $\frac{x-y}{x+y}$ with sum-to-product formulas, gives $\cot g\tan\frac\beta2=\cot\frac\beta2\tan\frac d2$, i.e. \[\tan\tfrac d2=T^2k.\] The point $E$. $AB-AC=4\cos g\sin\frac\beta2$ and $BE-CE=4\cos\frac\beta2\sin\frac\delta2$, so $AB+CE=AC+BE$ gives $\sin\frac\delta2=T\cos g$. If $\cos g=0$, then $d=\delta=0$ and $D=E=M$, which is impossible; so $k\ne0$.

The point $X$. The line through $E$ parallel to $BC$ is the horizontal line $y=-\cos\delta$. The bisector is line $AM$, whose points are $M+t(A-M)$ with height $-1+t(1-\cos2g)$. They meet at $X$ with \[t=\frac{1-\cos\delta}{1-\cos2g}=\frac{\sin^2(\delta/2)}{\sin^2g}=T^2k^2.\] Since $|\sin\frac\delta2|<\sin\frac\beta2<\sin g$, we have $0<t<1$: $X$ lies inside segment $MA$ and $\frac{MX}{XA}=\frac{t}{1-t}$.

The perpendicular at $D$. Let $A'=P(2g+\pi)$ be the antipode of $A$; by Thales, the line through $D$ perpendicular to $AD$ is $DA'$. The line $AM$ separates $P(\varphi)$, $0<\varphi<2g$, from $P(\varphi)$, $2g<\varphi<2\pi$. As $\sin g>0$, $d$ has the sign of $\cos g$. If $\cos g>0$: $0<d<\beta<2g$ while $2g<2g+\pi<2\pi$. If $\cos g<0$: $D=P(2\pi+d)$ with $2g<2\pi-\beta<2\pi+d$, while $A'=P(2g-\pi)$ with $0<2g-\pi<2g$. Either way, chords $DA'$ and $AM$ cross at an interior point $X_0$. Inscribed angles give $\triangle X_0MD\sim\triangle X_0A'A$ and $\triangle X_0MA'\sim\triangle X_0DA$, hence \[\frac{MX_0}{X_0A}=\frac{MX_0}{X_0A'}\cdot\frac{X_0A'}{X_0A}=\frac{MD}{AA'}\cdot\frac{MA'}{AD}.\] Here $MD=2|\sin\frac d2|$, $MA'=2|\cos g|$, $AD=2\sin(g-\frac d2)$, $AA'=2$, and $\sin\frac d2$, $\cos g$ have the same sign, so \[\frac{MX_0}{X_0A}=\frac{\sin\frac d2\cos g}{\sin g\cos\frac d2-\cos g\sin\frac d2}=\frac{\tan\frac d2}{\tan g-\tan\frac d2}=\frac{T^2k}{1/k-T^2k}=\frac{T^2k^2}{1-T^2k^2}=\frac{MX}{XA}.\] Both $X$ and $X_0$ lie inside segment $MA$ and divide it in the same ratio, so $X=X_0$. Thus the line through $E$ parallel to $BC$, the line through $D$ perpendicular to $AD$, and the bisector $AM$ all pass through $X$.

Problem 3

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments

Consider a polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients. For any positive integer $n$, define $f(n)$ to be the number of coefficients in $P(x)^n$ that are not multiples of $67$. Show that for all sufficiently large $n$, at least $n^{0.99}$ of the numbers $f(1),f(2),\dots,f(n)$ are perfect $2026$th powers.

Note: A perfect $2026$th power is in the form $k^{2026}$, where $k$ is an integer.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Reduce the polynomial’s coefficients modulo $67$ and ask what remains to be counted. Check what happens if the reduced polynomial has no positive-degree terms.
Hint 2: key idea
For a large power $q$ of $67$, use Frobenius to split a polynomial power into factors indexed by the base-$q$ digits of the exponent. Restrict those digits so that the degree of each unshifted factor is less than $q$, making the resulting exponents combine without carries. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the no-carry property to express the coefficient count as a product of counts for individual digits. Classify fixed-length strings of allowed digits by the prime exponents of that product modulo $2026$, then concatenate $2026$ strings from one largest class. Estimate how many concatenations fit below a given bound, choosing $q$ large enough that the supply of allowed digits has a growth exponent strictly above $0.99$.
Common trap
Different term choices in the product can collide unless the exponents in each block fit into one base-$q$ place.
Show the solution – Frobenius splits $P^n$ into base-$q$ blocks

Work modulo $p=67$ and let $\bar P\in\mathbb F_p[x]$ be the reduction of $P$; $f(n)$ is the number of nonzero coefficients of $\bar P^{\,n}$. If $\bar P=0$, then $f(n)=0=0^{2026}$ for all $n$; if $\bar P$ is a nonzero constant, $f(n)=1=1^{2026}$. So assume $e=\deg\bar P\ge1$.

Splitting. In $\mathbb F_p[x]$ we have $\bar P(x)^p=\bar P(x^p)$, since $(\sum c_ix^i)^p=\sum c_i^px^{ip}$ and $c^p=c$ for $c\in\mathbb F_p$. Fix $L\ge1$ and $q=p^L$; then $\bar P(x)^{q^j}=\bar P(x^{q^j})$. Write $n=\sum_jm_jq^j$ in base $q$. Then \[\bar P^{\,n}=\prod_j\bar P^{\,m_j}\bigl(x^{q^j}\bigr).\] Call a digit $m$ small if $me\le q-1$, and let $S=\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor (q-1)/e\rfloor\}$ be the set of small digits; $|S|\ge q/e$. If all digits of $n$ are small, then every exponent in $\bar P^{\,m_j}$ is at most $q-1$, so each exponent $\sum_ja_jq^j$ of the product arises from exactly one choice of terms (base-$q$ representations are unique). Hence the coefficient of $x^{\sum a_jq^j}$ is the product of the corresponding coefficients, and \[f(n)=\prod_j f(m_j)\qquad(\text{all digits of }n\text{ small}),\] with $f(0)=1$.

Classes modulo $2026$th powers. For $m\in S$, $1\le f(m)\le me+1\le q$, so all prime factors of the $f(m)$ are at most $q$. Map a positive integer whose prime factors are $\le q$ to its vector of exponents modulo $2026$, an element of the finite group $G=(\mathbb Z/2026)^{\pi(q)}$ of size $K=2026^{\pi(q)}$. The map turns products into sums, and the value is $0$ exactly for perfect $2026$th powers.

Counting. Let $N$ be large; we count the $n\le N$ for which $f(n)$ is a perfect $2026$th power. Choose $L$ so large that $|S|\ge q/e\ge q^{0.999}$ (possible since $\log e/\log q\to0$). Let $J=\lfloor\log_qN/2026\rfloor$, which is at least $1$ for $N\ge q^{2026}$, so $q^{2026J}\le N<q^{2026(J+1)}$. Among the $|S|^J$ strings of $J$ small digits, some class $c\in G$ is taken by a set $\mathcal C$ of at least $|S|^J/K$ strings. Concatenating $2026$ strings from $\mathcal C$ gives a string of $2026J$ small digits, i.e. a number $n<q^{2026J}\le N$, whose $f(n)$ has class $2026c=0$: a perfect $2026$th power. Distinct concatenations give distinct $n$. Discarding $n=0$, the number of good $n\le N$ is at least \[\frac{|S|^{2026J}}{K^{2026}}-1\ge\frac{q^{0.999\cdot2026J}}{K^{2026}}-1\ge\frac{(N/q^{2026})^{0.999}}{K^{2026}}-1\ge N^{0.99}\] for all sufficiently large $N$, since $q$ and $K$ are constants and $N^{0.009}\to\infty$.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Encoding and Representation

Let $D,d$ be positive integers with $D\ge 2$ and $d\le D$. Players $A$ and $B$ play the following game:

  • $A$ chooses an integer $n$ with exactly $D$ decimal digits and $\gcd(n,10)=1$, and announces it.

  • $B$ secretly chooses an integer $s\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$.

  • On each turn, $A$ asks a positive integer $q$, and $B$ replies with the $d$-th digit (from the right) of $qs \bmod n$.

$A$'s queries may depend on previous replies. Determine, in terms of $d$ and $D$, the minimum number of turns $A$ needs to guarantee identifying $s$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Think of each query as revealing just one of ten symbols. In a short decimal string, what digit goes missing when you multiply the number by $10$ and retain only its original number of places?
Hint 2: key idea
Look for a $D$-digit modulus coprime to $10$ for which multiplying a residue by $10$ acts as a cyclic shift of its $D$-digit string. Then asking for one fixed digit can reveal different digits of the secret. (LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence)
Hint 3: approach
For the lower bound, count the possible reply sequences even when the questions depend on earlier replies, and compare with the smallest admissible modulus. For the construction, write the secret with leading zeros as needed and track the position revealed by successive powers of $10$. Check whether those positions exhaust the string.
Common trap
Use a fixed-length string with leading zeros, so the queried digit has a well-defined position in every residue.
Show the answer

$D$ turns (independent of $d$)

Show the solution – All nines and digit rotation

Lower bound. Each reply is one of the $10$ digits, so after $T$ turns at most $10^T$ different reply sequences are possible, and they must separate the $n$ possible values of $s$. Since $n$ has $D$ digits and is coprime to $10$, $n\ge10^{D-1}+1>10^{D-1}$. Hence $10^T\ge n>10^{D-1}$ and $T\ge D$.

$D$ turns suffice. Let $A$ choose $n=10^D-1=\underbrace{99\dots9}_{D}$ (coprime to $10$). Write every $r\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ as a string of $D$ digits (with leading zeros); the string $99\dots9$ does not occur. Multiplying by $10$ modulo $n$ rotates the string cyclically by one place: if $r=10^{D-1}r_{D-1}+r'$ with $r'<10^{D-1}$, then \[10r=10^D r_{D-1}+10r'\equiv 10r'+r_{D-1}\pmod{10^D-1},\] and $10r'+r_{D-1}<n$. So $10^ks\bmod n$ is the string of $s$ rotated by $k$ places, and its $d$-th digit from the right is the digit of $s$ in position $d-k$ (indices taken modulo $D$). Asking $q=10^k$ for $k=0,1,\dots,D-1$ (here $q$ must be positive, and $10^0=1$) reveals all $D$ digits of $s$, hence $s$.

So the minimum is $\boxed{D}$, for every $d$.

Problem 5

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Suppose that there is a sequence of positive reals $a_0,a_1,\dots$ such that for all sufficiently large $n$, \[\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}a_ia_{n-i}\ge 2.01^n\cdot a_n.\] Show that there exists a positive integer $i$ satisfying $a_i\le 0.1^{6767}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Separate the two endpoint terms involving $a_0$. How does the sum of the remaining binomial weights compare with the exponential factor in the hypothesis for large $n$?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the sum of the weights to force at least one interior product $a_i a_{n-i}$ to be large relative to $a_n$. Take logarithms so this becomes a bound for $\log a_n$ by two smaller-index terms minus a positive multiple of $n$. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Subtract a linear function of the index to handle the finitely many starting values without changing the splitting inequality. Try an upper bound of order $-n\log_2 n$ by induction, using that the binary entropy of the split fractions is at most $1$. Restore the linear term and examine the resulting bound as $n$ grows.
Common trap
The endpoint terms would permit a split with no decrease in the index, so remove them before using induction.
Show the solution – A superadditive defect forces $a_n\to0$

We prove more: $a_n\to0$, so some $a_i$ with $i\ge1$ is below $0.1^{6767}$.

Step 1: a splitting inequality. Choose $N_0\ge2$ such that for all $n\ge N_0$ the hypothesis holds and $2.01^n-2a_0\ge2.005^n$. For $n\ge N_0$, moving the terms $i=0,n$ to the right, \[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom ni a_ia_{n-i}\ge(2.01^n-2a_0)a_n\ge2.005^na_n.\] As $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom ni<2^n$, some $1\le i\le n-1$ has $a_ia_{n-i}\ge(2.005/2)^na_n$. With $x_m=\ln a_m$ and $c=\ln1.0025>0$: \[\text{for every }n\ge N_0\text{ there is }1\le i\le n-1\text{ with } x_n\le x_i+x_{n-i}-cn.\] Step 2: remove linear terms. Let $B\ge0$ satisfy $x_m\le Bm$ for $1\le m<N_0$, and put $z_m=x_m-Bm$. The inequality of Step 1 holds for $z$ as well (the linear terms cancel), and $z_m\le0$ for $1\le m<N_0$.

Step 3: induction. We show $z_m\le-c\,m\log_2\frac m{N_0}$ for all $m\ge1$. For $m<N_0$ the right side is positive and $z_m\le0$. For $m\ge N_0$, take $i$ from Step 1, put $\lambda=i/m\in(0,1)$, and use the induction hypothesis for $i$ and $m-i$: \[z_m\le-c\Bigl(i\log_2\tfrac i{N_0}+(m-i)\log_2\tfrac{m-i}{N_0}+m\Bigr)=-c\,m\Bigl(\log_2\tfrac m{N_0}+1-H(\lambda)\Bigr)\le-c\,m\log_2\tfrac m{N_0},\] where $H(\lambda)=-\lambda\log_2\lambda-(1-\lambda)\log_2(1-\lambda)\le1$.

Conclusion. $\ln a_n=x_n\le Bn-c\,n\log_2\frac n{N_0}\to-\infty$, so $a_n\to0$. In particular some positive integer $i$ has $a_i\le0.1^{6767}$.

Problem 6

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABCDEF$ be a convex hexagon such that $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ concur at a point $O$ and $BC\parallel AD$, $DE\parallel CF$, $FA\parallel EB$. Let $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ be the midpoints of $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ respectively and assume that $O$ is not coincident with any of them. Prove that $O,X,Y,Z$ are concyclic if and only if $O$ lies on the radical axis of the circumcircles of $\triangle ACE$ and $\triangle BDF$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the three diagonal lines through $O$ and mark which vertices and midpoints lie on each. What data along those lines would suffice to locate all nine points?
Hint 2: key idea
Use signed coordinates on the three lines, with $O$ as the origin, and express the third unit direction as a combination of the first two. Invert around $O$ to turn the midpoint concyclicity condition into collinearity of three images. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Translate the three side parallelisms into equations in the signed coordinates, then use the linear equation of the image line to obtain a condition for midpoint concyclicity. Write each original circumcircle in vector form with constant term equal to the power of $O$, and eliminate its linear coefficients by using the three diagonal directions. Compare the difference of the two constant terms with the condition obtained from the image line.
Common trap
Use signed coordinates on the diagonals, since the two endpoints of a diagonal are on opposite sides of $O$.
Show the solution – Vectors along the three diagonals

Put $O$ at the origin and let $u,v,w$ be unit vectors along $AD,BE,CF$. Then $A=au$, $D=du$, $B=bv$, $E=ev$, $C=cw$, $F=fw$ with nonzero reals $a,\dots,f$, the vectors $u,v$ are independent, and $w=\alpha u+\beta v$ with $\alpha,\beta\ne0$ (the three diagonals are distinct lines).

The parallelisms. $C-B=c\alpha u+(c\beta-b)v\parallel u$ gives $b=\beta c$. $E-D=-du+ev\parallel\alpha u+\beta v$ gives $\alpha e+\beta d=0$, i.e. $d=-\alpha e/\beta$. $A-F=(a-\alpha f)u-\beta fv\parallel v$ gives $a=\alpha f$.

Concyclicity. $X=\frac{a+d}2u$, $Y=\frac{b+e}2v$, $Z=\frac{c+f}2w$, all nonzero. Under the inversion $P\mapsto P/|P|^2$ centred at $O$, circles through $O$ correspond to lines not through $O$, and $X,Y,Z$ go to $\frac{2}{a+d}u$, $\frac2{b+e}v$, $\frac2{c+f}w$ (which lie on three different lines through $O$, so they are not on a line through $O$). A line not through $O$ is $\{P:\ell\cdot P=1\}$; the images of $X,Y$ lie on it iff $\ell\cdot u=\frac{a+d}2$ and $\ell\cdot v=\frac{b+e}2$, which determines $\ell$. Then the image of $Z$ is on it iff $\ell\cdot w=\frac{c+f}2$. Hence \[O,X,Y,Z\text{ concyclic}\iff \Phi:=\alpha(a+d)+\beta(b+e)-(c+f)=0.\] Powers of $O$. Write the circle $ACE$ as $|P|^2+2\gamma\cdot P+\kappa_1=0$; the power of $O$ is $\kappa_1$. Plugging in $A,E,C$: $2\gamma\cdot u=-(a+\frac{\kappa_1}a)$, $2\gamma\cdot v=-(e+\frac{\kappa_1}e)$, $2\gamma\cdot w=-(c+\frac{\kappa_1}c)$, and $w=\alpha u+\beta v$ gives \[\kappa_1\Bigl(\frac1c-\frac\alpha a-\frac\beta e\Bigr)=\alpha a+\beta e-c.\] The bracket, $Q:=\frac1c-\frac1f-\frac\beta e$ (using $a=\alpha f$), is nonzero: otherwise this equation would hold for no $\kappa_1$ or for every $\kappa_1$, and since $\gamma$ is determined by $\gamma\cdot u,\gamma\cdot v$, there would be no circle or infinitely many circles through the non-collinear points $A,C,E$. So $\kappa_1=(\alpha^2f+\beta e-c)/Q$. In the same way, for circle $BDF$ through $du$, $bv$, $fw$, \[\kappa_2\Bigl(\frac1f-\frac\alpha d-\frac\beta b\Bigr)=\alpha d+\beta b-f,\] where $\frac\alpha d=-\frac\beta e$ and $\frac\beta b=\frac1c$, so the bracket is $-Q$ and $\kappa_2=(\frac{\alpha^2e}{\beta}-\beta^2c+f)/Q$. Therefore \[\kappa_1-\kappa_2=\frac{\alpha\bigl(\alpha f-\frac{\alpha e}\beta\bigr)+\beta(\beta c+e)-(c+f)}{Q}=\frac{\alpha(a+d)+\beta(b+e)-(c+f)}{Q}=\frac{\Phi}{Q}.\] The radical axis exists, so the two circles are not concentric, and $O$ lies on it iff $\kappa_1=\kappa_2$, iff $\Phi=0$, iff $O,X,Y,Z$ are concyclic.
