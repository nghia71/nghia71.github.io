Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

A smart lock consists of $9$ holes arranged as a $3\times 3$ grid. At any moment, exactly one of the holes is “active,” but this is not visible from the outside of the lock. If you insert the key into the active hole, the lock opens. If you insert the key into any other hole, the active hole changes to another hole according to the following rules:

  • If the chosen hole and the active hole are in the same row, then the third hole in that row (the one different from both) becomes active.

  • If the chosen hole and the active hole are in the same column, then the third hole in that column becomes active.

  • Otherwise (i.e., if they are neither in the same row nor in the same column), there is exactly one other hole that is neither in the same row nor in the same column as the chosen hole or the active hole; that hole becomes active.

Houshang knows the rules of the lock and wants to open it. Each time, he may insert the key into any of the $9$ holes (even holes he has already tried before). Show that Houshang can use a suitable strategy to guarantee opening the lock, regardless of which hole was initially active. What is the minimum number of key insertions required to guarantee that the lock opens?

Show the answer

$9$ insertions (and $9$ always suffice)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates modulo 3)

Label the hole in row $i$ and column $j$ by the pair $(i,j)$ with $i,j\in\{0,1,2\}$, and compute with these pairs modulo $3$ coordinatewise.

The rule in one formula. Let $k$ be the chosen hole and $a\ne k$ the active hole. The three numbers $0,1,2$ add up to $0\pmod 3$, so “the third value different from $u$ and $v$” is $-u-v \pmod 3$ when $u\ne v$, and $-2u\equiv u$ is the common value when $u=v$. Checking the three cases of the rule (same row, same column, neither), the new active hole is always \[a'=-k-a\pmod 3 \qquad(\text{coordinatewise}).\]

At most one starting hole is opened by each insertion. Houshang learns nothing while the lock stays closed, so a strategy is simply a fixed sequence $k_1,k_2,\dots$ of holes. Fix such a sequence. If the lock is still closed after $t$ insertions, the active hole is $a_t=-k_t-a_{t-1}$, so by induction $a_t=(-1)^t a_0+c_t$ for a vector $c_t$ that depends only on $k_1,\dots,k_t$. Insertion number $t$ opens the lock exactly when $a_{t-1}=k_t$, that is, for exactly one initial hole $a_0$. Hence $8$ insertions can open the lock for at most $8$ of the $9$ possible initial holes, and no strategy with fewer than $9$ insertions is guaranteed to work.

Nine insertions suffice. List the nine holes as $h_1,\dots,h_9$. Choose $k_t$ so that insertion $t$ opens the lock if the initial hole was $h_t$: this means $k_t=(-1)^{t-1}h_t+c_{t-1}$, which is possible because $c_{t-1}$ is already known from $k_1,\dots,k_{t-1}$. Whatever the initial hole $h_t$ is, the lock opens at insertion $t\le 9$ at the latest.

So the minimum number of insertions that guarantees opening the lock is $\boxed{9}$.

Solution 2 (Tracking the set of candidates)

Fix a chosen hole $k$ and look at the map $f_k$ that sends an active hole $a\ne k$ to the new active hole.

$f_k$ is an involution of the eight holes other than $k$. In the first two cases the holes $k$, $a$, $f_k(a)$ are the three holes of one row (or column), a description symmetric in $a$ and $f_k(a)$, so $f_k(f_k(a))=a$. In the third case $k$ and $a$ lie in different rows and different columns, and $f_k(a)$ lies in the third row and the third column; then $k,a,f_k(a)$ occupy all three rows and all three columns, again a description symmetric in $a$ and $f_k(a)$. Hence $f_k(f_k(a))=a$; in particular $f_k$ is a bijection of the holes other than $k$, and $f_k(a)\ne k$.

Candidates. Let $S$ be the set of holes that could be active given what Houshang has seen so far (the lock has not opened). At the start $|S|=9$. If he now inserts into $k$ and the lock stays closed, the new set of candidates is $f_k(S\setminus\{k\})$, which has $|S|-1$ elements if $k\in S$ and $|S|$ elements otherwise, because $f_k$ is injective.

So each insertion lowers the number of candidates by at most $1$, and the lock is guaranteed open only when no candidate is left: at least $9$ insertions are needed. Conversely, inserting each time into some hole of the current set $S$ lowers $|S|$ by exactly $1$; after $9$ insertions no candidate remains, which means the lock has opened.

The answer is $\boxed{9}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Consider a sequence $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{100}$ of natural numbers greater than $1405$, satisfying the following conditions:

  • For every $1\le i\le 99$, we have $x_i\ge x_{i+1}$ and $x_{50}>x_{51}$.

  • For every $1\le i<j<k\le 100$, we have \[\left\lfloor\sqrt[3]{x_ix_jx_k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{x_i+x_j+x_k}{3}\right\rfloor\]

Show that \[\sqrt[3]{x_1}-\sqrt[3]{x_{100}}<\frac{1}{5}.\] (Note: The notation $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to $x$.)

Show the solution

Solution (A gap of more than one third forces a residue)

For a triple of indices write $S=x_i+x_j+x_k$, $A=S/3$ (arithmetic mean) and $G=\sqrt[3]{x_ix_jx_k}$ (geometric mean), and let $m=\lfloor A\rfloor=\lfloor G\rfloor$.

Two facts about one triple.

  • (i) If $S\equiv 0\pmod 3$, then $x_i=x_j=x_k$. Indeed $A$ is an integer, so $m=A$; since $G\ge m=A\ge G$ (AM–GM), $G=A$, and equality in AM–GM means the three numbers are equal.

  • (ii) If $A-G>\frac13$, then $S\equiv 2\pmod 3$. Indeed $G\ge m$ gives $A-m\ge A-G>\frac13$, while $A-m<1$ is the fractional part of $S/3$, which is $0$, $\frac13$ or $\frac23$. So it is $\frac23$.

The gap for a wide triple. Put $u=\sqrt[3]{x_i}$, $v=\sqrt[3]{x_j}$, $w=\sqrt[3]{x_k}$. The identity \[\frac{u^3+v^3+w^3}{3}-uvw=\frac{u+v+w}{6}\bigl((u-v)^2+(v-w)^2+(w-u)^2\bigr)\] gives $A-G$. Suppose $u\ge v\ge w$ and $u-w\ge\frac15$. Then $(u-v)^2+(v-w)^2\ge\frac12(u-w)^2$, so the bracket is at least $\frac32(u-w)^2\ge\frac{3}{50}$. Since $11.2^3=1404.928<1405<x$ for every term, each of $u,v,w$ exceeds $11.2$, and \[A-G>\frac{33.6}{6}\cdot\frac{3}{50}=0.336>\frac13 .\]

Conclusion. Assume, for contradiction, that $\sqrt[3]{x_1}-\sqrt[3]{x_{100}}\ge\frac15$. For every $j$ with $2\le j\le 99$ the triple $(x_1,x_j,x_{100})$ has $x_1\ge x_j\ge x_{100}$, so its largest and smallest cube roots differ by at least $\frac15$. By the estimate and (ii), $x_1+x_j+x_{100}\equiv 2\pmod 3$. Hence all of $x_2,\dots,x_{99}$ are congruent modulo $3$, and the triple $(x_{50},x_{51},x_{52})$ has sum $\equiv 0\pmod 3$. By (i), $x_{50}=x_{51}$, contradicting $x_{50}>x_{51}$. Therefore $\sqrt[3]{x_1}-\sqrt[3]{x_{100}}<\frac15$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation

Let $ABC$ be a scalene acute triangle with circumcenter $O$, and let $I_a$ be the $A$-excenter. Line $AI_a$ meets $BC$ at point $K$. Let $T$ be a point on the circumcircle of $I_aBC$ such that the circumcenter of triangle $TKI_a$ lies on $BC$. Prove that $AO$ is tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $ATI_a$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion at K)

Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$, $I$ the incenter, $M$ the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$, and $\omega$ the circle $(I_aBC)$. By the incenter–excenter lemma $\omega$ has center $M$ and passes through $B,C,I,I_a$, with $M$ the midpoint of $II_a$. Let $\Gamma=(TKI_a)$ with center $Q$ on $BC$, and $\Phi=(ATI_a)$.

Reformulation. A circle through $A$ is tangent to line $AO$ at $A$ exactly when its center lies on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $AO$, i.e. when the radius $OA$ of $\Omega$ is tangent to it, i.e. when it is orthogonal to $\Omega$. So we must prove $\Phi\perp\Omega$.

The inversion. $K$ lies inside both $\Omega$ and $\omega$, and $KB\cdot KC=KA\cdot KM=KI\cdot KI_a=:p$ (power of $K$). Let $\Psi$ be the inversion with center $K$ and radius $\sqrt p$ followed by the half-turn about $K$. It swaps $B\leftrightarrow C$, $A\leftrightarrow M$, $I\leftrightarrow I_a$, hence maps $\Omega$ and $\omega$ to themselves, and it preserves angles between circles (in particular orthogonality).

The image of $\Gamma$. $\Gamma$ passes through $K$ and its center $Q$ lies on $BC$, so the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $K$ is perpendicular to $BC$. Hence $\Psi(\Gamma)$ is a line parallel to that tangent, i.e. perpendicular to $BC$, through $\Psi(I_a)=I$. Let $T'=\Psi(T)$; it lies on this line and on $\omega$, so $T'$ is the second intersection of $\omega$ with the line through $I$ perpendicular to $BC$. ($T'\ne I$: the line is not tangent to $\omega$ at $I$, since that tangent is perpendicular to $AI$, and $AI\not\parallel BC$.)

The image of $\Phi$. $K$ lies on the segment $AI_a$, so $K$ is inside $\Phi$ and $\Psi(\Phi)$ is a circle; it is the circle $(MIT')$, and $\Phi\perp\Omega$ iff $(MIT')\perp\Omega$. As $M\in\Omega$, this holds iff $(MIT')$ is tangent at $M$ to the radius $OM$, which is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. Now $MI=MT'$ (radii of $\omega$), so $M$ is the midpoint of arc $IT'$ of $(MIT')$ and the tangent at $M$ is parallel to the chord $IT'$, i.e. perpendicular to $BC$. It is therefore the line $OM$. (The points $M,I,T'$ are not collinear: otherwise the line $IT'$, which is perpendicular to $BC$, would be the line $MI=AI$, giving $AI\perp BC$ and $AB=AC$; this is where the triangle being scalene is used.)

Hence $(MIT')\perp\Omega$, so $\Phi\perp\Omega$, which means that $AO$ is tangent to the circle $ATI_a$.

Solution 2 (A pencil of circles in coordinates)

As in Solution 1, the claim is equivalent to: the circle $\Phi=(ATI_a)$ is orthogonal to the circumcircle $\Omega$, and $\omega=(BCI_a)$ is the circle with center $M$ (midpoint of arc $BC$) through $B,C,I,I_a$, with $I_a$ diametrically opposite to $I$.

Coordinates. Take $M=(0,0)$ and the radius of $\omega$ equal to $1$; let $BC$ be the line $y=h$ with $0<h<1$, so $B,C=(\mp\sqrt{1-h^2},h)$. Write $I=(c,s)$ with $c^2+s^2=1$, $s>h$, so $I_a=(-c,-s)$. The circle $\Omega$ passes through $B,C,M$, so it is $x^2+y^2-\frac{y}{h}=0$. Line $MI$ is $\{tI\}$; it meets $\Omega$ again at $A=\frac{s}{h}(c,s)$ and $BC$ at $K=\frac hs(c,s)$.

Pencils. For circles $x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+e=0$ and $x^2+y^2+2g'x+2f'y+e'=0$, orthogonality means $2(gg'+ff')=e+e'$. $\Gamma=(TKI_a)$ has center on $BC$: $\Gamma:\ x^2+y^2-2qx-2hy+e=0$. Since $\Gamma$ and $\omega:\ x^2+y^2-1=0$ meet at $T$ and $I_a$, every circle through $T,I_a$ has the form $\Phi_\lambda=\omega+\lambda(\Gamma-\omega)$, i.e. \[x^2+y^2-1+\lambda(-2qx-2hy+e+1)=0 .\] Orthogonality with $\Omega$ ($g'=0$, $f'=-\frac1{2h}$, $e'=0$) reads $2\lambda h\cdot\frac1{2h}=-1+\lambda(e+1)$, i.e. $\lambda e=1$ (we show below that $e\ne0$). So we must show that the member with $\lambda=\frac1e$ passes through $A$, i.e. (multiplying by $e$) \[e\,|A|^2-2qA_x-2hA_y+1=0.\tag{$*$}\]

Using the data. $\Gamma(I_a)=0$ gives $e=-1-2qc-2hs$. $\Gamma(K)=0$ gives $\frac{h^2}{s^2}-2h^2-\frac{2qhc}{s}+e=0$. Solving the two relations, $e=-\frac hs\ne0$, so the division by $e$ above is allowed. Substituting the first expression for $e$ into the second relation, \[2qcs(s+h)=h^2-2h^2s^2-s^2-2hs^3 .\] With $|A|^2=\frac{s^2}{h^2}$, $A_x=\frac{cs}{h}$, $A_y=\frac{s^2}{h}$, the left side of $(*)$ times $h^2$ is \[es^2-2qcsh-2h^2s^2+h^2=-s^2-2qcs(s+h)-2hs^3-2h^2s^2+h^2 ,\] and inserting the displayed value of $2qcs(s+h)$ everything cancels. So $(*)$ holds: $\Phi$ is orthogonal to $\Omega$, and $AO$ is tangent to the circle $ATI_a$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral such that $\angle B=\angle A+\angle C$. The bisector of $\angle CDA$ intersects $BC$ at $E$. We know that $\angle AED=90^\circ$, and $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $E$ to $AD$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $BE$. Prove that quadrilateral $CDHM$ is cyclic.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circumcenter of ABE lies on AD)

Write $\alpha=\angle DAB$, $\beta=\angle ABC$, $\gamma=\angle BCD$, $2\delta=\angle CDA$. From $\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2\delta=360^\circ$ and $\beta=\alpha+\gamma$ we get $\beta=180^\circ-\delta$; in particular $\beta>90^\circ$.

Angles at $E$. In the right triangle $AED$, $\angle EDA=\delta$, so $\angle DAE=90^\circ-\delta=\beta-90^\circ$. In triangle $ABE$, $\angle BAE=\alpha-(90^\circ-\delta)=90^\circ-\gamma$ (using $\alpha+\delta=180^\circ-\gamma$), hence $\angle AEB=180^\circ-\beta-(90^\circ-\gamma)=90^\circ-\alpha$, and on line $BC$, $\angle DEC=180^\circ-90^\circ-(90^\circ-\alpha)=\alpha$. (In particular $\gamma<90^\circ$.)

The circumcenter $O_1$ of $ABE$ lies on ray $AD$. Since $\angle ABE=\beta>90^\circ$, $O_1$ lies on the side of $AE$ opposite to $B$, which is the side of $D$, and the central angle is $\angle AO_1E=2(180^\circ-\beta)$. The isosceles triangle $AO_1E$ gives $\angle O_1AE=\beta-90^\circ=\angle DAE$. So $O_1$ lies on ray $AD$.

$EO_1\parallel CD$. In the isosceles triangle $EO_1B$, $\angle O_1EB=90^\circ-\angle BAE=\gamma$ (the inscribed angle $\angle BAE=90^\circ-\gamma$ is acute, so $O_1$ and $A$ are on the same side of $BE$). Thus $\angle O_1EB=\angle DCB$ with $O_1$ and $D$ on the same side of $BC$: $EO_1\parallel CD$.

Cyclic. $O_1M\perp BE$ ($M$ is the midpoint of the chord $BE$) and $EH\perp AD$, i.e. $\angle O_1ME=\angle O_1HE=90^\circ$; so $O_1,H,M,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $O_1E$. Using directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, if $H\ne O_1$, \[\angle(MH,MC)=\angle(MH,ME)=\angle(O_1H,O_1E)=\angle(AD,CD)=\angle(DH,DC),\] because $O_1H$ is the line $AD$ and $O_1E\parallel CD$. If $H=O_1$, then $\angle(MH,ME)=90^\circ$ (as $\angle O_1ME=90^\circ$) and $AD\perp CD$ (as $O_1E\perp AD$ and $O_1E\parallel CD$), so the same equality holds. Hence $C,D,H,M$ are concyclic.

Solution 2 (Law of sines and an SAS similarity)

Keep the notation $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,2\delta$ and the angles found at the start of Solution 1: $\beta=180^\circ-\delta$, $\angle DEC=\alpha$, $\angle AEB=90^\circ-\alpha$, $\angle BAE=90^\circ-\gamma$, so $\gamma<90^\circ$; note also $\alpha+\gamma+\delta=180^\circ$ (triangle $DEC$). Put $DE=1$.

Two lengths. In the right triangle $AED$: $AE=\tan\delta$ and, $H$ being the foot of the altitude, $\angle AEH=\delta$ and $EH=\sin\delta$. In triangle $ABE$ the law of sines gives $BE=AE\cdot\frac{\sin(90^\circ-\gamma)}{\sin\beta}=\frac{\tan\delta\cos\gamma}{\sin\delta}=\frac{\cos\gamma}{\cos\delta}$ (as $\sin\beta=\sin\delta$), so $EM=\frac{\cos\gamma}{2\cos\delta}$.

The triangle $HME$. The rays from $E$ appear in the order $EB$ ($=EM$), $EA$, $EH$, $ED$, $EC$, so $\angle HEM=\angle HEA+\angle AEB=\delta+90^\circ-\alpha$. Consider a triangle $H'M'E'$ with angles $90^\circ-\gamma$ at $H'$, $180^\circ-2\delta$ at $M'$ and $90^\circ-\alpha+\delta$ at $E'$; these are positive and add up to $360^\circ-(\alpha+\gamma+\delta)=180^\circ$. By the law of sines \[\frac{E'M'}{E'H'}=\frac{\sin(90^\circ-\gamma)}{\sin(180^\circ-2\delta)}=\frac{\cos\gamma}{2\sin\delta\cos\delta}=\frac{EM}{EH}.\] With the equal included angle at $E$, triangles $HME$ and $H'M'E'$ are similar (SAS), so $\angle HMC=\angle HME=180^\circ-2\delta=180^\circ-\angle HDC$.

Conclusion. $H$ lies on side $AD$ and $M$ on side $BC$, so segment $HM$ cuts the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ and $HMCD$ is a convex quadrilateral. Its opposite angles at $M$ and $D$ add up to $180^\circ$, so $C,D,H,M$ are concyclic.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Suppose that for every natural number $k$, $d(k)$ denotes the number of positive divisors of $k$. Prove that there is no infinite sequence of positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots$ such that for all $i>j$: \[d(a_i+a_j)=i+j\]

Show the solution

Solution (Two squares with a fixed difference)

Lemma. $d(n)$ is odd if and only if $n$ is a perfect square. (Divisors pair up as $t\leftrightarrow n/t$; only $t=\sqrt n$ is paired with itself.)

Suppose the sequence exists. For every even $i\ge 4$, the numbers $i+1$ and $i+3$ are odd, so by the lemma \[a_i+a_1=s_i^2,\qquad a_i+a_3=t_i^2\] for positive integers $s_i,t_i$. Let $c=a_3-a_1$. Then $c\ne0$: otherwise $d(a_4+a_3)=d(a_4+a_1)$, i.e. $7=5$. Hence $(t_i-s_i)(t_i+s_i)=c$ with $t_i\ne s_i$, so $t_i+s_i\le|c|$ and $s_i<|c|$.

So $a_i=s_i^2-a_1$ takes fewer than $|c|$ values over the infinitely many even $i\ge4$. Two different even indices $i\ne i'$ then have $a_i=a_{i'}$, and \[i+1=d(a_i+a_1)=d(a_{i'}+a_1)=i'+1,\] a contradiction. Hence no such sequence exists.

Remark. The same idea gives an explicit bound: $d(s^2)<2s$ gives $s_i,t_i>\frac{i+1}{2}$, so $|c|\ge s_i+t_i>i+1$; hence no even index $i\ge\max(4,|a_3-a_1|-1)$ can occur, even in a finite sequence.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Shakerestan has a number of villages, and there is a road between every two villages. Show that if the number of villages is more than 3, the governor of Shakerestan can send the headman of each village to visit all other villages, excluding their own village, according to the following rules:

  • Each headman starts from a village and returns to the same starting point after visiting all other villages exactly once.

  • Each road between two villages is traversed exactly twice by the headmen.

For example, suppose Shakerestan has 4 villages numbered 1 to 4. Each headman must traverse a closed path between 3 other villages, not visit their own village, and each of the six roads must be traversed exactly twice.

The visiting paths could be as follows:

  • Headman of village 1: $2\to 4\to 3\to 2$

  • Headman of village 2: $1\to 4\to 3\to 1$

  • Headman of village 3: $1\to 2\to 4\to 1$

  • Headman of village 4: $1\to 2\to 3\to 1$

Show the solution

Solution (Rotating a zigzag around a hub)

Let $n\ge4$ be the number of villages and $m=n-1\ge3$. Name the villages $\infty$ and the residues $0,1,\dots,m-1$ modulo $m$. A tour of headman $v$ must be a cycle through the $n-1$ villages other than $v$; we must choose such a cycle $C_v$ for every $v$ so that each road lies on exactly two of the cycles.

The tours.

  • Headman of $\infty$: $\;0\to1\to2\to\dots\to m-1\to0$.

  • Headman of $v\in\mathbb Z_m$: $\;\infty\to v+1\to v-1\to v+2\to v-2\to v+3\to\cdots\to\infty$,

where the offsets $1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\dots$ are taken until $m-1$ of them have been used. The first $m-1$ terms of this list are pairwise different and nonzero modulo $m$ (they are $1,m-1,2,m-2,\dots$), so $C_v$ visits $\infty$ and every residue except $v$ exactly once. Let $\ell$ be the last offset; $\ell\ne1$ since $m-1\ge2$.

Roads at $\infty$. In $C_v$ the neighbours of $\infty$ are $v+1$ and $v+\ell$. So the road $\{\infty,x\}$ is used by $C_v$ exactly when $x-v\in\{1,\ell\}$, i.e. for exactly two values of $v$; and $C_\infty$ does not use it.

Other roads. Call $\min(d,m-d)$ the class of a road $\{x,y\}$ with $x-y\equiv\pm d$. Consecutive offsets differ by \[-1-1=-2,\quad 2-(-1)=3,\quad -2-2=-4,\quad 5,\ \dots,\] so the $m-2$ roads of $C_v$ not at $\infty$ have differences $\pm2,\pm3,\dots,\pm(m-1)$, one each. Their classes are: class $1$ once (from $m-1$); each class $d$ with $2\le d<\frac m2$ twice (from $d$ and $m-d$); class $\frac m2$ once (if $m$ is even). Now translate: as $v$ runs over $\mathbb Z_m$, a road $\{x,x+d\}$ of $C_0$ is moved onto every road of its class exactly once if $d\ne\frac m2$, and exactly twice if $d=\frac m2$ (the class then has only $\frac m2$ roads). Hence, over all $C_v$ with $v\in\mathbb Z_m$, each road of class $d$ is used twice for $2\le d\le\frac m2$ and once for $d=1$. The tour $C_\infty$ uses each road of class $1$ once more. So every road is used exactly twice.

Thus the governor can send all headmen as required, for every number of villages $n\ge4$. (For $n=4$ the tours are $0\to1\to2\to0$ for $\infty$ and $\infty\to v+1\to v-1\to\infty$ for $v\in\mathbb Z_3$.)
