Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Prove that one can paint the non-zero coefficients of the polynomial \[f(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n-k)\] with $2^n-1$ colors such that the coefficients of each color have sum $0$, and each color is used at least once.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Colour by support, then inclusion-exclusion)

The support of a monomial $x_1^{e_1}\cdots x_n^{e_n}$ is the set $\{i:e_i\ge1\}$. We colour each non-zero coefficient by the support of its monomial.

There are exactly $2^n-1$ colours. The constant term of $f$ is $f(0,\dots,0)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(0-k)=0$ (the factor $k=0$), so the empty support does not occur and at most $2^n-1$ colours are used. Each non-empty $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ does occur: the part of $f$ of degree $n+1$ is $(x_1+\dots+x_n)^{n+1}$, whose coefficients are positive multinomial coefficients, and since $|T|\le n<n+1$ there is a monomial of degree $n+1$ in which exactly the variables $x_i$, $i\in T$, appear (give one of them the exponent $n+2-|T|$ and the others exponent $1$). Its coefficient in $f$ is non-zero and has colour $T$.

Each colour has sum $0$. For $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ let $c(T)$ be the sum of the coefficients of the monomials with support exactly $T$ (so $c(\emptyset)=0$ is the constant term), and let $\mathbf 1_T$ be the point with $x_i=1$ for $i\in T$ and $x_i=0$ otherwise. A monomial takes the value $1$ at $\mathbf 1_T$ if its support is contained in $T$, and $0$ otherwise. Hence \[f(\mathbf 1_T)=\sum_{U\subseteq T}c(U).\] On the other hand $x_1+\dots+x_n=|T|$ at $\mathbf 1_T$, so $f(\mathbf 1_T)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(|T|-k)=0$, because $0\le|T|\le n$. Thus \[\sum_{U\subseteq T}c(U)=0\qquad\text{for every }T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}.\] Now $c(T)=0$ follows for all $T$ by induction on $|T|$: for $T=\emptyset$ it is the statement above, and if $c(U)=0$ for all proper subsets $U$ of $T$, the displayed equation gives $c(T)=0$.

So the coefficients of each of the $2^n-1$ colours have sum $0$, and every colour is used.

Solution 2 (Falling factorials and the Vandermonde identity)

The support of a monomial is the set of indices $i$ whose variable $x_i$ occurs in it. We colour each non-zero coefficient by the support of its monomial (as in Solution 1), and prove that each colour has sum $0$ by an explicit decomposition of $f$.

For an integer $j\ge0$ write $(t)_j=t(t-1)\cdots(t-j+1)$ (with $(t)_0=1$), so that $f=(s)_{n+1}$ where $s=x_1+\dots+x_n$. The polynomials $(t)_j$ satisfy the binomial-type identity \[(u+v)_m=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom mj(u)_j(v)_{m-j}.\] (Divide by $m!$: it becomes the Vandermonde identity $\binom{u+v}{m}=\sum_j\binom uj\binom v{m-j}$, which holds for all non-negative integers $u,v$ by counting $m$-subsets of a set of $u+v$ elements, hence it is an identity of polynomials.) Applying it repeatedly, \[f=(x_1+\dots+x_n)_{n+1}=\sum_{j_1+\dots+j_n=n+1}\frac{(n+1)!}{j_1!\cdots j_n!}\ (x_1)_{j_1}(x_2)_{j_2}\cdots(x_n)_{j_n},\tag{1}\] the sum being over all $n$-tuples of non-negative integers with sum $n+1$.

Fix such a tuple and let $T=\{i:j_i\ge1\}$, a non-empty set. The product $g=\prod_{i\in T}(x_i)_{j_i}$ has two properties:

  • every monomial of $g$ has support exactly $T$, because $(x_i)_{j_i}$ is divisible by $x_i$ for $j_i\ge1$ and only the variables $x_i$, $i\in T$, occur;

  • the sum of the coefficients of $g$ is $g(1,\dots,1)=\prod_{i\in T}(1)_{j_i}=0$: since $\sum_{i\in T}j_i=n+1>n\ge|T|$, some $j_i\ge2$, and $(1)_j=0$ for $j\ge2$.

Group the terms of (1) according to $T$. For each non-empty $T$, the monomials of $f$ with support $T$ come exactly from the terms of (1) with that $T$, and each of these terms has coefficient sum $0$. Hence the coefficients of $f$ at the monomials with support $T$ have sum $0$.

Finally, every non-empty $T$ really occurs among the non-zero coefficients: the terms of degree $n+1$ of $f$ are those of $s^{n+1}$, all with positive coefficients, and one of them has support exactly $T$ because $|T|\le n<n+1$. The constant term is $f(0)=0$. So the colouring by supports uses exactly $2^n-1$ colours, each with sum $0$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $p$ be a prime and let $a$ and $b$ be positive integers less than $p$. Show that \[\sum_{k=1}^{b}(-1)^{\lfloor (a-1)k/p\rfloor+\lfloor ak/p\rfloor}\ge 0.\]

Show the solution

Solution (The negative terms are closed under addition)

If $a=1$ every exponent is $0+\lfloor k/p\rfloor=0$ and the sum equals $b\ge0$. Let $2\le a\le p-1$.

Step 1: the sign of a term. For $1\le k\le p-1$ write $ak=qp+r$ with $0\le r<p$; since $p$ is prime and $1\le a,k<p$, we have $r\ge1$. Then $\lfloor ak/p\rfloor=q$ and $(a-1)k=qp+(r-k)$ with $-p<r-k<p$, so \[\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(a-1)k}{p}\Bigr\rfloor=\begin{cases}q,&r\ge k,\\ q-1,&r<k.\end{cases}\] Hence the $k$th term of the sum is $-1$ if $r<k$ and $+1$ if $r\ge k$. Call $k\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ bad if $(ak\bmod p)<k$, and good otherwise. We must show: for every $b\le p-1$, at most half of the numbers $1,\dots,b$ are bad.

Step 2: two properties of bad numbers.

  • (i) If $k,k'$ are bad and $k+k'\le p-1$, then $k+k'$ is bad. Indeed, let $r,r'$ be the remainders of $ak,ak'$; then $1\le r+r'<k+k'\le p-1$ and $a(k+k')\equiv r+r'\pmod p$, so the remainder of $a(k+k')$ is $r+r'$, which is less than $k+k'$.

  • (ii) There are no bad $k,k'$ with $k+k'=p$. Otherwise $r+r'\equiv a(k+k')\equiv0\pmod p$, although $0<r+r'<k+k'=p$.

Declare, just for the next step, the number $p$ to be “not bad”. Then (i) and (ii) together say: if $N\le p$ is not bad and $1\le x\le N-1$, then $x$ and $N-x$ are not both bad.

Step 3: counting. Fix $b\le p-1$ and let $N$ be the smallest integer greater than $b$ that is not bad; it exists and $N\le p$. So $b+1,\dots,N-1$ are all bad. The map $x\mapsto N-x$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,N-1\}$ that sends bad numbers to numbers that are not bad. Hence at most half of the numbers $1,\dots,N-1$ are bad: \[\#\{\text{bad }k\le N-1\}\le\frac{N-1}{2}.\] Removing the $N-1-b$ bad numbers $b+1,\dots,N-1$ we get \[\#\{\text{bad }k\le b\}\le\frac{N-1}{2}-(N-1-b)=b-\frac{N-1}{2}\le\frac b2 ,\] because $N-1\ge b$. So among the first $b$ terms at most half are equal to $-1$, and the sum is non-negative.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Prove that for any subset $S$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$, there exists a (not necessarily axis-aligned) rectangle of area $1$ that contains either $0$ or more than $2025$ points in its strict interior.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The smallest number of points that cannot be squeezed together)

Write $C=2025$. “A rectangle contains a point” will mean that the point is in its strict interior when the rectangle is open; we shall also use closed rectangles (with boundary), which may be degenerate (a segment or a point, of area $0$). Suppose, for contradiction, that every open rectangle of area $1$ contains at least $1$ and at most $C$ points of $S$.

Three simple facts.

  • (F1) A closed rectangle of area less than $1$ contains at most $C$ points of $S$. If its sides are $a\ge b\ge0$ with $ab<1$, choose $a'>a$, $b'>b$ with $a'b'=1$; the open rectangle with the same center and axes and sides $a',b'$ contains it.

  • (F2) An open rectangle of area at least $k$ ($k$ a positive integer) contains at least $k$ points of $S$. Cut it by $k-1$ parallel segments into $k$ open rectangles of area at least $1$; each contains an open rectangle of area exactly $1$, hence a point of $S$.

  • (F3) A convex polygon $K$ of area $a$ (possibly degenerate) lies in a closed rectangle of area at most $2a$. Let $P,Q$ be two points of $K$ at the largest distance $d$. Then $K$ lies between the two lines through $P$ and $Q$ perpendicular to $PQ$. Let $h_1,h_2\ge0$ be the largest distances from the line $PQ$ of points of $K$ on its two sides, attained at $U$ and $V$. Then $K$ lies in a rectangle with sides $d$ and $h_1+h_2$, while $K$ contains the triangles $PQU$ and $PQV$, of total area $\frac12d(h_1+h_2)$.

The key definition. For $i\ge1$ let \[\beta_i=\inf\{\text{area}(R):\ R\text{ a closed rectangle containing at least $i$ points of }S\}.\] By (F2), $S$ is infinite, so all $\beta_i$ are defined; clearly $0=\beta_1\le\beta_2\le\cdots$, and $\beta_{C+1}\ge1$ by (F1). Let $j$ be the largest index with $\beta_j=0$; thus $1\le j\le C$ and \[\delta:=\beta_{j+1}>0 .\] In words: $j$ points of $S$ can be found in rectangles of arbitrarily small area, but $j+1$ points never fit into a rectangle of area less than $\delta$.

A large almost empty rectangle. Let $\varepsilon=\min\bigl(\frac{\delta}{40},\frac{4\delta^2}{25(C+1)}\bigr)$. Since $\beta_j=0$, there is a closed rectangle $R_0$ of area less than $\varepsilon$ containing at least $j$ points of $S$; enlarging it slightly if it is degenerate, we may assume that its sides are $D\ge w>0$, still with $Dw<\varepsilon$. As $Dw<\delta$, it contains exactly $j$ points of $S$. Choose coordinates with $R_0=[0,D]\times[0,w]$ and let $Q$ be the open rectangle \[Q=\Bigl\{(x,y):\ |x|<\frac{\delta}{5w},\ |y|<\frac{\delta}{5D}\Bigr\}.\] Claim: every point of $S$ in $Q$ lies in $R_0$. Suppose $z=(x,y)\in S\cap Q$ and $z\notin R_0$, and let $K$ be the convex hull of $R_0$ and $z$ (a convex polygon). Every point of $K$ lies on a segment $zp$ with $p\in R_0$; the part of this segment outside $R_0$ joins $z$ to a point of a side of $R_0$. Hence $K$ is covered by $R_0$ and the four triangles spanned by $z$ and the sides of $R_0$. The two triangles on the sides of length $w$ (on the lines $x=0$, $x=D$) have areas $\frac12w|x|$ and $\frac12w|x-D|$; the two on the sides of length $D$ have areas $\frac12D|y|$ and $\frac12D|y-w|$. Therefore \[\text{area}(K)\le Dw+\tfrac12w\bigl(2|x|+D\bigr)+\tfrac12D\bigl(2|y|+w\bigr)=2Dw+w|x|+D|y|<2\varepsilon+\frac\delta5+\frac\delta5\le\frac{9\delta}{20}.\] By (F3), $K$ lies in a closed rectangle of area at most $\frac{9\delta}{10}<\delta$. This rectangle contains the $j$ points of $S$ in $R_0$ and the point $z$: at least $j+1$ points of $S$, contradicting the definition of $\delta$. The claim is proved.

The contradiction. By the claim, $Q$ contains at most $j\le C$ points of $S$. But \[\text{area}(Q)=\frac{2\delta}{5w}\cdot\frac{2\delta}{5D}=\frac{4\delta^2}{25\,Dw}>\frac{4\delta^2}{25\,\varepsilon}\ge C+1,\] so by (F2) the open rectangle $Q$ contains at least $C+1$ points of $S$. This contradiction shows that some open rectangle of area $1$ contains no point of $S$ or more than $2025$ points of $S$.

Solution 2 (Squeezing points together with area-preserving affine maps)

“A rectangle contains a point” means that the point is in its strict interior. Suppose, for contradiction, that every open rectangle of area $1$ contains at least $1$ and at most $2025$ points of $S$.

Step 1: two facts about parallelograms. Let $\Pi$ be a closed parallelogram of area $F>0$. Choose coordinates in which a longest side of $\Pi$ is $[0,L]\times\{0\}$ and the opposite side is $[d,d+L]\times\{H\}$, with $H>0$ and (after a reflection) $d\ge0$. Then $F=LH$, and the other sides have length $\sqrt{d^2+H^2}\le L$, so $d\le L$.

  • (a) $\Pi$ lies in the rectangle $[0,L+d]\times[0,H]$, of area $(L+d)H\le2LH=2F$.

  • (b) For $\frac H4\le y\le\frac{3H}4$ the horizontal section of $\Pi$ at height $y$ is the segment $\bigl[\frac{dy}H,\frac{dy}H+L\bigr]$, which contains $\bigl[\frac{3d}4,L+\frac d4\bigr]$. So $\Pi$ contains the rectangle $\bigl[\frac{3d}4,L+\frac d4\bigr]\times\bigl[\frac H4,\frac{3H}4\bigr]$, whose width is $L-\frac d2\ge\frac L2$ and whose height is $\frac H2$; its area is at least $\frac F4$.

Step 2: what survives an area-preserving affine map. Let $\varphi(x)=Mx+c$ be an affine map of the plane with $|\det M|=1$, and let $S'=\varphi(S)$. The map $\varphi$ preserves areas, and the preimage of an open square is the interior of a parallelogram of the same area.

  • (i) Every open square of area $4$ contains a point of $S'$. The preimage of the square is the interior of a parallelogram $\Pi$ of area $4$. By (b), $\Pi$ contains a rectangle of area at least $1$, hence an open rectangle of area exactly $1$; this open set lies in the interior of $\Pi$. It contains a point of $S$, whose image lies in the square.

  • (ii) Every open square of area $\frac12$ contains at most $2025$ points of $S'$. The preimage of the square is the interior of a parallelogram $\Pi$ of area $\frac12$. By (a), $\Pi$ lies in a rectangle of area at most $1$; enlarging it if necessary, $\Pi$ lies in a closed rectangle of area exactly $1$, and then the interior of $\Pi$ lies in the open rectangle of area $1$ with the same boundary. That open rectangle contains at most $2025$ points of $S$.

Step 3: clusters of any size. We prove by induction on $k\ge1$: for every $\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon<1$ there is an affine map $\varphi$ with $|\det M|=1$ such that $\varphi(S)$ has at least $k$ points at distance less than $\varepsilon$ from the origin $O$.

For $k=1$: by (i) with $\varphi$ the identity, $S$ is not empty; translate one of its points to $O$.

Let $k\ge1$, assume the statement for $k$, and let $0<\varepsilon<1$. Put $\lambda=\frac\varepsilon{10}$. By the induction hypothesis (with $\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}2$ in place of $\varepsilon$) there is such a map $\varphi$ for which $S'=\varphi(S)$ has $k$ points of norm less than $\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}2$. By (i), the open square $(3,5)\times(-1,1)$, of area $4$, contains a point $P$ of $S'$; then $3<|P|<\sqrt{26}<6$, so $P$ is not one of those $k$ points. Let $e=P/|P|$, let $f$ be a unit vector perpendicular to $e$, and let $\psi$ be the linear map with $\psi(\alpha e+\beta f)=\lambda\alpha e+\frac1\lambda\beta f$. Then $\det\psi=1$, $\psi$ is injective, and $|\psi(x)|\le\frac1\lambda|x|$ for every $x$, because $\lambda<1$. Now $\psi\circ\varphi$ is again an affine map with determinant of absolute value $1$, and in $\psi(S')$:

  • the images of the $k$ old points have norm less than $\frac1\lambda\cdot\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}2=\frac\varepsilon2$;

  • the image of $P$ is $\lambda P$, of norm less than $6\lambda=\frac{3\varepsilon}5<\varepsilon$.

These $k+1$ image points are distinct, which completes the induction.

Step 4: the contradiction. Apply Step 3 with $k=2026$ and $\varepsilon=\frac14$: some $S'=\varphi(S)$ has $2026$ points in the open disc of radius $\frac14$ about $O$. This disc lies in the open square $\bigl(-\frac1{2\sqrt2},\frac1{2\sqrt2}\bigr)^2$ of area $\frac12$, because $\frac14<\frac1{2\sqrt2}$. This contradicts (ii). Hence some rectangle of area $1$ contains either $0$ or more than $2025$ points of $S$ in its strict interior.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive integer. In the infinite lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$, $n$ points are colored red while the rest are colored blue. Each red point is labeled with the distance to the nearest blue point in the same row or column. Find the smallest real number $\alpha$ for which the sum of all labels does not exceed $100n^\alpha$, independent of $n$ and the placement of the red points.

(Note: A row is the set of points with a given $y$-coordinate, and a column is the set of points with a given $x$-coordinate.)

Show the answer

$\alpha=\frac32$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Heavy and light rows)

For a red point $P$ let $\ell(P)$ be its label, $r(P)$ the number of red points in its row and $c(P)$ the number of red points in its column. If $\ell(P)=d$, the $d-1$ nearest points on each of the four sides of $P$ are red, so $r(P)\ge 2d-1\ge d$ and $c(P)\ge 2d-1\ge d$. Hence \[\ell(P)\le\min\bigl(r(P),c(P)\bigr).\tag{1}\]

The exponent $\frac32$ works. Call a row heavy if it contains at least $\sqrt n$ red points and light otherwise. There are at most $\sqrt n$ heavy rows.

  • For a red point $P$ in a light row, $\ell(P)\le r(P)<\sqrt n$. These points contribute less than $n\sqrt n$.

  • For a red point $P$ in a heavy row, $\ell(P)\le c(P)$. A column with $c$ red points contains at most $\sqrt n$ red points lying in heavy rows (one in each heavy row at most), so it contributes at most $c\cdot\sqrt n$. Summing over the columns, these points contribute at most $\sqrt n\cdot\sum c=n\sqrt n$.

So the sum of all labels is at most $2n^{3/2}\le100\,n^{3/2}$, for every $n$ and every placement.

No smaller exponent works. Let $n=k^2$ with $k=2m$ even and colour the square $\{1,\dots,k\}^2$ red. The label of $(x,y)$ is $\min(x,k+1-x,y,k+1-y)$. The points with label at least $d$ form the square $\{d,\dots,k+1-d\}^2$ with $(k-2d+2)^2$ points, so the sum of the labels is \[\sum_{d=1}^{m}(k-2d+2)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(2i)^2=\frac{2m(m+1)(2m+1)}{3}>\frac{4m^3}{3}=\frac{k^3}{6}=\frac{n^{3/2}}{6}.\] If $\alpha<\frac32$, then $\frac{n^{3/2}}6>100\,n^{\alpha}$ as soon as $n^{3/2-\alpha}>600$, which holds for large $k$. So the inequality fails for every $\alpha<\frac32$.

The smallest exponent is $\boxed{\alpha=\tfrac32}$.

Solution 2 (Counting the points with label at least d)

For $d\ge1$ let $A_d$ be the set of red points with label at least $d$. The sum of the labels equals $\sum_{d\ge1}|A_d|$ (a point with label $\ell$ is counted in $A_1,\dots,A_\ell$).

Upper bound. If $P\in A_d$, then the $2d-1$ points of its row at distance at most $d-1$ from $P$ are red, and the same holds in its column. So every row that meets $A_d$ contains at least $2d-1$ red points; there are at most $\frac{n}{2d-1}$ such rows, and likewise at most $\frac n{2d-1}$ columns meet $A_d$. A point of $A_d$ is determined by its row and its column, hence \[|A_d|\le\min\Bigl(n,\ \frac{n^2}{(2d-1)^2}\Bigr).\] Let $D\ge1$ be an integer. Using $|A_d|\le n$ for $d\le D$, and for $d>D$ the estimate $\frac1{(2d-1)^2}<\frac{1}{(2d-1)^2-1}=\frac14\bigl(\frac1{d-1}-\frac1d\bigr)$, which telescopes, \[\sum_{d\ge1}|A_d|\le nD+\frac{n^2}{4}\sum_{d>D}\Bigl(\frac1{d-1}-\frac1d\Bigr)=nD+\frac{n^2}{4D}.\] Take $D=\lceil\sqrt n/2\rceil$, so $\frac{\sqrt n}2\le D<\frac{\sqrt n}{2}+1$: the sum of the labels is less than $n\bigl(\frac{\sqrt n}2+1\bigr)+\frac{n^2}{2\sqrt n}=n^{3/2}+n\le2n^{3/2}\le100\,n^{3/2}$.

Lower bound. For the red square $\{1,\dots,k\}^2$ ($n=k^2$, $k=2m$), $A_d$ is the concentric square with side $k-2d+2$, so $|A_d|=(k-2d+2)^2$ and \[\sum_{d=1}^{m}|A_d|=4\,(1^2+2^2+\dots+m^2)>\frac{4m^3}{3}=\frac{n^{3/2}}{6}.\] For $\alpha<\frac32$ this exceeds $100\,n^\alpha$ when $n$ is large. Hence the answer is $\boxed{\alpha=\tfrac32}$.

Solution 3 (Cauchy-Schwarz on the row and column counts)

For a red point $P$ in row $y$ and column $x$, let $\ell(P)$ be its label, $r_y$ the number of red points in row $y$ and $c_x$ the number of red points in column $x$.

Upper bound. If $\ell(P)=\ell$, then the $\ell-1$ nearest points on each side of $P$ in its row are red, so $r_y\ge2\ell-1\ge\ell$; likewise $c_x\ge\ell$. Hence $\ell(P)\le\min(r_y,c_x)\le\sqrt{r_yc_x}$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, summing over the $n$ red points, \[\Bigl(\sum_{P}\ell(P)\Bigr)^2\le\Bigl(\sum_{P}\sqrt{r_yc_x}\Bigr)^2\le n\sum_{P}r_yc_x .\] All terms $r_yc_x$ are non-negative, so the last sum is at most the sum of $r_yc_x$ over all pairs (row $y$, column $x$), which factors: \[\sum_{P}r_yc_x\le\sum_{y}\sum_{x}r_yc_x=\Bigl(\sum_y r_y\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_x c_x\Bigr)=n\cdot n .\] Therefore the sum of the labels is at most $\sqrt{n\cdot n^2}=n^{3/2}\le100\,n^{3/2}$, for every $n$ and every placement.

Lower bound. Let $m\ge1$, $n=16m^2$, and colour the $4m\times4m$ square $\{1,\dots,4m\}^2$ red. A point $(x,y)$ with $m+1\le x,y\le3m$ is at distance at least $m+1$ from every point outside the square in its row and in its column, so its label is at least $m+1>m$. There are $(2m)^2$ such points, so the sum of the labels exceeds $4m^3=\frac{n^{3/2}}{16}$. If $\alpha<\frac32$, then $\frac{n^{3/2}}{16}>100\,n^\alpha$ as soon as $n^{3/2-\alpha}>1600$, which holds for large $m$.

So the smallest exponent is $\boxed{\alpha=\tfrac32}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of triangle formed by $BC$ and the two common external tangents of the circumcircles $ABM$ and $ACM$. Prove that the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ and the perpendicular bisector of $AM$ intersect on the radical axis of $\omega$ and $\Gamma$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The tangents cut BC symmetrically about M; then four concyclic points)

Notation. Let $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ be the circles $ABM$, $ACM$ with centers $O_1,O_2$ and radii $R_1,R_2$; they meet at $A$ and $M$, so the line $g=O_1O_2$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AM$. Let the common external tangents be $t,t'$; they are symmetric about $g$ and meet at a point $T\in g$ (see Step 2 for $R_1\neq R_2$). Let $t,t'$ meet $BC$ at $P,Q$, so $\omega$ is the circle $TPQ$. Let $m$ be the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, let $N$ be the second intersection of the bisector of $\angle BAC$ with $\Gamma$ (the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, a point of $m$), and let $X$ be the intersection of $g$ and the line $AN$. We must prove that $X$ has the same power with respect to $\omega$ and $\Gamma$. We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,\ell')$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: $M$ is the midpoint of $PQ$. Let $t$ touch $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ at $U_1,U_2$, let $V$ be the midpoint of $U_1U_2$ and $d=\frac12U_1U_2>0$ (the circles are not tangent to each other, so $U_1\ne U_2$). On $t$ use a coordinate with $V=0$, $U_1=-d$, $U_2=d$, and let $P$ have coordinate $x$; on $BC$ use a coordinate with $M=0$, $B=-\frac a2$, $C=\frac a2$ ($a=BC$), and let $P$ have coordinate $s$. Computing the power of $P$ with respect to $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ along the tangent and along the secant $BC$ (which meets $\Gamma_1$ at $M,B$ and $\Gamma_2$ at $M,C$): \[(x+d)^2=\overline{PM}\cdot\overline{PB}=s\Bigl(s+\frac a2\Bigr),\qquad (x-d)^2=\overline{PM}\cdot\overline{PC}=s\Bigl(s-\frac a2\Bigr).\] Adding and subtracting, $x^2+d^2=s^2$ and $4dx=as$. Hence \[s^2\Bigl(1-\frac{a^2}{16d^2}\Bigr)=d^2.\] The bracket is not $0$, since $d^2>0$; so this equation determines $s^2$. The same computation for $t'$ and $Q$ (with the same $d$, because the two external tangent segments are equal) shows that the coordinate $s'$ of $Q$ satisfies the same equation. So $s'^2=s^2$. As $P\ne Q$ (otherwise $T$ would lie on $BC$ and there would be no triangle), $s'=-s$: $M$ is the midpoint of $PQ$.

Step 2: the position of $T$. For directed angles, $\angle(BA,BM)=\angle(O_1A,O_1O_2)$: both are “half of the arc $AM$” of $\Gamma_1$, since $O_1O_2$ bisects the central angle $AO_1M$. Likewise $\angle(CA,CM)=\angle(O_2A,O_2O_1)$. So triangles $ABC$ and $AO_1O_2$ are similar with the same orientation, and there is a spiral similarity $\sigma$ with center $A$, $\sigma(B)=O_1$, $\sigma(C)=O_2$. In particular $\frac{R_1}{R_2}=\frac{AO_1}{AO_2}=\frac{AB}{AC}\neq1$ (the triangle is scalene), so the external tangents do meet, at the point $T$ of the line $O_1O_2$ outside the segment $O_1O_2$ with $\frac{TO_1}{TO_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}=\frac{AB}{AC}$. Let $D'$ be the point where the external bisector of $\angle BAC$ meets $BC$; it lies outside the segment $BC$ and $\frac{D'B}{D'C}=\frac{AB}{AC}$. Since $\sigma$ maps the line $BC$ to the line $O_1O_2$ and preserves ratios, $\sigma(D')=T$. A spiral similarity turns every line by the same angle, so \[\angle(AD',AT)=\angle(BC,O_1O_2)=\angle(BC,g).\tag{1}\]

Step 3: $A,N,T,W$ are concyclic. Let $W$ be the second intersection of $g$ with $\omega$. The line $g$ bisects an angle between the lines $TP$ and $TQ$, so $W$ is the midpoint of an arc $PQ$ of $\omega$ and lies on the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, which by Step 1 is the line $m$. So $W$ and $N$ lie on $m$, and \[\angle(WT,WN)=\angle(g,m)=\angle(AM,BC),\] because $g\perp AM$ and $m\perp BC$. On the other hand $AD'\perp AN$ (external and internal bisectors), and by (1), using $g\perp AM$ again, \[\angle(AT,AN)=\angle(AT,AD')+\angle(AD',AN)=-\angle(BC,g)+90^\circ=-\bigl(\angle(BC,AM)+90^\circ\bigr)+90^\circ=\angle(AM,BC).\] Thus $\angle(AT,AN)=\angle(WT,WN)$.

The points $A,N,T,W$ are distinct, except possibly $W=N$. The projections of $O_1,O_2$ to $BC$ are the midpoints of $BM$ and $MC$ (each center projects to the midpoint of its chord), which are different points; and $T$ lies on the line $O_1O_2$ outside the segment $O_1O_2$. Hence the projection of $T$ to $BC$ lies outside the segment between these two midpoints, so it is not $M$, i.e. $T\notin m$. As $W,N\in m$, we get $T\ne W$ (so $g$ is not tangent to $\omega$ at $T$, and $W$ is a well-defined second point) and $T\ne N$. Also $A\notin g$ (as $g$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AM$), so $A\ne T$ and $A\ne W$; and $A\ne N$. If $W=N$, this point lies on both $\omega$ and $\Gamma$ and on both lines $g$ and $AN$, so it is $X$, and $X$ has power $0$ with respect to both circles: we are done. Otherwise the four points are distinct, and the angle equality shows that $A$, $W$, $T$, $N$ lie on a circle.

Step 4: conclusion. The point $X$ exists: if $AN\parallel g$, then $AN\perp AM$; but the angle between the bisector $AN$ and the median $AM$ is less than $\frac12\angle BAC<90^\circ$. So $X$ is the intersection of the lines $AN$ and $TW$ ($=g$). In the circle through $A,N,T,W$, the power of $X$ gives $\overline{XA}\cdot\overline{XN}=\overline{XT}\cdot\overline{XW}$. But $A,N\in\Gamma$, so the left side is the power of $X$ with respect to $\Gamma$; and $T,W\in\omega$, so the right side is the power of $X$ with respect to $\omega$. Hence $X$, the intersection of the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ with the perpendicular bisector of $AM$, lies on the radical axis of $\omega$ and $\Gamma$.

Remark. The circles $\omega$ and $\Gamma$ can coincide for an acute scalene triangle: with $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and $A=\bigl(\frac{2\sqrt6}5,\frac{\sqrt6}5\bigr)$ the two tangents pass through $B$ and $C$, so $\{P,Q\}=\{B,C\}$ and $\omega=\Gamma$. Then there is no radical axis, but the argument above still shows that $X$ has equal powers with respect to the two circles, which is how the statement must be read in that case.

Solution 2 (Coordinates: the radical axis is a horizontal line)

Setup. Choose coordinates with $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, so $M=(0,0)$, and $A=(u,v)$ with $v>0$. Let $a=AB$, $b=AC$ and $S=u^2+v^2=MA^2$. The angles at $B$ and $C$ are acute, so $|u|<1$; the angle at $A$ is acute, so $A$ lies outside the circle with diameter $BC$, i.e. $S>1$. Since $a^2=S+2u+1$ and $b^2=S-2u+1$, we have $a^2-b^2=4u$, so $u\ne0$ (the triangle is scalene). Put \[K=\frac{(a+b)^2}{4},\qquad t=\frac{(a-b)^2}{4}.\] Then $u=\frac{(a-b)(a+b)}4$ gives $u^2=Kt$; also $K+t=\frac{a^2+b^2}2=S+1$; the triangle inequalities $a+b>2$ and $|a-b|<2$ give $K>1$ and $0<t<1$; and $v^2=S-u^2=K+t-1-Kt=(K-1)(1-t)$. We shall use \[u^2=Kt,\qquad S=K+t-1,\qquad v^2=(K-1)(1-t).\tag{1}\]

Step 1: the circles $ABM$ and $ACM$. Let $\Gamma_1=(ABM)$ and $\Gamma_2=(ACM)$. The center $O_1$ of $\Gamma_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=-\frac12$ of $BM$; write $O_1=(-\frac12,k_1)$. From $O_1M^2=O_1A^2$, that is $\frac14+k_1^2=(u+\frac12)^2+(v-k_1)^2$, we get $k_1=\frac{S+u}{2v}$. Its radius $r_1$ satisfies $r_1^2=\frac14+k_1^2=\frac{v^2+(S+u)^2}{4v^2}=\frac{Sa^2}{4v^2}$, because $v^2+(S+u)^2=S+2uS+S^2=S\,a^2$. In the same way $O_2=(\frac12,k_2)$ with $k_2=\frac{S-u}{2v}$ and $r_2^2=\frac{Sb^2}{4v^2}$. So $\frac{r_1}{r_2}=\frac ab\ne1$.

Step 2: the vertex of the tangent triangle. The circles $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ meet at the two points $A$ and $M$, and their radii differ, so their common external tangents meet at the external center of similitude $E$, the point of the line $O_1O_2$ with $E=\frac{r_2O_1-r_1O_2}{r_2-r_1}=\frac{bO_1-aO_2}{b-a}$; the homothety with center $E$ and ratio $\frac{r_2}{r_1}$ maps $\Gamma_1$ to $\Gamma_2$, so every line through $E$ tangent to $\Gamma_1$ is also tangent to $\Gamma_2$. Write $E=(e,h)$. Using $\frac{a+b}{a-b}=\frac{(a+b)^2}{a^2-b^2}=\frac Ku$, \begin{gather*}e=\frac{a+b}{2(a-b)}=\frac{K}{2u},\\ h=\frac{ak_2-bk_1}{a-b}=\frac{1}{2v}\Bigl(S-\frac{(a+b)u}{a-b}\Bigr)=\frac{S-K}{2v}=-\frac{1-t}{2v}<0 .\end{gather*}

Step 3: the tangents cut $BC$ at $(\pm R,0)$. A line through $E$ that is not horizontal meets the $x$-axis at one point $(z,0)$; put $w=e-z$, so the line has direction $(w,h)$. Let $p=e+\frac12$ and $q=k_1-h$, so that $O_1-E=(-p,q)$. The line is tangent to $\Gamma_1$ exactly when the distance from $O_1$ to it is $r_1$, that is $(wq+hp)^2=r_1^2(w^2+h^2)$, or \[(q^2-r_1^2)\,w^2+2pq\,h\,w+(p^2-r_1^2)\,h^2=0.\tag{2}\] We compute the coefficients with (1). First $q=\frac{S+u+1-t}{2v}=\frac{K+u}{2v}$ and $p=\frac{K+u}{2u}$, and $(K+u)^2=K^2+2Ku+Kt=Ka^2$ (as $a^2=S+2u+1=K+t+2u$). Hence \begin{gather*}q^2-r_1^2=\frac{(K-S)a^2}{4v^2}=\frac{a^2}{4(K-1)}>0,\qquad 2pqh=-\frac{(K+u)^2(1-t)}{4uv^2}=-\frac{Ka^2}{4u(K-1)},\\ p^2-r_1^2=\frac{a^2}{4}\Bigl(\frac1t-\frac S{v^2}\Bigr).\end{gather*} Dividing (2) by $q^2-r_1^2$ and using $\frac{K}{u}=2e$ and $h^2=\frac{1-t}{4(K-1)}$, it becomes \[w^2-2e\,w+c_0=0,\qquad c_0=(K-1)\Bigl(\frac1t-\frac S{v^2}\Bigr)h^2=\frac{1-t}{4t}-\frac{S}{4(K-1)} .\] Since $e^2=\frac{K^2}{4u^2}=\frac{K}{4t}$, we get $e^2-c_0=\frac{K-1+t}{4t}+\frac S{4(K-1)}=\frac{S}{4t}+\frac{S}{4(K-1)}=\frac{S^2}{4t(K-1)}$. Let $R>0$ with \[R^2=\frac{S^2}{4t(K-1)} .\] Then the equation reads $(w-e)^2=R^2$, with the two real solutions $w=e\pm R$, that is $z=\mp R$. So exactly two non-horizontal lines through $E$ are tangent to $\Gamma_1$; since there are at most two tangents to a circle through a point, these are the two common external tangents, and they meet $BC$ at $P_1=(-R,0)$ and $P_2=(R,0)$. The triangle formed by $BC$ and the tangents is $P_1P_2E$ (note $h\ne0$).

Step 4: the power difference. The center of $\omega=(P_1P_2E)$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=0$ of $P_1P_2$, so the power of a point $(x,y)$ with respect to $\omega$ is $x^2+y^2-Wy-R^2$ for some $W$; since $E\in\omega$, $W=\frac{e^2+h^2-R^2}{h}$. Similarly the power with respect to $\Gamma=(ABC)$ is $x^2+y^2-gy-1$, where $A\in\Gamma$ gives $g=\frac{S-1}{v}$. The difference of the two powers is \[\Delta(y)=(1-R^2)-(W-g)\,y .\] By (1), $h^2=\frac{1-t}{4(K-1)}$ and $hg=-\frac{(1-t)(S-1)}{2v^2}=-\frac{K+t-2}{2(K-1)}$, so over the common denominator $4t(K-1)$, \begin{align*}h(W-g)&=e^2+h^2-R^2-hg=\frac{K(K-1)+t(1-t)-S^2+2t(K+t-2)}{4t(K-1)}\\&=\frac{K-1-t}{4t(K-1)},\end{align*} where the numerator was simplified with $S=K+t-1$. Also, as $S=(K-1)+t$, we have $S^2-4t(K-1)=(K-1-t)^2$, so \[1-R^2=-\frac{(K-1-t)^2}{4t(K-1)} .\]

Step 5: the point $X$. The internal bisector from $A$ meets $BC$ at $D$ with $\frac{DB}{DC}=\frac ab$, so $D=\bigl(\frac{a-b}{a+b},0\bigr)=\bigl(\frac uK,0\bigr)$. The perpendicular bisector of $AM$ is the set of points $Y$ with $Y\cdot A=\frac S2$ (the condition $|Y|^2=|Y-A|^2$). A point $Y=(1-\lambda)A+\lambda D$ of the line $AD$ satisfies $Y\cdot A=(1-\lambda)S+\lambda\frac{u^2}K=S-\lambda(S-t)$, and $S-t=K-1>0$; so the two lines meet at exactly one point $X$, with $\lambda=\frac{S}{2(K-1)}$ and \[X_y=(1-\lambda)v=\frac{v(K-1-t)}{2(K-1)} .\] Now, using $\frac{v}{h}=-\frac{2v^2}{1-t}=-2(K-1)$, \[(W-g)X_y=\frac{K-1-t}{4t(K-1)h}\cdot\frac{v(K-1-t)}{2(K-1)}=-\frac{(K-1-t)^2}{4t(K-1)}=1-R^2,\] so $\Delta(X_y)=0$: the point $X$ has the same power with respect to $\omega$ and $\Gamma$.

Conclusion. If $K-1\ne t$, then $W\ne g$, so $\omega\ne\Gamma$ and the set of points with equal powers is the line $y=\frac{1-R^2}{W-g}$ parallel to $BC$, the radical axis; $X$ lies on it. If $K-1=t$, both coefficients of $\Delta$ vanish, so $\omega=\Gamma$ and every point, in particular $X$, has equal powers with respect to the two circles. (This really happens, for instance for $A=\bigl(\frac{2\sqrt6}5,\frac{\sqrt6}5\bigr)$, where $K=\frac85$, $t=\frac35$ and the triangle is acute and scalene; then the tangents pass through $B$ and $C$.) In every case the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ and the perpendicular bisector of $AM$ meet at a point with equal powers with respect to $\omega$ and $\Gamma$, which is the statement.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

A positive integer is called chaotic if it can be expressed as $a^3+b^3+abc$ for positive integers $a\ge b\ge c$. Show that there is no infinite increasing arithmetic progression consisting of only chaotic positive integers.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting triples in a residue class)

Suppose, for contradiction, that all terms of the arithmetic progression $x_0,\ x_0+d,\ x_0+2d,\dots$ ($x_0\ge1$, $d\ge1$) are chaotic. The idea: in a suitable sub-progression there are simply not enough triples $(a,b,c)$ to represent all terms.

Step 1: solutions modulo a prime. For a positive integer $D$ and a residue $y$ let $\rho_D(y)$ be the number of triples $(a,b,c)$ of residues modulo $D$ with $a^3+b^3+abc\equiv y\pmod D$. Let $q$ be a prime. We count the triples with $a^3+b^3+abc\equiv0\pmod q$: if $ab\not\equiv0$, then $c$ is determined ($c\equiv-(a^3+b^3)(ab)^{-1}$), which gives $(q-1)^2$ triples; if $ab\equiv0$, then $a^3+b^3\equiv0$ forces $a\equiv b\equiv0$, and $c$ is arbitrary, which gives $q$ triples. So \[\rho_q(0)=q^2-q+1=q^2\Bigl(1-\frac1q+\frac1{q^2}\Bigr)\le q^2\Bigl(1-\frac1{2q}\Bigr).\tag{1}\]

Step 2: a thin residue class. The sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges (Euler), and $1-t\le e^{-t}$; so we can choose distinct primes $q_1,\dots,q_s$, none dividing $d$, with \[\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Bigl(1-\frac1{2q_i}\Bigr)\le\exp\Bigl(-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^s\frac1{q_i}\Bigr)<\frac1{2d}.\] Let $D=d\,q_1q_2\cdots q_s$. By the Chinese remainder theorem there is a residue $y$ modulo $D$ with $y\equiv x_0\pmod d$ and $y\equiv0\pmod{q_i}$ for all $i$, and the solutions of $a^3+b^3+abc\equiv y\pmod D$ correspond to the tuples of solutions modulo $d,q_1,\dots,q_s$. With the trivial bound $\rho_d(x_0)\le d^3$ and (1), \[\rho_D(y)=\rho_d(x_0)\prod_{i=1}^s\rho_{q_i}(0)\le d^3\prod_{i=1}^{s}q_i^2\Bigl(1-\frac1{2q_i}\Bigr)<d^3\cdot\frac{1}{2d}\prod q_i^2=\frac{D^2}{2}.\tag{2}\]

Step 3: counting. The terms $x_0+kd$ with $x_0+kd\equiv y\pmod D$ are those with $k$ in one residue class modulo $q_1\cdots q_s$ (again by the Chinese remainder theorem, since $\gcd(d,q_i)=1$); they form an arithmetic progression with difference $D$. So for every $X$ the number of terms of our progression that are $\le X$ and $\equiv y\pmod D$ is at least $\frac{X-x_0}{D}-1$.

On the other hand, let $n\le X$ be chaotic with $n\equiv y\pmod D$, say $n=a^3+b^3+abc$ with $a\ge b\ge c\ge1$. Then $a^3<n\le X$, so $1\le c\le b\le a<X^{1/3}$, and $(a,b,c)$ reduces modulo $D$ to one of the $\rho_D(y)$ solution triples. Different chaotic numbers $n$ come from different triples $(a,b,c)$, so it suffices to count such triples. For a fixed residue triple there are at most $\bigl(\frac{X^{1/3}}{D}+1\bigr)^3$ triples of positive integers below $X^{1/3}$ in these residue classes. Hence, by (2), the number of chaotic $n\le X$ with $n\equiv y\pmod D$ is at most \[\rho_D(y)\Bigl(\frac{X^{1/3}}{D}+1\Bigr)^3<\frac{D^2}{2}\cdot\frac{X}{D^3}\Bigl(1+\frac{D}{X^{1/3}}\Bigr)^3=\frac{X}{2D}\Bigl(1+\frac{D}{X^{1/3}}\Bigr)^3 .\] As $X\to\infty$ this is $\frac{X}{2D}(1+o(1))$, which is smaller than $\frac{X-x_0}{D}-1$ for all large $X$. So for large $X$ the progression has more terms $\le X$ in the class $y$ modulo $D$ than there are chaotic numbers in that class: some term is not chaotic. This contradiction proves the statement.
