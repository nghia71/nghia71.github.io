Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots$ be an infinite sequence of integers. For all $n\ge 1$ integers, number $a_{n+34}$ is root of polynomial \[P_n(x)=a_{n+33}x^{33}+\dots+a_{n+1}x+a_n\] If the number $2026$ is element of this sequence find all possible values of $a_1$.

Show the answer

$a_1\in\{-1,\,0,\,2026\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Pure periodicity, then the shape of a window)

The answer is $\boxed{a_1\in\{-1,\,0,\,2026\}}$.

Throughout, $x=a_{n+34}$ and the condition for the index $n$ reads \[ a_n+a_{n+1}x+a_{n+2}x^2+\dots+a_{n+33}x^{33}=0,\qquad x=a_{n+34}. \tag{1} \]

Step 0. Two basic facts.

  • (a) From (1), $a_n=-a_{n+34}\bigl(a_{n+1}+a_{n+2}a_{n+34}+\dots+a_{n+33}a_{n+34}^{32}\bigr)$. Hence $a_{n+34}\mid a_n$; if $a_{n+34}=0$ then $a_n=0$, and if $a_n\ne0$ then $0<|a_{n+34}|\le|a_n|$.

  • (b) The same formula shows that $a_n$ is determined by the $34$ terms that follow it.

By (a), inside each residue class of indices modulo $34$ the absolute values are first $0$ (possibly never) and then positive and non-increasing (possibly never). So each class changes its absolute value only finitely often, and there is an index $N$ with \[ |a_{n+34}|=|a_n|\qquad\text{for all }n\ge N. \tag{2} \] For $n\ge N$ we call $|a_n|$ the size of the class of $n$. Step 0$'$ shows that in fact $N=1$ always works.

Step 0$'$. Every sequence is purely periodic, and $|a_{n+34}|=|a_n|$ for all $n\ge1$. Here $(a_n)$ is any sequence satisfying the condition, and $P_n(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{33}a_{n+j}t^j$.

(c) Bound. Let $B=\max(|a_1|,\dots,|a_{34}|)$. Then $|a_n|\le B$ for all $n$. By induction it suffices to show: if $|a_n|,\dots,|a_{n+33}|\le B$, then $x=a_{n+34}$ satisfies $|x|\le B$. If $x=0$ there is nothing to prove. If $x\ne0$ and $P_n$ is not the zero polynomial, let $a_{n+j}$ be its lowest non-zero coefficient; dividing $P_n(x)=0$ by $x^j$ gives $a_{n+j}+a_{n+j+1}x+\dots=0$, so $x\mid a_{n+j}$ and $|x|\le|a_{n+j}|\le B$. Finally, $P_n$ cannot be the zero polynomial when $x=c\ne0$. Indeed, if $a_n=\dots=a_{n+33}=0$, then $P_{n+1}(t)=c\,t^{33}$, whose only root is $0$, so $a_{n+35}=0$; then $P_{n+2}(t)=c\,t^{32}$, so $a_{n+36}=0$; continuing, $P_{n+i}(t)=c\,t^{34-i}$ and $a_{n+34+i}=0$ for $i=1,\dots,33$. But then $P_{n+34}(t)=c$ is a non-zero constant and has no root, a contradiction.

(d) Windows. Call a $34$-tuple $w\in\{-B,\dots,B\}^{34}$ alive if it is the beginning of some infinite integer sequence satisfying the condition, and let $\mathcal A$ be the (finite) set of alive tuples. Write $w\to w'$ if $w=(b_0,b_1,\dots,b_{33})$, $w'=(b_1,\dots,b_{34})$ and $b_{34}$ is a root of $b_0+b_1t+\dots+b_{33}t^{33}$.

  • Every $w\in\mathcal A$ has at least one $w'\in\mathcal A$ with $w\to w'$: take an infinite sequence beginning with $w$ and drop its first term. The new sequence still satisfies the condition, and by (c), applied to the old sequence, its terms have absolute value at most $B$; so its first $34$ terms form an alive tuple $w'$ with $w\to w'$.

  • Every $w'\in\mathcal A$ has at most one $w$ with $w\to w'$, because $b_0=-(b_1b_{34}+b_2b_{34}^2+\dots+b_{33}b_{34}^{33})$ is determined by $w'$ (Step 0(b)).

Count the arrows $w\to w'$ with both ends in $\mathcal A$. There are at least $|\mathcal A|$ of them (at least one out of each tuple) and at most $|\mathcal A|$ (at most one into each tuple). So every alive tuple has exactly one alive successor and exactly one alive predecessor: the map $\sigma:\mathcal A\to\mathcal A$ sending $w$ to its alive successor is a bijection of a finite set.

(e) Periodicity. All windows $w_n=(a_n,\dots,a_{n+33})$ of our sequence are alive (by (c) and because $(a_n,a_{n+1},\dots)$ satisfies the condition), and $w_n\to w_{n+1}$, so $w_{n+1}=\sigma(w_n)$. A permutation of a finite set has a power equal to the identity: $\sigma^T=\mathrm{id}$ for some $T\ge1$. Hence $w_{n+T}=w_n$, and so $a_{n+T}=a_n$ for all $n\ge1$.

(f) No class ever changes size. By Step 0(a), $a_{n+34T}\mid\dots\mid a_{n+68}\mid a_{n+34}\mid a_n$, and $a_{n+34T}=a_n$ by (e). If $a_n\ne0$, all these numbers are non-zero, their absolute values do not increase, and the first and the last are equal; so $|a_{n+34}|=|a_n|$. If $a_n=0$, then $a_{n+34T}=a_n=0$, and Step 0(a) (“$a_{k+34}=0\Rightarrow a_k=0$”), applied with $k=n+34(T-1),\dots,n+34$, gives $a_{n+34(T-1)}=\dots=a_{n+34}=0$. Hence (2) holds with $N=1$.

Step 1. The values $2026$, $-1$, $0$ occur. Let $(a_n)$ have period $34$ with \[ (a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{34})=(2026,\,-1,\,0,\,2026,\,-1,\,0,\,0,\dots,0). \] We check (1) for each residue of $n$ modulo $34$, writing $P_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{33}a_{n+j}x^j$.

  • $n\equiv1$: $P_n(x)=2026-x+2026x^3-x^4=(2026-x)(1+x^3)$, and $a_{n+34}=2026$ is a root.

  • $n\equiv4$: the non-zero coefficients are $a_n=2026$, $a_{n+1}=-1$, $a_{n+31}=2026$, $a_{n+32}=-1$, so $P_n(x)=(2026-x)(1+x^{31})$, and $a_{n+34}=2026$ is a root.

  • $n\equiv2$: $P_n(x)=-1+2026x^2-x^3+2026x^{33}$ and $a_{n+34}=-1$: $P_n(-1)=-1+2026+1-2026=0$.

  • $n\equiv5$: $P_n(x)=-1+2026x^{30}-x^{31}+2026x^{33}$ and $a_{n+34}=-1$: $P_n(-1)=-1+2026+1-2026=0$.

  • all other $n$: $a_n=0$ and $a_{n+34}=0$, so $P_n(a_{n+34})=P_n(0)=a_n=0$.

So this sequence satisfies the condition, contains $2026$, and has $a_1=2026$. Deleting its first term, or its first two terms, gives sequences that still satisfy the condition (the condition for $n$ only involves $a_n,\dots,a_{n+34}$) and still contain $2026$; they start with $-1$ and with $0$.

Step 2. Theorem. If a sequence satisfying the condition has infinitely many terms with $|a_n|\ge3$, then it is periodic with period $34$ and all its terms lie in $\{c,-1,0\}$ for one integer $c$ with $|c|\ge3$. In particular, if $2026$ is one of its terms, then $c=2026$ and $a_1\in\{2026,-1,0\}$.

Proof. Let $M\ge3$ be the largest size of a class. Below, all indices are at least $N$.

(i) After a class of size $M$ comes a class of size $1$. Let $|a_{n+33}|=M$ and let $d=|a_{n+34}|=|a_n|$. If $d\ge2$, then (1) gives \[ M d^{33}=|a_{n+33}x^{33}|\le\sum_{j=0}^{32}|a_{n+j}|\,d^{\,j}\le M\cdot\frac{d^{33}-1}{d-1}<Md^{33}, \] which is absurd. So $d\le1$. Next let $|a_{n'}|=M$ and $x=a_{n'+34}=\pm M$. Dividing (1) by $x$ gives $a_{n'}/x+a_{n'+1}+x(\dots)=0$ with $a_{n'}/x=\pm1$, so $a_{n'+1}\equiv\pm1\pmod M$ and $a_{n'+1}\ne0$. Hence the class after a class of size $M$ has size exactly $1$.

(ii) The window of a big term. Call $t$ big if $|a_t|=M$. Let $n$ be big, $x=a_{n+34}$ (so $|x|=M$), and put $R_i=\sum_{j=i}^{33}a_{n+j}x^{\,j-i}$ for $0\le i\le 33$ and $R_{34}=0$. Then $R_0=0$ and $R_i=a_{n+i}+xR_{i+1}$. Suppose $R_i=0$ for some $i\le33$. Then $M\mid a_{n+i}$ and $|a_{n+i}|\le M$, so $a_{n+i}\in\{0,\pm M\}$.

  • If $a_{n+i}=0$, then $R_{i+1}=0$.

  • If $a_{n+i}=\pm M$, then $i\le32$ (for $i=33$ we would get $R_{33}=a_{n+33}\ne0$), $a_{n+i+1}=\pm1$ by (i), and $0=R_i=(a_{n+i}+a_{n+i+1}x)+x^2R_{i+2}$. The bracket is $0$ or $\pm2M$ and is divisible by $M^2>2M$; so \[ a_{n+i}+a_{n+i+1}\,a_{n+34}=0 \tag{3} \] and $R_{i+2}=0$.

Starting from $R_0=0$ we run through the whole window $a_n,\dots,a_{n+33}$: it consists of zeros and of pairs $(a_t,a_{t+1})$ with $t$ big and $|a_{t+1}|=1$, and (3) holds for every big $t$ with $n\le t\le n+32$. Since the window meets every class, every class has size $M$, $1$ or $0$, and the classes of size $1$ are exactly those that follow a class of size $M$.

(iii) Signs. There are at least two classes of size $M$. Indeed, if there were only one, then for a big $n$ the only non-zero terms among $a_{n+1},\dots,a_{n+34}$ would be $a_{n+1}=\pm1$ and $a_{n+34}=\pm M$, and (1) for the index $n+1$ would read $a_{n+1}+a_{n+34}y^{33}=0$ with $y=a_{n+35}=\pm1$, that is $\pm1\pm M=0$.

So consecutive big indices $n<n'$ satisfy $n'\le n+32$: since there are at least two classes of size $M$ and the window $a_n,\dots,a_{n+33}$ meets every class, some big index lies in $\{n+1,\dots,n+33\}$, so $n'\le n+33$; and $n'=n+33$ is impossible, because then the class of $n'+1=n+34$, which follows the big class of $n'$, would have size $1$ by (i), while it is the class of $n$ and has size $M\ge3$. Applying (3) to the window of $n$ and to the window of $n'$, both with $t=n'$, gives $a_{n'}=-a_{n'+1}a_{n+34}=-a_{n'+1}a_{n'+34}$, hence $a_{n'+34}=a_{n+34}$. Therefore all big terms with index at least $N+34$ are equal to one number $c$, $|c|=M$. For such a big $t$, (3) with $n=t$ gives $c=-a_{t+1}c$, so $a_{t+1}=-1$. Thus from the index $N+35$ on, the sequence is: $c$ at the big indices, $-1$ right after them, $0$ elsewhere. It is periodic with period $34$ from there on.

(iv) Going back. Let $(b_n)_{n\in\mathbb Z}$ be the $34$-periodic extension of $(a_n)_{n\ge N+35}$ to all integers. It satisfies (1) for all large $n$, hence, by periodicity, for all $n$. By Step 0(b), $a_n$ and $b_n$ are computed from the following $34$ terms by the same formula, so $a_n=b_n$ for all $n\ge1$ by downward induction. Hence $(a_n)$ is periodic with terms in $\{c,-1,0\}$. If $2026$ is a term, then $c=2026$, and $a_1\in\{2026,-1,0\}$. $\blacksquare$

End of the proof. Let $2026$ be a term of the sequence. By Step 0$'$(f) every term in its class has absolute value $2026$, so the sequence has infinitely many terms of absolute value at least $3$, and the largest size of a class is $M\ge2026$. By the Theorem of Step 2 the sequence is $34$-periodic with terms in $\{c,-1,0\}$, $|c|=M$; as $2026$ is a term, $c=2026$ and $a_1\in\{2026,-1,0\}$. Together with Step 1, the possible values are exactly $\boxed{a_1\in\{-1,\,0,\,2026\}}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

There are $n$ balls labeled from $1$ to $n$, placed in a line in a random order. In each move, Aslı takes the ball at position $k$ and places it at the end of the line, where $k$ is the number written on the ball currently at the end of the line. The process stops when the number on the ball at the end of the line is $n$. What is the maximum possible number of moves Aslı can make?

Show the answer

$2^{n-1}-1$

Show the solution

Solution (Following ball n in a more general game)

The answer is $\boxed{2^{n-1}-1}$.

A more general game. Take a line of $L\ge1$ balls. Some balls carry labels, which are distinct numbers from $\{1,\dots,L-1\}$; the other balls (at least one) are blank. If the last ball has label $k$, a move takes the ball at position $k$ and puts it at the end. The game stops when the last ball is blank. Let $g(L)$ be the largest possible number of moves.

The process of the problem is this game with $L=n$, where ball $n$ is the only blank ball. So it is enough to prove that $g(L)\le 2^{L-1}-1$ and to find a start with $2^{n-1}-1$ moves.

Upper bound: $g(L)\le2^{L-1}-1$. Induction on $L$. For $L=1$ the only ball is blank and there are $0$ moves. Let $L\ge2$ and fix one blank ball $s$, at position $p$. A move with label $k$ shifts the balls at positions $k+1,\dots,L$ one place to the left and puts the ball of position $k$ last. Hence

  • if $k>p$, the ball $s$ and all balls before it stay where they are;

  • if $k<p$, the ball $s$ moves to position $p-1$;

  • if $k=p$, the ball $s$ becomes last and the game stops.

So the play splits into phases during which $s$ stays at one position $p$; a phase ends with one move of label $k\le p$, after which either $s$ is at position $p-1$ or the game is over. (A phase may consist of $0$ moves before this final move; and if $s$ starts in the last position, the game has $0$ moves.) The positions of $s$ in different phases are different numbers from $\{1,\dots,L-1\}$ (when $s$ is at position $L$ the game is over).

Consider the phase with $s$ at position $p$. Only the last $L'=L-p$ balls move, and every label used is larger than $p$. Subtract $p$ from the labels larger than $p$ (they become distinct numbers from $\{1,\dots,L'-1\}$) and regard the balls with labels at most $p$ as blank. Then the phase is a play of the general game with $L'$ balls: a label $k>p$ moves the ball at position $k-p$ among the last $L'$ balls, and among these $L'$ balls at least one is blank because only $L'-1$ labels larger than $p$ exist. By the induction hypothesis the phase has at most $g(L')\le2^{L'-1}-1$ moves, and it is followed by at most one more move. Summing over the possible positions $p=1,\dots,L-1$, \[ g(L)\le\sum_{L'=1}^{L-1}\bigl(2^{L'-1}-1+1\bigr)=2^{L-1}-1 . \]

A start with $2^{n-1}-1$ moves. We prove by induction on $L$:

Claim $E(L)$. The general game started from $(L-1,L-2,\dots,2,\ast,1)$, where $\ast$ is a blank ball, lasts exactly $2^{L-1}-1$ moves and ends in $(L-1,L-2,\dots,2,1,\ast)$. (For $L=1$ the start is $(\ast)$, for $L=2$ it is $(\ast,1)$.)

$E(1)$ is clear, and for $L=2$ the only move turns $(\ast,1)$ into $(1,\ast)$. Let $L\ge3$ and assume $E(L')$ for all $L'<L$. We show, for $j=1,2,\dots,L-1$, that after $2^{j}-2$ moves the position is \[ S_j=(L-j,\ L-j-1,\ \dots,\ 2,\ \ast,\ L-1,\ L-2,\ \dots,\ L-j+1,\ 1). \] $S_1$ is the start. Suppose we are in $S_j$ with $j\le L-2$. The last label is $1$, so the first ball (label $L-j$) goes to the end: \[ (L-j-1,\ \dots,\ 2,\ \ast,\ L-1,\ \dots,\ L-j+1,\ 1,\ L-j). \] Now $\ast$ is at position $p=L-j-1\ge1$. After subtracting $p$ from the labels larger than $p$, the last $j+1$ balls read $(j,\ j-1,\ \dots,\ 2,\ \ast',\ 1)$, where $\ast'$ is the ball with label $1$, which is blank for this phase. By $E(j+1)$ exactly $2^{j}-1$ moves follow, and they end with the last $j+1$ balls in the order $(j,\dots,1,\ast')$, that is $(L-1,\dots,L-j,1)$. This is $S_{j+1}$, reached after $(2^j-2)+1+(2^j-1)=2^{j+1}-2$ moves.

Finally $S_{L-1}=(\ast,L-1,\dots,2,1)$: the last label is $1$, the ball $\ast$ goes to the end and the game stops in $(L-1,\dots,1,\ast)$ after $2^{L-1}-2+1=2^{L-1}-1$ moves. This proves $E(L)$.

For the problem take $L=n$ and $\ast=$ ball $n$: the start $(n-1,n-2,\dots,2,n,1)$ gives $2^{n-1}-1$ moves (for $n=1$: $0$ moves). Together with the upper bound, the maximum is $\boxed{2^{n-1}-1}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $\omega$ be the incircle of triangle $ABC$ with center $I$. Let the points where $\omega$ is tangent to sides $BC$, $CA$, and $AB$ be $D$, $E$, and $F$, respectively. Let $\Omega$ be the circle passing through $A$ and tangent to $BC$ at $D$. Let the second intersection points of lines $DF$ and $DE$ with $\Omega$ be $F_1$ and $E_1$, respectively. Let $T$ be the intersection point of lines $BF_1$ and $CE_1$. Let $U$ be the second intersection point of the circumcircles of triangles $DBF_1$ and $DCE_1$. Prove that \[\angle BTA=\angle CTU.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Homothety at D, then an altitude and a diameter)

Let $r$ be the radius of $\omega$, let $O$ and $\varrho$ be the centre and radius of $\Omega$, and put $k=\varrho/r$. Both circles touch $BC$ at $D$ from the same side, so the homothety $h$ with centre $D$ and ratio $k$ maps $\omega$ to $\Omega$. Hence \[ F_1=h(F),\qquad E_1=h(E),\qquad O=h(I),\qquad E_1F_1\parallel EF . \] Since $A$ lies on $\Omega$ and outside $\omega$, the circle $\Omega$ is the larger one: $k>1$. So $F$ lies between $D$ and $F_1$, with $DF_1=k\cdot DF$ and $FF_1=(k-1)\cdot DF$; the same holds for $E,E_1$.

Step 1: $T$ lies on the line $AI$. For a point $P$ let $\delta_a(P),\delta_b(P),\delta_c(P)$ be the signed distances from $P$ to the lines $BC,CA,AB$, positive on the side where the triangle lies. As $BD=BF$, the line $DF$ makes the same angle $\varphi=90^\circ-\tfrac12\angle B$ with $BC$ and with $BA$. The point $F_1$ is on the ray $DF$ beyond $F$, so it lies on the triangle's side of $BC$ and on the other side of $AB$: \[ \delta_a(F_1)=DF_1\sin\varphi=k\,DF\sin\varphi,\qquad \delta_c(F_1)=-FF_1\sin\varphi=-(k-1)\,DF\sin\varphi . \] So the function $k\,\delta_c+(k-1)\,\delta_a$, which is linear in the coordinates and not constant, vanishes at $F_1$ and at $B$; its zero set is the line $BF_1$. In the same way $k\,\delta_b+(k-1)\,\delta_a$ vanishes exactly on the line $CE_1$. At the point $T$ both vanish, hence $\delta_b(T)=\delta_c(T)$. The points with $\delta_b=\delta_c$ form the bisector line $AI$ of angle $A$. So $T\in AI$.

We locate $T$ on this line. Let $h_a=\delta_a(A)$ be the altitude from $A$. The point of $\omega$ opposite to $D$ has $\delta_a=2r$, lies in the triangle and is not $A$ (as $A$ is outside $\omega$), so $h_a>2r$. Write the points of $AI$ as $X=A+s(I-A)$; since $\delta_a,\delta_b,\delta_c$ are linear, $\delta_b(X)=\delta_c(X)=sr$ and $\delta_a(X)=h_a+s(r-h_a)$. The condition $k\,\delta_c+(k-1)\,\delta_a=0$ becomes $s\bigl((2k-1)r-(k-1)h_a\bigr)=-(k-1)h_a\ne0$; so the bracket is not $0$, and \[ \delta_a(T)=\frac{k\,r\,h_a}{(2k-1)r-(k-1)h_a},\qquad \delta_b(T)=\delta_c(T)=-\frac{k-1}{k}\,\delta_a(T). \tag{$\ast$} \] In particular $\delta_a(T)\ne0,h_a$ (the latter because $s\ne0$), so $T\ne A$ and $T\notin BC$; the line $TA$ is well defined.

Since $AE=AF$, the line $AI$ is perpendicular to $EF$, hence to $E_1F_1$: \[ TA\perp E_1F_1. \tag{1} \]

Step 2: the point $U$. Use coordinates with origin $D$, the line $BC$ as $x$-axis and $I=(0,r)$; let $B=(-p,0)$. Write $F=(u,v)$. The line $BI$ is the perpendicular bisector of $DF$, so $\overrightarrow{DF}\perp\overrightarrow{BI}=(p,r)$, i.e. $pu+rv=0$; and $F\in\omega$ gives $u^2+v^2=2rv$. Let $U_0=(0,(2k-1)r)$. With $F_1=(ku,kv)$, \[ \overrightarrow{F_1U_0}\cdot\overrightarrow{F_1B}=(-ku)(-p-ku)+\bigl((2k-1)r-kv\bigr)(-kv)=k(pu+rv)+k^2(u^2+v^2-2rv)=0 . \] So $\angle BF_1U_0=90^\circ=\angle BDU_0$: the points $D$ and $F_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $BU_0$, which is therefore the circle $DBF_1$. The expression for $U_0$ does not involve $B$, so the same computation with $C$ shows that $U_0$ lies on the circle $DCE_1$. The two circles are different and meet at $D$ and at $U_0\ne D$, so $U=U_0$. (Thus $U$ is the reflection of $I$ in $O$.) We have shown \[ \angle BF_1U=\angle CE_1U=90^\circ . \tag{2} \]

Step 3: the angles. We use directed angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$ and first prove \[ \angle(TB,TA)=\angle(TU,TC). \tag{3} \] Note that $U\ne T$: by Step 2, $\delta_a(U)=DU=(2k-1)r$, and below we see that $\delta_a(T)\ne(2k-1)r$. Also $U\ne F_1$: otherwise $F=h^{-1}(F_1)$ would lie on the line $DI$, i.e. $F$ would be the point of $\omega$ opposite to $D$, where the tangent is parallel to $BC$, but the tangent at $F$ is $AB$. Similarly $U\ne E_1$.

Degenerate case $T=F_1$ (or similarly $T=E_1$). Then the line $TC$ is the line $CE_1=E_1F_1$, so $TA\perp TC$ by (1), and $TU\perp TB$ by (2). Hence $\angle(TB,TA)=\angle(TB,TC)+90^\circ=\angle(TU,TB)+\angle(TB,TC)=\angle(TU,TC)$, which is (3).

General case $T\ne F_1,E_1$. By (2), $UF_1\perp TF_1$ and $UE_1\perp TE_1$, so $F_1$ and $E_1$ lie on the circle with diameter $TU$. From this circle, from (2) and from (1), \begin{align*} \angle(TU,TE_1)&=\angle(F_1U,F_1E_1)=\angle(F_1U,F_1T)+\angle(F_1T,F_1E_1)=90^\circ+\angle(F_1T,F_1E_1)\\ &=\angle(F_1T,F_1E_1)+\angle(F_1E_1,TA)=\angle(TF_1,TA). \end{align*} Since $TF_1$ is the line $TB$ and $TE_1$ is the line $TC$, this is (3). (In triangle $TF_1E_1$ the lines $TA$ and $TU$ are the altitude from $T$ and the diameter of the circumcircle through $T$, which are isogonal.)

From lines to rays. Equation (3) is a statement about lines; we now show that it gives the equality of the ordinary angles. Let $A'=AI\cap BC$, a point strictly inside the segment $BC$. By $(\ast)$ there are two cases.

  • (I) $\delta_a(T)>0$. Then $\delta_b(T)=\delta_c(T)<0$, so $T$ lies on the line $AI$ beyond $A$, and $A$ lies between $T$ and $A'$.

  • (II) $\delta_a(T)<0$. Then $T$ lies on the line $AI$ beyond $BC$, and $A'$ lies between $A$ and $T$.

In both cases the ray $TA$ passes through $A'$, so the ray $TA$ lies inside the angle $BTC$. Let $\rho$ be the reflection in the bisector of the angle $BTC$; it swaps the rays $TB$ and $TC$. By (3), $\angle(\rho(TA),TC)=\angle(\rho(TA),\rho(TB))=-\angle(TA,TB)=\angle(TU,TC)$, so $\rho$ maps the line $TA$ to the line $TU$. Hence the ray $TU$ is either $\rho(\text{ray }TA)$, which lies inside the angle $BTC$, or the opposite ray, which lies inside the vertical angle. The points of the vertical angle are $X=T-\lambda(B-T)-\mu(C-T)$ with $\lambda,\mu\ge0$, and for them $\delta_a(X)=(1+\lambda+\mu)\,\delta_a(T)$, because $\delta_a(B)=\delta_a(C)=0$. So $\delta_a(X)\ge\delta_a(T)$ in case (I) and $\delta_a(X)<0$ in case (II). But $\delta_a(U)=(2k-1)r>0$, and in case (I) the denominator in $(\ast)$ is positive and smaller than $(2k-1)r-2(k-1)r=r$ (as $h_a>2r$, $k>1$), so \[ \delta_a(T)>\frac{k\,r\,h_a}{r}=k\,h_a>2kr>(2k-1)r=\delta_a(U). \] Thus in neither case is $U$ in the vertical angle (and $U\ne T$, as promised). So the ray $TU$ is $\rho(\text{ray }TA)$, and since $\rho$ maps the ray $TB$ to the ray $TC$, it maps the angle $BTA$ onto the angle $CTU$. Therefore $\angle BTA=\angle CTU$.

Solution 2 (Complex coordinates with the touch point at the origin)

We work in the complex plane, identifying the point $(x,y)$ with $x+yi$. Take $D=0$, the line $BC$ as the real axis, the triangle in the upper half-plane, and the inradius equal to $1$. Then $I=i$, $B=-b$ and $C=c$ with $b=DB>0$, $c=DC>0$. Put \[ p=bc,\qquad w=b-c . \] We assume, as the statement does, that the point $T$ and the circles $DBF_1$, $DCE_1$ exist.

The points $A$, $F$, $E$. In the right triangle $BDI$ we have $\tan\frac{\angle B}2=\frac{ID}{BD}=\frac1b$, hence \[ \cos\angle B=\frac{b^2-1}{b^2+1},\qquad \sin\angle B=\frac{2b}{b^2+1}. \] The ray $BA$ leaves $B$ in the direction $(\cos\angle B,\sin\angle B)$, so the line $BA$ is $2b(x+b)=(b^2-1)y$, and the touch point $F$, at distance $BF=BD=b$ from $B$ on this ray, is \[ F=-b+b(\cos\angle B+i\sin\angle B)=\frac{-2b+2b^2i}{1+b^2}. \] In the same way ($\tan\frac{\angle C}2=\frac1c$, the ray $CA$ has direction $(-\cos\angle C,\sin\angle C)$) the line $CA$ is $2c(c-x)=(c^2-1)y$ and \[ E=\frac{2c+2c^2i}{1+c^2}. \] The two line equations are satisfied by $x=\frac{b-c}{bc-1}$, $y=\frac{2bc}{bc-1}$ (substitute; for instance $2b\bigl(\frac{b-c}{bc-1}+b\bigr)=\frac{2bc(b^2-1)}{bc-1}$), so \[ A=\frac{w+2p\,i}{p-1}. \] As $A$ lies above $BC$, its imaginary part $\frac{2p}{p-1}$ is positive, so $p>1$.

The circle $\Omega$ and the points $F_1$, $E_1$. A circle touching the real axis at $0$ from above has an equation $x^2+y^2=qy$, where $q>0$ is its diameter. The incircle $\omega$ is $x^2+y^2=2y$; let $\Omega$ be $x^2+y^2=qy$. Then $q>2$: if $q\le2$, every point of $\Omega$ has $y\ge0$ and $x^2+y^2=qy\le2y$, so it lies on or inside $\omega$, while $A\in\Omega$ lies outside the incircle.

The map $z\mapsto\frac q2z$ sends the circle $x^2+y^2=2y$ onto the circle $x^2+y^2=qy$ and maps every line through $0$ onto itself. The line $DF$ meets $\omega$ at $D$ and $F\ne D$, hence it meets $\Omega$ at $D$ and $\frac q2F$. So \[ F_1=\frac q2F=\frac{-qb+qb^2i}{1+b^2},\qquad E_1=\frac q2E=\frac{qc+qc^2i}{1+c^2}. \]

The point $T$. We have $F_1-B=\frac{b}{1+b^2}\bigl((1+b^2-q)+qb\,i\bigr)$ and $E_1-C=\frac{c}{1+c^2}\bigl((q-1-c^2)+qc\,i\bigr)$, so \[ BF_1:\ qb(x+b)=(1+b^2-q)\,y,\qquad CE_1:\ qc(x-c)=(q-1-c^2)\,y . \] Multiply the first equation by $c$, the second by $b$, and subtract: $qp(b+c)=\bigl(c(1+b^2-q)+b(1+c^2-q)\bigr)y=(b+c)(p+1-q)\,y$. Put \[ \Delta=p+1-q . \] If $\Delta=0$ the two lines are parallel and $T$ does not exist; so $\Delta\ne0$ and $y_T=\frac{pq}{\Delta}$. The first equation then gives $x_T=\frac{(1+b^2-q)c}{\Delta}-b=\frac{c+b^2c-qc-b^2c-b+bq}{\Delta}=\frac{w(q-1)}{\Delta}$. Thus \[ T=\frac{w(q-1)+pq\,i}{\Delta}. \]

The point $U$. The circle $x^2+y^2+bx-(q-1)y=0$ passes through $D=0$, through $B=-b$, and through $F_1$, because \[ |F_1|^2+b\operatorname{Re}F_1-(q-1)\operatorname{Im}F_1=\frac{q^2b^2-qb^2-(q-1)qb^2}{1+b^2}=0 . \] As $F_1$ is not on $BC$, this is the circle $DBF_1$. In the same way $x^2+y^2-cx-(q-1)y=0$ is the circle $DCE_1$. A common point of the two circles satisfies $(b+c)x=0$, so $x=0$ and $y^2=(q-1)y$. The common point other than $D$ is therefore \[ U=(q-1)\,i . \]

Four differences. Multiplying by $\Delta$ and simplifying real and imaginary parts separately: \begin{align*} \Delta(A-T)&=-\frac{p(q-2)}{p-1}\bigl(w+(p+1)i\bigr),\\ \Delta(U-T)&=-\bigl(w(q-1)+(p+(q-1)^2)\,i\bigr),\\ \Delta(B-T)&=c(q-1-b^2)-pq\,i,\\ \Delta(C-T)&=-b(q-1-c^2)-pq\,i . \end{align*} For the first line: the real part is $\frac{w\Delta}{p-1}-w(q-1)=\frac{w(\Delta-(p-1)(q-1))}{p-1}=\frac{w(2p-pq)}{p-1}$, and the imaginary part is $\frac{2p\Delta}{p-1}-pq=\frac{p(2p+2-q-pq)}{p-1}=-\frac{p(p+1)(q-2)}{p-1}$. For the second: $(q-1)\Delta-pq=-p-(q-1)^2$. For the third: $-b\Delta-w(q-1)=-bp+c(q-1)=c(q-1-b^2)$, using $bp=b^2c$. For the fourth: $c\Delta-w(q-1)=cp-b(q-1)=-b(q-1-c^2)$.

The key identity. Let $Z=\bigl(w+(p+1)i\bigr)\bigl(w(q-1)+(p+(q-1)^2)i\bigr)$. We claim \[ \Delta^2(B-T)(C-T)=p\,Z. \tag{1} \] Imaginary parts. On the left, $pq\bigl(b(q-1-c^2)-c(q-1-b^2)\bigr)=pq\bigl(w(q-1)+bc(b-c)\bigr)=pqw(p+q-1)$. On the right, $p\,w\bigl(p+(q-1)^2+(p+1)(q-1)\bigr)=pw(q^2-q+pq)=pqw(p+q-1)$.

Real parts. The left side has real part $-p\bigl[(q-1-b^2)(q-1-c^2)+pq^2\bigr]$ and the right side $-p\bigl[(p+1)(p+(q-1)^2)-w^2(q-1)\bigr]$. With $b^2+c^2=w^2+2p$, \[ (q-1-b^2)(q-1-c^2)+pq^2=(q-1)^2-(q-1)w^2-2p(q-1)+p^2+pq^2, \] and the difference between $(p+1)(p+(q-1)^2)-w^2(q-1)$ and this is $p+p(q-1)^2+2p(q-1)-pq^2=p\bigl((q-1)+1\bigr)^2-pq^2=0$. This proves (1).

From the first two lines of the table, $\Delta^2(A-T)(U-T)=\frac{p(q-2)}{p-1}Z$. Together with (1), \[ (A-T)(U-T)=\lambda\,(B-T)(C-T),\qquad \lambda=\frac{q-2}{p-1}>0 . \] Here $Z\ne0$, since both of its factors have positive imaginary part; hence all four differences are non-zero, that is, $T$ is different from $A,B,C,U$. Dividing, \[ \frac{A-T}{B-T}=\lambda\cdot\frac{C-T}{U-T}. \]

Conclusion. For points $X,Y\ne T$, the argument of $\frac{Y-T}{X-T}$ is the angle of the rotation about $T$ that takes the ray $TX$ to the ray $TY$; if $\operatorname{Arg}$ denotes the argument chosen in $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$, then $\angle XTY=\bigl|\operatorname{Arg}\frac{Y-T}{X-T}\bigr|$. Two complex numbers that differ by a positive factor have the same argument. Hence \[ \angle BTA=\Bigl|\operatorname{Arg}\frac{A-T}{B-T}\Bigr|=\Bigl|\operatorname{Arg}\frac{C-T}{U-T}\Bigr|=\angle UTC, \] that is, $\angle BTA=\angle CTU$. (Because the factor $\lambda$ is positive, not merely real, this gives equal angles and not supplementary ones, whatever the position of $T$.)

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Aslı and Zehra play a game on a board with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Initially, Aslı writes integers on each cell of the board such that all rows contain different numbers internally. After that, Aslı and Zehra take turns making moves, with Zehra making the first move. In each of her moves, Zehra chooses an integer that hasn't been chosen yet, and colors all cells containing that number red. In each of her moves, Aslı chooses an integer that hasn't been chosen yet, and colors all cells containing that number blue. If Aslı can guarantee that there are at least $N$ rows that contain only blue cells after all the cells are colored, find the maximum possible value of $N$.

Show the answer

$\left\lfloor \dfrac{m}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor$

Show the solution

Solution (A weight that Zehra never lets grow, and a binary tree with a pairing strategy)

The answer is $N=\boxed{\left\lfloor \dfrac{m}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor}$. A player may choose any integer that has not been chosen; choosing an integer that is not on the board colours nothing. If every chosen integer has to be on the board, the game is finite; otherwise we only consider plays in which all cells get coloured, as the statement speaks of the moment “after all the cells are colored”. The argument below works in both readings.

Upper bound (Zehra's strategy). Fix any board. At any moment call a row alive if it has no red cell, and give an alive row with $j$ uncoloured cells the weight $2^{-j}$. Let $\Phi$ be the sum of the weights of the alive rows. At the start $\Phi=m\cdot2^{-n}$. At the end every alive row is completely blue and has weight $1$, so \[ \Phi_{\text{end}}=\text{number of rows that are completely blue}. \] For an integer $z$ not yet chosen let $w(z)$ be the total weight of the alive rows containing $z$. Zehra always chooses an integer $z$ with $w(z)$ as large as possible. Her move kills the alive rows containing $z$, so $\Phi$ decreases by $w(z)$. Then Aslı chooses some $y$: the alive rows containing $y$ lose one uncoloured cell each, so their weights double and $\Phi$ increases by their present total weight. These rows were alive and contained $y$ already before Zehra's move, so this total is at most $w(y)\le w(z)$. Hence after each pair of moves (Zehra, then Aslı) the value of $\Phi$ is not larger than before, and a last move of Zehra alone only decreases it. So the number of blue rows is $\Phi_{\text{end}}\le m/2^n$, and being an integer it is at most $\lfloor m/2^n\rfloor$.

Lower bound (Aslı's board and strategy). Let $q=\lfloor m/2^n\rfloor$; we may assume $q\ge1$. For every word $w$ of length $1\le j\le n$ in the letters $0,1$ choose an integer $c(w)$, different words getting different integers. For every word $b_1b_2\dots b_n$ Aslı writes $q$ rows equal to \[ \bigl(c(b_1),\ c(b_1b_2),\ \dots,\ c(b_1b_2\dots b_n)\bigr), \] and she fills the remaining $m-q\,2^n$ rows with copies of one of these rows. The numbers in a row are distinct. (The numbers are the vertices of a binary tree of depth $n$ without its root, and the rows are the paths from the root to the leaves.)

Pair the numbers $c(w0)$ and $c(w1)$ for every word $w$ of length less than $n$ (including the empty word). Every number on the board is in exactly one pair. Aslı plays as follows: when Zehra chooses a number of a pair, Aslı chooses the other number of that pair; when Zehra chooses an integer that is not on the board, so does Aslı. After each move of Aslı every pair is either untouched or has one red and one blue number, so her reply is always available. At the end each pair has exactly one blue number. Choose $b_1$ with $c(b_1)$ blue, then $b_2$ with $c(b_1b_2)$ blue, and so on. All cells of the $q$ rows of the word $b_1\dots b_n$ are blue. So Aslı gets at least $q$ blue rows.

Both bounds together give $N=\boxed{\lfloor m/2^n\rfloor}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Find all infinite positive integer sequences $a_1,a_2,\dots$ such that \[\varphi(a_i)\varphi(a_{i+1})=a_i^2\] holds for all positive integers $i$.

Note: For each positive integer $n$, define $\varphi(n)$ as the number of positive integers coprime with $n$ which are at most $n$.

Show the answer

Exactly the sequences in which every step $a_i\to a_{i+1}$ is one of $1\to1$, $1\to2$, $2^k\to2^{k+2}$, $2^k\to3\cdot2^{k+1}$, $2^a3^b\to2^a3^{b+2}$ ($k,a,b\ge1$). In particular $a_i=1$; $a_i=2^{k+2(i-1)}$; $a_i=2^a3^{b+2(i-1)}$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting the factors 2)

Answer. The sequences are exactly those in which every step $a_i\to a_{i+1}$ is of one of the five kinds \[ 1\to1,\qquad 1\to2,\qquad 2^k\to2^{k+2},\qquad 2^k\to3\cdot2^{k+1},\qquad 2^a3^b\to2^a3^{b+2}\qquad(k,a,b\ge1). \] So a sequence consists of a (possibly empty, possibly infinite) block of $1$'s, then a (possibly empty, possibly infinite) block of powers of $2$ whose exponents increase by $2$ (starting with $2^1$ if it follows a $1$), then possibly an infinite block $2^a3^b,2^a3^{b+2},2^a3^{b+4},\dots$ (with $b=1$ and $a=k+1$ if it follows $2^k$). The simplest ones are $a_i=1$, $a_i=2^{k+2(i-1)}$ and $a_i=2^a3^{b+2(i-1)}$.

These work. Check $\varphi(x)\varphi(y)=x^2$ for each step $x\to y$: $\varphi(1)\varphi(1)=1$; $\varphi(1)\varphi(2)=1$; $\varphi(2^k)\varphi(2^{k+2})=2^{k-1}2^{k+1}=2^{2k}$; $\varphi(2^k)\varphi(3\cdot2^{k+1})=2^{k-1}\cdot2^{k+1}=2^{2k}$; $\varphi(2^a3^b)\varphi(2^a3^{b+2})=2^a3^{b-1}\cdot2^a3^{b+1}=(2^a3^b)^2$.

There are no others. Let $(a_i)$ be a solution. We write the condition as \[ \varphi(a_{i+1})=\frac{a_i^2}{\varphi(a_i)} . \tag{1} \]

Step 1: parity. If $a_i>1$ were odd, then $\varphi(a_i)$ would be even and could not divide the odd number $a_i^2$. So every term is $1$ or even. If $a_i$ is even, then $a_i^2/\varphi(a_i)>a_i\ge2$, so $\varphi(a_{i+1})>1$ and $a_{i+1}$ is even too.

Step 2: the largest prime factor does not decrease. Let $a_i>1$ and let $P$ be its largest prime factor, with exponent $e$. In $\varphi(a_i)=\prod_{q\mid a_i}q^{e_q-1}(q-1)$ the prime $P$ occurs exactly $e-1$ times, because $P$ does not divide $q-1$ for $q\le P$. So $P$ occurs exactly $e+1\ge2$ times in $\varphi(a_{i+1})$. Hence $a_{i+1}$ is divisible by $P$ or by a prime $q\equiv1\pmod P$. In particular: if $a_i$ has an odd prime factor, so has $a_{i+1}$.

Step 3: counting twos. For an even number $x$ let $t(x)$ be the exponent of $2$ in $x$ and let $w(x)=\sum v_2(q-1)$ over the odd primes $q\mid x$; thus $w(x)\ge$ the number of odd prime factors of $x$, and $v_2(\varphi(x))=t(x)-1+w(x)$. For even $a_i$ (then $a_{i+1}$ is even) equation (1) gives $t_{i+1}-1+w_{i+1}=2t_i-(t_i-1+w_i)$, i.e. \[ t_{i+1}=t_i+2-w_i-w_{i+1}. \tag{2} \]

Step 4: sequences without odd prime factors. If no term has an odd prime factor, every term is $1$ or a power of $2$. From (1): after $1$ comes a number with $\varphi=1$, i.e. $1$ or $2$; after $2^k$ ($k\ge1$) comes a power of two $2^{j}$ with $2^{j-1}=2^{2k}/2^{k-1}$, i.e. $2^{k+2}$. These are steps from the list.

Step 5: the tail when an odd prime factor occurs. Suppose $a_s$ has an odd prime factor. By Step 2 so has every later term; these terms are even, so $w_i\ge1$ for $i\ge s$, and (2) gives $t_{i+1}\le t_i$ for $i\ge s$, with equality only if $w_i=w_{i+1}=1$. As $t_i\ge1$, the sequence $(t_i)$ is eventually constant: there are $J$ and $a\ge1$ with $t_i=a$ and $w_i=1$ for all $i\ge J$. Now $w_i=1$ means that $a_i$ has exactly one odd prime factor $q_i$ and $q_i\equiv3\pmod4$. So for $i\ge J$ we can write $a_i=2^aq_i^{e_i}$. Fix such an $i$ and write $q=q_i$, $e=e_i$; then (1) gives \[ \varphi(a_{i+1})=\frac{2^{2a}q^{2e}}{2^{a-1}q^{e-1}(q-1)}=\frac{2^{a+1}q^{e+1}}{q-1}. \] Thus $q-1$ divides $2^{a+1}q^{e+1}$; it is prime to $q$ and is twice an odd number, so $q-1=2$ and $q=3$. As $i\ge J$ was arbitrary, $q_i=3$ for every $i\ge J$, that is, $a_i=2^a3^{e_i}$ for all $i\ge J$, and (1) reads $2^{a}3^{e_{i+1}-1}=2^a3^{e_i+1}$, i.e. $e_{i+1}=e_i+2$. From $J$ on, all steps are of the kind $2^a3^b\to2^a3^{b+2}$.

Step 6: going back. It remains to see which $x$ can stand before a term $y$ of one of our types; by (1), $x^2/\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)$. If $x>1$ and $P$ is the largest prime factor of $x$, then $P$ divides $x^2/\varphi(x)$ (Step 2).

  • $y=2^a3^b$ ($a,b\ge1$): $\varphi(y)=2^a3^{b-1}$, so $x=1$ is impossible and $P\in\{2,3\}$. If $x=2^c3^d$ with $c,d\ge1$, then $x^2/\varphi(x)=2^c3^{d+1}$, so $c=a$, $d=b-2$: the step $2^a3^{b-2}\to2^a3^b$ (possible only if $b\ge3$, since $d\ge1$). If $x=2^c$ with $c\ge1$, then $x^2/\varphi(x)=2^{c+1}$, so $b=1$, $c=a-1$: the step $2^{a-1}\to3\cdot2^a$ (possible only if $a\ge2$, since $c\ge1$). ($x=3^d$ is excluded by Step 1.)

  • $y=2^k$ ($k\ge1$): $\varphi(y)=2^{k-1}$, so $x=1$ (and then $k=1$) or $x=2^c$ with $2^{c+1}=2^{k-1}$: the steps $1\to2$ and $2^{k-2}\to2^k$.

  • $y=1$: $x^2/\varphi(x)=1$ forces $x=1$.

In every case the step $x\to y$ is in the list and $x$ is again $1$, a power of $2$, or of the form $2^a3^b$. Starting from the tail found in Step 4 or Step 5 and going back term by term, all steps of the sequence are in the list. This proves the answer.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ such that for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^+$ \[f(x+yf(x))=f(f(x))f(x^2+y)\]

Show the answer

$f(x)=1$ for all $x$, and $f(x)=\dfrac1x$ for all $x$

Show the solution

Solution (Commuting affine substitutions and eventual periods)

Answer. $\boxed{f\equiv1\ \text{ and }\ f(x)=\dfrac1x}$.

Both work: for $f\equiv1$ both sides are $1$; for $f(x)=1/x$ the left side is $\dfrac{1}{x+y/x}=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y}$ and the right side is $f(1/x)\cdot\dfrac1{x^2+y}=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y}$.

Now let $f$ be any solution and write $c(x)=f(f(x))$. For $x>0$ and $t>x^2$ put $y=t-x^2$ in the equation: \[ f\bigl(g_x(t)\bigr)=c(x)\,f(t),\qquad g_x(t)=x+(t-x^2)f(x)\qquad(t>x^2). \tag{1} \] Here $g_x$ is an increasing linear map of $(x^2,\infty)$ onto $(x,\infty)$. Call a real number $p\neq0$ a tail period of $f$ if $f(z+p)=f(z)$ for all sufficiently large $z$. (If $p$ is a tail period, so is $-p$.)

Part A. If $f$ has a tail period, then $f\equiv1$.

A1: $f$ is eventually constant. Let $p>0$ and $f(z+p)=f(z)$ for all $z>Z$; enlarging $Z$ we may assume $Z>0$. Take $x>Z$ and put $v=f(x)=f(x+p)$; then also $c(x+p)=f(v)=c(x)$. The equation for $(x+p,y)$ and for $(x,y)$ gives \[ f(x+p+yv)=c(x)f\bigl((x+p)^2+y\bigr),\qquad f(x+yv)=c(x)f(x^2+y). \] The left sides are equal because $x+yv>Z$. Hence $f(x^2+y)=f((x+p)^2+y)$ for all $y>0$, that is \[ f(s)=f(s+2px+p^2)\qquad\text{whenever }x>Z,\ s>x^2 . \tag{2} \] Let $s>(Z+1)^2$. Then (2) applies for every $x$ with $Z<x<\sqrt s$, so $f$ equals $f(s)$ on the interval $J_s=(s+K,\ s+K+\ell_s)$, where $K=2pZ+p^2$ and $\ell_s=2p(\sqrt s-Z)>2p$. If $s<s'<s+2p$, the left end $s'+K$ of $J_{s'}$ lies inside $J_s$, so $J_s$ and $J_{s'}$ overlap and $f(s)=f(s')$. Therefore $f$ is constant on $((Z+1)^2,\infty)$: there are $S$ and $\kappa>0$ with $f(z)=\kappa$ for all $z>S$.

A2: $f(f(x))=1$ for all $x$. In (1) let $t$ be so large that $t>S$ and $g_x(t)>S$. Then $\kappa=c(x)\kappa$, so \[ f(f(x))=1\quad\text{for all }x>0. \tag{3} \] In particular $f(\kappa)=1$ (take $x>S$) and then $f(1)=f(f(\kappa))=1$. The equation becomes \[ f\bigl(x+yf(x)\bigr)=f(x^2+y)\qquad(x,y>0). \tag{4} \]

A3: $\kappa=1$. Suppose $\kappa\ne1$. Put $x=\kappa$ in (4): $f(\kappa+y)=f(\kappa^2+y)$ for all $y>0$. So $f$ has the period $|\kappa^2-\kappa|$ on $(m,\infty)$, $m=\max(\kappa,\kappa^2)$, and since $f=\kappa$ far out, $f=\kappa$ on all of $(m,\infty)$. If $\kappa<1$, then $m=\kappa<1$ and $f(1)=\kappa\ne1$, which is false. So $\kappa>1$, $m=\kappa^2$, and $f(\kappa+y)=f(\kappa^2+y)=\kappa$ for all $y>0$: $f=\kappa$ on $(\kappa,\infty)$. Choose $x$ with $\sqrt\kappa<x<\kappa$ and put $v=f(x)$, $y=(\kappa-x)/v$. In (4) the left side is $f(\kappa)=1$, and the right side is $f(x^2+y)=\kappa$ because $x^2+y>\kappa$. So $\kappa=1$, a contradiction.

A4: $f\equiv1$. Now $f=1$ on $(S,\infty)$. Let $x_0\ge0$ be the smallest number such that $f=1$ on $(x_0,\infty)$. Suppose $x_0>0$.

  • If $x_0<1$, choose $x$ with $x_0<x<1$ and $x^2<x_0$. As $f(x)=1$, (4) gives $f(x^2+y)=f(x+y)=1$ for all $y>0$, so $f=1$ on $(x^2,\infty)$, contradicting the choice of $x_0$.

  • If $x_0>1$, choose $x$ with $\sqrt{x_0}<x<x_0$ and put $v=f(x)$. The right side of (4) is $1$ for all $y>0$, so $f(x+yv)=1$ for all $y>0$: $f=1$ on $(x,\infty)$, a contradiction.

  • If $x_0=1$, then $f=1$ on $[1,\infty)$. Let $0<x<1$, $v=f(x)$. For $y>1-x^2$ the right side of (4) is $1$, so $f=1$ on $(x+v(1-x^2),\infty)$; hence $x+v(1-x^2)\ge1$, i.e. $v\ge\frac1{1+x}$. For $y\ge(1-x)/v$ the left side of (4) is $1$, so $f=1$ on $[x^2+(1-x)/v,\infty)$; hence $x^2+(1-x)/v\ge1$, i.e. $v\le\frac1{1+x}$. So $f(x)=\frac1{1+x}$ on $(0,1)$. Then $f(\tfrac12)=\tfrac23$ and $f(f(\tfrac12))=f(\tfrac23)=\tfrac35\ne1$, contradicting (3).

So $x_0=0$ and $f\equiv1$.

Part B. If $f$ has no tail period, then $f(x)=1/x$.

B1: an identity. Let $x_1,x_2>0$ and $f_i=f(x_i)$. For large $t$ we may apply (1) twice, in both orders: \[ f\bigl(g_{x_1}(g_{x_2}(t))\bigr)=c(x_1)c(x_2)f(t)=f\bigl(g_{x_2}(g_{x_1}(t))\bigr). \] Now $g_{x_1}(g_{x_2}(t))=f_1f_2\,t+x_1+f_1x_2-f_1f_2x_2^2-f_1x_1^2$, and symmetrically for the other composition, so the two arguments differ by the constant \[ D=(x_1-x_2)+f_1(x_2-x_1^2)-f_2(x_1-x_2^2)+f_1f_2(x_1^2-x_2^2). \] As $t$ grows, $z=g_{x_2}(g_{x_1}(t))$ runs through all large reals and $f(z+D)=f(z)$. Since $f$ has no tail period, $D=0$: \[ (x_1-x_2)+f(x_1)(x_2-x_1^2)-f(x_2)(x_1-x_2^2)+f(x_1)f(x_2)(x_1^2-x_2^2)=0\qquad(x_1,x_2>0). \tag{5} \]

B2: $f(1)=1$. Put $x_1=x$, $x_2=1$, $a=f(1)$ in (5). The left side factors as $(1-a)(x-1)\bigl(1-(x+1)f(x)\bigr)$. If $a\ne1$, then $f(x)=\frac1{x+1}$ for all $x\ne1$. But then the original equation fails for $x=2$, $y=3$: the left side is $f(3)=\frac14$ and the right side is $f(\tfrac13)f(7)=\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac18$. So $a=1$.

B3: a formula for $f$. Put $x_1=x$, $x_2=2$, $b=f(2)$ in (5) and collect the terms with $f(x)$: \[ f(x)\,(A+Bx^2)=A+Bx,\qquad A=2-4b,\quad B=b-1. \tag{6} \] $A$ and $B$ are not both $0$.

  • If $B=0$, then $f\equiv1$. (This has tail periods, so it does not occur in Part B.)

  • If $A=0$, then $f(x)=1/x$.

  • If $AB<0$, then $x_0=\sqrt{-A/B}$ is a zero of $A+Bx^2$, and by (6) also of $A+Bx$; so $x_0=-A/B=x_0^2$, $x_0=1$, $B=-A$, and $f(x)=\frac{1-x}{1-x^2}=\frac1{1+x}$ for $x\ne1$, $f(1)=1$.

  • If $AB>0$, then $f(x)=\dfrac{1+\lambda x}{1+\lambda x^2}$ with $\lambda=B/A>0$.

In the last two cases $t f(t)\to1$ as $t\to\infty$. Fix $x$, multiply the original equation by $y$ and let $y\to\infty$: \[ y\,f\bigl(x+yf(x)\bigr)=\frac{y}{x+yf(x)}\cdot\bigl(x+yf(x)\bigr)f\bigl(x+yf(x)\bigr)\to\frac1{f(x)},\qquad y\,f(x^2+y)\to1 . \] Hence $f(f(x))=1/f(x)$: every value $u=f(x)$ satisfies $f(u)=1/u$. For $f(x)=\frac{1+\lambda x}{1+\lambda x^2}$ the equation $f(u)=1/u$ means $u+\lambda u^2=1+\lambda u^2$, i.e. $u=1$; but $u=f(2)=\frac{1+2\lambda}{1+4\lambda}\ne1$. For $f(x)=\frac1{1+x}$ ($x\ne1$) take $u=f(2)=\frac13$: $f(\tfrac13)=\tfrac34\ne3$. Both cases are impossible, and only $f(x)=1/x$ remains.

Parts A and B show that the solutions are exactly $\boxed{f\equiv1\ \text{ and }\ f(x)=\dfrac1x}$.

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

Let $a,d,r$ be positive integers. Define the integer sequence $x_1,x_2,\dots$ by letting, for each $n\ge 1$, $x_n$ be the smallest prime divisor of $a^{dn+r}+1$.

  • (a) Prove that there exists a number that appears infinitely many times in the sequence $(x_n)$.

  • (b) Determine all triples $(a,d,r)$ for which the sequence $(x_n)$ is unbounded above.

Show the answer

(b) Exactly the triples with $a$ even and $v_2(d)\le v_2(r)$ (the highest power of $2$ dividing $d$ also divides $r$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Fermat for part (a), orders for part (b))

Write $k_n=dn+r$ and let $v_2(m)$ be the exponent of $2$ in $m$.

(a) Let $p$ be the smallest number occurring in the sequence, say $p=x_{n_0}$. Then $p\mid a^{k_{n_0}}+1$, so $p\nmid a$. For $t\ge0$ let $n=n_0+(p-1)t$. By Fermat's theorem $a^{d(p-1)t}\equiv1\pmod p$, hence \[ a^{k_n}+1=a^{k_{n_0}}\cdot a^{d(p-1)t}+1\equiv a^{k_{n_0}}+1\equiv0\pmod p . \] So $p$ is a prime divisor of $a^{k_n}+1$ and $x_n\le p$; by the choice of $p$ also $x_n\ge p$. Thus $x_n=p$ for all $n=n_0+(p-1)t$, and $p$ appears infinitely many times.

(b) Answer: the sequence is unbounded exactly when $a$ is even and $v_2(d)\le v_2(r)$.

$a$ odd. Then $a^{k}+1$ is even and $x_n=2$ for all $n$.

$a$ even and $v_2(r)<v_2(d)$. Put $s=v_2(r)$. Then $v_2(dn+r)=s$ for all $n$, so $k_n=2^su$ with $u$ odd, and $a^{2^s}+1$ divides $(a^{2^s})^u+1=a^{k_n}+1$. Hence $x_n$ is at most the smallest prime divisor of $a^{2^s}+1$: the sequence is bounded.

$a$ even and $v_2(d)\le v_2(r)$. We use the following lemma.

Lemma. Let $a$ be even, $k\ge1$ and $j=v_2(k)$. Every prime divisor $q$ of $a^k+1$ satisfies $q\equiv1\pmod{2^{j+1}}$.

Proof. $a^k+1$ is odd, so $q$ is odd, and $q\nmid a$. Let $o$ be the order of $a$ modulo $q$. From $a^k\equiv-1$ we get $a^{2k}\equiv1$, so $o\mid2k$, while $o\nmid k$ because $-1\not\equiv1\pmod q$. If $v_2(o)\le j$, then $o\mid 2k$ would give $o\mid k$. Hence $v_2(o)=j+1$, and $o\mid q-1$ gives $2^{j+1}\mid q-1$. $\blacksquare$

Write $d=2^ed'$ with $d'$ odd; then $2^e\mid r$, say $r=2^er'$. Let $j\ge1$. Since $d'$ is odd, there are infinitely many $n$ with $d'n+r'\equiv0\pmod{2^j}$, and for them $v_2(k_n)\ge e+j\ge j$. By the lemma $x_n\ge2^{j+1}+1$. As $j$ is arbitrary, the sequence is unbounded.

So the triples asked for are exactly those with $\boxed{a\text{ even and }v_2(d)\le v_2(r)}$.

Solution 2 (Every value comes back; one prime cannot serve two different powers of two)

Write $k_n=dn+r$.

(a) We show more: every value of the sequence appears infinitely many times. Fix $m$ and let $p=x_m$. Let $n=m+(p-1)!\,t$ with $t\ge0$, and let $q\le p$ be a prime. If $q\mid a$, then $q$ divides neither $a^{k_m}+1$ nor $a^{k_n}+1$. If $q\nmid a$, then $q-1$ divides $(p-1)!$, so $a^{k_n-k_m}=a^{d(p-1)!\,t}\equiv1\pmod q$ and $a^{k_n}+1\equiv a^{k_m}+1\pmod q$. So the primes up to $p$ dividing $a^{k_n}+1$ are the same as those dividing $a^{k_m}+1$; the smallest prime divisor of the latter is $p$, hence $x_n=p$ for all these $n$.

(b) The cases “$a$ odd” ($x_n=2$) and “$a$ even, $v_2(r)<v_2(d)$” (all $k_n$ are $2^{s}$ times an odd number, $s=v_2(r)$, so $a^{2^s}+1$ divides every $a^{k_n}+1$ and the sequence is bounded) are as in Solution 1. Let now $a$ be even and $v_2(d)\le v_2(r)$.

Claim. An odd prime $q$ cannot divide both $a^k+1$ and $a^{k'}+1$ if $v_2(k)<v_2(k')$.

Indeed, write $k=2^ju$, $k'=2^{j'}u'$ with $u,u'$ odd and $j<j'$, and let $E=2^{j'}uu'$. Then $E=k\cdot2^{j'-j}u'$ is an even multiple of $k$, so $a^{E}\equiv(-1)^{2^{j'-j}u'}=1\pmod q$; and $E=k'u$ is an odd multiple of $k'$, so $a^{E}\equiv(-1)^{u}=-1\pmod q$. Thus $1\equiv-1\pmod q$, impossible for odd $q$.

Suppose the sequence were bounded. All $x_n$ are odd primes ($a$ is even), so only finitely many primes occur as values, and by the claim each of them occurs only for indices $n$ with one fixed value of $v_2(k_n)$. Hence $v_2(k_n)$ would take only finitely many values. But $v_2(k_n)$ is unbounded: with $d=2^ed'$, $r=2^er'$, $d'$ odd, for each $j$ there is $n$ with $2^j\mid d'n+r'$. This contradiction shows that the sequence is unbounded.

Answer to (b): the triples with $\boxed{a\text{ even and }v_2(d)\le v_2(r)}$.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $P$ be a point on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Let $D$ and $K$ be points on line $AB$, and let $E$ and $L$ be points on line $AC$, chosen so that $PKDLE$ is a cyclic pentagon. Let $U$ be the second intersection point of the circumcircles of triangles $PBD$ and $PCL$, and let $V$ be the second intersection point of the circumcircles of triangles $PCE$ and $PBK$. Let $X$ be the intersection point of lines $BE$ and $DV$, and let $Y$ be the intersection point of lines $CD$ and $EU$. Prove that the lines $XL$, $YK$, and $BC$ are concurrent.

Show the solution

Solution (Three Miquel points and Desargues twice)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$, and assume the configuration is in general position (the points that are used are distinct and the lines that are intersected are not parallel). Let $\omega$ be the circle through $P,K,D,L,E$.

Step 1 (Miquel). Let $S$ be a point of line $AB$ and $R$ a point of line $AC$. Then the second intersection of the circles $PBS$ and $PCR$ lies on the line $SR$.

Let the line $SR$ meet the circle $PBS$ again at $M$. Then \[ \angle(MR,MP)=\angle(MS,MP)=\angle(BS,BP)=\angle(BA,BP)=\angle(CA,CP)=\angle(CR,CP), \] where the fourth equality holds because $A,B,C,P$ are concyclic. So $M,R,C,P$ are concyclic, i.e. $M$ lies on both circles; since $M\ne P$, it is their second intersection.

Applying this to $(S,R)=(D,L)$, to $(K,E)$ and to $(D,E)$ we get: \[ U\in DL,\qquad V\in KE,\qquad W\in DE, \] where $W$ denotes the second intersection of the circles $PBD$ and $PCE$. Note that $W$ lies on the circle $PBDU$ and on the circle $PCEV$.

Step 2: $B,V,W$ are collinear and $C,U,W$ are collinear. Using $V\in KE$, $W\in DE$, the circles $PBKV$, $PBDW$ and $\omega$: \[ \angle(BV,BW)=\angle(VB,VK)+\angle(EK,ED)+\angle(WD,WB)=\angle(PB,PK)+\angle(PK,PD)+\angle(PD,PB)=0 . \] So the lines $BV$ and $BW$ coincide. Similarly, using $U\in DL$, $W\in DE$, the circles $PCLU$, $PCEW$ and $\omega$: \[ \angle(CU,CW)=\angle(UC,UL)+\angle(DL,DE)+\angle(WE,WC)=\angle(PC,PL)+\angle(PL,PE)+\angle(PE,PC)=0 , \] so the lines $CU$ and $CW$ coincide. Hence the three lines $BV$, $CU$, $DE$ pass through $W$.

Step 3 (Desargues). Let $Z$ be the intersection of the lines $BC$ and $UV$.

The triangles $BEC$ and $VDU$ are in perspective from $W$: the lines $BV$, $ED$, $CU$ concur at $W$. By Desargues' theorem the intersections of corresponding sides are collinear: \[ BE\cap VD=X,\qquad EC\cap DU=L,\qquad BC\cap VU=Z . \] (Here $EC$ is the line $AC$ and $DU$ is the line $DL$, which meet at $L$.) So $Z$ lies on $XL$.

The triangles $CDB$ and $UEV$ are also in perspective from $W$ (lines $CU$, $DE$, $BV$). Hence \[ CD\cap UE=Y,\qquad DB\cap EV=K,\qquad CB\cap UV=Z \] are collinear. (Here $DB$ is the line $AB$ and $EV$ is the line $KE$, which meet at $K$.) So $Z$ lies on $YK$.

Therefore $XL$, $YK$ and $BC$ all pass through $Z$. Moreover $Z$ lies on $UV$.

Problem 9

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

On a planet with finitely many cities, some pairs of cities are connected by one-way flights. Show that the cities can be partitioned into several countries, and in each country one city can be chosen as the capital, in such a way that the following conditions hold (a city $v$ can be reached from a city $u$ if one can fly from $u$ to $v$ using one or more flights):

  • In each country, there do not exist two distinct cities such that one can be reached from the other.

  • For any two distinct capitals, at least one of them can be reached from the other.

Show the solution

Solution (Heaviest chains of clusters)

Write $u\to v$ if $u\ne v$ and $v$ can be reached from $u$. This relation is transitive: if $u\to v$, $v\to w$ and $u\ne w$, then $u\to w$.

Clusters. Say that two cities are equivalent if they are equal or each can be reached from the other. This is an equivalence relation; its classes are called clusters. If one city of a cluster $Y$ can be reached from one city of a different cluster $X$, then every city of $Y$ can be reached from every city of $X$; we then write $X<Y$. The relation $<$ on clusters is transitive, and $X<Y$, $Y<X$ never hold together (the two clusters would be one). A chain is a sequence of clusters $X_1<X_2<\dots<X_k$; its weight is $|X_1|+\dots+|X_k|$.

Numbering the cities. For a cluster $X$ let $h(X)$ be the largest weight of a chain whose last cluster is $X$; so $h(X)\ge|X|$. Let $W$ be the largest of all $h(X)$. Give the cities of $X$ the numbers \[ h(X)-|X|+1,\ h(X)-|X|+2,\ \dots,\ h(X), \] one number to each city, in any order. All numbers lie in $\{1,\dots,W\}$. Country $j$ consists of the cities with number $j$ $(1\le j\le W)$.

First condition. Let $u\ne v$ have the same number. They are not in the same cluster, because the cities of one cluster get different numbers. Let $u\in X$, $v\in Y$, and suppose $v$ can be reached from $u$, i.e. $X<Y$. Putting $Y$ at the end of a heaviest chain ending in $X$ gives a chain ending in $Y$, so $h(Y)\ge h(X)+|Y|$. Then the number of $v$ is at least $h(Y)-|Y|+1\ge h(X)+1$, larger than the number of $u$, a contradiction. So in a country no city can be reached from another one.

Capitals. Take a chain $X_1<\dots<X_k$ of weight $W$. For each $i$ we have $h(X_i)=|X_1|+\dots+|X_i|$: the chain $X_1<\dots<X_i$ shows “$\ge$”, and if a heavier chain ended in $X_i$, then following it by $X_{i+1},\dots,X_k$ would give a chain of weight more than $W$. Hence the cities of $X_i$ carry the numbers \[ |X_1|+\dots+|X_{i-1}|+1,\ \dots,\ |X_1|+\dots+|X_i| , \] and the cities of $X_1\cup\dots\cup X_k$ carry each of the numbers $1,2,\dots,W$ exactly once. So every country is non-empty and contains exactly one of these cities; we make it the capital. Two different capitals lie either in the same cluster, and then each can be reached from the other, or in clusters $X_i<X_j$, and then the second can be reached from the first. So for any two capitals at least one can be reached from the other.

Both conditions hold, which proves the statement.
