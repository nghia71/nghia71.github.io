Day 1

Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

Find all prime numbers $p,q,r,s$ such that \[p^2+q^2+r^2+s^2=2007.\]

Show the answer

there are no such primes

Show the solution

Solution (Modulo 8, modulo 3, then two squares)

One prime is $2$. The square of an odd prime is $\equiv1\pmod 8$ and $2^2=4$. With $j$ of the primes equal to $2$, the sum is $\equiv4j+(4-j)\pmod8$, i.e. $4,7,2,5,0$ for $j=0,1,2,3,4$. Since $2007\equiv7\pmod8$, exactly one prime equals $2$, and the other three are odd primes with squares summing to $2003$.

One prime is $3$. Squares of primes other than $3$ are $\equiv1\pmod3$, and $9\equiv0$. With $i$ of the three odd primes equal to $3$ the sum is $\equiv3-i\pmod3$; since $2003\equiv2\pmod3$, exactly one equals $3$, and the remaining two primes $u\le v$ satisfy $u^2+v^2=2003-9=1994$.

No such pair. Here $u^2\le997$, so $u\le31$, and checking the primes $u\in\{5,7,11,13,17,19,23,29,31\}$ (they are $\ne2,3$) gives $1994-u^2\in\{1969,1945,1873,1825,1705,1633,1465,1153,1033\}$, none of which is a square. (In fact the only representation of $1994$ as a sum of two positive squares is $25^2+37^2$, and $25$ is not prime.)

Hence the equation has no solution in primes.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

Let $ABC$ be right-angled at $A$, and let $D$ be a point lying on the side $AC$. Denote by $E$ the reflection of $A$ over line $BD$, and by $F$ the intersection of $CE$ with the perpendicular through $D$ to the line $BC$. Prove that $AF$, $DE$, and $BC$ are concurrent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(0,b)$, $C=(c,0)$ and $D=(d,0)$ with $0<d<c$. Line $BD$ is $bx+dy=bd$; the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to it is $\frac{bd}{b^2+d^2}(b,d)$, so the reflection of $A$ is \[E=\Bigl(\frac{2b^2d}{b^2+d^2},\ \frac{2bd^2}{b^2+d^2}\Bigr).\] The point $P=DE\cap BC$. Writing the line $BC$ as $\frac xc+\frac yb=1$ and intersecting it with line $DE$ one finds \[P=\Bigl(\frac{c(b^2+d^2)}{b^2+2cd-d^2},\ \frac{2bd(c-d)}{b^2+2cd-d^2}\Bigr).\] The point $F$. The perpendicular to $BC$ through $D$ is $D+u(b,c)$; intersecting it with line $CE$ gives \[F=\frac{\bigl(c^2d(b^2+d^2),\ 2bcd^2(c-d)\bigr)}{b^2c^2-2b^2cd+2b^2d^2+c^2d^2}.\] Collinearity of $A,F,P$. Both $F$ and $P$ lie on the line through $A$ with slope \[\frac{2bd(c-d)}{c(b^2+d^2)} :\] for $P$ divide its coordinates, for $F$ divide $2bcd^2(c-d)$ by $c^2d(b^2+d^2)$. Hence $AF$ passes through $P=DE\cap BC$, i.e. the three lines are concurrent. (When $b^2+2cd-d^2=0$ the lines $DE$ and $BC$ are parallel; then the same computation shows that $AF$ is parallel to them as well.)

Solution 2 (A harmonic division)

Let $Q$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $BC$, so that $F=CE\cap DQ$, and let $P=DE\cap BC$. We show that $A$ lies on line $FP$.

A harmonic quadruple on $AC$. Since $\angle BAD=\angle BED=\angle BQD=90^\circ$ ($E$ is the reflection of $A$ in $BD$), the points $A,E,Q$ lie on the circle with diameter $BD$. As $DA=DE$, the point $D$ is the midpoint of one of the two arcs $AE$ of this circle, so line $QD$ is one of the two bisectors (internal or external) of the angle between the lines $QA$ and $QE$; line $QC=BC$, perpendicular to $QD$, is the other one. If $E=Q$ (this happens when $BD$ bisects $\angle ABC$), then $F=E=Q$ lies on all three lines and we are done; so let $E\ne Q$ and put $R=QE\cap AC$. The two bisectors of the angle $AQR$ meet line $AR$ at $D$ and $C$, so by the internal and external bisector theorems $\frac{DA}{DR}=\frac{QA}{QR}=\frac{CA}{CR}$, where one of $D,C$ lies between $A$ and $R$ and the other does not. With signed lengths on line $AC$ this reads \[\frac{\overline{CA}}{\overline{AD}}=-\frac{\overline{CR}}{\overline{RD}}.\tag{1}\] Ceva and Menelaus in triangle $QCD$. The lines $QE$, $CE$, $DE$ through $E$ meet the opposite side lines $CD$, $DQ$, $QC$ at $R$, $F$, $P$. By Ceva's theorem in signed form, \[\frac{\overline{CR}}{\overline{RD}}\cdot\frac{\overline{DF}}{\overline{FQ}}\cdot\frac{\overline{QP}}{\overline{PC}}=1 .\] Let line $FP$ meet line $CD$ at $A'$. Menelaus' theorem for this line gives \[\frac{\overline{CA'}}{\overline{A'D}}\cdot\frac{\overline{DF}}{\overline{FQ}}\cdot\frac{\overline{QP}}{\overline{PC}}=-1 .\] Dividing the two equalities, $\frac{\overline{CA'}}{\overline{A'D}}=-\frac{\overline{CR}}{\overline{RD}}$, which by (1) is the ratio in which $A$ divides $CD$. A point of line $CD$ is determined by this ratio, so $A'=A$: line $FP$ passes through $A$. Hence $AF$, $DE$ and $BC$ all pass through $P$. (In the special positions where $QE\parallel AC$ or $DE\parallel BC$ the same argument works with points at infinity, or the statement follows by continuity.)

Problem 3

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Symmetry and Variable Exchange

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that \[f(f(x)+y)=f(f(x)-y)+4f(x)y\] for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

Show the answer

$f\equiv0$ and $f(x)=x^2+c$ for any constant $c$

Show the solution

Solution (Every difference of values is a period)

Both families work: for $f(x)=x^2+c$ and $a=f(x)$, $f(a+y)-f(a-y)=(a+y)^2-(a-y)^2=4ay$.

Let $f\not\equiv0$ and $g(x)=f(x)-x^2$. For every value $a$ of $f$ the equation says $f(a+y)-f(a-y)=4ay=(a+y)^2-(a-y)^2$, i.e. \[g(a+y)=g(a-y)\qquad\text{for all }y:\] $g$ is symmetric about every value $a$ of $f$. Composing the reflections about two values $a,b$ shows that $g$ has period $2(a-b)$.

Pick $a_0=f(x_0)\ne0$. For any real $t$ put $y=\frac{t}{4a_0}$; then $f(a_0+y)$ and $f(a_0-y)$ are values of $f$ whose difference is $4a_0y=t$. Hence every real number $2t$ is a period of $g$, so $g$ is constant: $f(x)=x^2+c$.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · Comparing Valuations Across Terms

Show that the function \[f(n)=n!-n^{2007}\] is injective for $n\in\mathbb{Z}^+$.

Show the solution

Solution (p-adic valuations for small a, growth for large a)

Suppose $f(a)=f(b)$ with $a<b$.

$a\ge4014$ is impossible. For $n\ge4014$ we have $n!\ge\left(\frac ne\right)^n\ge n^{2007}$ (the first because $e^n\ge\frac{n^n}{n!}$, the second because $(n-2007)\ln n\ge n$ for such $n$). Then \[f(n+1)-f(n)=n\cdot n!-\bigl((n+1)^{2007}-n^{2007}\bigr)>n^{2008}-(n+1)^{2007}=n^{2007}\Bigl(n-\bigl(1+\tfrac1n\bigr)^{2007}\Bigr)>n^{2007}(n-e^{1/2})>0,\] so $f$ is strictly increasing on $[4014,\infty)$ and $f(a)<f(b)$.

$2\le a<4014$ is impossible. Since $a\mid a!$, $a\mid a^{2007}$ and $a\mid b!$, from $f(a)=f(b)$ we get $a\mid b^{2007}$, so every prime factor of $a$ divides $b$. Choose a prime $p\mid a$ as follows: if $a$ has an odd prime factor take it, otherwise $a=2^k$ ($k\le11$) and $p=2$. Let $e=v_p(a!)$. Then $e\ge1$, and

  • if $p$ is odd: $e\le\frac{a-1}{p-1}\le\frac{4012}2<2007$;

  • if $a=2^k$: $e=2^k-1$, which is $\le1023$ for $k\le10$ and equals $2047$ for $k=11$.

In all cases $e<2007\,v_p(a)$ and $e$ is not a multiple of $2007$. Hence $v_p(f(a))=v_p(a!-a^{2007})=e$. On the other side, $v_p(b!)\ge v_p(a!)+v_p(b)\ge e+1$ (as $b!=a!\,(a+1)\cdots b$ and $p\mid b$), while $v_p(b^{2007})=2007\,v_p(b)$ is a positive multiple of $2007$, hence $\ne e$. If these two valuations differ, $v_p(f(b))$ is the smaller one, which is $\ne e$; if they are equal, $v_p(f(b))\ge e+1$. Either way $v_p(f(b))\ne v_p(f(a))$, a contradiction.

$a=1$. Then $f(b)=0$, i.e. $b!=b^{2007}$ with $b\ge2$. For $b\ge4014$, $b!>b^{2007}$ as shown above (the first inequality there is strict, since the series for $e^b$ has positive terms other than $\frac{b^b}{b!}$). For $2\le b<4014$ choose $p\mid b$ as before (with $b$ in place of $a$); then $v_p(b!)=e$ is not a multiple of $2007$, while $v_p(b^{2007})$ is. So $f(b)\ne0$.

Therefore $f$ is injective on the positive integers.

Day 2

Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

Do there exist two positive integers $a,b$ such that the number \[\overline{5^a5^b}\] is a power of $5$? Here $\overline{xy}$ denotes the number with the digits of $x$ and $y$. For example, if $x=25$ and $y=125$, then $\overline{xy}=25125$.

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (Factor out the power of 5)

Let $d$ be the number of digits of $5^b$. Then \[N=\overline{5^a5^b}=5^a\cdot10^d+5^b=5^{a+d}2^d+5^b .\] Suppose $N=5^k$.

  • If $a+d>b$: $N=5^b\bigl(5^{a+d-b}2^d+1\bigr)$, and the bracket is $\equiv1\pmod5$ and greater than $1$, so it is not a power of $5$.

  • If $a+d<b$: $N=5^{a+d}\bigl(2^d+5^{b-a-d}\bigr)$, and the bracket is not divisible by $5$ and greater than $1$.

  • If $a+d=b$: $N=5^{b}(2^d+1)$, so $2^d+1=5^m$, $m\ge1$. By the lifting-the-exponent lemma $v_2(5^m-1)=2+v_2(m)$, so $2^d=5^m-1$ forces $5^m-1=2^{2+v_2(m)}\le4m$, possible only for $m=1$, $d=2$. Then $5^b$ has two digits, so $b=2$, and $a=b-d=0$, which is not positive.

So $\overline{5^a5^b}$ is never a power of $5$.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB=\frac{AC}{2}+BC$. Let $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ be the semicircles outside of $ABC$ with diameters $AB$ and $BC$, respectively. Let $X$ be the projection of $A$ on the common tangent of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$. Find $\angle CAX$.

Show the answer

$\angle CAX=60^\circ$

Show the solution

Solution (Distances to the tangent)

Let $\ell$ be the common tangent, $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB=\frac b2+a$, and for a point $Z$ let $\delta(Z)$ be its distance to $\ell$. The circles with diameters $AB$ and $BC$ lie on one side of $\ell$ and touch it, so their centres $O_1$ (midpoint of $AB$) and $O_2$ (midpoint of $BC$) satisfy $\delta(O_1)=\frac c2$, $\delta(O_2)=\frac a2$, and the points $A,B,C$, which lie on these circles, are on the same side of $\ell$. Distances to a line on one side are affine, so \[\delta(A)+\delta(B)=2\delta(O_1)=c,\qquad \delta(B)+\delta(C)=2\delta(O_2)=a,\] hence $\delta(A)-\delta(C)=c-a=\frac b2$.

The vector $\overrightarrow{AX}$ is perpendicular to $\ell$ and points towards $\ell$. The component of $\overrightarrow{AC}$ in this direction is $\delta(A)-\delta(C)=\frac b2$, so \[\cos\angle CAX=\frac{b/2}{b}=\frac12,\qquad \angle CAX=\boxed{60^\circ}.\]

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

An $n\times n$ grid consists of $n^2$ unit squares. Initially all sides of the squares are white. Some of the sides are then colored black such that each unit square has exactly one black side. In terms of $n$, find

  • a) the minimal

  • b) the maximal

number of black sides in the grid.

Show the answer

a) $\left\lceil\frac{n^2}2\right\rceil$; b) $n^2-\left\lceil\frac{(n-2)^2}2\right\rceil$ for $n\ge2$ (and $1$ for $n=1$)

Show the solution

Solution (Count interior and boundary black sides)

A black side on the boundary of the board belongs to one square; a black side inside the board belongs to two squares, and each of them has it as its only black side. If $I$ black sides are interior and $J$ are on the boundary, counting squares gives $2I+J=n^2$, so the number of black sides is \[I+J=n^2-I .\] a) Minimum. $I\le\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor$, so the number is at least $\lceil n^2/2\rceil$. This is attained: tile the board (minus one corner square if $n$ is odd) by dominoes and colour the middle side of each domino, plus a boundary side of the leftover corner square. Every square then has exactly one black side.

b) Maximum. For $n=1$ the answer is $1$. Let $n\ge2$. The $(n-2)^2$ squares not touching the boundary have only interior sides, and each interior black side serves at most two of them, so $2I\ge(n-2)^2$ and $I\ge\lceil (n-2)^2/2\rceil$. This is attained: tile the inner $(n-2)\times(n-2)$ square by dominoes (if $n$ is odd, leave one inner square $s$ adjacent to the boundary row of squares, and pair it with its neighbour $t$ in that row by colouring their common side), colour the middle side of each domino, and give every remaining boundary square one of its boundary sides. So the maximum is $n^2-\lceil (n-2)^2/2\rceil$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure · Homogenization and Normalization

Let $x,y,z$ be positive real numbers such that $x+y+z=2$. Show that \[\frac{(x-1)^2}{y}+\frac{(y-1)^2}{z}+\frac{(z-1)^2}{x}\ge\frac14\left(\frac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}+\frac{y^2+z^2}{y+z}+\frac{z^2+x^2}{z+x}\right).\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sum of squares)

Rewriting both sides. Since $x+y+z=2$, we have $x-1=\frac{x-y-z}{2}$, so \[\frac{(x-1)^2}{y}=\frac{\bigl((x-z)-y\bigr)^2}{4y}=\frac{(x-z)^2}{4y}-\frac{x-z}{2}+\frac y4 .\] Summing cyclically, the middle terms cancel and $\frac14(x+y+z)=\frac12$, so \[\text{LHS}=\frac12+\frac{(x-y)^2}{4z}+\frac{(y-z)^2}{4x}+\frac{(z-x)^2}{4y}.\] On the right, $\frac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}=\frac{x+y}2+\frac{(x-y)^2}{2(x+y)}$, so \[\text{RHS}=\frac14\Bigl(x+y+z+\sum_{\text{cyc}}\frac{(x-y)^2}{2(x+y)}\Bigr)=\frac12+\frac{(x-y)^2}{8(x+y)}+\frac{(y-z)^2}{8(y+z)}+\frac{(z-x)^2}{8(z+x)}.\] Hence the inequality is equivalent to \[S_z(x-y)^2+S_x(y-z)^2+S_y(z-x)^2\ge0,\qquad S_z=\frac1{4z}-\frac1{8(x+y)},\ S_x=\frac1{4x}-\frac1{8(y+z)},\ S_y=\frac1{4y}-\frac1{8(z+x)} .\] Signs of the coefficients. $S_z<0$ exactly when $z>2(x+y)$, and similarly for $S_x,S_y$. At most one coefficient is negative: $z>2(x+y)$ and $x>2(y+z)$ cannot both hold. If none is negative we are done. Otherwise, by symmetry of the argument, let $S_z<0$, so $z>2(x+y)$; in particular $z>x$, $z>y$, and $y+z>x$, $z+x>y$. Then \[S_x\ge\frac1{4x}-\frac1{8x}=\frac1{8x},\qquad S_y\ge\frac1{8y},\qquad -S_z<\frac1{8(x+y)} .\] Say $x\ge y$ (the other case is the same with the roles of $x,y$ exchanged). Since $z-y>x-y\ge0$, \[S_x(y-z)^2\ge\frac{(z-y)^2}{8x}>\frac{(x-y)^2}{8x}\ge\frac{(x-y)^2}{8(x+y)}\ge-S_z(x-y)^2,\] and $S_y(z-x)^2\ge0$. So the sum is positive. In all cases the inequality holds, with equality only when $x=y=z=\frac23$.

Solution 2 (Homogenise and expand around the smallest variable)

Put $s=\frac{x+y+z}2$ $(=1)$ and write the inequality in homogeneous form: \[\sum_{\text{cyc}}\frac{(x-s)^2}{y}\ \ge\ \frac14\sum_{\text{cyc}}\frac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}.\] Multiply by $D=xyz(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)>0$ and call the resulting polynomial (left minus right) $F(x,y,z)$; it has degree $7$. The inequality is cyclic, so we may assume that $x$ is the smallest variable and write $x=t$, $y=t+p$, $z=t+q$ with $t>0$, $p,q\ge0$. Expanding and collecting powers of $t$ gives \[\begin{aligned}F={}&3(p^2-pq+q^2)\,t^5+\tfrac14(p+q)(26p^2-35pq+26q^2)\,t^4+\tfrac12(9p^4+8p^3q-17p^2q^2+8pq^3+9q^4)\,t^3\\ &+\tfrac14(p+q)(p^2+5pq+q^2)(4p^2-7pq+4q^2)\,t^2+\tfrac12pq(2p^4+p^3q-5p^2q^2+pq^3+2q^4)\,t+\tfrac14p^2q^2(p-q)^2(p+q).\end{aligned}\] Every coefficient is non-negative for $p,q\ge0$: $p^2-pq+q^2\ge0$; $26p^2+26q^2\ge52pq$; $9p^4+9q^4\ge18p^2q^2$; $4p^2+4q^2\ge8pq$; and $2p^4+2q^4\ge4p^2q^2$, $p^3q+pq^3\ge2p^2q^2$. Hence $F\ge0$, which proves the inequality. Equality holds only for $p=q=0$, i.e. $x=y=z=\frac23$.

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Density and Averaging Arguments

Let $AB$ be a line in the plane. The set $S$ contains $1390$ points which satisfy the following conditions:

  • a) The distance between a point in $S$ and line $AB$ is at most $1$.

  • b) The distance between any two points in $S$ is at least $2$.

Show that there exist two points in $S$ with distance at least $1000$.

Show the solution

Solution (Disjoint discs in a rectangle)

Suppose all pairwise distances are less than $1000$. Project the points orthogonally onto the line $AB$; projection does not increase distances, so all projections lie in a segment of length less than $1000$, say $[m,M]$ in a coordinate along $AB$.

Draw the open disc of radius $1$ around each point. Since the points are at distance at least $2$ from each other, these discs are disjoint. Each point is at distance at most $1$ from $AB$ and its projection lies in $[m,M]$, so each disc lies in the rectangle consisting of points at distance at most $2$ from $AB$ with projection in $[m-1,M+1]$. This rectangle has area less than $4\cdot1002=4008$, while the discs have total area $1390\pi>1390\cdot3.14>4364$. This is impossible, so some two points are at distance at least $1000$.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be a function such that for each $x\in\mathbb{R}$ \[f\left(x+\frac{13}{42}\right)+f(x)=f\left(x+\frac16\right)+f\left(x+\frac17\right)\] and $f(x)\le 1$. Show that $f(x)$ is periodic.

Show the answer

proof ($f$ has period $1$)

Show the solution

Solution (A periodic difference)

Since $\frac{13}{42}=\frac16+\frac17$, the condition reads $f\bigl(x+\frac16+\frac17\bigr)-f\bigl(x+\frac16\bigr)=f\bigl(x+\frac17\bigr)-f(x)$. So $g(x)=f\bigl(x+\frac17\bigr)-f(x)$ satisfies $g\bigl(x+\frac16\bigr)=g(x)$: $g$ has period $\frac16$, hence period $1$.

Let $T(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{6}g\bigl(x+\frac j7\bigr)$. As $g$ has period $1$, so has $T$. Therefore, for every integer $n\ge1$, \[f(x+n)=f(x)+nT(x),\qquad f(x-n)=f(x)-nT(x)\] (the second follows from the first applied at $x-n$). Since $f\le1$, letting $n\to\infty$ gives $T(x)\le0$ from the first identity and $T(x)\ge0$ from the second. So $T\equiv0$, i.e. $f(x+1)=f(x)$ for all $x$: $f$ is periodic with period $1$.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 42: Polynomial Reconstruction and Interpolation · Uniqueness by Degree Constraints

Find all polynomials $P$ with integer coefficients such that the set \[P(\mathbb{Z})=\{P(a)\mid a\in\mathbb{Z}\}\] contains an infinite geometric progression.

Show the answer

the polynomials $P(x)=a(bx+c)^d$ with $a,b,c\in\mathbb Z$, $a,b\ne0$, $d\ge1$ (for progressions with infinitely many distinct terms)

Show the solution

Solution (Compare terms d steps apart)

We consider geometric progressions $s,sq,sq^2,\dots$ with infinitely many distinct terms (constant progressions lie in $P(\mathbb Z)$ for every $P$, and ratio $-1$ gives only two values). All terms are integers; if $q=u/v$ in lowest terms, $v^k\mid s$ for all $k$, so $q$ is an integer, and $|q|\ge2$, $s\ne0$.

These polynomials work. If $c\ne0$, choose an integer $t$ with $|1+bt|\ge2$; the numbers $c(1+bt)^k$ are $\equiv c\pmod b$, so $c(1+bt)^k=bx_k+c$ for integers $x_k$, and $P(x_k)=ac^d(1+bt)^{dk}$ is a geometric progression. If $c=0$, take $x_k=2^k$.

Only these work. Let $P$ have degree $d\ge1$ and leading coefficient $A$, and $P(x_k)=sq^k$ for all $k\ge0$. Then $|x_k|\to\infty$. Put $\beta=\frac{a_{d-1}}{dA}$ (with $a_{d-1}$ the coefficient of $x^{d-1}$) and $y=x+\beta$; then $P=Ay^d+R(y)$ with $\deg R\le d-2$ and rational coefficients. Let $y_k=x_k+\beta$; all $y_k$ are rationals with denominator dividing $dA$. From $P(x_{k+d})=q^dP(x_k)$, \[y_{k+d}^{\,d}-(qy_k)^d=\frac{q^dR(y_k)-R(y_{k+d})}{A}=O\bigl(|y_k|^{d-2}\bigr),\] because $|y_{k+d}|$ is comparable to $|q||y_k|$. Dividing by a factor of size comparable to $|y_k|^{d-1}$ (for odd $d$ the factor $\sum y_{k+d}^iq^{d-1-i}y_k^{d-1-i}$ is $\ge c_d\max(|y_{k+d}|,|qy_k|)^{d-1}$; for even $d$ use $|y_{k+d}|^d-|qy_k|^d$ in the same way) we get $y_{k+d}-\varepsilon_kqy_k\to0$ with $\varepsilon_k=\pm1$. These differences are rationals with bounded denominators, so $y_{k+d}=\varepsilon qy_k$ exactly for infinitely many $k$, with a fixed sign $\varepsilon$. Substituting into $P(x_{k+d})=q^dP(x_k)$ gives $R(\varepsilon qy_k)=q^dR(y_k)$ for infinitely many $y_k$, hence the polynomial identity $R(\varepsilon qy)=q^dR(y)$. Comparing the coefficients of $y^j$ ($j\le d-2$): $(\varepsilon q)^jr_j=q^dr_j$ forces $r_j=0$ as $|q|\ge2$. So $P(x)=A(x+\beta)^d$.

Finally write $\beta=\frac uv$ in lowest terms: $P(x)=\frac{A}{v^d}(vx+u)^d$, and the constant term $\frac{Au^d}{v^d}$ is an integer, so $v^d\mid A$. Hence $P(x)=a(bx+c)^d$ with $a=A/v^d$, $b=v$, $c=u$.
