Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral satisfying $BC=CD=DA$. The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at $P$. Points $Q$, $A$, $B$, $R$ lie on the line $AB$ in this order and satisfy \[\angle AQD=90^\circ-\angle DAP,\qquad \angle BRC=90^\circ-\angle PBC.\] Let $S=CR\cap DQ$. Prove that $SP\perp AB$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A phantom point on the circle $PCD$)

Notation. We use directed angles between lines: $\measuredangle(m,n)$ is the angle through which the line $m$ must be turned counterclockwise to become parallel to $n$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Recall the criterion: if $X,Y,U,V$ are four distinct points and $X,U,V$ are not collinear, then $Y$ lies on the circle $XUV$ if and only if $\measuredangle(YU,YV)=\measuredangle(XU,XV)$.

Place $AB$ horizontally with $A$ on the left and the quadrilateral above $AB$; then $A,B,C,D$ are in counterclockwise order. The direction of a line is the angle it makes with $AB$, measured counterclockwise, modulo $180^\circ$; so $\measuredangle(m,n)=\operatorname{dir}n-\operatorname{dir}m$.

Let $\alpha=\angle DAP$ and $\gamma=\angle PBC$. Since $DA=DC$ and $P$ lies on the segment $AC$, $\angle DCP=\angle DCA=\angle DAC=\alpha$; since $CB=CD$ and $P$ lies on the segment $BD$, $\angle CDP=\angle CDB=\angle CBD=\gamma$. These are base angles of isosceles triangles, so $0^\circ<\alpha,\gamma<90^\circ$, and $\alpha+\gamma<180^\circ$ (two angles of triangle $PCD$).

Four directions.

  • $Q$ lies on line $AB$ to the left of $A$, so the ray $QA$ points in direction $0^\circ$; $D$ is above $AB$ and $\angle AQD=90^\circ-\alpha$, so $\operatorname{dir}DQ=90^\circ-\alpha$.

  • $R$ lies to the right of $B$, so the ray $RB$ points in direction $180^\circ$; $C$ is above $AB$ and $\angle BRC=90^\circ-\gamma$, so $\operatorname{dir}CR=180^\circ-(90^\circ-\gamma)=90^\circ+\gamma$.

  • $A,C,D$ are in counterclockwise order, so the ray $CA$ is obtained by turning the ray $CD$ counterclockwise through $\angle DCA=\alpha$: $\measuredangle(CD,CA)=\alpha$, that is, $\measuredangle(CP,CD)=-\alpha$.

  • $B,C,D$ are in counterclockwise order, so the ray $DB$ is obtained by turning the ray $DC$ clockwise through $\angle CDB=\gamma$: $\measuredangle(DP,DC)=\gamma$.

Let $\ell$ be the line through $P$ perpendicular to $AB$ ($\operatorname{dir}\ell=90^\circ$) and let $\omega$ be the circle through $P$, $C$, $D$ (they are not collinear, since $C\notin BD$). Since $\alpha\ne0^\circ$ and $\gamma\ne0^\circ$, the lines $DQ$ and $CR$ are not parallel to $\ell$; let $S_1=DQ\cap\ell$ and $S_2=CR\cap\ell$.

$S_1\ne P$. Otherwise $P$ lies on $DQ$, so the line $DQ$ is the line $DB$ and meets $AB$ at $B$; but it meets $AB$ at $Q\ne B$. In the same way $S_2\ne P$ (otherwise $CR$ would be the line $CA$ and $R=A$).

$S_1$ lies on $\omega$. If $S_1=D$ this is clear. Otherwise \[\measuredangle(S_1P,S_1D)=\measuredangle(\ell,DQ)=(90^\circ-\alpha)-90^\circ=-\alpha=\measuredangle(CP,CD),\] and the criterion (with the distinct points $S_1,C,P,D$) gives $S_1\in\omega$.

$S_2$ lies on $\omega$. If $S_2=C$ this is clear. Otherwise \[\measuredangle(S_2P,S_2C)=\measuredangle(\ell,CR)=(90^\circ+\gamma)-90^\circ=\gamma=\measuredangle(DP,DC),\] so $S_2\in\omega$.

Conclusion. The line $\ell$ meets the circle $\omega$ in at most two points, one of which is $P$. The points $S_1$ and $S_2$ are on $\ell\cap\omega$ and differ from $P$, so $S_1=S_2$. This point lies on $DQ$ and on $CR$. These two lines are distinct, because their directions $90^\circ-\alpha$ and $90^\circ+\gamma$ differ by $\alpha+\gamma\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$; so they have exactly one common point, which is $S$. Hence $S=S_1\ne P$ lies on $\ell$, that is, $SP\perp AB$. (We also learn that $S$ is the second intersection of $\omega$ with the perpendicular from $P$ to $AB$.)

Solution 2 (Chasing from the angle at $S$)

We use directed angles between lines: $\measuredangle(m,n)$ is the angle through which the line $m$ must be turned counterclockwise to become parallel to $n$, modulo $180^\circ$. Place $AB$ horizontally, $A$ on the left, the quadrilateral above it (so $A,B,C,D$ are counterclockwise), and write $\operatorname{dir}m$ for the counterclockwise angle from $AB$ to the line $m$, modulo $180^\circ$; thus $\measuredangle(m,n)=\operatorname{dir}n-\operatorname{dir}m$.

Let $\alpha=\angle DAP$ and $\gamma=\angle PBC$. As $DA=DC$ and $CB=CD$, the base angles give $\angle DCA=\alpha$ and $\angle CDB=\gamma$, with $0^\circ<\alpha,\gamma<90^\circ$.

The angle at $S$. The ray $QA$ points in direction $0^\circ$ ($Q$ is left of $A$) and $D$ is above $AB$, so $\operatorname{dir}DQ=90^\circ-\alpha$; likewise the ray $RB$ points in direction $180^\circ$, so $\operatorname{dir}CR=90^\circ+\gamma$. Since $Q$ is left of $R$ and $90^\circ-\alpha<90^\circ+\gamma$, the rays $QD$ and $RC$ meet above $AB$, so $S$ is above $AB$ and in triangle $QSR$ \[\angle QSR=180^\circ-(90^\circ-\alpha)-(90^\circ-\gamma)=\alpha+\gamma=180^\circ-\angle DPC ,\] because $\angle PDC=\gamma$ and $\angle PCD=\alpha$ in triangle $PDC$. This is the reason for the circle $SDPC$; to prove it in every position of $S$ we redo the computation with directed angles.

The angle at $P$. Because $A,C,D$ are counterclockwise, the ray $CA$ is the ray $CD$ turned counterclockwise through $\alpha$, so $\operatorname{dir}CA=\operatorname{dir}CD+\alpha$. Because $B,C,D$ are counterclockwise, the ray $DB$ is the ray $DC$ turned clockwise through $\gamma$, so $\operatorname{dir}DB=\operatorname{dir}CD-\gamma$. Hence \[\measuredangle(PD,PC)=\operatorname{dir}CA-\operatorname{dir}DB=\alpha+\gamma=(90^\circ+\gamma)-(90^\circ-\alpha)=\measuredangle(DQ,CR).\tag{1}\]

Degenerate positions. $S\ne P$, because $P\in DQ$ would make $DQ$ the line $DB$, which meets $AB$ at $B\ne Q$. If $S=D$, then $D$ lies on $CR$, so $\operatorname{dir}CD=\operatorname{dir}CR=90^\circ+\gamma$ and $\operatorname{dir}DB=\operatorname{dir}CD-\gamma=90^\circ$: the line $SP=DB$ is perpendicular to $AB$. If $S=C$, then $C$ lies on $DQ$, $\operatorname{dir}CD=90^\circ-\alpha$ and $\operatorname{dir}CA=90^\circ$: the line $SP=CA$ is perpendicular to $AB$. So assume from now on that $S$ differs from $P$, $C$, $D$.

The circle. Then $SD=DQ$ and $SC=CR$, and (1) reads $\measuredangle(SD,SC)=\measuredangle(PD,PC)$. The points $P,D,C$ are not collinear, so $S$ lies on the circle $PDC$.

The perpendicular. On this circle the chord $PD$ is seen from $S$ and from $C$ under the same directed angle: \[\measuredangle(SP,SD)=\measuredangle(CP,CD)=\operatorname{dir}CD-\operatorname{dir}CA=-\alpha .\] Therefore $\operatorname{dir}SP=\operatorname{dir}SD+\alpha=(90^\circ-\alpha)+\alpha=90^\circ$, that is, $SP\perp AB$.

In the figure ($S$ beyond the side $CD$) this is the familiar chase: $SDPC$ is cyclic because $\angle DSC+\angle DPC=180^\circ$, then $\angle DSP=\angle DCP=\alpha$, and the line $SP$ makes the angle $180^\circ-(90^\circ-\alpha)-\alpha=90^\circ$ with $QR$. The directed angles make the same chase valid when $S$ falls on the same side of $CD$ as $P$ (this happens, for example, when $\angle CAB$ or $\angle DBA$ is obtuse).

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

For nonnegative real numbers $a,b,c$ satisfying $a+b+c=3$, prove that \[\sqrt{ab|1-2c|}+\sqrt{bc|1-2a|}+\sqrt{ca|1-2b|}\le 3.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Cauchy–Schwarz with a split of the smallest variable)

Let $T$ denote the left side, and let $c=\min\{a,b,c\}$.

Case 1: $c\ge\frac12$. Then all three numbers $2a-1,2b-1,2c-1$ are non-negative and, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \[T^2\le(ab+bc+ca)\bigl((2c-1)+(2a-1)+(2b-1)\bigr)=3(ab+bc+ca)\le(a+b+c)^2=9 .\]

Case 2: $c<\frac12$. Write the three terms as \[\sqrt{1-2c}\cdot\sqrt{ab},\qquad \sqrt c\cdot\sqrt{b\,|1-2a|},\qquad \sqrt c\cdot\sqrt{a\,|1-2b|}.\] Since $(1-2c)+c+c=1$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives \[T^2\le ab+b\,|1-2a|+a\,|1-2b| .\] Let $a\ge b$ and $s=a+b=3-c\in(\tfrac52,3]$; then $a>\frac54$, so $|1-2a|=2a-1$.

  • If $b\ge\frac12$: the right side is $ab+b(2a-1)+a(2b-1)=5ab-s\le\frac54s^2-s\le\frac{45}{4}-3<9$ (the function $\frac54s^2-s$ is increasing for $s\ge\frac25$).

  • If $b<\frac12$: the right side is $ab+b(2a-1)+a(1-2b)=ab+a-b\le a(b+1)\le\Bigl(\dfrac{a+b+1}{2}\Bigr)^2\le4<9$.

In both cases $T<3$.

Hence $T\le3$ always, with equality only in Case 1 when $a=b=c=1$.

Solution 2 (A pointwise bound for $\sqrt{|1-2t|}$)

For every $t\ge0$, \[\sqrt{|1-2t|}\le\max\{t,\,1-t\}:\] if $t\ge\frac12$ then $|1-2t|=2t-1\le t^2$, and if $t<\frac12$ then $|1-2t|=1-2t\le(1-t)^2$. Put $x=\sqrt a$, $y=\sqrt b$, $z=\sqrt c$, so $x^2+y^2+z^2=3$, and let $a\ge b\ge c$. Denote the left side of the inequality by $T$.

(i) $c\ge\frac12$. Then $T\le xy\cdot c+yz\cdot a+zx\cdot b=xyz(x+y+z)$. Since $(p+q+r)^2\ge3(pq+qr+rp)$ for $p=xy$, $q=yz$, $r=zx$, \[xyz(x+y+z)\le\frac{(xy+yz+zx)^2}{3}\le\frac{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^2}{3}=3 .\]

(ii) $b\ge\frac12>c$. Then $T\le xy(1-c)+yz\cdot a+zx\cdot b=xy\bigl(1-c+z(x+y)\bigr)$. Here $xy\le\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{3-c}{2}$ and, by Cauchy–Schwarz and AM–GM, \[z(x+y)\le\sqrt{c}\,\sqrt{2(a+b)}=\sqrt{4c\cdot\tfrac{3-c}{2}}\le\frac12\Bigl(4c+\frac{3-c}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{3+7c}{4}.\] Therefore \[T\le\frac{3-c}{2}\cdot\frac{7+3c}{4}=\frac{21+2c-3c^2}{8}\le\frac{21+\frac13}{8}=\frac83<3 .\]

(iii) $b<\frac12$. Then also $c<\frac12$ and $a=3-b-c>2$. Now \[T\le xy(1-c)+zx(1-b)+yz\cdot a\le x(y+z)+a\,yz\le\sqrt{a}\sqrt{2(b+c)}+a\cdot\frac{b+c}{2}=\sqrt{2a(3-a)}+\frac{a(3-a)}{2}.\] For $2<a\le3$ we have $a(3-a)<2$, so $T<2+1=3$.

In all cases $T\le3$; equality requires case (i) and $x=y=z$, i.e. $a=b=c=1$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

For every natural number $k$, let $A_k$ be the set of points in the plane with real coordinates such that the origin is the $k$-th nearest point with integer coordinates to them. In other words, a point $P$ belongs to $A_k$ if and only if exactly $k-1$ points with integer coordinates are closer to $P$ than the origin, and no other point with integer coordinates is closer to $P$ than the origin.

For example, \[\left(\frac14,\frac14\right)\in A_1,\qquad \left(\frac12,\frac14\right)\in A_1\cap A_2,\qquad \left(\frac34,\frac14\right)\in A_2\cap A_3.\]

For every natural number $k$, prove that $A_k$ consists of finitely many polygons that can be placed next to one another to form $A_1$. The polygons may share only sides, and we are allowed to rotate, translate, and reflect them.

Show the solution

Solution (Translating by the $k$-th nearest lattice point)

The figure shows $A_1$ (blue), $A_2$ (orange) and $A_3$ (green).

Notation. Let $\Lambda=\mathbb Z^2$. For a point $X$ let $s(X)$ be the number of $\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$ with $|X-\lambda|<|X|$ and $t(X)$ the number of $\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}$ with $|X-\lambda|=|X|$. As the examples show, ties are allowed: $X\in A_k$ means that the origin can be put in the $k$-th place when the lattice points are listed by non-decreasing distance from $X$, that is, \[X\in A_k\iff s(X)\le k-1\le s(X)+t(X).\] For $k=1$ this gives the square $A_1=[-\frac12,\frac12]^2$: if $|x|,|y|\le\frac12$ then $|X-(m,n)|^2-|X|^2=m^2-2mx+n^2-2ny\ge m^2-|m|+n^2-|n|\ge0$, and if, say, $x>\frac12$ then $(1,0)$ is closer than the origin.

Fact. For every point $X$ there are at least $k$ lattice points at distance less than $k$ from $X$. Indeed, if $\mu$ is a nearest lattice point, then $|X-\mu|\le\frac{\sqrt2}{2}<1$, and the points $\mu+(i,0)$, $0\le i\le k-1$, are at distance less than $1+(k-1)=k$.

Consequently, if $X\in A_k$ then $|X|<k$ (otherwise $s(X)\ge k$). Let $\mathcal K=[-k,k]^2$; so $A_k$ lies in the interior of $\mathcal K$. For $X\in\mathcal K$ every lattice point $\lambda$ with $|X-\lambda|\le|X|$ has $|\lambda|\le2|X|<3k$. Let $V=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:0<|\lambda|<3k\}$, a finite set, and for $\lambda\in V$ let $\ell_\lambda$ be the perpendicular bisector of the segment from $0$ to $\lambda$.

Step 1: $A_k$ is a finite union of convex polygons. The finitely many lines $\ell_\lambda$ cut $\mathcal K$ into finitely many closed convex polygons with disjoint interiors. In the interior of such a polygon no point lies on a line $\ell_\lambda$, so $t=0$ there, and each $\lambda\in V$ is either closer than $0$ for all interior points or farther for all interior points; so $s$ is constant on the interior. Call the polygon good if this constant is $k-1$. We claim that $A_k$ is the union of the good polygons.

A good polygon $G$ lies in $A_k$. Let $X\in G$ and let $X_j\to X$ with $X_j$ in the interior of $G$. Every $\lambda\in V$ that is strictly closer to $X$ than $0$ is strictly closer to $X_j$ for large $j$; so $s(X)\le k-1$. Every $\lambda\in V$ that is strictly farther from $X$ than $0$ is strictly farther from $X_j$ for large $j$ (and lattice points outside $V$ are always farther); so $k-1=s(X_j)\le s(X)+t(X)$.

Every $X\in A_k$ lies in a good polygon. Let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_t$ be the lattice points with $|X-\lambda_i|=|X|$, and let $j=k-1-s(X)$, so $0\le j\le t$. For a unit vector $u$ and $\varepsilon>0$, \[|X+\varepsilon u-\lambda|^2-|X+\varepsilon u|^2=\bigl(|X-\lambda|^2-|X|^2\bigr)-2\varepsilon\,u\cdot\lambda .\] So, for all sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, a point $\lambda\in V$ that was strictly closer (farther) stays strictly closer (farther), while $\lambda_i$ becomes closer than $0$ if $u\cdot\lambda_i>0$ and farther if $u\cdot\lambda_i<0$. We need a unit vector $u$ with $u\cdot\lambda_i\ne0$ for all $i$ and with exactly $j$ positive values $u\cdot\lambda_i$. The points $0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_t$ lie on one circle (centre $X$), so no two of the $\lambda_i$ are collinear with $0$; hence the lines $\lambda_i^{\perp}$ through $0$ are distinct. Also $|X-\lambda_i|=|X|$ gives $X\cdot\lambda_i=\frac12|\lambda_i|^2>0$. Rotate $u$ through a half-turn from $-X/|X|$ (where all $u\cdot\lambda_i$ are negative) to $X/|X|$ (where all are positive). Each line $\lambda_i^\perp$ is crossed exactly once, at different moments, and at each crossing exactly one of the numbers $u\cdot\lambda_i$ changes sign. So the number of positive values goes from $0$ to $t$ in $t$ steps of $\pm1$, hence takes each value $0,1,\dots,t$ on an open arc of directions. Choose $u$ in the arc with value $j$. Then for all small $\varepsilon>0$ the point $X+\varepsilon u$ has $t=0$ and $s=s(X)+j=k-1$; it lies in $\mathcal K$ and on no line $\ell_\lambda$, so in the interior of a good polygon. There are finitely many good polygons, so one of them contains $X+\varepsilon u$ for a sequence $\varepsilon\to0$, and, being closed, it contains $X$.

Step 2: the pieces. Cut every good polygon $G$ further by the lines $x=m+\frac12$ and $y=m+\frac12$ ($m\in\mathbb Z$): the pieces are the convex polygons $G\cap(\mu+A_1)$, $\mu\in\Lambda$, with non-empty interior. Altogether we get finitely many convex polygons $\Pi_1,\dots,\Pi_N$ with disjoint interiors whose union is $A_k$; let $\Pi_i\subseteq\mu_i+A_1$ and translate: $\Pi_i'=\Pi_i-\mu_i\subseteq A_1$.

Step 3: the translated pieces tile $A_1$. Let $W=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:|\lambda|<k+1\}$. Call a point $Y$ in the interior of $A_1$ generic if it lies on none of the finitely many perpendicular bisectors of pairs of distinct points of $W$. Let $Y$ be generic. By the Fact, at least $k$ lattice points are at distance less than $k$ from $Y$; they belong to $W$ (as $|Y|<1$), and the distances from $Y$ to the points of $W$ are distinct. So the $k$ nearest lattice points $w_1,\dots,w_k$ of $Y$ are well defined, with \[|Y-w_1|<|Y-w_2|<\dots<|Y-w_k|<|Y-\nu|\quad\text{for every other }\nu\in\Lambda .\] For $\lambda\in\Lambda$, the substitution $\nu=\lambda+\mu$ shows that $s(Y-\lambda)$ is the number of lattice points $\nu\ne\lambda$ strictly closer to $Y$ than $\lambda$, and $t(Y-\lambda)$ the number of $\nu\ne\lambda$ at the same distance from $Y$ as $\lambda$. Hence:

  • for $\lambda=w_k$: $s=k-1$, $t=0$;

  • for $\lambda=w_i$, $i<k$: $s+t=i-1<k-1$, so $Y-\lambda\notin A_k$;

  • for any other $\lambda$: $s\ge k$, so $Y-\lambda\notin A_k$.

So exactly one lattice translate of $Y$ lies in $A_k$, namely $Y-w_k$; since $t(Y-w_k)=0$, it lies on no line $\ell_\lambda$, hence in the interior of a (unique) good polygon $G$, and also in the interior of the square $-w_k+A_1$. Thus $Y-w_k$ is an interior point of the piece $\Pi_i=G\cap(-w_k+A_1)$, and $Y$ is an interior point of $\Pi_i'$. Conversely, if $Y\in\Pi_j'$ for some $j$, then $Y+\mu_j\in\Pi_j\subseteq A_k$ is a lattice translate of $Y$ in $A_k$; so $\mu_j=-w_k$. The good polygon containing $\Pi_j$ then contains $Y-w_k$, an interior point of $G$, so it is $G$ itself, and $\Pi_j=G\cap(-w_k+A_1)=\Pi_i$.

We have shown: every generic point of $A_1$ lies in exactly one of the polygons $\Pi_i'$. Therefore:

  • two different $\Pi_i'$, $\Pi_j'$ have disjoint interiors (a common interior point would have a neighbourhood of common points, and this neighbourhood would contain a generic point, because the non-generic points lie on finitely many lines);

  • the union of the $\Pi_i'$ is a closed subset of $A_1$ containing all generic points, which are dense in $A_1$; so the union is $A_1$.

So $A_k$ is the union of the finitely many polygons $\Pi_1,\dots,\Pi_N$ (overlapping only along their boundaries), and the translated polygons $\Pi_i-\mu_i$ fit together, overlapping only along their boundaries, to form the square $A_1$. Only translations by lattice vectors are needed. This completes the proof.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

The numbers $1,2,\ldots,1405$ are initially written in increasing order. In each move, we may choose three numbers $i,j,k$ and replace them, in their current positions, by $j,k,i$, respectively. What is the minimum number of moves required to arrange the numbers in decreasing order?

Show the answer

$702$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting cycles of odd length)

Let $n=1405$. Describe an arrangement by the permutation $\sigma$ with $\sigma(p)=$ the number in position $p$. A move replaces $\sigma$ by $\tau\circ\sigma$, where $\tau$ is a $3$-cycle on three numbers $i,j,k$. We start from the identity and want the permutation $\rho(p)=1406-p$, which consists of the fixed point $703$ and the $702$ transpositions $(p\ \ 1406-p)$.

Lower bound. For a permutation $\pi$ let $o(\pi)$ be the number of its cycles of odd length (fixed points included).

Claim: a move changes $o$ by at most $2$. Let $\pi'=\tau\circ\pi$ with $\tau$ a $3$-cycle on $\{i,j,k\}$, and let $U$ be the union of the cycles of $\pi$ that contain $i$, $j$ or $k$ (at most three cycles). Both $\pi$ and $\tau$ map $U$ to itself, and $\pi'=\pi$ outside $U$; so the cycles outside $U$ are unchanged, and $U$ is a union of cycles of $\pi'$. A cycle of $\pi'$ that contains none of $i,j,k$ is also a cycle of $\pi$ (on its elements $x$ we have $\pi(x)\notin\{i,j,k\}$, so $\pi'(x)=\pi(x)$), hence lies outside $U$. So $U$ consists of at most three cycles of $\pi'$ as well. The number of odd cycles inside $U$ has the parity of $|U|$, before and after the move, and lies in $\{0,1,2,3\}$; two such numbers of equal parity differ by at most $2$. This proves the claim.

Now $o(\text{identity})=1405$ and $o(\rho)=1$, so at least $\dfrac{1405-1}{2}=702$ moves are needed.

Construction. Split the $702$ pairs $\{p,1406-p\}$, $p=1,\dots,702$, into $351$ groups of two pairs. For a group $\{x,x'\}$, $\{y,y'\}$ (the numbers are still in their original positions) make two moves: \[(x,\;x',\;y)\ \longrightarrow\ (x',\;y,\;x),\qquad\text{then}\qquad (y,\;x,\;y')\ \longrightarrow\ (x,\;y',\;y).\] After the first move the positions of $x,x',y,y'$ contain $x',y,x,y'$; after the second they contain $x',x,y',y$. So the two pairs are swapped, with $2$ moves per group and $351\cdot2=702$ moves in total; the number $703$ stays in the middle.

The minimum is $\boxed{702}$.

Solution 2 (Connected blocks of positions)

Lower bound. Suppose the numbers are reversed after $m$ moves. Each move involves three positions. Draw a graph on the $1405$ positions: for every move, join its three positions pairwise (a triangle). A number only ever moves between positions of one triangle, so every number stays inside the connected component of its starting position. The number $p$ starts in position $p$ and ends in position $1406-p$; hence $p$ and $1406-p$ are in the same component. So every component is a union of pairs $\{p,1406-p\}$, together with the position $703$ for exactly one component.

Consider a component with $v$ positions that was created by $m'$ of the triangles. Adding the triangles one at a time, each triangle reduces the number of connected pieces by at most $2$; going from $v$ single positions to one piece needs $v-1\le2m'$. If the component does not contain $703$, then $v$ is even, so in fact $m'\ge\frac v2$. If it contains $703$, then $m'\ge\frac{v-1}{2}$. Adding over all components, \[m\ \ge\ \frac{1405-1}{2}=702 .\]

Construction. Group the $702$ pairs $\{p,1406-p\}$ two by two. For two pairs $\{x,x'\}$ and $\{y,y'\}$ whose numbers are in their original positions, the two moves \[(x,\;x',\;y)\ \longrightarrow\ (x',\;y,\;x),\qquad (y,\;x,\;y')\ \longrightarrow\ (x,\;y',\;y)\] turn the contents $x,x',y,y'$ of the four positions into $x',y,x,y'$ and then into $x',x,y',y$, i.e. they swap both pairs. This uses $2\cdot351=702$ moves.

Therefore the minimum number of moves is $\boxed{702}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Find all strictly increasing sequences of natural numbers $\{a_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, \[1+a_na_{n+2}\mid 1+a_{n+1}^2.\]

Show the answer

$a_n=c\,q^{\,n-1}$ with integers $c\ge1$, $q\ge2$

Show the solution

Solution (The ratios cannot keep dropping)

Answer. Exactly the geometric sequences $a_n=c\,q^{n-1}$ with integers $c\ge1$ and $q\ge2$. They work, because then $1+a_na_{n+2}=1+a_{n+1}^2$.

Let $(a_n)$ be a solution and define the positive integers $k_n$ by \[1+a_{n+1}^2=k_n\,(1+a_na_{n+2}).\tag{1}\]

Step 1: $k_n=1$ for all large $n$. Let $x_n=\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}>1$. From (1), $k_na_na_{n+2}=a_{n+1}^2+1-k_n\le a_{n+1}^2$, that is, \[x_{n+1}\le\frac{x_n}{k_n}.\] Hence $x_1\ge k_1k_2\cdots k_n\,x_{n+1}>k_1k_2\cdots k_n$ for every $n$. The $k_i$ are positive integers, so only finitely many of them are greater than $1$: there is an index $m$ with $k_n=1$ for all $n\ge m$.

Step 2: the tail is geometric. For $n\ge m$, (1) reads $a_{n+1}^2=a_na_{n+2}$, so $x_{n+1}=x_n$: the ratio $q=a_{n+1}/a_n$ is the same rational number for all $n\ge m$, and $a_{m+j}=a_mq^{\,j}$ is an integer for every $j\ge0$. Writing $q=u/v$ in lowest terms, $v^{\,j}\mid a_m$ for all $j$, so $v=1$: $q$ is an integer, and $q\ge2$.

Step 3: going backwards. Suppose that for some $j\ge1$ we know $a_{j+2}=q\,a_{j+1}$ (true for $j\ge m-1$). We show $k_j=1$. Since $a_{j+1}\mid a_{j+2}$, reducing (1) (with $n=j$) modulo $a_{j+1}$ gives $k_j\equiv1\pmod{a_{j+1}}$. If $k_j\ne1$, then $k_j\ge a_{j+1}+1$ and \[1+a_{j+1}^2=k_j(1+a_ja_{j+2})\ge(a_{j+1}+1)(1+a_{j+2})>(a_{j+1}+1)\,a_{j+1}+1>1+a_{j+1}^2 ,\] a contradiction. So $k_j=1$, i.e. $a_{j+1}^2=a_ja_{j+2}=a_j\,q\,a_{j+1}$, which gives $a_{j+1}=q\,a_j$. Thus the relation $a_{j+2}=qa_{j+1}$ implies $a_{j+1}=qa_j$, and by downward induction $a_{n+1}=q\,a_n$ for all $n\ge1$.

Therefore $a_n=a_1q^{\,n-1}$ for all $n$, with integers $a_1\ge1$ and $q\ge2$, and the answer is \[\boxed{a_n=c\,q^{\,n-1}\ \ (c\ge1,\ q\ge2\text{ integers})}.\]

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

In a scalene triangle $ABC$, let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ onto $BC$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of the circumcircle not containing $A$. Let $K$ be the reflection of $M$ across $AD$. The line through $D$ perpendicular to $AM$ intersects $KB$ and $KC$ at $E$ and $F$, respectively. Prove that the quadrilateral $AEKF$ is cyclic.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two angle lemmas from an orthocentre)

Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle, $\ell$ the line through $D$ perpendicular to $AM$, and $\sigma$ the reflection in the line $AD$, so $K=\sigma(M)$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(p,q)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$; a reflection reverses their sign. Since the triangle is scalene, $AM\ne AD$, so $M$ and $K$ are not on $AD$, and $MK\parallel BC$.

Step 1: $\ell$ is parallel to $A'K$, where $A'$ is the second intersection of $AD$ with $\omega$. Let $M'$ be the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite to $M$. The diameter $MM'$ is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, so $MM'\parallel AD$, and $AM'\perp AM$. The parallel chords $AA'$ and $M'M$ have a common perpendicular bisector $d$ (through the centre, parallel to $BC$), and the reflection in $d$ maps the line $AM'$ to the line $A'M$. Then $\sigma$ maps the line $A'M$ to $A'K$. The composition of the reflections in $d$ and in $AD$ (perpendicular lines) is a half-turn, which keeps the direction of every line. Hence $A'K\parallel AM'$, so $A'K\perp AM$ and $A'K\parallel\ell$.

Step 2: a power-of-a-point relation. Let $G=AK\cap BC$ and $T=A'K\cap BC$ (these lines are not parallel to $BC$, because $AM$ is neither perpendicular nor parallel to $BC$). Rotating both lines by $90^\circ$ and then reflecting, \[\measuredangle(TA',TG)=\measuredangle(A'K,BC)=\measuredangle(AM,AD)=-\measuredangle(AK,AD)=\measuredangle(AA',AG).\] So $A,A',T,G$ lie on a circle. Computing the power of $D$ with respect to this circle and to $\omega$ (signed lengths on the lines $BC$ and $AD$): \[\overline{DT}\cdot\overline{DG}=\overline{DA}\cdot\overline{DA'}=\overline{DB}\cdot\overline{DC}.\] Here $G\ne D$ and $T\ne D$. First, $A'\ne D$: otherwise $D$ is on $\omega$, so $D=B$ or $D=C$; if $D=B$ (a right angle at $B$), then by Step 1 the line $A'K=BK$ is parallel to $\ell$ and passes through $B=D$, so $\ell=BK$ and $E$ is not defined; similarly $D=C$ would leave $F$ undefined. Now $G=D$, or $T=D$ with $A'\ne D$, would put $K$ on the line $AD$, which is false. Put $\lambda=\overline{DB}/\overline{DT}=\overline{DG}/\overline{DC}$. A one-line computation with coordinates on $BC$ centred at $D$ gives \[\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{BT}}=\frac{-\lambda}{1-\lambda}=\frac{\overline{GD}}{\overline{GC}}=:\rho .\] (Here $\lambda\ne1$: $T=B$ would mean $KB=A'K\parallel\ell$, and then $E$ would not exist.)

Step 3: $E$ is an orthocentre. The homothety with centre $B$ and ratio $\rho$ maps $T$ to $D$, hence the line $TK$ to the parallel line through $D$, which is $\ell$ by Step 1; so it maps $K$ to the point of $BK$ on $\ell$: $E=B+\rho(K-B)$. The homothety with centre $G$ and ratio $\rho$ maps $C$ to $D$ and $K$ to a point $V=G+\rho(K-G)$ of the line $AK$ with $DV\parallel CK$. Subtracting, \[E-V=(1-\rho)(B-G),\] so the vector $E-V$ is parallel to $BC$ (or zero). Let $V'=\sigma(V)$, a point of the line $AM$; then $V-V'$ is also parallel to $BC$, hence so is $E-V'$.

Suppose first $V\ne A$, so that $ADV'$ is a triangle. Its altitude from $D$ is perpendicular to $AV'=AM$: it is $\ell$. Its altitude from $V'$ is the line through $V'$ parallel to $BC$. The point $E$ lies on both (and they are different lines), so $E$ is the orthocentre of $ADV'$ and $AE\perp DV'$. As $DV'=\sigma(DV)$ and $DV\parallel CK$, \[\measuredangle(AE,AD)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(DV',AD)=90^\circ-\measuredangle(CK,AD)=\measuredangle(BC,AD)+\measuredangle(AD,CK)=\measuredangle(BC,CK).\tag{1}\] If $V=A$, then $CK\parallel DA$ and $E-A$ is parallel to $BC$, so both sides of (1) equal $90^\circ$ and (1) still holds.

Steps 2 and 3 are symmetric in $B$ and $C$ (the points $A',T,G$ do not depend on the choice), so in the same way \[\measuredangle(AF,AD)=\measuredangle(BC,BK).\tag{2}\]

Conclusion. Subtracting (2) from (1), \[\measuredangle(AE,AF)=\measuredangle(BC,CK)-\measuredangle(BC,BK)=\measuredangle(BK,CK)=\measuredangle(KE,KF).\] Hence $A,E,K,F$ are concyclic, i.e. the quadrilateral $AEKF$ is cyclic.

Solution 2 (Coordinates for the key lemma)

It is enough to prove the two relations \[\measuredangle(AE,AD)=\measuredangle(BC,CK),\qquad \measuredangle(AF,AD)=\measuredangle(BC,BK)\tag{$*$}\] (directed angles modulo $180^\circ$): subtracting them gives $\measuredangle(AE,AF)=\measuredangle(BK,CK)=\measuredangle(KE,KF)$, which means that $A,E,K,F$ are concyclic. Here we prove $(*)$ by a short computation.

Let $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,a)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $a>0$, and $M=(m,-n)$, where $m=\frac{b+c}{2}$ (the point $M$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$) and $n>0$. Then $K=(-m,-n)$. The circumcircle is \[x^2+y^2-(b+c)x-\frac{a^2+bc}{a}\,y+bc=0\] (it passes through $A,B,C$), and $M$ lies on it, which gives \[a\,n^2+(a^2+bc)\,n=a\,(m^2-bc).\tag{3}\] The line $\ell$ through $D$ perpendicular to $AM$ (direction $(m,-(a+n))$) is $y=\tau x$ with $\tau=\dfrac{m}{a+n}$. Intersecting with the line $BK=\{(b-s(m+b),\,-sn)\}$ gives \[E=(x_E,\tau x_E),\qquad x_E=\frac{bn}{\,n-\tau(m+b)\,}\] (the denominator is non-zero because $E$ exists). Let $E^*=(-x_E,\tau x_E)$ be the reflection of $E$ in $AD$. We claim $AE^*\perp CK$, i.e. \[(-x_E)(c+m)+(\tau x_E-a)\,n=0\iff b(\tau n-c-m)=a\bigl(n-\tau(m+b)\bigr)\iff \tau\,(bn+am+ab)=an+bc+bm .\] Substituting $\tau=\frac{m}{a+n}$ and expanding, this becomes \[bmn+am^2+abm=a^2n+abc+abm+an^2+bcn+bmn\iff am^2=a^2n+abc+an^2+bcn,\] which is exactly (3). So $AE^*\perp CK$. Since also $AD\perp BC$, rotating by $90^\circ$ gives $\measuredangle(AE^*,AD)=\measuredangle(CK,BC)$, and reflecting in $AD$, $\measuredangle(AE,AD)=-\measuredangle(CK,BC)=\measuredangle(BC,CK)$. This is the first relation in $(*)$; the second follows by exchanging $b$ and $c$ (which keeps $m$, $n$, $\tau$ and (3)). Hence $AEKF$ is cyclic.

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral. Points $X$, $Y$, $Z$, $T$ lie on the sides $AB$, $BC$, $CD$, $DA$, respectively. The circumcircles of triangles $BXY$ and $DZT$ are tangent to the line $AC$ and to each other. Prove that the quadrilateral $XYZT$ is tangential.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The tangency point is the incentre)

Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $BXY$ and $\omega_2$ the circle $DZT$.

The common point. The circle $\omega_1$ touches the line $AC$, so it lies in the closed half-plane of $AC$ containing $B$; similarly $\omega_2$ lies in the closed half-plane containing $D$. Since $B$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$, a common point of the two circles lies on $AC$, where each circle has only its tangency point. So both circles touch $AC$ at the same point $P$.

$P$ lies between $A$ and $C$. $P\ne A$, since $P=A$ would give $AX\cdot AB=AP^2=0$, so $X=A$, and likewise $T=A$; similarly $P\ne C$. Suppose $P$ lies on the line $AC$ beyond $A$, so that $CP>CA$. The power of $C$ with respect to $\omega_1$ is $CY\cdot CB=CP^2$, and $CY\le CB$; hence $CB\ge CP>CA$. In the same way ($\omega_2$ meets the line $CD$ at $Z$ and $D$) $CD>CA$. But $\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ$, so one of these angles is at least $90^\circ$, and in that triangle ($ABC$ or $ADC$) the side $CA$ opposite it is the longest; this contradicts $CB>CA$ and $CD>CA$. Exchanging the roles of $A$ and $C$, $P$ is not beyond $C$ either.

Angle at $X$. By the power of the point $A$: $AX\cdot AB=AP^2=AT\cdot AD$. Hence $X,B,D,T$ are concyclic, so $\angle AXT=\angle ADB$; and the triangles $APX$, $ABP$ are similar ($AX/AP=AP/AB$, common angle at $A$), so $\angle AXP=\angle APB$. By the exterior angle of triangle $PBC$ and the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, \[\angle APB=\angle PBC+\angle PCB=\angle PBC+\angle ADB>\angle ADB .\] Since $T$ and $P$ lie on the same side of the line $AB$ (the side of the quadrilateral) and $\angle AXT<\angle AXP$, the ray $XT$ lies inside the angle $AXP$, and \[\angle TXP=\angle AXP-\angle AXT=\angle PBC .\] On the other hand the line $XY$ separates $B$ from the segment $AC$, so $P$ lies on the arc $XY$ of $\omega_1$ not containing $B$: the points $X,B,Y,P$ lie on $\omega_1$ in this order, so the ray $XY$ lies inside the angle $BXP$ and \[\angle PXY=\angle PBY=\angle PBC .\] Thus $XP$ is the internal bisector of the angle $TXY$ of the quadrilateral $XYZT$.

All four angles. The same argument at $Y$ (with $C$ in the role of $A$), at $Z$ and at $T$ (with $D$ in the role of $B$) shows that $P$ lies on the internal bisectors of all four angles of the convex quadrilateral $XYZT$. Hence $P$ is inside $XYZT$ and has the same distance $\varrho$ to the lines $TX,XY$ (bisector at $X$), $XY,YZ$ (at $Y$), $YZ,ZT$ (at $Z$). The feet of the perpendiculars from $P$ lie on the sides themselves, because the half-angles $\angle PXY,\angle PYX,\dots$ are acute. So the circle with centre $P$ and radius $\varrho$ touches all four sides: $XYZT$ is tangential.

Solution 2 (Lengths and the converse of Pitot)

As in Solution 1, the two circles touch $AC$ at the same point $P$ (they lie on opposite sides of $AC$). Let $R$ be the circumradius of $ABCD$. We show $XY+ZT=YZ+TX$; by the converse of Pitot's theorem, a convex quadrilateral with this property has an inscribed circle.

The sides $TX$ and $YZ$. From $AX\cdot AB=AP^2=AT\cdot AD$, the triangles $AXT$ and $ADB$ are similar with ratio $\frac{AX}{AD}=\frac{AP^2}{AB\cdot AD}$. So, and similarly at $C$, \[TX=\frac{BD\cdot AP^2}{AB\cdot AD},\qquad YZ=\frac{BD\cdot CP^2}{CB\cdot CD}.\]

The sides $XY$ and $ZT$. A circle through $B$ touching the line $AC$ at $P$ has radius $R_1=\dfrac{BP^2}{2h_B}$, where $h_B$ is the distance from $B$ to $AC$ (with $P$ as origin and $AC$ as $x$-axis, $B=(u,h_B)$ and centre $(0,R_1)$ give $u^2+(h_B-R_1)^2=R_1^2$). In triangle $ABC$, $h_B=\dfrac{AB\cdot BC}{2R}$ and $\sin\angle ABC=\dfrac{AC}{2R}$. Hence \[XY=2R_1\sin\angle XBY=\frac{BP^2}{h_B}\cdot\frac{AC}{2R}=\frac{AC\cdot BP^2}{AB\cdot BC},\qquad ZT=\frac{AC\cdot DP^2}{AD\cdot DC}.\]

The identity. We must prove \[AC\,\bigl(BP^2\cdot AD\cdot DC+DP^2\cdot AB\cdot BC\bigr)=BD\,\bigl(AP^2\cdot BC\cdot CD+CP^2\cdot AB\cdot AD\bigr).\tag{1}\] Let $p=AP$, so $CP=AC-p$. By the law of cosines $BP^2$ and $DP^2$ are quadratic polynomials in $p$ with leading coefficient $1$. So both sides of (1) are quadratic polynomials in $p$, with leading coefficients $AC\,(AD\cdot DC+AB\cdot BC)$ and $BD\,(BC\cdot CD+AB\cdot AD)$; these are equal by the second theorem of Ptolemy, $\dfrac{AC}{BD}=\dfrac{AB\cdot AD+CB\cdot CD}{BA\cdot BC+DA\cdot DC}$. At $p=0$ (that is, $P=A$) the left side is $AC\cdot AB\cdot AD\,(AB\cdot CD+AD\cdot BC)=AC^2\cdot BD\cdot AB\cdot AD$ by Ptolemy's theorem, which equals the right side; by symmetry the two sides also agree at $p=AC$. Two quadratic polynomials with the same leading coefficient and equal values at two points coincide. So (1) holds for every $P$ on $AC$, hence $XY+ZT=YZ+TX$ and $XYZT$ is tangential.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

In triangle $ABC$, let $I_b$ and $I_c$ be the excenters opposite vertices $B$ and $C$, respectively. A point $P$ lies inside the triangle and satisfies \[\angle BPC=90^\circ+\frac{\angle BAC}{2}.\] Let $R$ be the intersection of the perpendicular bisector of $PI_c$ with the line through $I_c$ perpendicular to $I_bI_c$. Similarly, let $Q$ be the intersection of the perpendicular bisector of $PI_b$ with the line through $I_b$ perpendicular to $I_bI_c$. Prove that, as $P$ varies, the line $QR$ passes through a fixed point.

Show the answer

proof; the fixed point is the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Three circles through two points)

The fixed circle. Let $I$ be the incentre and $N$ the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$. It is well known that $NB=NI=NC$; let $\Sigma$ be the circle with centre $N$ through $B,I,C$. Since $\angle BIC=90^\circ+\frac12\angle BAC=\angle BPC$ and $P$ is inside the triangle (on the same side of $BC$ as $I$), the point $P$ lies on the arc $BIC$ of $\Sigma$, and $P\ne B,C$. We prove that $QR$ always passes through $N$.

The midpoint $W$ of $I_bI_c$. The lines $BI_b$ and $BI_c$ are the internal and external bisectors of angle $B$, so $\angle I_bBI_c=90^\circ$; similarly $\angle I_bCI_c=90^\circ$. Hence $B$ and $C$ lie on the circle with diameter $I_bI_c$, whose centre $W$ satisfies \[WB=WC=WI_b=WI_c .\] So $W$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ and on the line $I_bI_c$, which is the external bisector of angle $A$. The midpoint of the arc $BAC$ lies on the same two lines, and these lines are different (they are perpendicular if $AB=AC$, and otherwise only one of them passes through $A$); so $W$ is that arc midpoint. Therefore $WN$ is a diameter of the circumcircle, $\angle WBN=90^\circ$, and $WB$ is tangent to $\Sigma$ at $B$. The power of $W$ with respect to $\Sigma$ is \[WB^2=WI_b^2=WI_c^2 .\tag{1}\]

The circles of $Q$ and $R$. By definition $RP=RI_c$ and $RI_c\perp I_bI_c$; so the circle $\Gamma_c$ with centre $R$ through $P$ touches the line $I_bI_c$ at $I_c$. In the same way the circle $\Gamma_b$ with centre $Q$ through $P$ touches $I_bI_c$ at $I_b$. The power of $W$ with respect to $\Gamma_c$ is $WI_c^2$ and with respect to $\Gamma_b$ it is $WI_b^2$.

The second common point. Let $P'$ be the point of the ray $WP$ with $WP\cdot WP'=WB^2$. By (1), $W$ has the same power $WB^2$ with respect to $\Sigma$, $\Gamma_b$ and $\Gamma_c$, and all three circles pass through $P$; so the line $WP$ meets each of them again at $P'$. Moreover $P'\ne P$: otherwise $WP$ would be tangent to $\Sigma$, but the tangents from $W$ touch $\Sigma$ only at $B$ and $C$.

So the three circles $\Sigma,\Gamma_b,\Gamma_c$ pass through the two distinct points $P$ and $P'$, and their centres $N$, $Q$, $R$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $PP'$. Since $Q\ne R$, the line $QR$ is this perpendicular bisector and passes through the fixed point $N$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates on the line $I_bI_c$)

Let $I$ be the incentre, $N$ the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$, and $W$ the midpoint of $I_bI_c$. We use two facts.

  • $P$ lies on the circle $\Sigma$ with centre $N$ through $B$, $I$, $C$. It is well known that $NB=NI=NC$, and $\angle BPC=90^\circ+\frac12\angle BAC=\angle BIC$ with $P$, $I$ on the same side of $BC$.

  • The power of $W$ with respect to $\Sigma$ is $WI_b^2$. $BI_b\perp BI_c$ and $CI_b\perp CI_c$ (internal and external bisectors), so $WB=WC=WI_b$; thus $W$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ and on the external bisector $I_bI_c$ of angle $A$, so it is the midpoint of the arc $BAC$; then $WN$ is a diameter of the circumcircle, $WB\perp BN$, $WB$ is tangent to $\Sigma$, and the power of $W$ is $WB^2=WI_b^2$.

Take $W$ as origin and $I_bI_c$ as $x$-axis: $I_c=(-w,0)$, $I_b=(w,0)$. Because the power of the origin is $w^2$, the circle $\Sigma$ has an equation \[x^2+y^2-2ux-2vy+w^2=0,\tag{1}\] where $N=(u,v)$ is its centre. Let $P=(x,y)$; here $y\ne0$, because the triangle lies strictly on one side of the external bisector $I_bI_c$.

The point $R=(-w,r)$ lies on the perpendicular to $I_bI_c$ at $I_c$, and $RP=RI_c$ gives $(x+w)^2+(y-r)^2=r^2$, so \[r=\frac{(x+w)^2+y^2}{2y},\qquad\text{and similarly } Q=(w,q),\quad q=\frac{(x-w)^2+y^2}{2y}.\] The line $QR$ has slope $\dfrac{q-r}{2w}=-\dfrac{x}{y}$ and passes through the midpoint $\bigl(0,\frac{q+r}{2}\bigr)=\bigl(0,\frac{x^2+y^2+w^2}{2y}\bigr)$ of $QR$. At the abscissa $u$ its ordinate is \[\frac{x^2+y^2+w^2}{2y}-\frac{xu}{y}=\frac{2ux+2vy}{2y}-\frac{xu}{y}=v,\] where we used (1) for the point $P$. So the line $QR$ passes through $N=(u,v)$, the centre of $\Sigma$, for every position of $P$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 43: Locus, Transformation, and Construction Geometry

Let $\omega$ and $\Omega$ be concentric circles with center $O$, with $\omega$ lying inside $\Omega$. Let $P$ be a fixed point on $\omega$, and let $QR$ be a variable chord of $\Omega$ passing through $P$. Let $S$ be the intersection of $\omega$ with the circumcircle of triangle $OQR$ such that $S$ and $Q$ lie on opposite sides of the line $OR$. Let $T$ be a point on $QR$ such that \[PT=ST.\] Prove that, as $QR$ varies, the points $T$ lie on a fixed circle.

Show the answer

proof; the circle has centre $O'$ on segment $OP$ with $PO'=\dfrac{r^3}{r^2+\rho^2}$ and radius $\dfrac{r\rho^2}{r^2+\rho^2}$ ($r,\rho$ the radii of $\omega,\Omega$)

Show the solution

Solution (Inversion, an angle bisector and a homothety)

Let $r<\rho$ be the radii of $\omega$ and $\Omega$. The chord $QR$ is not on the line $OP$ (otherwise the circle $OQR$ does not exist).

Step 1: the point $S'$. Let $S'$ be the point of the ray $OS$ with $OS\cdot OS'=\rho^2$, i.e. the image of $S$ under the inversion in $\Omega$; so $OS'=\rho^2/r$. This inversion fixes $Q$ and $R$ and maps the circle $OQR$, which passes through its centre $O$, to the line $QR$. Hence $S'$ lies on the line $QR$. Since $OS'>\rho$, the point $S'$ is outside $\Omega$, so outside the segment $QR$. The ray $OS'$ is the ray $OS$, which lies on the side of the line $OR$ opposite to $Q$; the points of the line $QR$ on that side are those beyond $R$. So $S'$ lies on the line $QR$ beyond $R$.

Step 2: $OT$ bisects the angle $POS'$. We have $OP=OS=r$ and $TP=TS$, so $O$ and $T$ both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $PS$. (Here $S\ne P$, because $S'$ is on the ray $OS$ and on the line $QR$, which meets the line $OP$ only at $P\ne S'$; for the same reason $O,P,S'$ are not collinear.) In the isosceles triangle $OPS$ this perpendicular bisector is the bisector of the angle $POS=\angle POS'$. So $T$ is the point where the internal bisector of the angle at $O$ of triangle $OPS'$ meets the side $PS'$ (the line $PS'$ is the line $QR$). By the angle bisector theorem \[\frac{PT}{TS'}=\frac{OP}{OS'}=\frac{r}{\rho^2/r}=\frac{r^2}{\rho^2},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \overrightarrow{PT}=k\,\overrightarrow{PS'},\quad k=\frac{r^2}{r^2+\rho^2}.\]

Step 3: the fixed circle. The point $S'$ lies on the fixed circle $\Omega'$ with centre $O$ and radius $\rho^2/r$. By Step 2, $T$ is the image of $S'$ under the homothety with centre $P$ and ratio $k$, which depends only on $r$ and $\rho$. Hence $T$ lies on the image of $\Omega'$ under this homothety: the circle with centre $O'$ on the segment $PO$, $PO'=k\,r=\dfrac{r^3}{r^2+\rho^2}$, and radius $k\cdot\dfrac{\rho^2}{r}=\dfrac{r\rho^2}{r^2+\rho^2}$. This circle does not depend on the chord $QR$.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Find all polynomials $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that every positive integer $n$ has a unique representation \[n=aP(b),\] where $a$ is a square-free positive integer and $b$ is a positive integer.

Show the answer

$P(x)=x^2$ and $P(x)=(x-1)^2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The values must be the squares, in order)

Answer. $P(x)=x^2$ and $P(x)=(x-1)^2$.

We use the standard fact $(\star)$: every positive integer $n$ can be written in exactly one way as $n=a_0c^2$ with $a_0$ square-free and $c$ a positive integer (for each prime $p$, the exponent of $p$ in $a_0$ must be the parity of the exponent of $p$ in $n$).

Both polynomials work. For $P(x)=x^2$ this is $(\star)$ with $b=c$. For $P(x)=(x-1)^2$: $b=1$ gives $aP(b)=0$, never a positive integer, and for $b\ge2$ we get $(\star)$ with $c=b-1$.

Step 1: the positive values of $P$. Let $P$ have the property. For $m\ge1$ let $r(m)$ be the number of positive integers $b$ with $P(b)=m$. The number of representations of $n$ is $\sum r(d)$ over the divisors $d$ of $n$ with $n/d$ square-free, so \[\sum_{d\mid n,\ n/d\ \text{square-free}} r(d)=1\qquad\text{for every } n\ge1 .\tag{1}\] We prove by strong induction that $r(n)=1$ if $n$ is a perfect square and $r(n)=0$ otherwise. Write $n=a_0c^2$ as in $(\star)$. By the induction hypothesis, a divisor $d<n$ contributes to (1) only if $d=e^2$ is a square with $n/e^2$ square-free; by $(\star)$ this forces $e=c$. So the divisors $d<n$ contribute $1$ if $c^2<n$ (i.e. $n$ is not a square) and $0$ if $n$ is a square. Then (1) gives $r(n)=0$ in the first case and $r(n)=1$ in the second.

So: on the positive integers, $P$ takes every positive perfect square exactly once and takes no other positive value.

Step 2: $P(x)=(x-e)^2$. $P$ takes infinitely many positive values, so it is non-constant with positive leading coefficient; choose $B$ such that $P$ is strictly increasing on $[B,\infty)$, and let $L=\max\{0,P(1),\dots,P(B)\}$. Let $c_0$ be the least positive integer with $c_0^2>L$, and let $b_0$ be the positive integer with $P(b_0)=c_0^2$; then $b_0>B$. We claim $P(b_0+j)=(c_0+j)^2$ for all $j\ge0$, by induction on $j$. Indeed, $P(b_0+j+1)>P(b_0+j)=(c_0+j)^2$ is a positive value of $P$, hence a square, so $P(b_0+j+1)\ge(c_0+j+1)^2$. Conversely the square $(c_0+j+1)^2>L$ equals $P(b^*)$ for some $b^*>B$; as $P(b^*)>P(b_0+j)$ and $P$ is increasing on $[B,\infty)$, we get $b^*\ge b_0+j+1$ and $(c_0+j+1)^2=P(b^*)\ge P(b_0+j+1)$. So equality holds.

The polynomials $P(x)$ and $(x-e)^2$, with $e=b_0-c_0$, agree at infinitely many points, hence $P(x)=(x-e)^2$ with $e$ an integer.

Step 3: $e\in\{0,1\}$. If $e\le-1$, the values $P(b)=(b-e)^2$, $b\ge1$, are all at least $4$, and the value $1$ is missing. If $e\ge2$, then $P(e-1)=P(e+1)=1$, so the value $1$ is taken twice. Both contradict Step 1. Hence $e=0$ or $e=1$: \[\boxed{P(x)=x^2\quad\text{or}\quad P(x)=(x-1)^2}\]

Solution 2 (A recursion for the counting function and a degree count)

We show that only $P(x)=x^2$ and $P(x)=(x-1)^2$ are possible; that they work follows from the unique factorisation $n=a_0c^2$ (square-free times square) of every positive integer, with $b=c$, respectively $b=c+1$ (for $(x-1)^2$ the value at $b=1$ is $0$ and represents nothing).

The counting function. Let $r(m)$ be the number of positive integers $b$ with $P(b)=m$ ($m\ge1$). The hypothesis says \[\sum_{d\mid n,\ n/d\ \text{square-free}} r(d)=1\quad(n\ge1),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad r(n)=1-\sum_{d\mid n,\ d<n,\ n/d\ \text{square-free}}r(d).\] This recursion determines $r$ uniquely. The indicator function of the perfect squares satisfies the same relations, because every $n$ has exactly one divisor $d$ that is a square with $n/d$ square-free. Hence $r$ is the indicator of the squares: $P$ takes each positive square exactly once, and no other positive value.

Degree and leading coefficient. $P$ has positive leading coefficient, so there is $B$ such that $P$ is increasing on $[B,\infty)$ and $P(B)>0$; let $L=\max\{P(1),\dots,P(B)\}$ and take $X>B$ with $P(X)>L$. The values $P(B+1),\dots,P(X)$ are $X-B$ distinct squares in $(0,P(X)]$, so $X-B\le\sqrt{P(X)}$. Every square in $(L,P(X)]$ is a value $P(b)$ with $B<b\le X$, so $\sqrt{P(X)}-1-\sqrt L\le X-B$. Thus \[(X-B)^2\le P(X)\le(X-B+1+\sqrt L)^2\qquad\text{for all large }X,\] which forces $\deg P=2$ with leading coefficient $1$: $P(x)=x^2+ux+v$ with integers $u,v$.

The discriminant. For large $b$, $P(b)=y^2$ is a square and $(2y)^2=4P(b)=(2b+u)^2+\Delta$ with $\Delta=4v-u^2$. Two different squares of integers, one of which is $(2b+u)^2$, differ by at least $2(2b+u)-1$, which exceeds $|\Delta|$ for large $b$. So $(2y)^2=(2b+u)^2$ and $\Delta=0$: $u=-2e$ is even and $v=e^2$, i.e. $P(x)=(x-e)^2$.

Conclusion. If $e\le-1$ the value $1$ is never taken at a positive integer; if $e\ge2$ it is taken twice ($b=e\pm1$). So $e\in\{0,1\}$ and \[\boxed{P(x)=x^2\quad\text{or}\quad P(x)=(x-1)^2}\]

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

A positive integer $n>1$ is called interesting if there exists a prime $p$ such that the set of prime divisors of $n$ is exactly the set of odd primes not exceeding $p$. Prove that, for every positive integer $N$, there are at most $N$ distinct positive integers $s\le 2^N$ for which $2^s-1$ is interesting.

Show the answer

proof (in fact only $s=2$ and $s=4$ occur)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Interesting exponents form a divisibility chain)

Call $s$ good if $2^s-1$ is interesting, and let $p(s)$ be the corresponding prime. An interesting number is divisible by $3$ (it is greater than $1$, so its set of prime divisors is non-empty, which forces $p\ge3$). Hence a good $s$ is even; in particular $s\ge2$.

Lemma. Let $g$ be a proper divisor of $s$. Then $2^s-1$ has a prime divisor that does not divide $2^g-1$.

Proof. Let $s=gm$, $m\ge2$, $x=2^g$, and \[\Phi=\frac{x^m-1}{x-1}=1+x+\dots+x^{m-1},\] an odd number with $\Phi\ge1+2^{m-1}>m$. Suppose every prime divisor of $2^s-1=x^m-1$ divides $x-1$. Let $q$ be a prime divisor of $\Phi$; then $q$ is odd and $q\mid x-1$, so by the lifting-the-exponent lemma $v_q(x^m-1)=v_q(x-1)+v_q(m)$, i.e. $v_q(\Phi)=v_q(m)$. Hence $\Phi$ divides $m$, contradicting $\Phi>m$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Claim. If $s<t$ are good, then $s\mid t$; in particular $t\ge2s$.

Let $g=\gcd(s,t)$; recall $\gcd(2^s-1,2^t-1)=2^g-1$.

  • If $p(s)\le p(t)$: every prime divisor of $2^s-1$ is an odd prime not exceeding $p(s)\le p(t)$, so it divides $2^t-1$, hence it divides $2^g-1$. By the Lemma, $g$ is not a proper divisor of $s$; so $g=s$ and $s\mid t$.

  • If $p(s)>p(t)$: every prime divisor of $2^t-1$ divides $2^s-1$, hence $2^g-1$, where $g\le s<t$ is a proper divisor of $t$. This contradicts the Lemma.

Conclusion. Let $s_1<s_2<\dots<s_k$ be the good numbers not exceeding $2^N$. Then $s_1\ge2$ and $s_{i+1}\ge2s_i$, so $2^N\ge s_k\ge2^k$ and $k\le N$. This completes the proof.

Solution 2 (With two quoted theorems, only $s=2$ and $s=4$)

This solution proves much more, but it quotes two classical results.

  • Bang's theorem (a case of Zsigmondy's theorem): for every $s\ge2$, $s\ne6$, the number $2^s-1$ has a prime divisor $q$ such that the order of $2$ modulo $q$ is exactly $s$; then $s\mid q-1$, so $q\ge s+1$.

  • A primorial bound: for every integer $n\ge29$ the product of all primes not exceeding $n$ is greater than $2^n$ (a consequence of the Rosser–Schoenfeld estimate $\theta(x)>x\bigl(1-\frac1{\ln x}\bigr)$ for $x\ge41$, together with a direct check for $29\le n\le40$).

Let $2^s-1$ be interesting, with prime $p$. As in Solution 1, $s$ is even. Also $s\ne6$, since $2^6-1=63=3^2\cdot7$ is not divisible by $5$. For $s\ge8$, Bang's theorem gives a prime divisor $q\ge s+1$ of $2^s-1$; then $p\ge q\ge s+1$, so every odd prime up to $s+1$ divides $2^s-1$.

If $s+1\ge29$, the product of the odd primes up to $s+1$ is greater than $2^{s+1}/2=2^s>2^s-1$, which is impossible. So $s\le27$. For $8\le s\le27$ we have $p\ge s+1\ge9$, hence $p\ge11$, and $5$, $7$, $11$ divide $2^s-1$. The orders of $2$ modulo $5,7,11$ are $4,3,10$, so $60\mid s$, impossible. The remaining even values: $2^2-1=3$ and $2^4-1=15=3\cdot5$ are interesting.

So $2^s-1$ is interesting only for $s=2$ and $s=4$. For $N=1$ only $s=2$ is at most $2^N$, and for $N\ge2$ there are $2\le N$ such $s$. Hence there are at most $N$ values, as required.

Remark. This also gives the stronger version mentioned with the problem: for a given real $C>0$ and every $N\ge C+2$, the number of such $s\le2^N$ is $2\le N-C$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $r$ be a positive integer, and let $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_r$ be distinct positive integers. For each $i\ge r+1$, define $a_i$ to be the smallest positive multiple of $i$ that does not occur among $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{i-1}$. Prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that \[a_n\in\{n,2n\}\qquad\text{for every integer } n>N.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Tokens ahead of the clock and their odd parts)

Step 1: the values that are “ahead”. All terms are distinct. For $n\ge r$ let $U_n=\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}$ and $H_n=\{a\in U_n:a>n\}$. If $m>r$ and $m\notin U_{m-1}$, then $a_m=m$; so $m\in U_m$ for every $m>r$. Hence $U_n$ contains all of $r+1,\dots,n$, and exactly $r$ of its $n$ elements are outside this range. Among them, those that are at most $r$ are among $a_1,\dots,a_r$ (since $a_m\ge m>r$ for $m>r$), so their number does not depend on $n$. Therefore \[|H_n|=h\quad\text{is the same for all } n\ge r .\] Think of $H_n$ as a set of $h$ tokens on the integers greater than $n$. For $n>r$:

  • if $n\notin H_{n-1}$, then $a_n=n$ and $H_n=H_{n-1}$;

  • if $n\in H_{n-1}$, then $a_n=kn$, where $k\ge2$ is the least integer with $kn\notin H_{n-1}$ (a multiple $kn$ with $k\ge2$ has been used before iff it is in $H_{n-1}$), and $H_n=(H_{n-1}\setminus\{n\})\cup\{kn\}$: the token at $n$ jumps to $kn$, and $2n,\dots,(k-1)n$ carry tokens.

A token at position $x$ stays there until time $x$. We must show that from some moment on every jump has $k=2$.

Step 2: odd parts. For a positive integer $x$ let $\mathrm{od}(x)$ be its largest odd divisor, and let $O_n=\{\mathrm{od}(x):x\in H_n\}$. If the token at $n$ jumps with $k=2$, its odd part does not change. If it jumps with $k\ge3$, then $2n\in H_n$ still carries a token with odd part $\mathrm{od}(n)$. So in all cases $O_{n-1}\subseteq O_n$. As $|O_n|\le h$, the sets $O_n$ are equal to a fixed finite set $O$ for all $n\ge N_1$.

In a jump $n\to kn$ the odd part of the token is multiplied by $\mathrm{od}(k)\ge1$. So the sum $\sum_{x\in H_n}\mathrm{od}(x)$ is non-decreasing in $n$, and for $n\ge N_1$ it is at most $h\cdot\max O$. Hence it is constant for $n\ge N_2$ (some $N_2\ge N_1$): for $n>N_2$ every jump has $k$ equal to a power of $2$.

Step 3: no big jumps after $N_2$. Call a jump big if $k\ge3$. Suppose the token at $p>N_2$ makes a big jump. Then $k$ is a power of $2$, so $k\ge4$, and $2p,3p\in H_{p-1}$. Moreover $4p\in H_p$ (either $k=4$ and the token lands there, or $k>4$ and $4p\in H_{p-1}$).

  • At time $2p$ the tokens at $2p$ and $4p$ are still in place, so the token at $2p$ makes a big jump. As $2p>N_2$, its multiplier is a power of $2$, hence at least $4$, so $3\cdot2p=6p\in H_{2p-1}$.

  • At time $3p$ the tokens at $3p$ (present since time $p-1$) and at $6p$ (present at time $2p-1$) are still in place. So the token at $3p$ makes a big jump.

Thus a big jump at $p>N_2$ implies a big jump at $3p$, hence at $9p,27p,\dots$. But then $\mathrm{od}(3^ip)=3^i\,\mathrm{od}(p)$ belongs to the finite set $O$ for every $i\ge0$, a contradiction.

So for every $n>N_2$ either $a_n=n$, or the token at $n$ jumps with $k=2$ and $a_n=2n$. Taking $N=N_2$ completes the proof.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (One weighted AM–GM)

The expression does not change if all four numbers are multiplied by the same constant or permuted, so let the numbers be $1\le x\le y\le12$ together with $1$ and $12$. Then the sum is \[\frac1{12}+\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac xy+\frac x{12}+\frac y{12},\] and we must prove $\dfrac1x+\dfrac1y+\dfrac xy+\dfrac x{12}+\dfrac y{12}\ge\dfrac53$. Split the left side into ten terms and apply the AM–GM inequality: \[3\cdot\frac1{3x}+\frac1y+2\cdot\frac{x}{2y}+\frac{x}{12}+3\cdot\frac{y}{36}\ \ge\ 10\sqrt[10]{\frac{1}{27x^3}\cdot\frac1y\cdot\frac{x^2}{4y^2}\cdot\frac{x}{12}\cdot\frac{y^3}{36^3}} =10\sqrt[10]{\frac1{6^{10}}}=\frac{10}{6}=\frac53 ,\] since $27\cdot4\cdot12\cdot36^3=6^4\cdot6^6$. Order the numbers, $a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le a_4$, and let $p=\dfrac{a_2}{a_1}$, $q=\dfrac{a_3}{a_2}$, $s=\dfrac{a_4}{a_3}$; then $p,q,s\ge1$ and $pqs=12$. Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool

Find all polynomials $P(z)$ with complex coefficients satisfying the following property: Whenever three distinct, noncollinear complex numbers $a,b,c$ are the vertices of an acute triangle in the complex plane and provided that $P(a)$, $P(b)$, $P(c)$ are distinct and noncollinear, the points $P(a)$, $P(b)$, $P(c)$ are also the vertices of an acute triangle.

Show the answer

exactly the polynomials of degree at most $1$: $P(z)=\alpha z+\beta$, $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Huge triangles see only the leading term)

Answer. $P(z)=\alpha z+\beta$ with arbitrary complex $\alpha,\beta$.

These work. If $\alpha\ne0$, the map $z\mapsto\alpha z+\beta$ is a similarity (rotation, scaling, translation), so it maps acute triangles to acute triangles. If $\alpha=0$, the images $P(a),P(b),P(c)$ are never distinct, and the condition is empty.

No polynomial of degree $n\ge2$ works. Let $P(z)=c_nz^n+\dots+c_0$ with $c_n\ne0$, $n\ge2$. Put $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and \[\varphi=\frac{2\pi m-\frac{\pi}{3}}{n},\qquad u_1=1,\quad u_2=e^{i\varphi},\quad u_3=e^{-i\varphi}.\] Then $\varphi<\frac{2\pi m}{n}\le\pi$, and $\varphi>\frac\pi2$ because $4m-n\ge1>\frac23$ ($4m-n=n$ for even $n$ and $n-2$ for odd $n$). The three points lie on the unit circle and cut it into arcs $\varphi,\ \varphi,\ 2\pi-2\varphi$, all less than $\pi$; so every angle of the triangle $u_1u_2u_3$ (half of the opposite arc) is acute. On the other hand \[u_1^n=1,\qquad u_2^n=e^{i(2\pi m-\pi/3)}=e^{-i\pi/3},\qquad u_3^n=e^{i\pi/3},\] and the triangle with vertices $1,e^{-i\pi/3},e^{i\pi/3}$ has an obtuse angle $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ at the vertex $1$. In formulas, \[(u_2^n-u_1^n)\,\overline{(u_3^n-u_1^n)}=\bigl(e^{-i\pi/3}-1\bigr)^2=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,i .\tag{1}\] Now take a large real $R$ and the acute triangle $Ru_1,Ru_2,Ru_3$. Let \[w_j(R)=\frac{P(Ru_j)}{c_nR^n}=u_j^n+\sum_{k<n}\frac{c_k}{c_n}\,u_j^k\,R^{k-n}\ \longrightarrow\ u_j^n\qquad(R\to\infty).\] By (1), for all large $R$ the number $(w_2-w_1)\overline{(w_3-w_1)}$ has negative real part and non-zero imaginary part. Non-zero imaginary part means that $w_1,w_2,w_3$ are distinct and not collinear; negative real part means that the angle at $w_1$ is obtuse. The triangle $P(Ru_1),P(Ru_2),P(Ru_3)$ is similar to $w_1w_2w_3$ (multiply by $c_nR^n$). So $P$ maps an acute triangle to a non-degenerate obtuse triangle and does not have the property.

Hence the polynomials with the property are exactly those of degree at most $1$: \[\boxed{P(z)=\alpha z+\beta}\qquad(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C).\]

Solution 2 (A tiny, almost right triangle and the second derivative)

As in Solution 1, the polynomials $\alpha z+\beta$ have the property (similarity, or empty condition for $\alpha=0$). Let $\deg P\ge2$; we find an acute triangle with an obtuse image.

Normalisation. The polynomial $P'P''$ is not identically zero, so there is $z_0$ with $P'(z_0)\ne0$ and $P''(z_0)\ne0$. Let $\lambda=\dfrac{2P'(z_0)}{P''(z_0)}$ and \[Q(z)=\frac{P(z_0+\lambda z)-P(z_0)}{\lambda P'(z_0)}=z+z^2+z^3S(z),\] where $S$ is a polynomial (Taylor expansion at $z_0$). The maps $z\mapsto z_0+\lambda z$ and $w\mapsto(w-P(z_0))/(\lambda P'(z_0))$ are similarities; so it suffices to find an acute triangle whose image under $Q$ is a non-degenerate obtuse triangle.

The triangle. Let $0<\varepsilon<1$, $v=i\,e^{-i\varepsilon^2}=\sin\varepsilon^2+i\cos\varepsilon^2$, and take the triangle $0,\ \varepsilon,\ \varepsilon v$. It is isosceles with apex angle $\frac\pi2-\varepsilon^2$ at $0$ and base angles $\frac\pi4+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}$, so it is acute.

Its image. $Q(0)=0$, and with $C=\max_{|z|\le1}|S(z)|$, \[\varrho:=\frac{Q(\varepsilon v)}{Q(\varepsilon)}=v\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon v+\varepsilon^2v^2S(\varepsilon v)}{1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2S(\varepsilon)}=v+\varepsilon\,(v^2-v)+O(\varepsilon^2),\] where the constant in $O(\varepsilon^2)$ depends only on $C$ (recall $|v|=1$). Now $\operatorname{Re}v=\sin\varepsilon^2\le\varepsilon^2$ and $\operatorname{Re}(v^2-v)=-\cos2\varepsilon^2-\sin\varepsilon^2\le-\frac12$ for small $\varepsilon$, so \[\operatorname{Re}\varrho\le-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+O(\varepsilon^2)<0,\qquad \operatorname{Im}\varrho=\cos\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon)>0\] for all sufficiently small $\varepsilon$. Thus $Q(\varepsilon)\ne0$, the points $0,Q(\varepsilon),Q(\varepsilon v)$ are distinct and not collinear ($\varrho$ is not real), and the angle at $Q(0)=0$, which is $|\arg\varrho|$, is greater than $\frac\pi2$. So $Q$, and hence $P$, sends an acute triangle to a non-degenerate obtuse triangle.

Therefore exactly the polynomials of degree at most $1$ have the property: \[\boxed{P(z)=\alpha z+\beta}\qquad(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C).\]

Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n$ be a natural number. $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_{2^n}$ is an arbitrary permutation of all subsets of the set $\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Find the maximum possible value of the following expression in terms of $n$: \[\sum_{i=1}^{2^n}|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|\] (Note that $A_{2^n+1}=A_1$.)

Show the answer

$(n-1)\,2^{\,n-1}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A bound for each pair and a Gray code)

Let $S$ be the sum and $N=2^n$; indices are taken modulo $N$.

Upper bound. For two different sets $A,B$ we have \[|A\cap B|\le\frac{|A|+|B|-1}{2}.\] Indeed, $|A\cap B|\le\min\{|A|,|B|\}$; if $|A|\ne|B|$ the minimum is at most $\frac{|A|+|B|-1}{2}$, and if $|A|=|B|$ then $A\cap B$ is a proper subset of $A$, so $|A\cap B|\le|A|-1$. Summing over the $N$ consecutive pairs, each set is counted twice: \[S\le\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|A_i|+|A_{i+1}|-1}{2}=\sum_{A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}|A|-\frac N2=n2^{n-1}-2^{n-1}=(n-1)2^{n-1}.\]

Construction. Equality holds in the bound for a pair when one of the sets is obtained from the other by adding one element. So we need a cyclic order of all subsets in which consecutive subsets differ by exactly one element (a cyclic Gray code). For $n=1$ the order $\emptyset,\{1\}$ works (the sum is $0$). If $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_{2^{n-1}}$ is such a cyclic order for $n-1\ge1$, then \[B_1,\;B_2,\;\dots,\;B_{2^{n-1}},\;B_{2^{n-1}}\cup\{n\},\;\dots,\;B_2\cup\{n\},\;B_1\cup\{n\}\] is one for $n$ (for $n=2$: $\emptyset,\{1\},\{1,2\},\{2\}$). Hence the maximum is \[\boxed{(n-1)\,2^{\,n-1}}\]

Solution 2 (Counting runs of each element)

Place the sets $A_1,\dots,A_{N}$, $N=2^n$, around a circle. For an element $x$, call a position $i$ an $x$-position if $x\in A_i$; there are $2^{n-1}$ of them, and they form $r_x\ge1$ maximal blocks of consecutive positions (runs) on the circle. (Not all positions are $x$-positions, since half of the subsets do not contain $x$.)

The sum. The number of indices $i$ with $x\in A_i\cap A_{i+1}$ is the number of $x$-positions minus the number of runs, $2^{n-1}-r_x$. Hence \[S=\sum_{i}|A_i\cap A_{i+1}|=\sum_{x=1}^{n}\bigl(2^{n-1}-r_x\bigr)=n2^{n-1}-\sum_{x}r_x .\]

A bound for the number of runs. Each run of $x$ has two ends: one index $i$ with $x\notin A_i$, $x\in A_{i+1}$ and one with $x\in A_i$, $x\notin A_{i+1}$. So $2r_x$ is the number of indices $i$ for which $x$ belongs to exactly one of $A_i,A_{i+1}$, and \[2\sum_x r_x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|A_i\,\triangle\,A_{i+1}|\ \ge\ N,\] because consecutive sets are different. Thus $\sum_xr_x\ge2^{n-1}$ and $S\le(n-1)2^{n-1}$.

Equality. We need $|A_i\triangle A_{i+1}|=1$ for all $i$: a closed walk through all vertices of the $n$-dimensional cube along edges. It exists for every $n\ge1$: for $n=1$ take $\emptyset,\{1\}$; from a cyclic order $B_1,\dots,B_{2^{n-1}}$ for $n-1$ we get the order $B_1,\dots,B_{2^{n-1}},B_{2^{n-1}}\cup\{n\},\dots,B_1\cup\{n\}$ for $n$.

So the maximum is $\boxed{(n-1)\,2^{\,n-1}}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Find all even natural numbers $n$, such that an $n\times n$ table can be filled with the numbers $1,2,3,\ldots,n^2$ (each number exactly once) in such a way that for every cell containing an odd number, the sum of the numbers in its row is strictly greater than the sum of the numbers in its column, and for every cell containing an even number, the sum of the numbers in its row is strictly less than the sum of the numbers in its column.

Show the answer

all even $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Odd numbers in the left half, even numbers in the right half)

Answer: such a table exists for every even $n$.

Let $n$ be even. For $m=1,2,\dots,\frac{n^2}{4}$ define the four numbers \[o_m=2m-1,\qquad o_m'=n^2-2m+1,\qquad e_m=2m,\qquad e_m'=n^2+2-2m .\] As $m$ runs from $1$ to $\frac{n^2}4$, the numbers $o_m$ are the odd numbers below $\frac{n^2}{2}$ and the $o_m'$ are the odd numbers above $\frac{n^2}{2}$; the $e_m$ are the even numbers up to $\frac{n^2}2$ and the $e_m'$ are the even numbers from $\frac{n^2}{2}+2$ to $n^2$. So these are all the numbers $1,\dots,n^2$, each once, and \[o_m+o_m'=n^2,\qquad e_m+e_m'=n^2+2,\qquad o_m+e_m'=o_m'+e_m=n^2+1 .\]

The table. Number the pairs $(k,j)$ with $1\le k,j\le\frac n2$ by $m=(k-1)\frac n2+j$. For each such pair put

  • in row $2k-1$: $o_m$ in column $j$ and $e_m'$ in column $\frac n2+j$;

  • in row $2k$: $o_m'$ in column $j$ and $e_m$ in column $\frac n2+j$.

For $n=4$ this is \[\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}\hline 1&3&16&14\\\hline 15&13&2&4\\\hline 5&7&12&10\\\hline 11&9&6&8\\\hline\end{array}\]

Sums. Each row consists of $\frac n2$ pairs $\{o_m,e_m'\}$ or $\frac n2$ pairs $\{o_m',e_m\}$, so every row sum is $\frac n2(n^2+1)=\frac{n^3}{2}+\frac n2$. Each of the first $\frac n2$ columns consists of $\frac n2$ pairs $\{o_m,o_m'\}$, so its sum is $\frac{n^3}{2}$. Each of the last $\frac n2$ columns consists of $\frac n2$ pairs $\{e_m',e_m\}$, so its sum is $\frac{n^3}{2}+n$.

Check. All odd numbers are in the first $\frac n2$ columns, where (row sum) $=\frac{n^3}2+\frac n2>\frac{n^3}{2}=$ (column sum). All even numbers are in the last $\frac n2$ columns, where (row sum) $=\frac{n^3}2+\frac n2<\frac{n^3}{2}+n=$ (column sum). So the table satisfies the condition, and the answer is \[\boxed{\text{all even } n}.\]

Remark. In any valid table the cell in row $i$ and column $j$ holds an odd number exactly when (row sum $i$) $>$ (column sum $j$); in our table the odd numbers fill whole columns. Filling whole rows with them instead cannot work: the $\frac n2$ odd rows would contain all odd numbers, with total $\bigl(\frac{n^2}{2}\bigr)^2$, so their average sum is $\frac{n^3}{2}$, while the column sums average $\frac1n\cdot\frac{n^2(n^2+1)}{2}=\frac{n^3}{2}+\frac n2$. Some odd row then has sum at most $\frac{n^3}{2}$ and some column has sum at least $\frac{n^3}{2}+\frac n2$, and their common cell is odd with row sum smaller than column sum.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n$ be a natural number. $G_n$ is a graph with $\binom{2n+1}{n}$ vertices, where each vertex corresponds to one of the $n$-element subsets of the set $\{1,2,3,\ldots,2n+1\}$. In this graph, an edge is drawn between two vertices if and only if their corresponding $n$-element subsets have no common element. Find the minimum number of edges of the graph $G_n$ in terms of $n$ that we must remove to reach a bipartite graph.

Show the answer

$\dfrac12\dbinom{2n}{n}=\dbinom{2n-1}{n-1}$

Show the solution

Solution (Averaging over the images of one odd cycle)

Let $X=\{1,\dots,2n+1\}$. Two $n$-subsets $A,B$ are adjacent iff they are disjoint; then exactly one element of $X$ lies outside $A\cup B$.

Upper bound: $\frac12\binom{2n}{n}$ edges suffice. Let $\mathcal V_1$ be the family of $n$-subsets containing the element $2n+1$ and $\mathcal V_0$ the family of the others. Two sets of $\mathcal V_1$ intersect, so there are no edges inside $\mathcal V_1$. Two sets of $\mathcal V_0$ are disjoint iff they are complementary in $\{1,\dots,2n\}$; so the edges inside $\mathcal V_0$ form a perfect matching with $\frac12\binom{2n}{n}$ edges. Removing these edges leaves a bipartite graph with parts $\mathcal V_0,\mathcal V_1$.

An odd cycle. Identify $X$ with the residues modulo $2n+1$ and let $I_i=\{i,i+1,\dots,i+n-1\}$. The sets $I_i$ and $I_{i+n}=\{i+n,\dots,i+2n-1\}$ are disjoint, so they are adjacent. Since $\gcd(n,2n+1)=1$, the sequence $I_0,I_n,I_{2n},\dots$ (indices modulo $2n+1$) runs through all $2n+1$ sets $I_i$ before returning to $I_0$. So \[C_0:\quad I_0-I_n-I_{2n}-\dots-I_{2n\cdot n}-I_0\] is a cycle of odd length $2n+1$ in $G_n$ (for $n=1$ it is the triangle).

Lower bound. Let $F$ be a set of edges whose removal leaves a bipartite graph. A bipartite graph has no odd cycle, so every odd cycle of $G_n$ contains an edge of $F$. Every permutation $\pi$ of $X$ acts on the $n$-subsets and preserves disjointness, so $\pi(C_0)$ is again a cycle of length $2n+1$. Count the pairs $(\pi,e)$ with $\pi$ a permutation of $X$ and $e\in F$ an edge of $\pi(C_0)$.

  • For each of the $(2n+1)!$ permutations there is at least one such edge. So the number of pairs is at least $(2n+1)!$.

  • Fix an edge $e=\{A,B\}$ and an edge $e'=\{A',B'\}$ of $C_0$. A permutation $\pi$ maps $e'$ to $e$ iff it maps $A'\to A$, $B'\to B$ or $A'\to B$, $B'\to A$ (and then the remaining element to the remaining element); there are $2\,(n!)^2$ such permutations. As $C_0$ has $2n+1$ edges, the edge $e$ lies on $\pi(C_0)$ for exactly $(2n+1)\cdot2\,(n!)^2$ permutations $\pi$. So the number of pairs is $|F|\cdot(2n+1)\cdot2\,(n!)^2$.

Therefore \[|F|\ \ge\ \frac{(2n+1)!}{(2n+1)\cdot2\,(n!)^2}=\frac{(2n)!}{2\,(n!)^2}=\frac12\binom{2n}{n}.\]

Both bounds agree, so the minimum number of edges to remove is \[\boxed{\frac12\binom{2n}{n}=\binom{2n-1}{n-1}}\]
