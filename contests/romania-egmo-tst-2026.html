Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

Let $n$ be a positive integer and $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \in [0,1]$ be real numbers. Prove the following inequality holds and specify the equality cases: \[(nx_1+(n-1)x_2+\ldots+2x_{n-1}+x_n)^2 \ge x_1^3+(x_1+x_2)^3+\ldots+(x_1+x_2+\ldots+x_n)^3.\]

Show the answer

proof; equality iff $(x_1,\dots,x_n)=(0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1)$ (any number of zeros followed by ones)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Telescoping with a lower bound for the partial sums)

Let $S_k=x_1+\dots+x_k$ ($S_0=0$) and $T_j=S_1+\dots+S_j$ ($T_0=0$). Since $nx_1+(n-1)x_2+\dots+x_n=S_1+S_2+\dots+S_n=T_n$, we must prove $T_n^2\ge\sum_{j=1}^nS_j^3$. It suffices to prove, for each $j$, \[ T_j^2-T_{j-1}^2=S_j\,(2T_{j-1}+S_j)\ \ge\ S_j^3 ,\tag{1} \] and add these for $j=1,\dots,n$.

If $S_j=0$, (1) is an equality. Let $s=S_j>0$. For $k<j$ we have $s-S_k=x_{k+1}+\dots+x_j\le j-k$, so $S_k\ge s-(j-k)$, and also $S_k\ge0$. Let $m=\lfloor s\rfloor\le j$ and $f=s-m\in[0,1)$. Using $S_{j-i}\ge s-i$ for $i=1,\dots,m$ (when $m=j$ the missing term $i=j$ would be $s-j=0$) and $S_k\ge0$ for the other $k$: \[ 2T_{j-1}+s\ \ge\ 2\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s-i)+s=2ms-m^2-m+s=m^2+2mf+f\ \ge\ m^2+2mf+f^2=s^2 , \] because $f\ge f^2$. Multiplying by $s>0$ gives (1).

Equality. We need equality in (1) for every $j$. Take $j=n$. If $S_n=0$, all $x_i=0$. Otherwise equality forces $f=0$ (so $m=S_n$ is an integer) and $S_{n-i}=m-i$ for $i=1,\dots,m$, $S_k=0$ for $k\le n-m$: that is, $x_1=\dots=x_{n-m}=0$ and $x_{n-m+1}=\dots=x_n=1$. Conversely, for this $x$ we have $S_k=\max(0,k-n+m)$, and the inequality reads $(1+2+\dots+m)^2\ge1^3+2^3+\dots+m^3$, which is the classical identity, so equality holds. Hence equality holds exactly for $x=(0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1)$ (including all zeros and all ones).

Solution 2 (Squares of partial sums telescope too)

Let $S_k=x_1+\dots+x_k$ ($S_0=0$) and $T_j=S_1+\dots+S_j$ ($T_0=0$). The left side is $T_n^2$, since $nx_1+(n-1)x_2+\dots+x_n=S_1+\dots+S_n$.

For each $k$, since $0\le x_k\le1$ and $S_k+S_{k-1}\ge0$, \[ S_k^2-S_{k-1}^2=x_k(S_k+S_{k-1})\le S_k+S_{k-1}. \] Adding for $k=1,\dots,j$: $S_j^2\le S_j+2T_{j-1}$. Multiplying by $S_j\ge0$: \[ S_j^3\le S_j^2+2S_jT_{j-1}=T_j^2-T_{j-1}^2 . \] Adding for $j=1,\dots,n$ gives $\sum S_j^3\le T_n^2$, the required inequality.

Equality. We need $S_j^3=T_j^2-T_{j-1}^2$ for all $j$; for $j=n$ this means $S_n=0$ or $(1-x_k)(S_k+S_{k-1})=0$ for all $k\le n$. If $S_n=0$ all $x_k$ are $0$. Otherwise each $k$ has $x_k=1$ or $S_k=0$. Let $k_0$ be the first index with $x_{k_0}\ne0$; then $x_{k_0}=1$, and for $k>k_0$ we have $S_k>0$, hence $x_k=1$. So $x=(0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1)$. Conversely, for such $x$ and every $j$ either $S_j=0$ or each $k\le j$ has $x_k=1$ or $S_k=S_{k-1}=0$, so every step above is an equality. Equality holds exactly for $x=(0,\dots,0,1,\dots,1)$, including the all-zero and all-one tuples.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Let $T$ be a $99 \times 99$ square grid from which we remove the $1 \times 1$ square in the center. We want to partition $T$ into smaller squares with integer side lengths and sides parallel to the edges of $T$. Call a square in such a partition good if it has an odd side length.

  • (a) What is the minimum number of good squares in a partition of $T$?

  • (b) What is the smallest value of the sum of the side lengths of the good squares in a partition of $T$?

Show the answer

(a) $8$; (b) $104$

Show the solution

Solution (Three colourings and a size bound)

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,99$; the removed cell is $(50,50)$. A square of the partition is described by its top row $a$, left column $b$ and side $s$.

Step 0: every square has side at most $49$. A square meeting both row $50$ and column $50$ contains the cell $(50,50)$ (its rows and its columns form intervals). So each square avoids row $50$ or column $50$, and therefore lies inside a strip of $49$ rows or $49$ columns.

Step 1: three weights. Give the cell $(r,c)$ the weights $(-1)^r$, $(-1)^c$ and $(-1)^{r+c}$. For a square $(a,b,s)$ the sums of these weights over its cells are $0,0,0$ if $s$ is even, and $(-1)^as$, $(-1)^bs$, $(-1)^{a+b}$ if $s$ is odd (an even number of consecutive rows has weight sum $0$, an odd number has the weight of its first row). Over the whole board, $\sum_{r=1}^{99}(-1)^r=-1$, so the three sums are $-99$, $-99$ and $(-1)^2=1$; removing the cell $(50,50)$, of weights $1,1,1$, gives $-100$, $-100$, $0$ for $T$.

Step 2: four classes of good squares. Put a good square into class $(\varepsilon,\delta)=((-1)^a,(-1)^b)$ (so the class $--$ consists of the good squares whose top row and left column are both odd), and let $n_{\varepsilon\delta}$, $\sigma_{\varepsilon\delta}$ be the number of squares and the sum of their sides in that class. Adding the weights over the partition: \[ -\sigma_{--}-\sigma_{-+}+\sigma_{+-}+\sigma_{++}=-100,\qquad -\sigma_{--}+\sigma_{-+}-\sigma_{+-}+\sigma_{++}=-100,\qquad n_{--}-n_{-+}-n_{+-}+n_{++}=0 . \] Adding and subtracting the first two equations: \[ \sigma_{--}=\sigma_{++}+100,\qquad \sigma_{-+}=\sigma_{+-}. \]

Step 3: lower bounds. As $\sigma_{--}\ge100$ and every side is at most $49<50$, we get $n_{--}\ge3$. By the third equation $n_{-+}+n_{+-}=n_{--}+n_{++}\ge3$, so the number of good squares is $2(n_{--}+n_{++})\ge6$. Moreover the area $99^2-1=9800$ is divisible by $4$, even squares have area divisible by $4$ and odd squares have area $\equiv1\pmod 4$, so the number of good squares is a multiple of $4$; hence it is at least $8$.

For the sides: since $n_{-+}+n_{+-}\ge3$, one of these two classes has at least two squares, so its side sum is at least $2$; as $\sigma_{-+}=\sigma_{+-}$, both are at least $2$. Hence the sum of the sides of the good squares is \[ \sigma_{--}+\sigma_{++}+\sigma_{-+}+\sigma_{+-}=100+2\sigma_{++}+2\sigma_{-+}\ge104 . \]

Step 4: one partition attaining both bounds. Take

  • the $49\times49$ squares with rows and columns $1$–$49$ and with rows and columns $51$–$99$;

  • six unit squares at $(49,50)$, $(49,51)$, $(50,51)$ and $(51,50)$, $(51,49)$, $(50,49)$;

  • $2\times2$ squares for the rest.

The rest is even: the region of rows $1$–$50$ and columns $50$–$99$ minus the hole and the three unit squares near it consists of the $48\times50$ rectangle (rows $1$–$48$) and the $2\times48$ rectangle (rows $49$–$50$, columns $52$–$99$); the opposite region (rows $50$–$99$, columns $1$–$50$) is symmetric, giving a $48\times50$ and a $2\times48$ rectangle. All four are tiled by $2\times2$ squares, and these four rectangles, the two large squares, the six unit squares and the hole cover the board exactly once.

This partition has $8$ good squares with side sum $49+49+6=104$. So the answers are (a) $\boxed{8}$ and (b) $\boxed{104}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral, and $I_1, I_2$ be the incenters of triangles $ABC$ and $ABD$ respectively. Let line $I_1I_2$ meet $BC$ and $AD$ at $E$ and $F$.

  • (a) Prove that there exists a circle $\omega$ tangent to $BC$ at point $E$ and to $AD$ at point $F$.

  • (b) Prove that $\omega$ is tangent to the circumcircle of quadrilateral $ABCD$.

Show the solution

Solution (Reim, the shooting lemma and a homothety)

We use directed angles $\angle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$; so the argument does not depend on the position of $E$, $F$ on the lines $BC$, $AD$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle, $M_1$ the midpoint of the arc $BC$ not containing $A$ (nor $D$) and $M_2$ the midpoint of the arc $AD$ not containing $B$ (nor $C$).

Facts.

  • (F1) (Incentre–excentre lemma.) $A,I_1,M_1$ are collinear and $M_1B=M_1C=M_1I_1$; likewise $B,I_2,M_2$ are collinear and $M_2A=M_2D=M_2I_2$.

  • (F2) $A,B,I_1,I_2$ are concyclic, since $\angle AI_1B=90^\circ+\tfrac12\angle ACB=90^\circ+\tfrac12\angle ADB=\angle AI_2B$ with $I_1,I_2$ on the same side of $AB$. By Reim's theorem for the circles $ABI_1I_2$ and $\Gamma$ and the lines $AI_1M_1$, $BI_2M_2$: $\angle(I_1I_2,I_1A)=\angle(BI_2,BA)=\angle(BM_2,BA)=\angle(M_1M_2,M_1A)$, so $EF=I_1I_2\parallel M_1M_2$.

  • (F3) (Shooting lemma.) If the line $M_1E$ meets $\Gamma$ again at $T$, then $M_1E\cdot M_1T=M_1B^2$. Indeed $\angle(BE,BM_1)=\angle(BC,BM_1)=\angle(CM_1,CB)=\angle(TM_1,TB)$ (isosceles triangle $M_1BC$, then the circle $\Gamma$), so $M_1B$ is tangent to the circle $BET$, and the power of $M_1$ gives the claim. ($T$ exists and $T\ne M_1$: the tangent at $M_1$ is parallel to $BC$, so the line $M_1E$ is not tangent to $\Gamma$.)

  • (F4) $\angle(M_1A,M_1M_2)=\angle(DA,DM_2)=\angle(AM_2,AD)$ (inscribed angles, then the isosceles triangle $AM_2D$).

Step 1: $A,I_1,F,T$ are concyclic, where $T$ is the second intersection of $M_1E$ with $\Gamma$. By (F1) and (F3), $M_1I_1^2=M_1E\cdot M_1T$. The points $I_1,E,T$ are not collinear, since $I_1\notin M_1E$ (otherwise $E=AM_1\cap I_1I_2=I_1$, which is not on $BC$). So $M_1I_1$ is tangent to the circle $I_1ET$ and $\angle(TI_1,TE)=\angle(I_1M_1,I_1E)=\angle(M_1A,M_1M_2)$ (as $I_1M_1=AM_1$ and $I_1E\parallel M_1M_2$). Then, using the circle $\Gamma$ and adding directed angles, \[ \angle(TA,TI_1)=\angle(TA,TM_1)-\angle(TI_1,TE)=\angle(M_2A,M_2M_1)-\angle(M_1A,M_1M_2)=\angle(AM_2,AM_1). \] On the other hand, by (F2) and (F4), \[ \angle(FA,FI_1)=\angle(AD,M_1M_2)=\angle(AD,AM_1)+\angle(AM_1,M_1M_2)=\angle(AD,AM_1)+\angle(AM_2,AD)=\angle(AM_2,AM_1). \] Hence $\angle(TA,TI_1)=\angle(FA,FI_1)$ and $A,I_1,F,T$ are concyclic.

Step 2: $T,F,M_2$ are collinear. $\angle(TF,TA)=\angle(I_1F,I_1A)=\angle(M_1M_2,M_1A)=\angle(TM_2,TA)$, using Step 1, (F2) and the circle $\Gamma$. So $TF$ and $TM_2$ are the same line.

Step 3: the circle. Let $h$ be the homothety with centre $T$ that maps $M_1$ to $E$, and $\omega=h(\Gamma)$; it is a circle tangent to $\Gamma$ at $T$. The tangent to $\Gamma$ at $M_1$ is parallel to $BC$ ($OM_1\perp BC$), so its image, the tangent to $\omega$ at $E$, is the parallel to $BC$ through $E$, namely $BC$. The point $h(M_2)$ lies on line $TM_2$ and on the parallel to $M_1M_2$ through $E$, which is line $EF$ by (F2); these lines meet only at $F$ (Step 2; they are different lines, since otherwise $I_1\in M_1M_2$ and then $I_1=M_1$). So $h(M_2)=F$, and the tangent to $\omega$ at $F$ is parallel to the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $M_2$, which is parallel to $AD$; as it passes through $F\in AD$, it is $AD$.

Therefore $\omega$ is tangent to $BC$ at $E$ and to $AD$ at $F$, which proves (a), and $\omega$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $T$, which proves (b). (A circle tangent to two given lines at two given points is unique, so this $\omega$ is the circle of part (a).)

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

Let $p > 3$ be a prime number, and denote by $k = \left\lfloor \frac{2p}{3} \right\rfloor$. Prove that $p^2$ divides the following expression: \[\binom{p}{1} + \binom{p}{2} + \ldots + \binom{p}{k}.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Harmonic sums modulo $p$)

All congruences below are modulo $p$ between rationals whose denominators are not divisible by $p$.

Step 1. For $1\le i\le p-1$, $\binom pi=\frac pi\binom{p-1}{i-1}$ and $\binom{p-1}{i-1}=\prod_{t=1}^{i-1}\frac{p-t}{t}\equiv(-1)^{i-1}$. Hence \[ \frac1p\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom pi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac1i\binom{p-1}{i-1}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{i-1}}{i}, \] and the left side is a rational with denominator prime to $p$ (in fact an integer, since $p\mid\binom pi$). So it suffices to show that the alternating sum on the right is $\equiv0$.

Step 2. With $H_m=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1m$, \[ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{i-1}}{i}=H_k-2\sum_{2j\le k}\frac1{2j}=H_k-H_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}. \] Write $t=\lfloor p/3\rfloor$. As $p>3$ is a prime, $3\nmid p$, so $p=3t+1$ or $p=3t+2$ (this is where $p>3$ is used; for $p=3$ the sum is $6$). If $p=3t+1$ then $k=2t$; if $p=3t+2$ then $k=2t+1$. In both cases $\lfloor k/2\rfloor=t$ and $k=p-1-t$.

Step 3: $H_{p-1-t}\equiv H_t$. Pairing $i$ with $p-i$, $\frac1i+\frac1{p-i}=\frac{p}{i(p-i)}\equiv0$, so $H_{p-1}\equiv0$. Also \[ H_{p-1}-H_{p-1-t}=\sum_{i=p-t}^{p-1}\frac1i=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac1{p-j}\equiv-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac1j=-H_t . \] Hence $H_{p-1-t}\equiv H_t$, i.e. $H_k-H_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\equiv0$. By Step 1, $\frac1p\sum_{i=1}^k\binom pi$ is an integer divisible by $p$, so $p^2\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom pi$.

Day 2

Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Find all functions $f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, with $f(0) \ne 0$, satisfying the following relation: \[f(x^2 - f(y)) = f(x+y)f(x-y), \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb{Z}.\]

Show the answer

$f\equiv1$, and $f(x)=-1$ for $3\mid x$, $f(x)=1$ for $3\nmid x$

Show the solution

Solution (A multiplicative recurrence with step 3)

Write $E(x,y)$ for the equation $f(x^2-f(y))=f(x+y)f(x-y)$ and $c=f(0)\ne0$.

Step 1: $f(u)=f(-u)$ for even $u$. $E(x,y)$ and $E(-x,y)$ have the same left side, so $f(x+y)f(x-y)=f(-x+y)f(-x-y)$. For even $u$ take $x=y=u/2$: $f(u)\,c=c\,f(-u)$, so $f(u)=f(-u)$.

Step 2: $f(-1)=f(1)=f(2)=1$. $E(0,0)$ gives $f(-c)=c^2$. Then $E(x,-c)$ reads $f(x^2-c^2)=f(x-c)f(x+c)$; at $x=c$ this is $c=c\,f(2c)$, so $f(2c)=1$, and $f(-2c)=1$ by Step 1. Now $E(0,2c)$ gives $f(-1)=f(2c)f(-2c)=1$, $E(0,-1)$ gives $f(-f(-1))=f(-1)f(1)$, i.e. $f(1)=f(-1)=1$, and $E(1,1)$ gives $f(0)=f(2)f(0)$, so $f(2)=1$.

Step 3: the recurrence. $E(x,1)$ and $E(x,2)$ have the same left side $f(x^2-1)$, so \[ f(x+2)f(x-2)=f(x+1)f(x-1)\qquad\text{for all }x . \] We claim $f(n)=c$ if $3\mid n$ and $f(n)=1$ otherwise. This holds for $n=-1,0,1,2$, and this pattern satisfies the recurrence (both sides equal $c$ when $3\nmid x$ and $1$ when $3\mid x$). All these values are nonzero. Going up: if the claim holds for $n-3,n-2,n-1,n$ (a window of four consecutive integers, starting with $-1,0,1,2$), then $x=n-1$ gives $f(n+1)f(n-3)=f(n)f(n-2)$, which determines $f(n+1)$, and it agrees with the pattern. Going down: if the claim holds for $n,n+1,n+2,n+3$, then $x=n+1$ gives $f(n+3)f(n-1)=f(n+2)f(n)$, which determines $f(n-1)$ in the same way. By induction in both directions the claim holds for all integers.

Step 4: $c=\pm1$. We know $f(-c)=c^2$. If $3\mid c$ then $f(-c)=c$, so $c=1$, contradicting $3\mid c$. So $3\nmid c$, $f(-c)=1=c^2$ and $c=\pm1$.

Check. $c=1$ gives $f\equiv1$, which works. For $c=-1$, $f(t)=-1$ iff $3\mid t$. If $3\mid y$: then $x+y\equiv x-y\pmod 3$, so the right side is $1$, and $x^2-f(y)=x^2+1\not\equiv0\pmod3$, so the left side is $1$. If $3\nmid y$: $f(y)=1$; since $2y\not\equiv0$, at most one of $x+y$, $x-y$ is divisible by $3$, and exactly one is iff $3\nmid x$; so the right side is $-1$ iff $3\nmid x$, iff $3\mid x^2-1$, iff the left side is $-1$.

The solutions are $\boxed{f\equiv1}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&3\mid x\\ 1,&3\nmid x\end{cases}}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

In an acute-angled non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ with orthocenter $H$, the perpendicular bisectors of the segments $AB$ and $AC$ meet the line $BC$ at points $D$ and $E$ respectively. Let $S$ be the second intersection of the circles $(ABC)$ and $(ADE)$, distinct from $A$.

Prove that $\angle ASH = 90^\circ$ if and only if $\angle BAC = 45^\circ$.

Show the solution

Solution (Radical axes and a computation on $BC$)

Let $\Gamma=(ABC)$, $\omega=(ADE)$, and let $\gamma$ be the circle with diameter $AH$. Write $A,B,C$ for the angles of the triangle and take the circumradius as the unit of length, so $BC=2\sin A$, $CA=2\sin B$, $AB=2\sin C$.

Step 1: $\angle ASH=90^\circ$ iff $S=Q$, where $Q$ is the second intersection of $\Gamma$ and $\gamma$. Indeed $\angle ASH=90^\circ$ iff $S$ lies on $\gamma$ ($S\ne H$, as $H$ is inside $\Gamma$), and $S\in\Gamma$, $S\ne A$. The circles $\Gamma$ and $\gamma$ are not tangent at $A$: otherwise $A$, the circumcentre $O$ and the midpoint of $AH$ would be collinear, so $O$ would lie on the altitude from $A$ and $AB=AC$. So $\Gamma\cap\gamma=\{A,Q\}$ with $Q\ne A$.

Step 2: a coordinate on $BC$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. For a point $X$ of the line $BC$ let $x$ be its signed distance from $M$, positive towards $C$; so $B$ has coordinate $-\sin A$ and $C$ has coordinate $\sin A$.

  • Let $A_1$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$. Then $BA_1=AB\cos B=2\sin C\cos B$, so $A_1$ has coordinate \[ t=2\sin C\cos B-\sin(B+C)=\sin(C-B)\ne0 . \]

  • Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AB$. In the right triangle $BND$ the angle at $B$ is the acute angle $B$, so $D$ is on the ray $BC$ and $BD=\dfrac{BN}{\cos B}=\dfrac{\sin C}{\cos B}$. Using $\sin A=\sin B\cos C+\cos B\sin C$ and $\cos A=\sin B\sin C-\cos B\cos C$, the coordinate of $D$ is \[ d=\frac{\sin C}{\cos B}-\sin A=\frac{\sin C(1-\cos^2B)-\sin B\cos B\cos C}{\cos B}=\tan B\,(\sin B\sin C-\cos B\cos C)=\tan B\cos A . \] In the same way $E$ has coordinate $e=-\tan C\cos A$. As $d-e=\cos A(\tan B+\tan C)>0$, $D\ne E$.

Step 3: three powers. Let $X$ be the point of $BC$ with coordinate $x$.

  • $\mathrm{pow}_\Gamma(X)=\overline{XB}\cdot\overline{XC}=x^2-\sin^2A$.

  • $\mathrm{pow}_\omega(X)=\overline{XD}\cdot\overline{XE}=(x-d)(x-e)$.

  • The centre $Z$ of $\gamma$ is the midpoint of $AH$, on the altitude, so $XZ^2=XA_1^2+A_1Z^2$ and $\mathrm{pow}_\gamma(X)=(x-t)^2+\mathrm{pow}_\gamma(A_1)$. The line $A_1A$ meets $\gamma$ at $H$ and $A$, on the same side of $A_1$, so $\mathrm{pow}_\gamma(A_1)=A_1H\cdot A_1A$. In the right triangles $A_1BH$ and $A_1AC$ we have $\angle HBA_1=90^\circ-C$, so $A_1H=A_1B\cot C$ and $A_1A=A_1C\tan C$; hence $A_1H\cdot A_1A=A_1B\cdot A_1C=(\sin A+t)(\sin A-t)$. Therefore \[ \mathrm{pow}_\gamma(X)=(x-t)^2+\sin^2A-t^2=x^2-2tx+\sin^2A . \]

Step 4: reduction to one point. $\mathrm{pow}_\Gamma(X)=\mathrm{pow}_\gamma(X)$ holds iff $2tx=2\sin^2A$, i.e. for exactly one point $X_0$ of $BC$, with coordinate \[ x_0=\frac{\sin^2A}{t}. \] So $X_0$ lies on the radical axis of $\Gamma$ and $\gamma$, which is the line $AQ$; and $X_0\ne A$. The radical axis of $\Gamma$ and $\omega$ is the line $AS$. A line through $A$ meets $\Gamma$ in only one more point, so \[ S=Q\iff AS=AQ\iff X_0\in AS\iff\mathrm{pow}_\Gamma(X_0)=\mathrm{pow}_\omega(X_0). \]

Step 5: the computation. By Step 3, \[ \mathrm{pow}_\Gamma(X_0)-\mathrm{pow}_\omega(X_0)=(d+e)x_0-de-\sin^2A . \] Here $d+e=\cos A(\tan B-\tan C)=\dfrac{\cos A\sin(B-C)}{\cos B\cos C}=-\dfrac{t\cos A}{\cos B\cos C}$, so $(d+e)x_0=-\dfrac{\sin^2A\cos A}{\cos B\cos C}$, and $-de=\cos^2A\tan B\tan C$. Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{pow}_\Gamma(X_0)-\mathrm{pow}_\omega(X_0)&=\frac{\cos^2A\sin B\sin C-\sin^2A\,(\cos A+\cos B\cos C)}{\cos B\cos C}\\ &=\frac{(\cos^2A-\sin^2A)\sin B\sin C}{\cos B\cos C}=\cos2A\,\tan B\tan C , \end{aligned} \] because $\cos A+\cos B\cos C=\sin B\sin C$. Since $\tan B\tan C>0$, Steps 1 and 4 give \[ \angle ASH=90^\circ\iff\cos2A=0\iff\angle BAC=45^\circ \] (as $0^\circ<A<90^\circ$).

Remark. The computation also shows $MD=OM\tan B$ and $ME=OM\tan C$ (as $OM=\cos A$), and $x_0\,t=\sin^2A$, i.e. $\overline{MX_0}\cdot\overline{MA_1}=MB^2$: the point $X_0$ is the harmonic conjugate of $A_1$ with respect to $B$ and $C$.
