Short Answer

Problem 1

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Constraint-Based Enumeration

A die with faces numbered $1,2,3,4,5,6$ is rolled $11$ times. The number of possibilities in which the number $1$ is rolled exactly $6$ times, but never on two consecutive rolls, is …

Show the answer

$3125$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The positions of the ones are forced)

Six rolls among $11$ show a $1$, and no two of them are consecutive. Between two consecutive $1$s there is at least one other roll, so the $6$ ones need at least $6+5=11$ rolls. Hence the ones are exactly in rolls $1,3,5,7,9,11$, and the rolls $2,4,6,8,10$ show numbers from $\{2,3,4,5,6\}$, in $5^5$ ways. The answer is $\boxed{3125}$.

Solution 2 (Choosing non-adjacent positions)

The number of ways to choose $k$ pairwise non-adjacent positions among $n$ in a row is $\binom{n-k+1}{k}$ (delete one position after each of the first $k-1$ chosen ones to get an arbitrary $k$-subset of $n-k+1$ positions). Here it is $\binom{6}{6}=1$. Each of the remaining $5$ rolls shows one of $2,3,4,5,6$, so the count is $1\cdot5^5=\boxed{3125}$.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 16: Diophantine Equations: Linear and Structural Foundations · Linear Diophantine Equations

Positive integers $a,b$ satisfy $23a+20b=528$. The smallest possible value of $a+b$ is …

Show the answer

$24$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reduce modulo 20)

Modulo $20$ the equation gives $3a\equiv 528\equiv 8\pmod{20}$. Since $3\cdot7=21\equiv1$, we get $a\equiv 56\equiv16\pmod{20}$. Also $23a<528$ gives $a\le22$, so $a=16$ and $20b=528-368=160$, $b=8$. This is the only solution in positive integers, so the smallest (indeed the only) value of $a+b$ is $\boxed{24}$.

Solution 2 (Rewrite with a+b)

Write $23a+20b=20(a+b)+3a=528$. Then $3a=528-20(a+b)$ must be positive and divisible by $3$. Since $528\equiv0\pmod 3$, we need $3\mid 20(a+b)$, i.e. $3\mid a+b$; and $a+b<\frac{528}{20}$, so $a+b\le 26$, hence $a+b\in\{3,6,\dots,24\}$. Then $a=\frac{528-20(a+b)}{3}$ and $b=(a+b)-a$; we need $b>0$, i.e. $a+b>\frac{528-20(a+b)}3$, i.e. $23(a+b)>528$, $a+b\ge 23$. So $a+b=24$ (with $a=16$, $b=8$), and the answer is $\boxed{24}$.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

Suppose the quadratic equation $x^2+4x+1=0$ has roots $p$ and $q$, and the quadratic equation $x^2+12x+1=0$ has roots $r$ and $s$.

The value of $(p-r)(q-r)(p-s)(q-s)$ is …

Show the answer

$64$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Evaluate the first quadratic at r and s)

Let $f(x)=x^2+4x+1=(x-p)(x-q)$. Then $(p-r)(q-r)=f(r)$ and $(p-s)(q-s)=f(s)$. Since $r^2+12r+1=0$, we have $f(r)=r^2+4r+1=-8r$, and likewise $f(s)=-8s$. Therefore \[(p-r)(q-r)(p-s)(q-s)=f(r)f(s)=64rs=64\cdot1=\boxed{64},\] using $rs=1$ by Vieta.

Solution 2 (Explicit roots)

By the quadratic formula, $p,q=-2\pm\sqrt3$ and $r,s=-6\pm\sqrt{35}$; take $p=-2+\sqrt3$, $q=-2-\sqrt3$. Group the product as $\bigl[(p-r)(p-s)\bigr]\bigl[(q-r)(q-s)\bigr]$. For any number $z$, \[(z-r)(z-s)=\bigl(z+6-\sqrt{35}\bigr)\bigl(z+6+\sqrt{35}\bigr)=(z+6)^2-35 .\] For $z=p$ this is $(4+\sqrt3)^2-35=19+8\sqrt3-35=-16+8\sqrt3$, and for $z=q$ it is $(4-\sqrt3)^2-35=-16-8\sqrt3$. Hence \[(p-r)(q-r)(p-s)(q-s)=(-16+8\sqrt3)(-16-8\sqrt3)=256-192=\boxed{64}.\]

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $\Gamma_1$ be a circle with center $A$ and $\Gamma_2$ be a circle with center $B$. All points of circle $\Gamma_2$ lie inside or on circle $\Gamma_1$. Let $C$ be a point lying inside or on circle $\Gamma_2$. If the radius of circle $\Gamma_1$ is $16$, the maximum possible area of triangle $ABC$ is …

Show the answer

$32$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Base times height)

Let $r$ be the radius of $\Gamma_2$. Since $\Gamma_2$ lies inside $\Gamma_1$, the point of $\Gamma_2$ farthest from $A$ (at distance $AB+r$) lies in $\Gamma_1$, so $AB+r\le16$. The point $C$ is within distance $r$ of $B$, so its distance to line $AB$ is at most $r$. Hence \[[ABC]\le\tfrac12\,AB\cdot r\le\tfrac12(16-r)\,r\le\tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{(16-r)+r}{2}\Bigr)^2=32\] by AM–GM. Equality holds for $r=8$, $AB=8$ and $C$ on $\Gamma_2$ with $BC\perp AB$ (see the figure). The maximum area is $\boxed{32}$.

Solution 2 (Two sides and the included angle)

Let $r$ be the radius of $\Gamma_2$ and $d=AB$; as $\Gamma_2$ lies inside $\Gamma_1$, $d+r\le16$. Then $[ABC]=\frac12\,AB\cdot BC\sin\angle ABC\le\frac12\,d\,r$ because $BC\le r$. For fixed sum $d+r\le16$ the product $dr$ is at most $8\cdot8=64$ (it equals $\frac{(d+r)^2-(d-r)^2}{4}$). So $[ABC]\le32$, attained at $d=r=8$, $BC=8$, $\angle ABC=90^\circ$. The answer is $\boxed{32}$.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Arithmetic Structure of Products and Powers

The number of ordered pairs of positive integers $(a,b)$ such that $a$ divides $b^2$, $b$ divides $a^2$, and $ab=10^{10}$ is …

Show the answer

$9$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Exponents of 2 and 5)

Write $a=2^x5^y$; then $b=2^{10-x}5^{10-y}$ with $0\le x,y\le10$. The condition $a\mid b^2$ means $x\le2(10-x)$ and $y\le2(10-y)$, i.e. $x,y\le6$; the condition $b\mid a^2$ means $10-x\le 2x$ and $10-y\le2y$, i.e. $x,y\ge4$. So $x,y\in\{4,5,6\}$ independently, giving $3\cdot3=\boxed{9}$ pairs.

Solution 2 (A ratio condition)

Since $ab=10^{10}$, multiplying by $a^2$ shows $a\mid b^2\iff a^3\mid a^2b^2=10^{20}$; similarly $b\mid a^2\iff b^3\mid10^{20}\iff\bigl(\tfrac{10^{10}}{a}\bigr)^3\mid10^{20}\iff10^{10}\mid a^3$. So we need $10^{10}\mid a^3\mid10^{20}$, where $a\mid10^{10}$. For each of the primes $2$ and $5$, the exponent $e$ of $a$ must satisfy $10\le3e\le20$, i.e. $e\in\{4,5,6\}$. Hence there are $3^2=\boxed{9}$ pairs.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

Let $a$ and $b$ be positive real numbers. If the maximum possible value of $(a+7b)(9-a^2-b^2)$ is $M$, then the value of $M^2$ is …

Show the answer

$5400$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Cauchy–Schwarz, then AM–GM)

Let $\rho=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. If $\rho\ge3$ the product is $\le0$, so assume $\rho<3$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $a+7b\le\sqrt{1^2+7^2}\,\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=5\sqrt2\,\rho$, with equality when $b=7a$. So the expression is at most $5\sqrt2\,\rho(9-\rho^2)$. By AM–GM applied to $2\rho^2,\ 9-\rho^2,\ 9-\rho^2$ (sum $18$), \[\rho^2(9-\rho^2)^2=\tfrac12\cdot2\rho^2(9-\rho^2)(9-\rho^2)\le\tfrac12\cdot6^3=108,\] so $\rho(9-\rho^2)\le\sqrt{108}=6\sqrt3$, with equality at $\rho^2=3$. Hence $M=5\sqrt2\cdot6\sqrt3=30\sqrt6$, attained at $b=7a$, $a^2+b^2=3$ (i.e. $a=\sqrt{3/50}$, $b=7\sqrt{3/50}$). Then $M^2=900\cdot6=\boxed{5400}$.

Solution 2 (Polar coordinates and calculus)

Put $a=\rho\cos\theta$, $b=\rho\sin\theta$ with $0<\theta<\frac\pi2$. Then $a+7b=\rho(\cos\theta+7\sin\theta)$, and $\cos\theta+7\sin\theta=\sqrt{50}\sin(\theta+\varphi)$ with $\tan\varphi=\frac17$; its maximum $\sqrt{50}$ is attained at $\tan\theta=7$, inside the allowed range. For $0<\rho<3$ the function $g(\rho)=\rho(9-\rho^2)$ has $g'(\rho)=9-3\rho^2$, which vanishes at $\rho=\sqrt3$, where $g$ attains its maximum $\sqrt3\cdot6=6\sqrt3$. Hence $M=\sqrt{50}\cdot6\sqrt3$ and $M^2=50\cdot108=\boxed{5400}$.

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Addition and Balance Relations

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle BAC=60^\circ$. Points $D$, $E$, and $F$ lie on sides $AB$, $BC$, and $AC$ respectively, such that $BD=100\sqrt3$, $FC=50\sqrt3$, and $BE=2EC$. Let $G$ be a point on $DF$ such that $DG=2GF$. The length of the segment $EG$ is …

Show the answer

$100$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors)

Since $E$ divides $BC$ with $BE:EC=2:1$ and $G$ divides $DF$ with $DG:GF=2:1$, as vectors \[\vec E=\tfrac13\vec B+\tfrac23\vec C,\qquad \vec G=\tfrac13\vec D+\tfrac23\vec F,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \overrightarrow{EG}=\tfrac13\overrightarrow{BD}+\tfrac23\overrightarrow{CF}.\] Here $\overrightarrow{BD}$ points from $B$ towards $A$ with length $100\sqrt3$, and $\overrightarrow{CF}$ points from $C$ towards $A$ with length $50\sqrt3$. So $\overrightarrow{EG}=\frac{100\sqrt3}{3}(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)$, where $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ are the unit vectors along $\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\overrightarrow{CA}$. The angle between $\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\overrightarrow{CA}$ equals the angle between $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$, which is $60^\circ$, so $|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|^2=2+2\cos60^\circ=3$. Therefore \[EG=\frac{100\sqrt3}{3}\cdot\sqrt3=\boxed{100},\] independently of the shape of the triangle.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(c,0)$, $C=\bigl(\frac b2,\frac{\sqrt3}2b\bigr)$, where $c=AB$, $b=AC$ (so $\angle A=60^\circ$). Then $D=(c-100\sqrt3,0)$ and, moving from $C$ towards $A$ by $50\sqrt3$, $F=\frac{b-50\sqrt3}{b}\,C=\bigl(\frac{b-50\sqrt3}2,\frac{\sqrt3}2(b-50\sqrt3)\bigr)$. Further $E=\frac{B+2C}{3}=\bigl(\frac{c+b}3,\frac{\sqrt3 b}{3}\bigr)$ and $G=\frac{D+2F}3=\bigl(\frac{c-100\sqrt3+b-50\sqrt3}{3},\frac{\sqrt3(b-50\sqrt3)}3\bigr)$. Hence \[G-E=\Bigl(-\frac{150\sqrt3}{3},\,-\frac{150}{3}\Bigr)=(-50\sqrt3,\,-50),\] and $EG=\sqrt{7500+2500}=\boxed{100}$.

Problem 8

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · Recursive Counting

The number of permutations $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_8$ of $1,2,\dots,8$ such that \[a_i<a_{i+2}\ \text{for all } i\in\{1,2,\dots,6\}\quad\text{and}\quad a_i<a_{i+3}\ \text{for all } i\in\{1,2,\dots,5\}\] is …

Show the answer

$34$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Where the smallest number can be)

Every integer $d\ge2$ is a sum of $2$s and $3$s, so by chaining the two conditions, $a_i<a_j$ whenever $j-i\ge2$. Conversely this stronger condition implies the given ones. Let $f(n)$ be the number of permutations of $1,\dots,n$ with $a_i<a_j$ whenever $j\ge i+2$.

The number $1$ cannot be in position $j\ge3$ (else $a_{j-2}<1$). If $a_1=1$, then $a_2,\dots,a_n$ is such a permutation of $2,\dots,n$: $f(n-1)$ ways. If $a_2=1$, then $a_1<a_j$ for all $j\ge3$, so $a_1=2$, and $a_3,\dots,a_n$ is such a permutation of $3,\dots,n$: $f(n-2)$ ways. All conditions involving positions $1,2$ hold automatically, as $a_1,a_2$ are the two smallest numbers. So \[f(n)=f(n-1)+f(n-2),\qquad f(1)=1,\ f(2)=2,\] giving $f=1,2,3,5,8,13,21,34$. The answer is $\boxed{34}$.

Solution 2 (Disjoint adjacent swaps)

Every integer $d\ge2$ is a sum of $2$s and $3$s, so by chaining the two conditions, $a_i<a_j$ whenever $j-i\ge2$; conversely this implies the given conditions. Call such permutations valid.

In a valid permutation, $a_i$ is smaller than the $8-i-1$ entries in positions $i+2,\dots,8$, so $a_i\le i+1$; and $a_i$ is larger than the $i-2$ entries in positions $1,\dots,i-2$, so $a_i\ge i-1$. Thus every entry is at most one place away from its own position. Such a permutation is the identity with some disjoint adjacent pairs swapped: if $i$ is the first position with $a_i\ne i$, then $a_1,\dots,a_{i-1}$ are $1,\dots,i-1$, so $a_i=i+1$, and the value $i$ must sit in position $i+1$ (it is at most one place from position $i$, and position $i-1$ is taken); then we continue from position $i+2$.

Conversely, the identity with disjoint adjacent swaps is valid: every entry moves at most one place, so for $j\ge i+2$ we get $a_i\le i+1\le j-1\le a_j$, and $a_i=a_j$ is impossible.

So valid permutations correspond to ways of choosing disjoint adjacent pairs among positions $1,\dots,8$, i.e. tilings of a $1\times8$ strip by $1\times1$ and $1\times2$ tiles. Their number $t_n$ satisfies $t_n=t_{n-1}+t_{n-2}$ with $t_1=1$, $t_2=2$, so $t_8=\boxed{34}$.

Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Angle Chasing and Hidden Equalities

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle. Point $D$ lies on $BC$ such that $AD$ is perpendicular to $BC$. Let the reflections of $D$ in $AB$ and $AC$ be $P$ and $Q$ respectively. Let lines $AP$ and $AQ$ intersect the circumcircle of $ABC$ again at $R\ne A$ and $S\ne A$.

Prove that $AR=AS$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Inscribed angles)

Write $A,B,C$ for the angles of the triangle, all acute, and let $\rho$ be the circumradius. Since $D$ is the foot of the altitude, $\angle BAD=90^\circ-B$. The reflection in $AB$ maps $D$ to $P$, so $\angle BAP=\angle BAD=90^\circ-B$, and ray $AP$ lies on the other side of line $AB$ from $C$. Hence $R$ lies on arc $AB$ not containing $C$, $ACBR$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, and $\angle ARB=180^\circ-C$. In triangle $ABR$, \[\angle ABR=180^\circ-(90^\circ-B)-(180^\circ-C)=B+C-90^\circ=90^\circ-A,\] which is positive. By the law of sines in the circumcircle, $AR=2\rho\sin\angle ABR=2\rho\cos A$.

In the same way, $\angle CAS=90^\circ-C$, $S$ lies on arc $AC$ not containing $B$, $\angle ASC=180^\circ-B$, and $\angle ACS=B+C-90^\circ=90^\circ-A$, so $AS=2\rho\cos A$. Therefore $AR=AS$.

Solution 2 (Symmetry in the line AO)

Let $O$ be the circumcenter. As the triangle is acute, $O$ lies inside the triangle, and the isosceles triangles $OAB$, $OAC$ (with apex angles $\angle AOB=2C$, $\angle AOC=2B$) give $\angle BAO=90^\circ-C$ and $\angle CAO=90^\circ-B$.

Reflecting $D$ in $AB$ gives $\angle BAP=\angle BAD=90^\circ-B$, with ray $AP$ on the side of $AB$ opposite to $C$ and $O$. So \[\angle RAO=\angle PAB+\angle BAO=(90^\circ-B)+(90^\circ-C)=A.\] Similarly $\angle QAC=90^\circ-C$, with ray $AQ$ on the side of $AC$ opposite to $B$ and $O$, so $\angle SAO=(90^\circ-C)+(90^\circ-B)=A$. The rays $AR$ and $AS$ lie on different sides of line $AO$ (one beyond $AB$, the other beyond $AC$) and make equal angles with it. Hence the reflection in line $AO$ maps ray $AR$ to ray $AS$. This reflection maps the circumcircle to itself (the line passes through its center), so it maps $R$, the second intersection of ray $AR$ with the circle, to $S$. Reflections preserve distances, so $AR=AS$.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Elimination and Reduction

Let $(x,y)$ be a pair of real numbers that satisfies the system of equations \[\sqrt{6x}\left(1+\frac1{6x-y}\right)=2,\qquad \sqrt{2y}\left(1-\frac1{6x-y}\right)=6.\] Find the minimum possible value of $\dfrac yx$.

Show the answer

$9$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The ratio as the unknown)

The square roots must be defined and the left sides are nonzero, so $x>0$ and $y>0$. Put $u=\sqrt{6x}>0$, $w=\sqrt y>0$ and $t=6x-y=u^2-w^2\ne0$. The equations become \[1+\frac1t=\frac2u,\qquad 1-\frac1t=\frac{6}{\sqrt2\,w}=\frac{3\sqrt2}{w}.\] Adding and subtracting: $\frac1u+\frac{3\sqrt2}{2w}=1$ and $\frac1t=\frac1u-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2w}$. Let $k=\frac wu>0$, so that $\frac yx=\frac{6w^2}{u^2}=6k^2$. The first relation gives $\frac1u\bigl(1+\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}\bigr)=1$, and the second gives $\frac1t=\frac1u\bigl(1-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}\bigr)$. Multiplying $t=u^2(1-k^2)$ by $\frac1t$: \[1=u\,(1-k^2)\Bigl(1-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}\Bigr)=(1-k^2)\Bigl(1-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}\Bigr)\Bigl(1+\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}\Bigr)=(1-k^2)\Bigl(1-\frac{9}{2k^2}\Bigr).\] Expanding, $-k^2-\frac9{2k^2}+\frac92=0$, i.e. $2k^4-9k^2+9=0$, so $k^2=3$ or $k^2=\frac32$.

Both occur. Reversing the steps, for each such $k$ put $u=1+\frac{3\sqrt2}{2k}>0$, $w=ku$, $x=\frac{u^2}6$, $y=w^2$; the derived relations then give back both equations. For $k^2=\frac32$: $u=1+\sqrt3$, $x=\frac{2+\sqrt3}{3}$, $y=3(2+\sqrt3)$. For $k^2=3$: $u=1+\sqrt{\tfrac32}$. Hence $\frac yx\in\{9,18\}$ and the minimum is $\boxed{9}$.

Solution 2 (Sum and difference of the roots)

The square roots must be defined and the left sides are nonzero, so $x,y>0$. Put $u=\sqrt{6x}$, $w=\sqrt y$, $t=u^2-w^2=(u+w)(u-w)\ne0$. The equations read $u+\frac ut=2$ and $w-\frac wt=3\sqrt2$. Adding and subtracting, and using $\frac{u\mp w}{t}=\frac1{u\pm w}$: \[(u+w)+\frac1{u+w}=2+3\sqrt2,\qquad (u-w)+\frac1{u-w}=2-3\sqrt2.\] With $\alpha=u+w$ and $\beta=u-w$, the first equation is $\alpha^2-(2+3\sqrt2)\alpha+1=0$, with discriminant $18+12\sqrt2=6(\sqrt2+1)^2$, so $\alpha=\frac{2+3\sqrt2\pm\sqrt6(\sqrt2+1)}2$; the second has discriminant $18-12\sqrt2=6(\sqrt2-1)^2$, so $\beta=\frac{2-3\sqrt2\pm\sqrt6(\sqrt2-1)}2$. We need $u=\frac{\alpha+\beta}2>0$; the smaller $\alpha$ (about $0.165$) gives $u<0$ with both $\beta$ (about $-0.61$ and $-1.63$), so $\alpha=\frac{2+3\sqrt2+2\sqrt3+\sqrt6}{2}$. Then

  • $\beta=\frac{2-3\sqrt2+2\sqrt3-\sqrt6}{2}$: $u=1+\sqrt3$, $w=\frac{3\sqrt2+\sqrt6}2=\frac{\sqrt6}{2}(1+\sqrt3)$, so $\frac yx=\frac{6w^2}{u^2}=6\cdot\frac64=9$;

  • $\beta=\frac{2-3\sqrt2-2\sqrt3+\sqrt6}{2}$: $u=\frac{2+\sqrt6}2$, $w=\frac{3\sqrt2+2\sqrt3}2=\sqrt3\,u$, so $\frac yx=18$.

Each pair $(\alpha,\beta)$ gives $u,w>0$, $t=\alpha\beta\ne0$, and satisfies the two equations, which are equivalent to the original system. The minimum of $\frac yx$ is $\boxed{9}$.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting

Let $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_{10}$ be subsets of a set $X$. It is known that \[A_i\cup A_j\cup A_k=X\ \text{ for all } 1\le i<j<k\le10,\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_i\cup A_j\ne X\ \text{ for all } 1\le i<j\le10.\] Prove that $X$ has at least $45$ elements.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (One witness element for each pair)

For each pair $\{i,j\}$ with $1\le i<j\le10$, the set $A_i\cup A_j$ is not all of $X$, so we can choose an element $x_{ij}\in X$ with $x_{ij}\notin A_i$ and $x_{ij}\notin A_j$. For every $k\ne i,j$ we have $A_i\cup A_j\cup A_k=X$, so $x_{ij}\in A_k$. Thus \[\{\,m: x_{ij}\notin A_m\,\}=\{i,j\}\] exactly. Consequently different pairs give different elements: if $x_{ij}=x_{kl}$, the set of indices $m$ with $x_{ij}\notin A_m$ is both $\{i,j\}$ and $\{k,l\}$. Hence $X$ contains at least $\binom{10}{2}=45$ distinct elements $x_{ij}$.

Solution 2 (Counting the sets that miss an element)

For $x\in X$ let $M(x)=\{m: x\notin A_m\}$. The first condition says that no three indices lie in a common $M(x)$: if $i,j,k\in M(x)$ then $x\notin A_i\cup A_j\cup A_k=X$, impossible. So $|M(x)|\le2$ for every $x$.

The second condition says that for every pair $\{i,j\}$ some $x$ has $x\notin A_i\cup A_j$, i.e. $\{i,j\}\subseteq M(x)$. Since $|M(x)|\le2$, this forces $M(x)=\{i,j\}$. So the map $x\mapsto M(x)$ takes each of the $\binom{10}2=45$ two-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ as a value. A map onto a set of $45$ values needs at least $45$ arguments, so $|X|\ge45$. (Equality is possible: take $X$ to be the set of pairs and $A_m$ the pairs not containing $m$.)

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · Comparing Valuations Across Terms

Let $a$ and $b$ be two distinct positive integers. Prove that \[\prod_{i=0}^{2026}\gcd(a+i,\,b+i)\] divides $2026!\times(a-b)$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting multiples of prime powers)

We may assume $a>b$ (the statement is symmetric up to the sign of $a-b$, which does not affect divisibility). Put $D=a-b>0$ and $g_i=\gcd(a+i,b+i)$. Since $\gcd(a+i,b+i)=\gcd(a+i,(a+i)-(b+i))$, we have $g_i=\gcd(a+i,D)$.

Fix a prime $p$ and let $e=v_p(D)$. Then $v_p(g_i)=\min\bigl(v_p(a+i),e\bigr)=\#\{k: 1\le k\le e,\ p^k\mid a+i\}$. Summing over $i=0,\dots,2026$ and swapping the order of counting, \[v_p\Bigl(\prod_{i=0}^{2026}g_i\Bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{e}N_k,\qquad N_k=\#\{i: 0\le i\le2026,\ p^k\mid a+i\}.\] Among the $2027$ consecutive integers $a,a+1,\dots,a+2026$, the multiples of $p^k$ number at most $\bigl\lfloor\frac{2026}{p^k}\bigr\rfloor+1$ (if the first one is $a+i_0$, the others are $a+i_0+p^k,\dots$, and $i_0+mp^k\le2026$ gives $m\le\frac{2026}{p^k}$). Therefore \[v_p\Bigl(\prod g_i\Bigr)\le\sum_{k=1}^{e}\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2026}{p^k}\Bigr\rfloor+1\Bigr)\le\sum_{k\ge1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{2026}{p^k}\Bigr\rfloor+e=v_p(2026!)+v_p(D)\] by Legendre's formula. This holds for every prime $p$, so $\prod_{i=0}^{2026}\gcd(a+i,b+i)$ divides $2026!\,(a-b)$.
