Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Find all positive integers $n$ with the property that the binomial coefficients \[\binom{n}{0},\ \binom{n}{1},\ \binom{n}{2},\ \ldots,\ \binom{n}{n-1}\ \text{and}\ \binom{n}{n}\] are all odd numbers.

Show the answer

$n=2^k-1$ with $k\ge1$, i.e. $n=1,3,7,15,\dots$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting odd coefficients with polynomials mod 2)

We work with polynomials whose coefficients are read modulo $2$ and write $F\equiv G$ when all coefficients of $F-G$ are even. Since $(1+x)^2=1+2x+x^2\equiv1+x^2$, squaring repeatedly gives $(1+x)^{2^j}\equiv1+x^{2^j}$ for every $j\ge0$.

Write $n$ in binary, $n=2^{j_1}+2^{j_2}+\dots+2^{j_s}$ with $j_1>j_2>\dots>j_s\ge0$; here $s$ is the number of binary digits $1$ of $n$. Then \[(1+x)^n=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(1+x)^{2^{j_i}}\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{s}\bigl(1+x^{2^{j_i}}\bigr).\] Expanding the right-hand product gives $2^s$ terms $x^{e}$, where $e$ runs over the sums of the subsets of $\{2^{j_1},\dots,2^{j_s}\}$. These sums are pairwise different (binary representations are unique), so no two terms combine. Comparing with $(1+x)^n=\sum_k\binom nk x^k$, the number of odd coefficients $\binom nk$ is exactly $2^s$.

All $n+1$ coefficients are odd if and only if $n+1=2^s$, i.e. $n=2^s-1$. Conversely, for $n=2^k-1=1+2+\dots+2^{k-1}$ we have $s=k$ and $n+1=2^k=2^s$. Hence the required numbers are exactly \[n=\boxed{2^k-1}\qquad(k=1,2,3,\dots).\]

Solution 2 (Carries in binary (Legendre))

For a positive integer $m$ let $s(m)$ be the number of binary digits $1$ of $m$ (and $s(0)=0$). Legendre's formula for the prime $2$ reads $v_2(m!)=m-s(m)$, where $v_2$ is the exponent of $2$. Hence for $0\le k\le n$ \[v_2\binom nk=v_2(n!)-v_2(k!)-v_2((n-k)!)=s(k)+s(n-k)-s(n).\] When two numbers are added in binary, every carry turns two $1$'s of one position into a single $1$ one position higher, so $s(u)+s(w)-s(u+w)$ equals the number of carries in the binary addition $u+w$. Thus $\binom nk$ is odd exactly when the addition $k+(n-k)$ produces no carry.

If $n=2^k-1$: all $k$ binary digits of $n$ are $1$. For $0\le j\le n$, the binary digits of $n-j$ are those of $j$ flipped (within the $k$ positions), so the addition $j+(n-j)$ has no carry and every $\binom nj$ is odd.

If $n$ is not of this form: let $2^t$ be the leading binary digit of $n$; some position $i<t$ carries the digit $0$ in $n$. Take $j=2^i\le n$. In the addition $2^i+(n-2^i)=n$ the lower positions of $2^i$ are $0$, so no carry reaches position $i$; there the summand $2^i$ contributes a $1$, while the result $n$ has digit $0$. This is only possible if a carry is produced at position $i$. Hence $\binom n{2^i}$ is even.

Therefore all binomial coefficients in row $n$ are odd exactly for $n=\boxed{2^k-1}$, $k\ge1$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Let two integers $m$ and $n$ with $0<m<n$ be given. Consider all non-negative integers. Initially, only $m$ and $n$ are colored red, while all others are white.

The following process recolors the numbers step by step. In each step, the following happens:

  • 1. It is checked whether there are red numbers. If there is at most one, the process terminates.

  • 2. Otherwise, let $k$ be the smallest red number. This number $k$ is now colored black.

  • 3. Next, $k+m$ and $k+n$ are considered. Any red number among these two is colored black, any white number among these two is colored red, and any black number among these two remains black.

Show that:

  • (a) This process always terminates after a finite number of steps with a single red number $r$, regardless of how the numbers $m$ and $n$ were chosen.

  • (b) For the single red number at termination, $r\leq 2^n-1$ holds.

Show the answer

proof (the last red number is the least $r>0$ for which $1+x^m+x^n$ divides $1+x^r$ over $\mathbb F_2$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Polynomials over the field with two elements)

Step 1: the rule “black stays black” is never used. Call a number $t$ touched in a step with smallest red number $k$ if $t\in\{k+m,k+n\}$; we also say that $m$ and $n$ are touched once by a virtual step $k=0$ before the start. A number $t$ can only be touched by the steps $k=t-m$ and $k=t-n$, so it is touched at most twice. A white number that is touched becomes red, a red number that is touched becomes black, and the smallest red number becomes black. In every step the smallest red number increases strictly, because $k$ is removed and only numbers larger than $k$ are touched. Now suppose a black number $t$ is touched. If $t$ became black as the smallest red number $k'$ of an earlier step, then all later steps have $k>k'$ and touch only numbers $>k'=t$; impossible. Otherwise $t$ became black by a touch while red; it had become red by an earlier touch, so both possible touches are used up; impossible. Hence in each step the colour of $k+m$ and of $k+n$ simply switches between “red” and “not red”.

Step 2: an algebraic model. Let $\mathbb F_2[x]$ be the polynomials with coefficients modulo $2$ and encode the red set by $R=\sum_{t\text{ red}}x^t$. By Step 1, a step with smallest red number $k$ changes $R$ into $R+x^k+x^{k+m}+x^{k+n}=R+x^kf$, where \[f(x)=1+x^m+x^n .\] Initially $R=x^m+x^n=1+f$. If the steps so far had smallest red numbers $k_1<\dots<k_j$, then \[R=1+fG,\qquad G=1+x^{k_1}+\dots+x^{k_j}. \tag{1}\] $R$ is never $0$: otherwise $fG=1$, impossible because $\deg f=n\ge1$. So the process can only stop with exactly one red number. Moreover, all exponents of $G$ are smaller than the current smallest red number $s$, and $R$ has no term below $x^s$, so \[fG\equiv1\pmod{x^s}. \tag{2}\]

Step 3: the order of $x$ modulo $f$. Divide $x^0,x^1,\dots,x^{2^n-1}$ by $f$. The $2^n$ remainders are polynomials of degree $<n$ and none is $0$ (as $f(0)=1$, $f$ has no common factor with $x^j$). There are only $2^n-1$ nonzero polynomials of degree $<n$, so two remainders agree: $f\mid x^i(x^{j-i}+1)$ with $0\le i<j\le 2^n-1$, hence $f\mid x^{j-i}+1$. Let $r$ be the least positive integer with $f\mid x^r+1$; then $n\le r\le 2^n-1$. Write $x^r+1=fH$; then $\deg H=r-n$ and, as $f$ has constant term $1$, $fH\equiv 1\pmod{x^N}$ for every $N\le r$.

Step 4: the process ends with the single red number $r$. Let $s$ be the smallest red number at some moment. Initially $s=m<n\le r$. As long as all smallest red numbers $k_1<\dots<k_j$ so far are $\le r-n$, we have $\deg R\le\deg(fG)=n+k_j\le r$ by (1) (read $k_0=0$ before the first step), so all red numbers are $\le r$; hence at the first moment when $s>r-n$, still $s\le r$. So at every moment up to that one we are in one of the following two cases.

  • If $s\le r-n$: suppose only one number $s$ is red. Then $1+fG=x^s$ by (1), so $f\mid x^s+1$ with $s<r$, contradicting the choice of $r$. So at least two numbers are red and the process continues.

  • If $r-n<s\le r$: from (2) and $fH\equiv1\pmod{x^s}$ we get $f(G-H)\equiv0\pmod{x^s}$; since $f$ has constant term $1$, this forces $G\equiv H\pmod{x^s}$. Both $G$ and $H$ have degree $<s$, hence $G=H$ and $R=1+fH=x^r$: the only red number is $r$.

The smallest red number increases in every step, so after finitely many steps it exceeds $r-n$, and at that moment the red set is $\{r\}$ and the process stops. This proves (a), and (b) follows from $r\le 2^n-1$. (The process cannot stop earlier, by the first bullet.)

Solution 2 (A reversible 0-1 recurrence (state counting))

The rule “black stays black” is never used. Say $t$ is touched by the step with smallest red number $k$ if $t\in\{k+m,k+n\}$, and let the initial colouring of $m,n$ count as one touch by a virtual step $k=0$. Then $t$ can be touched only by $k=t-m$ and $k=t-n$, i.e. at most twice. The smallest red number grows strictly from step to step ($k$ disappears, only larger numbers are touched), so a number that was black as a smallest red number is never touched again; a number that became black by a second touch has no touch left. Hence a number is red exactly when it has been touched an odd number of times and has not yet been the smallest red number.

A recurrence. Define $p_t\in\{0,1\}$ by $p_t=0$ for $t<0$, $p_0=1$ and \[p_t\equiv p_{t-m}+p_{t-n}\pmod 2\qquad(t\ge1).\] Claim. The numbers $k\ge1$ that ever become the smallest red number are exactly those with $p_k=1$ (as long as the process runs), and when every number $<s$ has been dealt with (that is, all smallest red numbers so far are $<s$ and the current one is $\ge s$), a number $t\ge s$ is red if and only if \[c_s(t):=[t-m<s]\,p_{t-m}+[t-n<s]\,p_{t-n}\ \text{ is odd}\] (here $[\dots]$ is $1$ if the condition holds and $0$ otherwise). Indeed, $t\ge s$ has received one touch from each $j\in\{t-m,t-n\}$ with $j<s$ that was $0$ or a smallest red number; by induction on $s$ these $j$ are exactly the ones with $p_j=1$. And $s$ itself is the next smallest red number iff it is red, iff $c_s(s)=p_{s-m}+p_{s-n}\equiv p_s$ is odd, which continues the induction.

Since $c_s(t)=0$ for $t\ge s+n$, all red numbers then lie in $[s,s+n-1]$. For $s\ge1$ and $t$ in this window we get from the recurrence \[c_s(t)\equiv p_t\ \ (s\le t<s+m),\qquad c_s(t)=p_{t-n}\ \ (s+m\le t\le s+n-1). \tag{1}\]

States. Let $S_t=(p_{t-n+1},\dots,p_t)\in\{0,1\}^n$. The recurrence computes $S_{t+1}$ from $S_t$, and also backwards $S_t$ from $S_{t+1}$, because $p_{t+1-n}\equiv p_{t+1}+p_{t+1-m}$. The zero state goes to the zero state, and $S_0=(0,\dots,0,1)\ne0$, so by invertibility no $S_t$ ($t\ge0$) is zero. Among $S_0,\dots,S_{2^n-1}$ (that is $2^n$ states, but only $2^n-1$ nonzero ones) two coincide, $S_i=S_j$ with $i<j$, and stepping back $i$ times gives $S_0=S_{j-i}$. Let $L\ge1$ be the least index with $S_L=S_0$; then $L\le2^n-1$, \[p_L=1,\qquad p_{L-1}=p_{L-2}=\dots=p_{L-n+1}=0,\] and $L\ge n$ (otherwise $p_0=1$ would lie in this zero block).

Never zero red numbers. If at some stage $s\ge1$ no number were red, (1) would give $p_s=\dots=p_{s+m-1}=0$ and $p_{s+m-n}=\dots=p_{s-1}=0$, i.e. $S_{s+m-1}=0$, impossible. So the process can only stop with exactly one red number.

The stage $s_0=L-n+1\ (\ge1)$. Use (1). For $s_0\le t\le s_0+m-1=L-n+m\le L-1$ we get $c\equiv p_t=0$. For $s_0+m\le t\le L$ we have $p_{t-n}\equiv p_t+p_{t-m}$ with $t-m\in[L-n+1,L-m]$, so $p_{t-m}=0$ and $p_{t-n}=p_t$, which is $0$ for $t<L$ and $1$ for $t=L$. Hence if the process reaches stage $s_0$, the red set is exactly $\{L\}$ and it stops with $r=L\le 2^n-1$.

Conclusion. The smallest red number increases in every step, so the process reaches stage $s_0$ after finitely many steps unless it stops before. If it stops before, at a stage $s<s_0$, it has one red number $r\le s+n-1<s_0+n-1=L\le 2^n-1$. In every case the process terminates with a single red number $r\le 2^n-1$, which proves (a) and (b).

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

Let $a$, $b$, and $c$ be the side lengths of a triangle. Determine the largest positive integer $M$ with the property that \[\left|\frac{a-b}{a+b}+\frac{b-c}{b+c}+\frac{c-a}{c+a}\right|<\frac1M\] holds for all such $a$, $b$, and $c$.

Show the answer

$M=22$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor, push the largest side to the limit)

Let $E$ be the expression inside the absolute value. Bringing it to a common denominator (or checking the identity by expansion) gives \[E=\frac{a-b}{a+b}+\frac{b-c}{b+c}+\frac{c-a}{c+a}=-\frac{(a-b)(b-c)(c-a)}{(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)}.\] Hence $|E|$ is symmetric in $a,b,c$, and we may assume $c\ge a\ge b$ (so $c$ is the largest side). If $a=b$ then $E=0$. Otherwise \[|E|=\frac{a-b}{a+b}\cdot\frac{c-b}{c+b}\cdot\frac{c-a}{c+a}.\] The last two factors are positive and strictly increasing in $c$ (each has the form $\frac{c-k}{c+k}=1-\frac{2k}{c+k}$), and the triangle inequality gives $c<a+b$. Therefore, with $t=b/a\in(0,1)$, \[|E|<\frac{a-b}{a+b}\cdot\frac{a}{a+2b}\cdot\frac{b}{2a+b}=\frac{t(1-t)}{(1+t)(1+2t)(2+t)}=:g(t).\]

Upper bound. $(1+t)(1+2t)(2+t)=2+7t+7t^2+2t^3$, so \[(1+t)(1+2t)(2+t)-22t(1-t)=2t^3+29t^2-15t+2=2t^3+29\Bigl(t-\frac{15}{58}\Bigr)^2+\frac{7}{116}>0 .\] Thus $g(t)<\frac1{22}$ and $|E|<\frac1{22}$ for every triangle; $M=22$ has the property.

$M=23$ fails. For $t=\frac14$ we get $g(\tfrac14)=\frac{3/16}{135/32}=\frac2{45}>\frac1{23}$, so the bound $\frac1{23}$ is beaten by triangles close to the degenerate triangle $(a,b,c)=(4,1,5)$. Concretely, for the triangle $(400,100,499)$, \[|E|=\frac{300\cdot399\cdot99}{500\cdot599\cdot899}=\frac{118503}{2692505}>\frac1{23},\] because $23\cdot118503=2725569>2692505$. Since $M$ with the property must satisfy $\frac1M>|E|>\frac1{23}$, every $M\ge23$ fails.

Hence the largest such integer is $M=\boxed{22}$. (The supremum of $|E|$ is $\max g\approx0.04446\approx\frac1{22.49}$, not attained.)

Solution 2 (Ravi substitution)

Write the sides as $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$ with $x,y,z>0$ (possible exactly for triangles: $x=\frac{b+c-a}2$, etc.). The expression equals \[E=-\frac{(a-b)(b-c)(c-a)}{(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)}=-\frac{(y-x)(z-y)(x-z)}{(x+y+2z)(2x+y+z)(x+2y+z)}\] (the first equality is checked by expanding; then $a-b=y-x$, $a+b=x+y+2z$, and so on). The value of $|E|$ is symmetric in $x,y,z$, so let $z$ be the smallest and $x\ge y$; if two of them are equal, $E=0$. Then $0\le x-z<x$, $0\le y-z<y$, and each factor of the denominator decreases when $z$ is replaced by $0$, so \[|E|<\frac{(x-y)\,xy}{(2x+y)(x+2y)(x+y)}=\frac{t(1-t)}{(2+t)(1+2t)(1+t)},\qquad t=\frac yx\in(0,1].\] (The strict inequality comes from $z>0$.) We show $22t(1-t)\le(1+t)(1+2t)(2+t)$, i.e. $2t^3+29t^2-15t+2\ge0$. By AM-GM, $29t^2+2\ge2\sqrt{58}\,t>15t$ because $4\cdot58=232>225$; adding $2t^3>0$ proves it. Hence $|E|<\frac1{22}$ for all triangles.

For the other direction, take $x=4$, $y=1$ and a small $z>0$: the triangle has sides $a=1+z$, $b=4+z$, $c=5$. As $z\to0$, $|E|$ tends to $\frac{3\cdot4\cdot1}{9\cdot6\cdot5}=\frac2{45}>\frac1{23}$, so for small $z$ (for example $z=\frac1{100}$, giving sides $101,401,500$ after scaling by $100$, where one computes $|E|=\frac{300\cdot99\cdot399}{502\cdot901\cdot601}=\frac{11850300}{271833502}\approx0.04359>\frac1{23}\approx0.04348$) the inequality with $M=23$ fails, and so it fails for every $M\ge23$.

The answer is $M=\boxed{22}$.

Day 2

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

For a sequence $(a_n)$, let two integers $a_1$ and $a_2$ be given; the subsequent terms are then defined by the recursive formula \[a_n=a_{n-1}+(-1)^{a_{n-1}}\cdot a_{n-2},\qquad n=3,4,5\ldots\] Determine all possibilities for the initial values $a_1$ and $a_2$ for which the sequence becomes periodic, and find the corresponding period length in each case.

Note: A sequence $(a_n)$ is called periodic if there exist positive integers $k$ and $l$ such that $a_{n+l}=a_n$ holds for all $n\geq k$. In this context, $l$ is called the period length of the sequence if, additionally, for no $k'\geq k$ a smaller value $0<l'<l$ already exists such that $a_{n+l'}=a_n$ holds for all $n\geq k'$.

Show the answer

periodic exactly when $a_1,a_2$ are not both even, or $a_1=a_2=0$. Period $1$ for $(0,0)$; period $3$ for $(x,x)$, $(x,0)$, $(0,x)$ with $x$ odd; period $6$ for all other pairs that are not both even.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Compute six steps; a sign argument for the even case)

Parities. If $a_{n-1}$ is even, $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$; if it is odd, $a_n=a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}$. In both cases $a_n\equiv a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}\pmod2$. So if $a_1,a_2$ are both even, all terms are even; otherwise the parities run through the cycle odd, odd, even, odd, odd, even, … (after at most two terms).

A pair of odd terms. Suppose $a_k=u$ and $a_{k+1}=v$ are both odd. Applying the rule (each sign is fixed by the parity cycle): \[u,\ v,\ v-u,\ 2v-u,\ v,\ u-v,\ u,\ v,\ \dots\] Indeed $a_{k+2}=v-u$ (even), $a_{k+3}=(v-u)+v$, $a_{k+4}=(2v-u)-(v-u)=v$, $a_{k+5}=v-(2v-u)=u-v$ (even), $a_{k+6}=(u-v)+v=u$, $a_{k+7}=u-(u-v)=v$. So from index $k$ on the sequence is periodic with period $6$.

Reaching two odd terms. If $a_1,a_2$ are odd, take $k=1$. If $a_1=x$ is odd and $a_2=y$ even, then $a_3=y+x$ and $a_4=a_3-a_2=x$ are odd: take $k=3$ with $(u,v)=(x+y,x)$. If $a_1=x$ is even and $a_2=y$ odd, then $a_3=y-x$ is odd: take $k=2$ with $(u,v)=(y,y-x)$.

The exact period. If $l$ is any period (from some index on) then so is $\gcd(l,6)$, because the tail is periodic with both $l$ and $6$. So the least period divides $6$; we test $1,2,3$ on the tail $u,v,v-u,2v-u,v,u-v$. Period $1$ or $2$ would need $a_{k+2}=a_k$, i.e. $v=2u$, impossible as $v$ is odd. Period $3$ holds iff $2v-u=u$, i.e. $u=v$. Translating: the period is $3$ iff $a_1=a_2$ (both odd), or $y=0$ in the second case, or $x=0$ in the third case; otherwise it is $6$.

Both even. Then $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$ for all $n$. For $(0,0)$ the sequence is constant $0$ (period $1$). Otherwise not all terms vanish from some point on (going backwards, $a_{n-2}=a_n-a_{n-1}$, so two consecutive zeros force all earlier terms to be $0$). If two consecutive terms $a_k,a_{k+1}$ are $\ge0$ and not both $0$, then $a_{k+2}\ge a_{k+1}$ and from then on all terms are positive and strictly increase after one more step, so the sequence is unbounded; the same holds with the signs reversed. If instead consecutive terms always have opposite strict signs from some point, then $a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n$ has the sign of $a_n$ and $|a_{n+2}|=|a_n|-|a_{n+1}|<|a_n|$, an infinite decrease of positive integers; impossible. (A zero term $a_n=0$ gives $a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}$, two consecutive terms of the same sign.) So the sequence is unbounded and not periodic.

Answer. The sequence is periodic exactly when $a_1,a_2$ are not both even, or $a_1=a_2=0$. The period is $\boxed{1}$ for $(0,0)$; $\boxed{3}$ for $(a_1,a_2)=(x,x),(x,0),(0,x)$ with $x$ odd (the sequence then repeats $x,x,0$); and $\boxed{6}$ for every other pair that is not both even. (In particular the sequence is periodic whenever $\gcd(a_1,a_2)=1$, the Grade 11 version.)

Solution 2 (A three-step involution; the maximum argument for the even case)

Parities. Since $(-1)^{a}a'\equiv a'\pmod 2$, always $a_n\equiv a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}\pmod2$. Thus both-even starting values give only even terms, and otherwise the parity pattern of $(a_n,a_{n+1})$ becomes (odd, odd) within two steps and then repeats every three steps: (odd,odd) $\to$ (odd,even) $\to$ (even,odd) $\to$ (odd,odd). (As computed: from $(x,y)$ = (odd, even) one step gives $(y,x+y)$ (even, odd), and from (even, odd) $(x,y)$ one step gives $(y,y-x)$, both odd.)

Three steps as a linear map. Starting from an odd pair $(u,v)$ the next three terms are $v-u$, $(v-u)+v=2v-u$ and $(2v-u)-(v-u)=v$. So the pair three positions later is \[T(u,v)=(2v-u,\ v),\qquad\text{and}\qquad T(T(u,v))=(2v-(2v-u),\ v)=(u,v).\] $T$ is an involution (a reflection of the plane), hence the sequence of odd pairs repeats after two applications of $T$, i.e. after $6$ terms, and the tail is periodic with period $6$. Its fixed points are the pairs with $2v-u=u$, i.e. $u=v$; exactly then the pattern repeats after $3$ terms ($u,u,0$). Periods $1$ and $2$ are impossible since every third term is even and the others odd. As any period of the tail can be replaced by its gcd with $6$, the least period is $3$ if the first odd pair has equal entries and $6$ otherwise. The first odd pair is $(a_1,a_2)$ if both are odd; $(a_1+a_2,a_1)$ (at positions $3,4$) if $a_1$ odd, $a_2$ even, which has equal entries iff $a_2=0$; $(a_2,a_2-a_1)$ if $a_1$ even, $a_2$ odd, with equal entries iff $a_1=0$.

Both even, not both zero. Suppose the sequence were periodic from index $k$ on. A periodic sequence takes finitely many values, so $\max_{n\ge k}|a_n|=K$ is attained at some $n\ge k+2$ (take a later copy). Since all terms are even, $a_{j}=a_{j-1}+a_{j-2}$ for all $j$, so \[a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n=2a_n+a_{n-1},\qquad a_{n-2}=a_n-a_{n-1},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad a_{n+2}+a_{n-2}=3a_n .\] Then $3K=|a_{n+2}+a_{n-2}|\le2K$, so $K=0$: the tail is identically $0$, and running the recurrence backwards ($a_{j-2}=a_j-a_{j-1}$) makes every term $0$, contradicting $(a_1,a_2)\ne(0,0)$.

Answer. Periodic iff $a_1,a_2$ are not both even or $a_1=a_2=0$. The period is $\boxed{1}$ for $(0,0)$, $\boxed{3}$ for $(x,x),(x,0),(0,x)$ with $x$ odd, and $\boxed{6}$ for all other pairs not both even.

Solution 3 (Three-step involutions on parity classes; a sum of squares over one period)

Write $P_j=(a_j,a_{j+1})$ for the pair of consecutive terms at position $j$. The pair $P_{j+1}$ is computed from $P_j$ alone, so if $P_i=P_j$ for some $i<j$, then $P_{i+t}=P_{j+t}$ for all $t\ge0$.

Parities. Since $(-1)^{a}a'\equiv a'\pmod 2$, we always have $a_j\equiv a_{j-1}+a_{j-2}\pmod 2$. Hence if $a_1,a_2$ are both even, all terms are even. Otherwise the parity type of $P_j$ runs through the cycle \[(\text{odd},\text{odd})\to(\text{odd},\text{even})\to(\text{even},\text{odd})\to(\text{odd},\text{odd}),\] so the parity type of $P_{j+3}$ equals that of $P_j$.

Not both even: three steps. Apply the rule three times; each sign is fixed by the parities above.

  • $P_j=(a,b)$ with $a,b$ odd: the next terms are $b-a$, then $(b-a)+b=2b-a$, then $(2b-a)-(b-a)=b$; so $P_{j+3}=(2b-a,\,b)$.

  • $P_j=(a,b)$ with $a$ odd, $b$ even: the next terms are $b+a$, then $(a+b)-b=a$, then $a-(a+b)=-b$; so $P_{j+3}=(a,\,-b)$.

  • $P_j=(a,b)$ with $a$ even, $b$ odd: the next terms are $b-a$, then $(b-a)-b=-a$, then $-a+(b-a)=b-2a$; so $P_{j+3}=(-a,\,b-2a)$.

Call these maps $T_{oo}(a,b)=(2b-a,b)$, $T_{oe}(a,b)=(a,-b)$ and $T_{eo}(a,b)=(-a,b-2a)$. Each is an involution: $T_{oo}(2b-a,b)=(a,b)$, $T_{oe}(a,-b)=(a,b)$ and $T_{eo}(-a,b-2a)=(a,b-2a+2a)=(a,b)$. Since $P_{j+3}$ has the same parity type as $P_j$, the same map is applied again, and $P_{j+6}=P_j$ for every $j\ge1$. So the sequence is periodic from the start with period $6$.

Least period. If $l$ is a period from some index on, so is $\gcd(l,6)$ (from a later index the tail has both periods $l$ and $6$, and $\gcd(l,6)$ is an integer combination of them). So the least period divides $6$. Periods $1$ and $2$ are impossible: exactly one of any three consecutive terms is even, so the parities do not repeat with period $1$ or $2$. Period $3$ from some index $k$ on means $P_{j+3}=P_j$ for all $j\ge k$; taking $j\equiv1\pmod 6$, where $P_j=P_1$, this gives $P_4=P_1$. Conversely $P_4=P_1$ gives $P_{t+3}=P_t$ for all $t\ge1$. So the period is $3$ exactly when $P_1=(a_1,a_2)$ is a fixed point of its map:

  • $T_{oo}(a,b)=(a,b)$ iff $2b-a=a$, i.e. $a_1=a_2$ (odd);

  • $T_{oe}(a,b)=(a,b)$ iff $b=-b$, i.e. $a_2=0$ (and $a_1$ odd);

  • $T_{eo}(a,b)=(a,b)$ iff $-a=a$, i.e. $a_1=0$ (and $a_2$ odd).

In all other cases with $a_1,a_2$ not both even the period is $6$.

Both even. Now every term is even, so $a_{j+2}=a_{j+1}+a_j$ for all $j\ge1$. For $(a_1,a_2)=(0,0)$ the sequence is constantly $0$, with period $1$. Suppose now that the sequence is periodic with period $l$ from index $k$ on. Multiply $a_{i+2}=a_{i+1}+a_i$ by $a_{i+1}$ and add for $i=k,k+1,\dots,k+l-1$: \[\sum_{i=k}^{k+l-1}a_{i+1}a_{i+2}=\sum_{i=k}^{k+l-1}a_{i+1}^2+\sum_{i=k}^{k+l-1}a_ia_{i+1}.\] The products $c_i=a_ia_{i+1}$ also satisfy $c_{i+l}=c_i$ for $i\ge k$, so the sum of $c_{i+1}$ over $l$ consecutive indices equals the sum of $c_i$ over the same indices (both are the sum of one full period of $(c_i)$). The first and last sums cancel, leaving $\sum_{i=k}^{k+l-1}a_{i+1}^2=0$. Hence $a_{k+1}=\dots=a_{k+l}=0$; in particular two consecutive terms are $0$. Running the recurrence backwards, $a_j=a_{j+2}-a_{j+1}$, all earlier terms are $0$ too, so $a_1=a_2=0$. Thus a both-even starting pair other than $(0,0)$ never gives a periodic sequence.

Answer. The sequence is periodic exactly when $a_1,a_2$ are not both even, or $a_1=a_2=0$. The period is $\boxed{1}$ for $(0,0)$; $\boxed{3}$ for $(a_1,a_2)=(x,x),(x,0),(0,x)$ with $x$ odd; and $\boxed{6}$ for every other pair that is not both even.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

Let a prime $p$ and a positive integer $a$ be given such that $a-1$ is not divisible by $p$. Furthermore, let \[n=1+a^p+a^{2p}+\cdots+a^{(p-1)p}.\qquad(1)\] Show that $a^n-a$ is then divisible by $n$.

Note: For $p=2$, the sum in (1) is contextually understood to be $1+a^2$, and for $p=3$ as $1+a^3+a^6$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reduce to $p^2\mid n-1$ and use Euler)

Note $a\ne1$ (for $a=1$ the prime $p$ would divide $a-1=0$), so $a^p\ne1$ and the geometric sum gives \[n=\frac{a^{p^2}-1}{a^p-1},\qquad\text{so}\qquad n\mid a^{p^2}-1 .\] Claim: $p^2\mid n-1$. Then $a^{n-1}-1$ is divisible by $a^{p^2}-1$ (as $x^{p^2}-1\mid x^{p^2j}-1$), hence by $n$, and $a^n-a=a(a^{n-1}-1)$ is divisible by $n$, as required.

Proof of the claim. We have $n-1=b+b^2+\dots+b^{p-1}$ with $b=a^p$.

  • If $p\mid a$, every term is divisible by $a^p$, hence by $p^p$, and $p^p$ is divisible by $p^2$ since $p\ge2$.

  • If $p\nmid a$: by Fermat $b=a^p\equiv a\not\equiv1\pmod p$, so $p\nmid b-1$. By Euler's theorem ($\varphi(p^2)=p(p-1)$), $b^{p-1}=a^{p(p-1)}\equiv1\pmod{p^2}$. Now \[(b-1)(b+b^2+\dots+b^{p-1})=b^p-b=b\,(b^{p-1}-1)\equiv0\pmod{p^2},\] and $b-1$ is coprime to $p$, so $p^2\mid b+\dots+b^{p-1}=n-1$.

(For $p=2$ the second case cannot occur: $a$ odd would mean $2\mid a-1$.) This proves the claim and the statement.

Solution 2 (Lifting the exponent)

As $a\ne1$ (otherwise $p\mid a-1=0$), summing the geometric series gives $n(a^p-1)=a^{p^2}-1$, so $n$ divides $a^{p^2}-1$, and therefore $n$ divides $a^{p^2j}-1$ for every $j\ge0$. It suffices to show $p^2\mid n-1$: then $a^{n-1}\equiv1$ and $a^n\equiv a\pmod n$.

Write $n-1=\dfrac{a^{p^2}-a^p}{a^p-1}=a^p\cdot\dfrac{a^{p(p-1)}-1}{a^p-1}$ and compare $p$-adic valuations $v_p$.

If $p\mid a$, then $v_p(n-1)\ge v_p(a^p)\ge p\ge2$, since the fraction is an integer ($a^p-1\mid a^{p(p-1)}-1$).

If $p\nmid a$, then $p$ is odd (for $p=2$, $a$ odd would give $2\mid a-1$). By Fermat $a^p\equiv a\not\equiv 1\pmod p$, so $v_p(a^p-1)=0$. Put $c=a^{p-1}$; then $p\mid c-1$ and the Lifting-the-Exponent lemma for odd $p$ gives \[v_p\bigl(a^{p(p-1)}-1\bigr)=v_p(c^p-1)=v_p(c-1)+v_p(p)\ge1+1=2 .\] Hence $v_p(n-1)=0+v_p(a^{p(p-1)}-1)-v_p(a^p-1)\ge2$.

In both cases $p^2\mid n-1$, so $n\mid a^{p^2}-1\mid a^{n-1}-1$ and $n\mid a(a^{n-1}-1)=a^n-a$.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $k$ and $k'$ be two intersecting circles and $P$ one of the two intersection points. Let line $g$ run parallel to the common tangent to $k$ and $k'$ that is further away from $P$. This line intersects $k$ in $A$ and $C$, and $k'$ in $B$ and $D$, such that the four points $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ lie on $g$ in this order. The intersection points of $g$ with the angle bisectors of $\angle APB$ and $\angle CPD$ are denoted by $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $PXC$ and $PBY$, as well as the angle bisector of $\angle BPC$, have a point in common that is different from $P$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Ptolemy, the angle bisector theorem and one identity)

Let $\tau$ be the common tangent farther from $P$, touching $k$ at $T$ and $k'$ at $T'$, and let $Q$ be the second intersection point of the circles. For two points $U,V$ of $g$ put \[\lambda(U,V)=\frac{UV}{PU+PV}.\]

Configuration. Reflection in the line of centres swaps $P,Q$ and the two common tangents, so “$\tau$ is farther from $P$” means that $P$ is farther from $\tau$ than $Q$. On $g$ the order is $A,B,C,D$, so $B$ lies inside $k$ and $D$ outside $k$. Each of the two arcs of $k'$ from $B$ to $D$ (one on each side of $g$) goes from inside $k$ to outside $k$ and therefore meets $k$ in $P$ or $Q$; so $P$ and $Q$ lie on opposite sides of $g$, and $P$, being farther from $\tau\parallel g$, lies on the side of $g$ away from $\tau$. The point of $k$ where the tangent is parallel to $g$, on the side away from $P$, is $T$; it is the midpoint of the arc $AC$ not containing $P$, so $TA=TC$ and $P,A,T,C$ lie on $k$ in this order. Likewise $T'$ is the midpoint of the arc $BD$ of $k'$ not containing $P$.

Step 1: the tangent condition. Ptolemy's theorem in the cyclic quadrilateral $PATC$ with $TA=TC$ gives $PT\cdot AC=PA\cdot TC+PC\cdot TA=TA\,(PA+PC)$, so $\lambda(A,C)=\frac{TA}{TP}$. Let $M=PT\cap g$; it lies between $P$ and $T$, as $g$ separates them. Triangles $TAM$ and $TPA$ are similar ($\angle TAM=\angle TAC=\angle TPC=\angle TPA$, inscribed angles over the equal arcs $TC$ and $TA$, and the angle at $T$ is common), so $TA^2=TM\cdot TP$. If $d$ and $h$ are the distances of $T$ and $P$ from $g$, then $\frac{TM}{TP}=\frac d{d+h}$. Altogether \[\lambda(A,C)^2=\frac{TA^2}{TP^2}=\frac{TM}{TP}=\frac d{d+h}.\] The same argument for $k'$ gives $\lambda(B,D)^2=\frac d{d+h}$ with the same $d$, because $T$ and $T'$ both lie on $\tau\parallel g$. Hence \[\lambda(A,C)=\lambda(B,D). \tag{1}\]

Step 2: an identity. Let $H$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $g$, so $PH=h$. For $U\in g$ let $x_U$ be its signed abscissa on $g$ (origin $H$, increasing from $A$ towards $D$), and put $q_U=PU+x_U$; since $PU=\sqrt{h^2+x_U^2}>|x_U|$, $q_U>0$. For $U$ before $V$ on $g$ we have $UV=x_V-x_U$, and we claim \[\lambda(U,V)=\frac{q_V-q_U}{q_V+q_U}.\] Indeed, cross-multiplying, we must show $(x_V-x_U)(PU+PV+x_U+x_V)=(PV-PU+x_V-x_U)(PU+PV)$. The terms $(x_V-x_U)(PU+PV)$ appear on both sides, and what remains is $x_V^2-x_U^2=PV^2-PU^2$, true because $PU^2=h^2+x_U^2$ and $PV^2=h^2+x_V^2$.

The function $q\mapsto\frac{q-1}{q+1}$ is injective on $q>0$, and $\lambda(U,V)=\frac{q_V/q_U-1}{q_V/q_U+1}$. Therefore (1) gives $\frac{q_C}{q_A}=\frac{q_D}{q_B}$, that is $\frac{q_B}{q_A}=\frac{q_D}{q_C}$, and so \[\lambda(A,B)=\lambda(C,D). \tag{2}\]

Step 3: a product of lengths. By the angle bisector theorem in triangle $APB$, $\frac{AX}{XB}=\frac{PA}{PB}$, so $XB=\frac{AB\cdot PB}{PA+PB}$, i.e. $\frac{XB}{PB}=\lambda(A,B)$. Likewise $\frac{YC}{PC}=\lambda(C,D)$. By (2), $\frac{XB}{YC}=\frac{PB}{PC}$. Let $W$ be the point where the bisector $\ell$ of $\angle BPC$ meets $g$; by the angle bisector theorem in triangle $BPC$, $\frac{PB}{PC}=\frac{WB}{WC}$. So, with $\kappa=\frac{XB}{WB}=\frac{YC}{WC}$, and since the order on $g$ is $X,B,W,C,Y$, \[WX=WB+BX=(1+\kappa)\,WB,\qquad WY=WC+CY=(1+\kappa)\,WC,\] hence \[WX\cdot WC=(1+\kappa)\,WB\cdot WC=WB\cdot WY. \tag{3}\]

Step 4: power of the point $W$. The point $W$ lies inside the segment $XC$, a chord of the circle $(PXC)$, so $W$ is inside this circle, and the line $\ell=PW$ meets it again at a point $Z_1$ on the other side of $W$ from $P$; by the intersecting chords theorem $WP\cdot WZ_1=WX\cdot WC$. In the same way $W$ is inside the chord $BY$ of $(PBY)$, and $\ell$ meets this circle again at $Z_2$ beyond $W$ with $WP\cdot WZ_2=WB\cdot WY$. By (3), $WZ_1=WZ_2$, and $Z_1,Z_2$ lie on the same ray from $W$, so $Z_1=Z_2$.

Therefore the circles $(PXC)$, $(PBY)$ and the bisector of $\angle BPC$ pass through a common point $Z=Z_1\ne P$.

Solution 2 (Ptolemy and the sine-area formula at $P$)

Let $\tau$ be the common tangent farther from $P$, touching $k$ at $T$ and $k'$ at $T'$.

Configuration. As $A,B,C,D$ lie on $g$ in this order, $B$ is inside $k$ and $D$ outside, so each of the two arcs of $k'$ between $B$ and $D$ crosses $k$; thus the two intersection points of the circles lie on different sides of $g$. Since $\tau$ is the tangent farther from $P$ (equivalently, by the symmetry in the line of centres, $P$ is the intersection point farther from $\tau$), $P$ is on the side of $g$ away from $\tau$, and $T$ is the midpoint of the arc $AC$ of $k$ not containing $P$; likewise $T'$ for $BD$ on $k'$.

Step 1: the tangent condition as a ratio. Ptolemy in the cyclic quadrilateral $PATC$ with $TA=TC$ gives $PT\cdot AC=PA\cdot TC+PC\cdot TA=TA\,(PA+PC)$, so $\frac{AC}{PA+PC}=\frac{TA}{TP}$. Let $M=PT\cap g$. Triangles $TAM$ and $TPA$ are similar ($\angle TAM=\angle TAC=\angle TPC=\angle TPA$ and the angle at $T$ is common), so $TA^2=TM\cdot TP$. If $s$ and $h$ are the distances of $T$ and $P$ from $g$, then $\frac{TM}{TP}=\frac{s}{s+h}$. Altogether $\bigl(\frac{AC}{PA+PC}\bigr)^2=\frac s{s+h}$, and the same for $k'$ with the same $s$. Hence \[\frac{AC}{PA+PC}=\frac{BD}{PB+PD}. \tag{1}\]

Notation. Put $\angle APB=2\alpha$, $\angle BPC=2\beta$, $\angle CPD=2\gamma$ (all positive, $\alpha+\beta+\gamma<90^\circ$) and $u_U=1/PU$ for $U\in g$. For $U,V\in g$ the area of $PUV$ gives $UV=\frac{PU\cdot PV\sin\angle UPV}{h}$. Write $S_1=\sin(2\alpha+2\beta)$, $S_2=\sin(2\beta+2\gamma)$, $\sigma=\sin2\beta$.

Step 2: three linear relations. By the area formula, (1) becomes $\frac{S_1}{u_A+u_C}=\frac{S_2}{u_B+u_D}$. Splitting the triangle $APC$ at $B$ and $BPD$ at $C$ (areas add) gives, after dividing by the product of the three lengths, \[S_1(u_B+u_D)=S_2(u_A+u_C),\qquad \sigma u_A=S_1u_B-u_C\sin2\alpha,\qquad \sigma u_D=S_2u_C-u_B\sin2\gamma .\] Substituting the last two into the first: $u_BS_1(\sigma-\sin2\gamma-S_2)=u_CS_2(\sigma-\sin2\alpha-S_1)$. By sum-to-product, $\sigma-\sin2\alpha-S_1=2\cos(\alpha+\beta)\bigl(\sin(\beta-\alpha)-\sin(\beta+\alpha)\bigr)=-4\sin\alpha\cos\beta\cos(\alpha+\beta)$ and similarly $\sigma-\sin2\gamma-S_2=-4\sin\gamma\cos\beta\cos(\beta+\gamma)$. With $S_1=2\sin(\alpha+\beta)\cos(\alpha+\beta)$, $S_2=2\sin(\beta+\gamma)\cos(\beta+\gamma)$ (all cosines are nonzero) this simplifies to \[r:=\frac{u_B}{u_C}=\frac{\sin\alpha\,\sin(\beta+\gamma)}{\sin\gamma\,\sin(\alpha+\beta)}. \tag{2}\]

Step 3: reformulation. Let $W$ be the foot of the bisector of $\angle BPC$ on $g$. Since $X<B<W<C<Y$, $W$ is inside the circles $(PXC)$ and $(PBY)$, so line $PW$ meets them again at points $Z_1,Z_2$ beyond $W$ (thus $\ne P$), with $WP\cdot WZ_1=WX\cdot WC$ and $WP\cdot WZ_2=WB\cdot WY$. So we must show $WX\cdot WC=WB\cdot WY$. By the area formula ($\angle XPW=\alpha+\beta$, $\angle WPC=\angle BPW=\beta$, $\angle WPY=\beta+\gamma$) this is \[PX\cdot PC\,\sin(\alpha+\beta)=PB\cdot PY\,\sin(\beta+\gamma). \tag{3}\] From $[APB]=[APX]+[XPB]$ we get $PX=\frac{2PA\cdot PB\cos\alpha}{PA+PB}$, i.e. $\frac1{PX}=\frac{u_A+u_B}{2\cos\alpha}$, and similarly $\frac1{PY}=\frac{u_C+u_D}{2\cos\gamma}$. So (3) is equivalent to $\cos\alpha\sin(\alpha+\beta)\,u_B(u_C+u_D)=\cos\gamma\sin(\beta+\gamma)\,u_C(u_A+u_B)$.

Step 4: verification. From Step 2, $\sigma(u_A+u_B)=u_B(S_1+\sigma)-u_C\sin2\alpha=2\cos\alpha\,[u_B\sin(\alpha+2\beta)-u_C\sin\alpha]$ and $\sigma(u_C+u_D)=2\cos\gamma\,[u_C\sin(2\beta+\gamma)-u_B\sin\gamma]$. Multiplying the condition by $\sigma$ and dividing by $2\cos\alpha\cos\gamma\,u_C^2$, it becomes \[\sin(\alpha+\beta)\,r\,[\sin(2\beta+\gamma)-r\sin\gamma]=\sin(\beta+\gamma)\,[r\sin(\alpha+2\beta)-\sin\alpha].\] Using (2), $r\sin\gamma\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\sin(\beta+\gamma)$, so the left side is $r\,[\sin(\alpha+\beta)\sin(2\beta+\gamma)-\sin\alpha\sin(\beta+\gamma)]$, and the right side is $\frac{\sin\alpha\sin(\beta+\gamma)}{\sin\gamma\sin(\alpha+\beta)}[\sin(\beta+\gamma)\sin(\alpha+2\beta)-\sin\gamma\sin(\alpha+\beta)]= r\,[\sin(\beta+\gamma)\sin(\alpha+2\beta)-\sin\gamma\sin(\alpha+\beta)]$. By the product-to-sum formula both brackets equal \[\tfrac12\bigl[\cos(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\cos(\alpha+3\beta+\gamma)\bigr]=\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha+2\beta+\gamma).\] So (3) holds, $Z_1=Z_2$, and this point $\ne P$ lies on both circles and on the bisector of $\angle BPC$.

Solution 3 (Coordinates with the parameter $a=\frac12\left(u-\frac1u\right)$)

Let $Q$ be the second intersection point of $k$ and $k'$, and let $\tau,\tau'$ be the two common tangents, $\tau$ the one farther from $P$.

Configuration. On $g$ the order is $A,B,C,D$, so $B$ lies inside $k$ (inside its chord $AC$) and $D$ outside $k$. Each of the two arcs of $k'$ from $B$ to $D$, one on each side of $g$, goes from inside $k$ to outside $k$ and therefore meets $k$, at $P$ or at $Q$. Neither $P$ nor $Q$ lies on $g$ (the points of $k$ on $g$ are $A,C$, those of $k'$ are $B,D$), so $P$ and $Q$ lie on opposite sides of $g$. The reflection $\sigma$ in the line of centres maps each circle to itself, swaps $P$ and $Q$, and maps the pair of common tangents to itself; it does not fix $\tau$ (a line fixed by $\sigma$ is the line of centres or perpendicular to it, and such a line tangent to both circles would touch both at the same point of the line of centres, which is impossible for circles meeting in two points), so $\sigma(\tau')=\tau$. Hence the distance from $Q$ to $\tau$ equals the distance from $P$ to $\tau'$, which is smaller than the distance from $P$ to $\tau$: $P$ is farther from $\tau$ than $Q$.

Choose coordinates with $P=(0,0)$ and $g$ the line $y=1$, with $A,B,C,D$ in increasing order of abscissa (scale so that $P$ is at distance $1$ from $g$). Then $Q$ has ordinate $y_Q>1$. The line $\tau$ is parallel to $g$, so $\tau$ is $y=t$ for some $t$, and both circles, hence $P$ and $Q$, lie on the same side of $\tau$. If $t<0$, the distances of $P$ and $Q$ from $\tau$ would be $-t<y_Q-t$, contradicting the previous paragraph. So $t>y_Q>1$, and $\tau$ is the line touching each circle at its highest point. Therefore the condition on $g$ says exactly: the highest points of $k$ and $k'$ have the same ordinate.

The parameter. The map $u\mapsto s(u)=\frac12\left(u-\frac1u\right)$ is a strictly increasing bijection from $(0,\infty)$ onto $\mathbb R$, so every point of $g$ is $(s(u),1)$ for exactly one $u>0$; call $u$ its parameter. Since $1+s(u)^2=\frac14\left(u+\frac1u\right)^2$, the distance from $P$ to this point is $\frac12\left(u+\frac1u\right)$, and the unit vector from $P$ towards it is \[e(u)=\left(\frac{u^2-1}{u^2+1},\ \frac{2u}{u^2+1}\right).\] Let $A,B,C,D$ have parameters $p_A<p_B<p_C<p_D$.

Step 1: the tangency condition. A circle through $P=(0,0)$ has an equation $x^2+y^2-2\alpha x-2\beta y=0$, with centre $(\alpha,\beta)$ and radius $\sqrt{\alpha^2+\beta^2}$. If it meets $g$ at abscissas $a$ and $c$, then $a,c$ are the roots of $x^2-2\alpha x+1-2\beta=0$, so $2\alpha=a+c$ and $2\beta=1-ac$. The radius squared is $\frac14\left((a+c)^2+(1-ac)^2\right)=\frac14(1+a^2)(1+c^2)$, and the highest point has ordinate \[\beta+\tfrac12\sqrt{(1+a^2)(1+c^2)}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-ac+\sqrt{(1+a^2)(1+c^2)}\Bigr).\] For $a=s(u)$, $c=s(w)$ we have $\sqrt{(1+a^2)(1+c^2)}-ac=\frac14\left[\left(u+\frac1u\right)\left(w+\frac1w\right)-\left(u-\frac1u\right)\left(w-\frac1w\right)\right]=\frac12\left(\frac wu+\frac uw\right)$. So the highest point of the circle through $P$ and the points of $g$ with parameters $u<w$ has ordinate $\frac12+\frac14\varphi(w/u)$, where $\varphi(q)=q+\frac1q$. As $\varphi$ is strictly increasing on $(1,\infty)$, the tangency condition becomes \[\frac{p_C}{p_A}=\frac{p_D}{p_B}=:\rho>1 .\] Write $p_A=u$, $p_B=v$; then $p_C=\rho u$, $p_D=\rho v$, and $u<v<\rho u<\rho v$.

Step 2: where a bisector meets $g$. For points $U,V$ of $g$ with parameters $i,j$, the internal bisector of $\angle UPV$ has direction $e(i)+e(j)$, whose second coordinate is positive, so it meets $g$ at the abscissa (first coordinate)/(second coordinate): \[\frac{\frac{i^2-1}{i^2+1}+\frac{j^2-1}{j^2+1}}{\frac{2i}{i^2+1}+\frac{2j}{j^2+1}}=\frac{2(i^2j^2-1)}{2(i+j)(ij+1)}=\frac{ij-1}{i+j}=:\Gamma(i,j).\] Note that $\Gamma(i,i)=\frac{i^2-1}{2i}=s(i)$ is the abscissa of the point with parameter $i$ itself. Let $W$ be the point where the bisector of $\angle BPC$ meets $g$. With abscissas written by the same letters, \[X=\Gamma(u,v),\quad W=\Gamma(v,\rho u),\quad Y=\Gamma(\rho u,\rho v),\quad B=\Gamma(v,v),\quad C=\Gamma(\rho u,\rho u).\] Directly from the definition, \[\Gamma(i,j)-\Gamma(i,k)=\frac{(ij-1)(i+k)-(ik-1)(i+j)}{(i+j)(i+k)}=\frac{(1+i^2)(j-k)}{(i+j)(i+k)} .\]

Step 3: equal powers of $W$. Using this formula with $i=v$ for $W-X$ and $W-B$, and with $i=\rho u$ for $W-C$ and $W-Y$: \[W-X=\frac{(1+v^2)(\rho-1)u}{(v+\rho u)(v+u)},\qquad W-B=\frac{(1+v^2)(\rho u-v)}{2v(v+\rho u)},\] \[W-C=\frac{(1+\rho^2u^2)(v-\rho u)}{2\rho u(\rho u+v)},\qquad W-Y=\frac{(1+\rho^2u^2)(1-\rho)v}{(\rho u+v)\,\rho(u+v)} .\] Multiplying, \[(W-X)(W-C)=\frac{(1+v^2)(1+\rho^2u^2)(\rho-1)(v-\rho u)}{2\rho\,(u+v)(v+\rho u)^2}=(W-B)(W-Y).\] The segment $XC$ is the chord of the circle $(PXC)$ on $g$, so the power of the point $W$ with respect to $(PXC)$ is $(W-X)(W-C)$ (signed product along $g$); likewise its power with respect to $(PBY)$ is $(W-B)(W-Y)$. These are equal.

Step 4: the common point. The bisector of $\angle APB$ meets $g$ between $A$ and $B$, the bisector of $\angle BPC$ between $B$ and $C$, and that of $\angle CPD$ between $C$ and $D$, so $X<B<W<C<Y$ on $g$. Thus $W$ lies inside the chords $XC$ and $BY$, hence inside both circles, and the common power $(W-X)(W-C)$ is negative. The line $PW$, which is the bisector of $\angle BPC$, meets $(PXC)$ again at a point $Z_1$ on the other side of $W$ from $P$, with $WP\cdot WZ_1=WX\cdot WC$, and meets $(PBY)$ again at a point $Z_2$ beyond $W$ with $WP\cdot WZ_2=WB\cdot WY$. By Step 3, $WZ_1=WZ_2$, and $Z_1,Z_2$ lie on the same ray from $W$, so $Z_1=Z_2$.

Therefore the circumcircles of $PXC$ and $PBY$ and the bisector of $\angle BPC$ pass through the common point $Z_1\ne P$.
