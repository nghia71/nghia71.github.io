Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 19: Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Tracking

Find all pairs of non-negative integers $(a,b)$ such that \[6^a-7^b\] is a perfect square.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(0,0)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Modulo 3)

A perfect square is a non-negative integer $k^2$, and $k^2\equiv 0$ or $1\pmod 3$.

If $a\ge 1$, then $6^a\equiv 0$ and $7^b\equiv 1\pmod 3$, so $6^a-7^b\equiv-1\equiv 2\pmod 3$, which is never a square. Hence $a=0$, and $1-7^b\ge 0$ forces $b=0$. Indeed $6^0-7^0=0=0^2$.

The only pair is $(a,b)=\boxed{(0,0)}$.

Solution 2 (Modulo 7 and a factorisation)

If $b=0$: $6^a-1$ is $0$ for $a=0$ (a square), $5$ for $a=1$ (not a square), and $\equiv 3\pmod 4$ for $a\ge 2$ (not a square, since squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$).

Let $b\ge 1$ and suppose $6^a-7^b=k^2$ with $k\ge 0$. Then $6^a>1$, so $a\ge 1$. Modulo $7$, $6^a\equiv(-1)^a$, and the squares modulo $7$ are $0,1,2,4$; since $-1\equiv 6$ is not among them, $a$ is even, $a=2s$ with $s\ge 1$. Then \[(6^s-k)(6^s+k)=7^b .\] Both factors are positive (their product and the second factor are positive), so both are powers of $7$: $6^s-k=7^i$, $6^s+k=7^j$ with $i\le j$. If $i\ge 1$, then $7$ divides their sum $2\cdot 6^s$, which is impossible. So $i=0$ and $2\cdot 6^s=1+7^b$. But the left side is divisible by $3$ while $1+7^b\equiv 2\pmod 3$, a contradiction.

So the only pair is $(a,b)=\boxed{(0,0)}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

The $2026$ balls numbered from $1$ to $2026$ are distributed into $1013$ bags so that each bag contains $2$ balls. Asli colors one of the balls in each bag blue and the other red.

Let $M$ be the sum of the numbers on the blue balls and let $K$ be the sum of the numbers on the red balls. Find the smallest possible value of the integer $N$ such that, no matter how the balls are distributed among the bags, Asli can color them so that $|M-K|\le N$.

Show the answer

$1013$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Alternating cycles)

$N\ge 1013$. Put $1$ and $2026$ in one bag, and pair the remaining numbers as $\{2,3\},\{4,5\},\dots,\{2024,2025\}$ ($1012$ bags). Each of these $1012$ bags contributes $\pm1$ to $M-K$, and the first bag contributes $\pm2025$. Hence $|M-K|\ge 2025-1012=1013$ for every colouring.

$N=1013$ always suffices. Call $\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{2025,2026\}$ the twin pairs. Draw a graph on the $2026$ balls with two kinds of edges: the $1013$ bag edges and the $1013$ twin edges (if a bag is itself a twin pair, it gives two parallel edges). Every ball lies on exactly one edge of each kind, so the graph splits into disjoint cycles whose edges alternate between bag edges and twin edges; in particular every cycle has even length. Colour the balls of each cycle alternately blue and red. Then every bag has one blue and one red ball, and so does every twin pair.

Since every twin pair $\{2i-1,2i\}$ has one blue and one red ball, it contributes $+1$ or $-1$ to $M-K$. Hence $M-K=\sum_{i=1}^{1013}(\pm1)$, and $|M-K|\le 1013$.

So the smallest possible $N$ is $\boxed{1013}$.

Solution 2 (Induction by merging bags)

The example of Solution 1 (bags $\{1,2026\},\{2,3\},\dots,\{2024,2025\}$) shows $N\ge 1013$.

For the upper bound we prove: for every $k\ge 1$ and every distribution of the balls $1,2,\dots,2k$ into $k$ bags of two, there is a colouring with $|M-K|\le k$. For $k=1013$ this gives $N\le 1013$.

For $k=1$ the only bag is $\{1,2\}$ and $|M-K|=1$. Let $k\ge 2$ and assume the claim for $k-1$. Look at the balls $2k$ and $2k-1$.

  • If they share a bag, colour the other $k-1$ bags (balls $1,\dots,2k-2$) by the induction hypothesis with $|M'-K'|\le k-1$, and colour $2k,2k-1$ in either way. The last bag changes $M-K$ by $\pm1$, so $|M-K|\le k$.

  • Otherwise the bags are $\{2k,a\}$ and $\{2k-1,b\}$ with $a,b\le 2k-2$. Replace these two bags by the single bag $\{a,b\}$: this is a distribution of $1,\dots,2k-2$ into $k-1$ bags. By the induction hypothesis it has a colouring with $|M'-K'|\le k-1$; in it $a$ and $b$ have different colours. Keep these colours and give $2k$ the colour opposite to $a$, and $2k-1$ the colour opposite to $b$. Then both original bags $\{2k,a\}$ and $\{2k-1,b\}$ are two-coloured, and $2k$, $2k-1$ have different colours, so they change $M'-K'$ by $\pm1$. Hence $|M-K|\le (k-1)+1=k$.

This completes the induction, and the answer is $\boxed{1013}$.

Solution 3 (Sorted differences and a greedy choice of signs)

Put $m=1013$, so the balls are $1,2,\dots,2m$ and there are $m$ bags.

$N\ge 1013$. Use the bags $\{i,i+m\}$ for $i=1,\dots,m$. Each bag contributes $+m$ or $-m$ to $M-K$, so $M-K=m(\varepsilon_1+\dots+\varepsilon_m)$ with every $\varepsilon_i=\pm1$. A sum of $m=1013$ odd numbers is odd, hence not $0$, so $|M-K|\ge m=1013$ for every colouring.

$N=1013$ suffices. For each bag let its difference be the larger ball minus the smaller one, and list the differences as $d_1\le d_2\le\dots\le d_m$. Colouring the larger ball of a bag blue adds its difference to $M-K$, colouring it red subtracts it; so $M-K=\pm d_1\pm d_2\pm\dots\pm d_m$, and every choice of signs is possible.

Lemma: $d_i\le m+(d_1+\dots+d_{i-1})$ for every $i$. If $d_i\le m$ there is nothing to prove. Let $d_i=m+k$ with $k\ge1$. A bag $\{x,y\}$ with $x<y$ and $y-x\ge d_i$ has $x\le y-d_i\le 2m-(m+k)=m-k$. Different bags have different smaller balls, so at most $m-k$ bags have difference at least $d_i$, and at least $k$ bags have difference smaller than $d_i$. In the sorted list these come before $d_i$, and each is at least $1$, so $d_1+\dots+d_{i-1}\ge k=d_i-m$.

The greedy colouring. Choose the signs of $d_m,d_{m-1},\dots,d_1$ in this order (largest first), keeping a running total that starts at $0$: give each new difference the sign opposite to the sign of the current total (either sign if the total is $0$). If the total has absolute value $\rho$ before a step with difference $d$, its absolute value after the step is $|\rho-d|$.

Before the first step $\rho=0<d_m$, so there is a last step at which $\rho<d$; say this step handles $d_i$ and $\rho=\rho_0$ before it. Right after it the absolute value is $d_i-\rho_0\le d_i$. At every later step (handling $d_{i-1},\dots,d_1$) we have $\rho\ge d$, so the absolute value drops by exactly that difference. Hence the final value satisfies \[|M-K|=d_i-\rho_0-(d_1+\dots+d_{i-1})\le d_i-(d_1+\dots+d_{i-1})\le m\] by the Lemma. So Asli can always reach $|M-K|\le 1013$, and the smallest possible $N$ is $\boxed{1013}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

All vertices of a hexagon $ABCDEF$ lie on a circle with center $O$, and $|AB|=|CD|=|EF|$. The diagonals $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ intersect at the point $K$. Let $S$ be the center of the circumcircle of the triangle formed by the lines $AF$, $BC$, and $DE$. Prove that the points $K$, $O$, and $S$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Parallelograms and antiparallels)

Let the lines $FA$, $BC$, $DE$ form the triangle $XYZ$ with \[X=FA\cap BC,\qquad Y=BC\cap DE,\qquad Z=DE\cap FA,\] so $S$ is its circumcenter.

Parallel chords. Each of the arcs $AB$, $CD$, $EF$ (not containing other vertices) is less than a semicircle: these three arcs are disjoint, and since the chords are equal the arcs are equal or supplementary, so if one were at least $180^\circ$ the other two would each equal $360^\circ$ minus it and the three would add up to more than $360^\circ$. So the equal chords $AB$ and $CD$ cut off equal arcs, so the inscribed angles $\angle ADB$ and $\angle DBC$ are equal; these are alternate angles, hence $AD\parallel BC$. In the same way $CD=EF$ gives $CF\parallel DE$, and $EF=AB$ gives $BE\parallel FA$.

Three parallelograms. The lines $AD$ and $BE$ pass through $K$; the line $AD$ is parallel to $BC\ni X,B$ and the line $BE$ is parallel to $FA\ni X,A$. Hence $KAXB$ is a parallelogram, and the midpoint of $AB$ is the midpoint of $KX$. In the same way $KDYC$ and $KFZE$ are parallelograms, so the midpoints of $CD$ and $EF$ are the midpoints of $KY$ and $KZ$.

$AB$ is perpendicular to $XS$. We use directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Since $A,B,C,F$ are concyclic, \[\measuredangle(AB,XZ)=\measuredangle(AB,AF)=\measuredangle(CB,CF)=\measuredangle(XY,YZ),\] because $CB$ is the line $XY$ and $CF\parallel YZ$. If $t$ is the tangent at $X$ to the circumcircle of $XYZ$, the tangent–chord angle gives $\measuredangle(t,XZ)=\measuredangle(YX,YZ)$. Hence $AB\parallel t$, and since $t\perp XS$, we get $AB\perp XS$. Likewise $CD\perp YS$ and $EF\perp ZS$.

Conclusion. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $KS$. The perpendicular bisector of $AB$ passes through the midpoint of $AB$, which is the midpoint of $XK$, and it is parallel to $XS$; in triangle $XKS$ the line through the midpoint of $XK$ parallel to $XS$ passes through the midpoint $N$ of $KS$ (this is also true if $X,K,S$ are collinear). So the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ passes through $N$, and in the same way so do the perpendicular bisectors of $CD$ and $EF$. All three also pass through $O$. Three equal chords of a circle cannot all be parallel (two parallel equal chords are symmetric about $O$, and there is no room for a third), so two of these perpendicular bisectors are different lines; both contain $O$ and $N$, hence $O=N$. Thus $O$ is the midpoint of $KS$, and $K$, $O$, $S$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Projections onto the sides)

Use the triangle $XYZ$, the parallel chords $AD\parallel BC$, $BE\parallel FA$, $CF\parallel DE$ and the parallelograms $KAXB$, $KDYC$, $KFZE$ from Solution 1. In vector form the parallelograms say \[K+X=A+B,\qquad K+Y=C+D,\qquad K+Z=E+F.\] Let $S'=2O-K$ be the reflection of $K$ in $O$. We show that $S'$ is the circumcenter of $XYZ$.

Let $u$ be a unit vector along $BC$ and write $\pi(V)=\overrightarrow{OV}\cdot u$ for the signed projection of a point $V$. The perpendicular bisector of the chord $BC$ passes through $O$ and is perpendicular to $u$, so $\pi(B)+\pi(C)=2\pi(O)=0$. The chord $AD$ is parallel to $BC$, so its perpendicular bisector is the same line, and $\pi(A)+\pi(D)=0$ too. Adding the first two vector equations and projecting, \[2\pi(K)+\pi(X)+\pi(Y)=\pi(A)+\pi(B)+\pi(C)+\pi(D)=0,\] so $\pi(S')=-\pi(K)=\frac{\pi(X)+\pi(Y)}{2}$. Thus $S'$ and the midpoint of $XY$ have the same projection on the line $XY$, which means that $S'$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$.

In the same way, with a unit vector $v$ along $DE$: the chords $DE$ and $CF$ are parallel, so $\pi_v(C)+\pi_v(D)+\pi_v(E)+\pi_v(F)=0$; adding $K+Y=C+D$ and $K+Z=E+F$ gives $\pi_v(S')=\frac{\pi_v(Y)+\pi_v(Z)}{2}$, so $S'$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $YZ$.

Therefore $S'=S$, i.e. $O$ is the midpoint of $KS$, and $K$, $O$, $S$ are collinear.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Find the greatest real number $T$ with $0\le T\le 33$ and the following property: whenever $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{33}$ are real numbers with $0\le x_i\le 1$ for every $i$ and \[x_1+x_2+\dots+x_{33}=T,\] these numbers can be divided into two groups so that the sum of the numbers in the first group is at most $5$ and the sum of the numbers in the second group is at most $15$.

Show the answer

$\frac{115}{6}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two greedy splittings)

We call a group with sum at most $5$ the small group and a group with sum at most $15$ the big group.

Every $T>\frac{115}{6}$ fails. Let $\frac{115}{6}<T\le 33$. If $T\le 23$, take $23$ numbers equal to $\frac{T}{23}$ and ten zeros; if $T>23$, take $23$ ones and ten numbers equal to $\frac{T-23}{10}\le 1$. In both cases $23$ of the numbers exceed $\frac56$. Six of them would have sum more than $5$, and $18$ of them would have sum more than $15$; so the small group contains at most $5$ and the big group at most $17$ of them, $22$ in total, and one is left over. Hence no splitting exists.

$T=\frac{115}{6}$ works. Let $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_{33}$ lie in $[0,1]$ with sum $T\le\frac{115}{6}$.

  • If at least five numbers exceed $\frac56$, put $x_1,\dots,x_5$ in the small group: their sum is at most $5$ (each is at most $1$) and more than $\frac{25}{6}$, so the big group gets less than $\frac{115}{6}-\frac{25}{6}=15$.

  • Otherwise $x_5\le\frac56$. If $T\le 15$, put everything in the big group. If not, let $k$ be the largest index with $x_1+\dots+x_k\le 15$; then $x_1+\dots+x_{k+1}>15$, and since every number is at most $1$, $k+1\ge 16$, so $x_{k+1}\le x_5\le\frac56$. Put $x_1,\dots,x_k$ in the big group and the rest in the small group, whose sum is \[T-(x_1+\dots+x_k)=T-(x_1+\dots+x_{k+1})+x_{k+1}<\tfrac{115}{6}-15+\tfrac56=5.\]

So the greatest value is $T=\boxed{\frac{115}{6}}$.

Solution 2 (An extremal splitting)

The example of Solution 1 ($23$ numbers just above $\frac56$, the rest small) shows that every $T>\frac{115}{6}$ fails.

Now let the numbers have sum $T\le\frac{115}{6}$ and suppose that no valid splitting exists. Among all subsets with sum at most $5$ (the empty set is one) choose a subset $G$ with the largest sum $g$. Its complement has sum $T-g$, which must exceed $15$ (otherwise $G$ and its complement would be a valid splitting). Hence $g<T-15\le\frac{25}{6}$.

Every number $y$ outside $G$ satisfies $g+y>5$, since otherwise $G\cup\{y\}$ would be a subset with sum at most $5$ and larger sum. So every number outside $G$ exceeds $5-g>\frac56$. The numbers outside $G$ have sum more than $15$ and each is at most $1$, so there are at least $16$ of them. Any five of them form a subset with sum at most $5$ and greater than $\frac{25}{6}>g$, contradicting the choice of $G$.

Therefore every choice with sum at most $\frac{115}{6}$ can be split, and the answer is $\boxed{\frac{115}{6}}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

Prove that for all positive real numbers $x,y,z$ satisfying $x^2+y^2+z^2=2$, we have \[\frac{1+xy}{xz+yz}+\frac{1+xz}{xy+yz}+\frac{1+yz}{xy+xz}\ge\frac{15}{4}.\]

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Homogenise, then Nesbitt)

Since $x^2+y^2+z^2=2$, we have $1=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{2}$ and \[1+xy=\frac{x^2+y^2+2xy+z^2}{2}=\frac{(x+y)^2+z^2}{2}.\] Hence, with $t_1=\frac{x+y}{z}$, \[\frac{1+xy}{xz+yz}=\frac{(x+y)^2+z^2}{2z(x+y)}=\frac12\Big(t_1+\frac1{t_1}\Big),\] and similarly for the other two terms with $t_2=\frac{x+z}{y}$, $t_3=\frac{y+z}{x}$. So we must show $\sum t_i+\sum\frac1{t_i}\ge\frac{15}{2}$.

First, $\sum t_i=\big(\frac xy+\frac yx\big)+\big(\frac xz+\frac zx\big)+\big(\frac yz+\frac zy\big)\ge 2+2+2=6$ by AM–GM. Second (Nesbitt), \[\sum\frac1{t_i}+3=\sum\Big(\frac{z}{x+y}+1\Big)=(x+y+z)\sum\frac1{x+y}\ge (x+y+z)\cdot\frac{9}{2(x+y+z)}=\frac92\] by the AM–HM inequality for $x+y$, $y+z$, $z+x$, so $\sum\frac1{t_i}\ge\frac32$. Adding, $\sum t_i+\sum\frac1{t_i}\ge\frac{15}{2}$, and the left side of the problem is at least $\frac{15}{4}$. Equality holds for $x=y=z=\sqrt{2/3}$.

Solution 2 (One AM-GM per term)

As in Solution 1, the $i$-th term equals $\frac12\big(t_i+\frac1{t_i}\big)$ with $t_1=\frac{x+y}{z}$, $t_2=\frac{x+z}{y}$, $t_3=\frac{y+z}{x}$. At equality all $t_i=2$, which suggests splitting \[t+\frac1t=\Big(\frac t4+\frac1t\Big)+\frac{3t}{4}\ge 2\sqrt{\frac t4\cdot\frac1t}+\frac{3t}{4}=1+\frac{3t}{4}\qquad(t>0).\] Summing over $i$ and using $t_1+t_2+t_3\ge 6$ (AM–GM on $\frac xy+\frac yx$ etc., as in Solution 1), \[\sum\Big(t_i+\frac1{t_i}\Big)\ge 3+\frac34\cdot 6=\frac{15}{2},\] so the given sum is at least $\frac{15}{4}$, with equality for $x=y=z=\sqrt{2/3}$.

Solution 3 (Each numerator is a constant minus its denominator)

Put $s=xy+xz+yz>0$. The denominators are $xz+yz=s-xy$, $xy+yz=s-xz$ and $xy+xz=s-yz$; they are positive and their sum is $2s$. Since $1+xy=(1+s)-(s-xy)$, and similarly for the other two numerators, \[\frac{1+xy}{xz+yz}+\frac{1+xz}{xy+yz}+\frac{1+yz}{xy+xz}=(1+s)\Big(\frac1{s-xy}+\frac1{s-xz}+\frac1{s-yz}\Big)-3.\] By the AM–HM inequality for the three positive denominators, \[\frac1{s-xy}+\frac1{s-xz}+\frac1{s-yz}\ge\frac{9}{(s-xy)+(s-xz)+(s-yz)}=\frac{9}{2s},\] so the left side is at least \[\frac{9(1+s)}{2s}-3=\frac{9}{2s}+\frac32 .\] Finally $2(x^2+y^2+z^2)-2s=(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2\ge 0$, so $s\le x^2+y^2+z^2=2$ and $\frac{9}{2s}\ge\frac94$. Hence the left side is at least $\frac94+\frac32=\frac{15}{4}$. Equality needs $s=2$, i.e. $x=y=z$, so $x=y=z=\sqrt{2/3}$; there all denominators are equal and the value is indeed $\frac{15}{4}$.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Let $ABC$ be a right triangle with $\angle BAC=90^\circ$. Let $P$ and $Q$ be points satisfying \[\angle BPA=\angle BPC=90^\circ+\angle ABC\] and \[\angle CQA=\angle CQB=90^\circ+\angle ACB.\] Let $R$ be the intersection point of the lines $BP$ and $CQ$. Prove that \[|BC|=4|AR|.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Law of sines: $\tan\angle PBA=\frac13\tan B$)

Write $\beta=\angle ABC$, $\gamma=\angle ACB$ (so $\beta+\gamma=90^\circ$), and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. We show that $R$ is the midpoint of $AM$; since $AM=\frac{BC}{2}$ (median to the hypotenuse), this gives $AR=\frac{BC}{4}$.

$P$ lies inside the triangle. If the rays $PA$ and $PC$ coincided, $P$ would lie on line $AC$ outside the segment $AC$, and then $\angle BPA<90^\circ$ because triangle $ABP$ has a right angle at $A$; impossible. So the rays $PA$, $PC$ are symmetric about the line $PB$, and as $2(90^\circ+\beta)>180^\circ$, $\angle APC=360^\circ-2(90^\circ+\beta)=2\gamma<180^\circ$ and the ray opposite to $PB$ bisects $\angle APC$. That ray meets segment $AC$ at a point $T$, and $P$ lies strictly between $B$ and $T$; hence $P$ is inside triangle $ABC$. In the same way $Q$ is inside the triangle.

Computing $\angle PBA$. Let $\varphi=\angle PBA$, so $\angle PBC=\beta-\varphi$. The angle sums in triangles $ABP$ and $CBP$ give \[\angle PAB=180^\circ-(90^\circ+\beta)-\varphi=\gamma-\varphi,\qquad \angle PCB=180^\circ-(90^\circ+\beta)-(\beta-\varphi)=\gamma-\beta+\varphi.\] The law of sines in these triangles (common side $BP$, and $\sin(90^\circ+\beta)$ in both) gives $AB\sin(\gamma-\varphi)=BC\sin(\gamma-\beta+\varphi)$. With $AB=BC\cos\beta$, $\sin(\gamma-\varphi)=\cos(\beta+\varphi)$ and $\sin(\gamma-\beta+\varphi)=\sin(90^\circ-2\beta+\varphi)=\cos(2\beta-\varphi)$, this is \[\cos\beta\cos(\beta+\varphi)=\cos(2\beta-\varphi).\] Expanding and dividing by $\cos\varphi\ne0$: $\cos^2\beta-\sin\beta\cos\beta\tan\varphi=\cos2\beta+2\sin\beta\cos\beta\tan\varphi$, so $\sin^2\beta=\cos^2\beta-\cos2\beta=3\sin\beta\cos\beta\tan\varphi$, that is, \[\tan\angle PBA=\tfrac13\tan\beta .\] By the same computation with $B$ and $C$ exchanged, $\tan\angle QCA=\frac13\tan\gamma$.

Locating $R$. Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(c,0)$, $C=(0,b)$, so $\tan\beta=\frac bc$, $M=\big(\frac c2,\frac b2\big)$, and let $R_0=\big(\frac c4,\frac b4\big)$ be the midpoint of $AM$. Then $\tan\angle R_0BA=\frac{b/4}{3c/4}=\frac13\tan\beta=\tan\angle PBA$, and $R_0$, $P$ lie on the same side of $AB$ inside angle $ABC$, so $R_0$ lies on line $BP$. Similarly $\tan\angle R_0CA=\frac{c/4}{3b/4}=\frac13\tan\gamma=\tan\angle QCA$, so $R_0$ lies on line $CQ$. The lines $BP$ and $CQ$ are different, so $R=R_0$, and \[AR=\tfrac12 AM=\tfrac14 BC,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad |BC|=4|AR|.\]

Solution 2 (Two circles and a radical axis)

As shown at the start of Solution 1, $P$ and $Q$ lie inside the triangle. Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(c,0)$, $C=(0,b)$, $a=BC=\sqrt{b^2+c^2}$, $\beta=\angle ABC$, $\gamma=\angle ACB=90^\circ-\beta$.

The circle $\omega_1$. The circumcircle of $ABC$ has center $M=\big(\frac c2,\frac b2\big)$ (Thales). Let $\omega_1$ be its reflection in $AB$: center $O_1=\big(\frac c2,-\frac b2\big)$, through $A$ and $B$. The reflection $C'$ of $C$ lies on $\omega_1$ below $AB$ and sees $AB$ under the angle $\gamma$; so the points of $\omega_1$ above $AB$ see $AB$ under $180^\circ-\gamma=90^\circ+\beta$, and they are exactly the points above $AB$ with this property. Hence $P\in\omega_1$. Its equation is \[\omega_1:\ x^2+y^2-cx+by=0.\]

The circle $\omega_2$. Let $\omega_2$ be the circle through $B$ tangent to $AC$ at $C$. Its center lies on the line $y=b$, say $O_2=(h,b)$, and $O_2C=O_2B$ gives $h^2=(c-h)^2+b^2$, so $h=\frac{a^2}{2c}$. By the tangent–chord angle, the points of $\omega_2$ on the side of $BC$ away from $A$ see $BC$ under $\angle ACB=\gamma$, so the points of $\omega_2$ on $A$'s side see it under $180^\circ-\gamma=90^\circ+\beta$, and they are exactly the points on that side with this property. Hence $P\in\omega_2$, and \[\omega_2:\ x^2+y^2-\frac{a^2}{c}x-2by+b^2=0.\]

The radical axis. Both $B$ and $P$ lie on $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, so line $BP$ is their radical axis. Subtracting the equations and using $\frac{a^2}{c}-c=\frac{b^2}{c}$, \[\frac{b^2}{c}x+3by-b^2=0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad BP:\ \frac xc+\frac{3y}{b}=1 .\] Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $C$ (the reflection of the circumcircle in $AC$, and the circle through $C$ tangent to $AB$ at $B$) gives in the same way $CQ:\ \frac{3x}{c}+\frac yb=1$. Solving, $\frac xc=\frac yb=\frac14$, so $R=\big(\frac c4,\frac b4\big)$ and \[AR=\frac{\sqrt{b^2+c^2}}{4}=\frac{BC}{4}.\]

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A set $S$ consisting of $n$ integers is called beautiful if the remainders of any two distinct elements of $S$ modulo $n$ are different.

Find all positive integers $n$ for which there exist integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ such that both sets \[\{a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{a_1,2a_2,\dots,na_n\}\] are beautiful.

Show the answer

$n=1$ and $n=2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (gcd classes and Wilson)

Both sets are beautiful exactly when $a_1,\dots,a_n$ form a complete residue system modulo $n$ and so do $1\cdot a_1,2a_2,\dots,na_n$. Work modulo $n$; then the indices $i=1,\dots,n$ are also all residues (with $n\equiv 0$).

Examples. For $n=1$ take $a_1=0$. For $n=2$ take $a_1=1$, $a_2=0$: both $\{1,0\}$ and $\{1,0\}$ are complete systems modulo $2$.

Key lemma. For a residue $r$ let $g(r)=\gcd(r,n)$ (so $g(0)=n$). Then $g(a_i)=g(i)=g(ia_i)$ for every $i$.

Proof. Clearly $g(i)\mid g(ia_i)$ and $g(a_i)\mid g(ia_i)$. As $i$ runs from $1$ to $n$, each of $i$, $a_i$, $ia_i$ runs over all residues modulo $n$ exactly once, so \[\prod_i g(i)=\prod_i g(a_i)=\prod_i g(ia_i).\] The positive integers $\frac{g(ia_i)}{g(i)}$ have product $1$, so all equal $1$; the same holds for $\frac{g(ia_i)}{g(a_i)}$. $\square$

Only $n\le 2$. Let $n\ge 2$, let $p$ be a prime divisor of $n$ and $m=\frac np$. The residues $r$ with $g(r)=m$ are exactly $m,2m,\dots,(p-1)m$ (a multiple $mj$ with $0\le j<p$ has $g(mj)=m\gcd(j,p)$). By the lemma, for these $p-1$ indices $i=mj$ both $a_i$ and $ia_i$ are again in this set, and since $i\mapsto a_i$ and $i\mapsto ia_i$ are injective, each of them permutes it.

If $p^2\mid n$, then $p\mid m$, so $n\mid m^2$ and $ia_i$ is a multiple of $m^2$, i.e. $ia_i\equiv 0$, contradicting $g(ia_i)=m<n$. Hence $p\nmid m$.

Write $a_{mj}\equiv mb_j$; then $j\mapsto b_j$ permutes $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ modulo $p$. Since $m x\bmod n$ depends only on $x\bmod p$, \[(mj)(mb_j)=m\cdot(m j b_j)\equiv m\,c_j\pmod n,\qquad c_j\equiv m j b_j\pmod p,\] and $j\mapsto c_j$ also permutes $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ modulo $p$. Multiplying over $j=1,\dots,p-1$: \[(p-1)!\equiv\prod c_j\equiv m^{p-1}\prod j\prod b_j\equiv m^{p-1}\big((p-1)!\big)^2\pmod p.\] By Fermat's little theorem $m^{p-1}\equiv 1$, and by Wilson's theorem $(p-1)!\equiv-1$, so $-1\equiv 1\pmod p$, i.e. $p=2$.

Therefore $2$ is the only prime divisor of $n$ and $4\nmid n$, so $n=2$. Together with $n=1$, the answer is $n\in\boxed{\{1,2\}}$.

Solution 2 (Divisibility by $p$ and Wilson over a class of units)

As in Solution 1, the condition says that $a_1,\dots,a_n$ and $1\cdot a_1,\dots,na_n$ are complete residue systems modulo $n$; we work modulo $n$, and $n=1$, $n=2$ work (Solution 1). Note that for a prime $p\mid n$, divisibility of a residue modulo $n$ by $p$ is well defined.

Step 1: $p\mid i\iff p\mid a_i$. Let $p$ be a prime divisor of $n$, $A=\{i:p\mid i\}$ and $B=\{i:p\mid a_i\}$. Exactly $n/p$ residues modulo $n$ are divisible by $p$, so $|A|=n/p$, and $|B|=n/p$ because $i\mapsto a_i$ is a bijection. Likewise exactly $n/p$ indices have $p\mid ia_i$. Since $p$ is prime, $p\mid ia_i$ iff $i\in A\cup B$. So $|A\cup B|=n/p=|A|=|B|$, which forces $A=B$.

Step 2: $p^2\nmid n$. If $p^2\mid n$, some index $i$ has $ia_i\equiv p\pmod n$, hence $ia_i\equiv p\pmod{p^2}$. Then $p\mid ia_i$, so by Step 1 $p$ divides both $i$ and $a_i$, and $p^2\mid ia_i$, contradicting $ia_i\equiv p\pmod{p^2}$.

Step 3: an odd prime divisor is impossible. Suppose $n$ has an odd prime divisor $p$. Put $d=p$ if $n$ is odd and $d=2p$ if $n$ is even, and let $U$ be the set of residues modulo $n$ that are coprime to $d$. By Step 1 (for the prime divisors of $d$), $i\in U\iff a_i\in U$, and $ia_i\in U\iff$ both $i,a_i\in U\iff i\in U$. Hence $i\mapsto a_i$ and $i\mapsto ia_i$ both permute $U$. Let $P$ be the product of the elements of $U$. Then \[P\equiv\prod_{i\in U}ia_i=\Big(\prod_{i\in U}i\Big)\Big(\prod_{i\in U}a_i\Big)\equiv P^2\pmod p,\] and $p\nmid P$, so $P\equiv 1\pmod p$.

Now count how often each nonzero residue $c$ modulo $p$ occurs among the elements of $U$, reduced modulo $p$.

  • $n$ odd ($d=p$): $U$ consists of the residues not divisible by $p$; the residues $0,1,\dots,n-1$ form $n/p$ blocks of $p$ consecutive numbers, so $c$ occurs $n/p$ times, an odd number.

  • $n$ even: by Step 2 (for $p=2$), $n=2q$ with $q$ odd. An element of $U$ is odd and $\equiv c\pmod p$; by the Chinese remainder theorem these two conditions fix it modulo $2p$, and there are $\frac{n}{2p}=\frac qp$ such residues modulo $n$, again an odd number.

In both cases each nonzero residue occurs an odd number $e$ of times, so by Wilson's theorem \[P\equiv\big((p-1)!\big)^{e}\equiv(-1)^{e}=-1\pmod p.\] Together with $P\equiv 1$ this gives $p\mid 2$, impossible for an odd prime $p$.

Conclusion. So $n$ has no odd prime divisor, and by Step 2 it is not divisible by $4$. Hence $n\in\boxed{\{1,2\}}$, and both values work.

Problem 8

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n\ge 1$ be a positive integer. Initially, $n$ points are marked on a board, with no three of them collinear. In each move, Asli chooses two points among them and joins them by a line segment, then marks a new point on this segment.

In this move, if Asli joins a point that has been joined to an even number of points with a point that has been joined to an odd number of points, she earns $1$ point; otherwise, she earns $0$ points.

Find the maximum number of points Asli can earn by the time every pair of points on the board has been joined.

Show the answer

$0$ for $n=1$; $\frac{n(n-2)}{2}$ for even $n$; $\binom n2-2=\frac{n^2-n-4}{2}$ for odd $n\ge 3$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting odd points, and an inductive construction)

We read the problem as follows (the marked points on the segments play no role): each move joins two of the original $n$ points that are not yet joined, and the game ends when all $\binom n2$ pairs are joined. The degree of a point is the number of points it has been joined to. A move is of type $EE$, $OO$ or $EO$ according to the parities of the degrees of its two endpoints just before the move; Asli scores exactly on the $EO$ moves. For $n=1$ there are no moves and the answer is $0$, so let $n\ge 2$.

Upper bound. Let $o$ be the number of points of odd degree. An $EE$ move increases $o$ by $2$, an $OO$ move decreases it by $2$, an $EO$ move does not change it. At the start $o=0$; at the end every degree is $n-1$. The score is $\binom n2-\#EE-\#OO$.

  • $n$ even: at the end $o=n$, so $\#EE-\#OO=\frac n2$, hence $\#EE\ge\frac n2$ and the score is at most $\binom n2-\frac n2=\frac{n(n-2)}{2}$.

  • $n$ odd: at the end $o=0$, so $\#EE=\#OO$. The first move is $EE$, so $\#EE=\#OO\ge 1$ and the score is at most $\binom n2-2$.

A tool. Suppose $u$ and $v$ currently have degrees of different parity, and $W$ is a set of points none of which is yet joined to $u$ or $v$. Then Asli can join every $w\in W$ to both $u$ and $v$ and score on all $2|W|$ moves: for each $w$ in turn, join $w$ first to the one of $u,v$ whose parity differs from that of $w$ (this scores, and swaps both parities), and then to the other one (now again of parity different from $w$, so this scores too). After the two moves, $w$ has its old parity and $u,v$ have exchanged parities; in particular $u$ and $v$ still differ, so we can continue with the next $w$.

Construction, $n$ even. For $n=2$ the single move scores $0=\frac{2\cdot 0}{2}$. Suppose an order of the $\binom n2$ moves on a set $V$ of $n$ points scores $\frac{n(n-2)}2$, and add two new points $u,v$. First play:

  • 1. $u w_0$ for some $w_0\in V$ ($EE$, no point; now $u,w_0$ are odd and $v$ is even);

  • 2. the tool with $W=V\setminus\{w_0\}$: $2(n-1)$ points; since $n-1$ is odd, afterwards $u$ is even and $v$ is odd;

  • 3. $uv$ ($EO$, one point; now $u$ is odd, $v$ is even);

  • 4. $vw_0$ ($v$ even, $w_0$ odd: one point).

Now every point of $V$ has degree $2$, which is even, exactly as at the start, and $u,v$ are finished. The score of a move depends only on the current parities, so playing the old order on $V$ again scores $\frac{n(n-2)}2$. In total $2n+\frac{n(n-2)}{2}=\frac{(n+2)n}{2}$, the bound for $n+2$.

Construction, $n$ odd. For $n=3$ the order $12,13,23$ scores $0+1+0=1=\binom32-2$. Suppose an order on a set $V$ of $n$ points scores $\binom n2-2$, and add $u,v$. Play the first move of the old order (now its endpoints, say $w_0,w_1$, are odd and all other points even), then insert:

  • 1. $uw_0$ ($u$ even, $w_0$ odd: one point; now $u$ is odd and $w_0$ even);

  • 2. the tool with $W=V\setminus\{w_0\}$: $2(n-1)$ points; since $n-1$ is even, $u$ stays odd and $v$ even;

  • 3. $uv$: one point; now $u$ is even and $v$ odd;

  • 4. $vw_0$ ($v$ odd, $w_0$ even): one point; now $w_0$ is odd again.

All points of $V$ have the same parities as just after the first old move, so the rest of the old order scores as before. The inserted $2n+1$ moves all score, so the total is $\binom n2-2+2n+1=\binom{n+2}2-2$.

Answer. The maximum number of points is \[\boxed{\ 0\ \text{for } n=1;\qquad \frac{n(n-2)}{2}\ \text{for even } n;\qquad \binom n2-2=\frac{n^2-n-4}{2}\ \text{for odd } n\ge 3\ }.\]
