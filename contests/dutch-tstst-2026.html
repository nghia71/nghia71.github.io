Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABCD$ be a rectangle with $|AB|<|BC|$, and let $\Gamma$ be its circumcircle. Point $P$ lies on the arc $BC$ of $\Gamma$ not containing $A$, and point $Q$ lies on the arc $CD$ of $\Gamma$ not containing $A$, such that $|BP|=|CQ|$. The perpendicular from $B$ to $AQ$ intersects the line $AP$ at $X$.

Prove that $|PQ|=|XQ|$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 ($X$ is an orthocentre)

The vertices lie on $\Gamma$ in the order $A,B,P,C,Q,D$, and $AC$, $BD$ are diameters. Arcs below are arcs of $\Gamma$ not containing the other named points.

$AP\perp BQ$. Since $BP=CQ$, the arcs $BP$ and $CQ$ are equal. The chords $AP$ and $BQ$ cross at a point $S$ (the points $A,B,P,Q$ alternate on $\Gamma$). By inscribed angles, \[\angle BAP+\angle ABQ=\tfrac12\bigl(\operatorname{arc}BP+\operatorname{arc}QDA\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(\operatorname{arc}CQ+\operatorname{arc}QD+\operatorname{arc}DA\bigr)=\tfrac12\cdot180^\circ=90^\circ,\] because the arc from $C$ through $Q$ and $D$ to $A$ is a semicircle ($AC$ is a diameter). Hence $\angle ASB=90^\circ$.

$X$ is the orthocentre $H$ of triangle $ABQ$. The line $BX$ is the altitude from $B$ (it is perpendicular to $AQ$), and the line $AP$ is the altitude from $A$ (it is perpendicular to $BQ$). Their intersection $X$ is therefore the orthocentre $H$ of triangle $ABQ$, and $P$ is the second intersection of the altitude from $A$ with the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $ABQ$.

$P$ is the reflection of $H$ in $BQ$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$: \[\measuredangle(BH,BQ)=\measuredangle(BH,AQ)+\measuredangle(AQ,BQ)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(AQ,BQ),\] \[\measuredangle(BQ,BP)=\measuredangle(AQ,AP)=\measuredangle(AQ,BQ)+\measuredangle(BQ,AP)=\measuredangle(AQ,BQ)+90^\circ,\] using the inscribed angle theorem ($A,B,P,Q\in\Gamma$) and $BH\perp AQ$, $AP\perp BQ$. So the lines $BH$ and $BP$ are mirror images in the line $BQ$. The points $H$ and $P$ lie on these two lines and on the line $AP$, which is perpendicular to $BQ$; the reflection in $BQ$ maps the line $AP$ to itself and the line $BH$ to the line $BP$, so it maps $H$ to $P$.

Thus $BQ$ is the perpendicular bisector of $XP$, and since $Q$ lies on it, $|QX|=|QP|$.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers)

Let $\Gamma$ be the unit circle and $a,b$ the complex coordinates of $A,B$; then $c=-a$ and $d=-b$. The vertices go around $\Gamma$ in the order $A,B,P,C,Q,D$, and the equal chords $BP$, $CQ$ cut off equal arcs in the same sense of rotation, so there is a unit complex number $w$ with $p=bw$ and $q=cw=-aw$.

For points $u,v$ on the unit circle, the line $uv$ is $z+uv\,\bar z=u+v$. So $X$ on line $AP$ gives \[x+ap\,\bar x=a+p. \tag{1}\] $BX\perp AQ$ means that $\frac{x-b}{q-a}$ is purely imaginary. As $\overline{q-a}=\frac1q-\frac1a=\frac{a-q}{aq}$, this reads $x-b=aq(\bar x-\bar b)$, i.e. \[x-aq\,\bar x=b-\frac{aq}{b}. \tag{2}\] Subtracting (2) from (1): $a(p+q)\bar x=a+p-b+\frac{aq}{b}$. With $p+q=w(b-a)$ and $\frac{aq}{b}=-\frac{a^2w}{b}$, the right side is $(a-b)+\frac{w(b^2-a^2)}{b}=\frac{(a-b)\bigl(b-w(a+b)\bigr)}{b}$, so \[\bar x=\frac{w(a+b)-b}{abw},\qquad x=\overline{\bar x}=\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{abw}-\frac1b\Bigr)abw=a+b-aw .\] Therefore \[x-q=a+b-aw+aw=a+b,\qquad p-q=bw+aw=w(a+b),\] and since $|w|=1$, $|XQ|=|a+b|=|PQ|$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Determine all integers $z$ that can be written in the form \[z=\frac{a^2-b^2}{b},\] where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers.

Show the answer

all integers except $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Products that are squares)

Put $c=a^2/b$. Then $z=c-b$, and $c$ is a positive integer with $bc=a^2$. Conversely, if $b,c$ are positive integers with $bc$ a perfect square $a^2$, then $\frac{a^2-b^2}{b}=c-b$. So the possible values of $z$ are exactly the differences $c-b$ of positive integers whose product is a square.

Write $b=ku^2$ with $k$ squarefree. Then $bc$ is a square iff $kc$ is a square iff $c=kv^2$ for some positive integer $v$. Hence the possible values are \[z=k(v^2-u^2)\qquad(k,u,v\ \text{positive integers}).\] In particular $z=0$ occurs, and $z$ occurs iff $-z$ does (swap $u$ and $v$). For $z>0$: $z$ occurs iff $z$ has a divisor $e$ of the form $v^2-u^2$ with $v>u\ge1$.

Which $e$ have this form? $e=(v-u)(v+u)$ with two factors of the same parity and $v+u\ge v-u+2$. An odd $e\ge3$ works: $e=(t+1)^2-t^2$ with $e=2t+1$. A multiple $e=4t$ with $t\ge2$ works: $4t=(t+1)^2-(t-1)^2$. No other $e$ works: $e\equiv2\pmod4$ is impossible (two factors of the same parity give an odd number or a multiple of $4$), $e=1$ would need $v+u=1$, and $e=4$ would need $v-u=v+u=2$, i.e. $u=0$.

So a positive $z$ fails exactly when it has no odd divisor greater than $1$ (it is a power of $2$) and is not divisible by $8$, i.e. $z\in\{1,2,4\}$. Hence the answer is: all integers except $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$.

Solution 2 (Squeezing and explicit families)

The values $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$ do not occur. The equation is $a^2=b(b+z)$.

  • $z=1,2$: $b^2<b^2+zb<(b+1)^2$, so $b(b+z)$ is not a square.

  • $z=4$: $a^2=(b+2)^2-4$, so $(b+2-a)(b+2+a)=4$ with two factors of the same parity, the second positive: both equal $2$, giving $a=0$.

  • $z=-1$: $a^2=b^2-b$; for $b\ge2$, $(b-1)^2<b^2-b<b^2$, and $b=1$ gives $a=0$.

  • $z=-2$: $a^2=(b-1)^2-1$, so $(b-1-a)(b-1+a)=1$ and $a=0$.

  • $z=-4$: $a^2=(b-2)^2-4$, so $(b-2-a)(b-2+a)=4$, both factors even, hence equal ($\pm2$), and $a=0$.

In all cases there is no positive $a$.

All other integers occur. $z=0$: $a=b=1$. If $|z|$ has an odd divisor $2t+1\ge3$, write $|z|=k(2t+1)$; then \[(a,b)=\bigl(kt(t+1),\,kt^2\bigr)\ \text{gives}\ \frac{a^2-b^2}{b}=k(t+1)^2-kt^2=k(2t+1),\] \[(a,b)=\bigl(kt(t+1),\,k(t+1)^2\bigr)\ \text{gives}\ kt^2-k(t+1)^2=-k(2t+1).\] Otherwise $|z|$ is a power of $2$, and if $|z|\ne1,2,4$ then $|z|=8k$; now $(a,b)=(3k,k)$ gives $9k-k=8k$ and $(a,b)=(3k,9k)$ gives $k-9k=-8k$. So every integer other than $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$ occurs.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Kees studies the weather and records each day whether it rains or not. On day $1$ it certainly rains, and on day $2$ it certainly does not. Starting from day $3$, at the end of each day Kees writes down the fraction of days so far on which it has rained. For example, if it rains on day $3$, and it does not rain on days $4$ and $5$, then the first three numbers Kees writes are $\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{5}$.

Thus the outcomes for days $1$ and $2$ are fixed, but depending on what happens from day $3$ onward, different sequences of numbers may arise. A number $c\in(0,1)$ is called indispensable if it has the following property: if a sequence contains a number smaller than $c$ and a number greater than $c$, then the number $c$ must also appear in the sequence.

Determine all indispensable numbers $c\in(0,1)$.

Show the answer

$c=\frac1k$ and $c=1-\frac1k$ for integers $k\ge2$ (that is, $\frac12,\frac13,\frac23,\frac14,\frac34,\dots$)

Show the solution

Solution (Which jumps are possible)

Let $R_n$ be the number of rainy days among days $1,\dots,n$ and $r_n=R_n/n$; the written sequence is $r_3,r_4,\dots$, and $R_2=1$.

Two observations. (a) A rainy day does not decrease the fraction and a dry day does not increase it: $\frac{R+1}{n+1}\ge\frac Rn$ (as $R\le n$) and $\frac{R}{n+1}\le\frac Rn$. (b) A dry day cannot jump strictly over $\frac1k$ from above: $\frac Rn>\frac1k>\frac R{n+1}$ would mean $n<kR<n+1$.

Symmetry. Exchanging rain and dry replaces every fraction $r$ by $1-r$; the fixed start (one rainy and one dry day) is unchanged. So $c$ is indispensable iff $1-c$ is. In particular, by (b) and symmetry, a rainy day cannot jump strictly over $1-\frac1k$ from below.

The numbers $\frac1k$ are indispensable. Suppose a sequence has terms below and above $c=\frac1k$ but never equals $c$. If $k\ge3$: $r_3\in\{\frac13,\frac23\}$, so $r_3\ge\frac13\ge c$ and hence $r_3>c$. Let $r_{n+1}$ be the first term below $c$; then $r_n>c>r_{n+1}$, so day $n+1$ is dry by (a), and this contradicts (b). If $k=2$: some two consecutive terms lie on opposite sides of $\frac12$; a downward jump over $\frac12$ contradicts (b) with $k=2$, and an upward jump over $\frac12=1-\frac12$ contradicts the mirror image of (b). So every $\frac1k$ is indispensable, and by symmetry every $1-\frac1k$.

No other number is indispensable. If $c$ is irrational it never appears, and a sequence that rains until the fraction exceeds $c$ and is then dry until it is below $c$ (the fractions tend to $1$, respectively $0$) has terms on both sides. By symmetry it remains to treat rational $c>\frac12$ that is not of the form $1-\frac1k$:

  • $\frac12<c<\frac23$: rain on day $3$ ($r_3=\frac23>c$), then dry forever: $r_n=\frac2n\le\frac12<c$ for $n\ge4$; $c$ never appears.

  • $c>\frac23$: rain from day $3$ on: $r_n=\frac{n-1}{n}=1-\frac1n$, starting at $\frac23<c$ and tending to $1>c$, never equal to $c$.

($c=\frac23=1-\frac13$ is of the excluded form.) Hence the indispensable numbers are exactly $\frac1k$ and $1-\frac1k$, $k\ge2$.

Remark (an integer walk). Observation (b) can be seen as follows. Put $f_n=kR_n-n$; then $f_2=k-2\ge0$, a rainy day adds $k-1$ and a dry day subtracts $1$, and $r_n$ is above, equal to or below $\frac1k$ according as $f_n$ is positive, zero or negative. A walk whose downward steps are $-1$ cannot pass from a positive value to a negative one without visiting $0$. For a general $c=\frac pq$ in lowest terms the walk $qR_n-pn$ has steps $+(q-p)$ and $-p$; when $p\ge2$ and $q-p\ge2$ both steps have size at least $2$, and the walk may jump over $0$ (the counterexamples above show that it does). This is the reason behind the answer: only for $p=1$ or $q-p=1$, that is $c=\frac1k$ or $c=1-\frac1k$, is one of the two steps of size $1$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $\triangle ABC$ be an acute triangle with $|BC|>|AC|$, and let $Z$ be its centroid. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$, and let $F$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$ onto $AB$. The median $CM$ meets the circumcircle $\Gamma$ of $\triangle ABC$ again at $P$. Let $Q$ be the intersection of $ZF$ with $\Gamma$ such that $F$ lies between $Z$ and $Q$.

Prove that $FMPQ$ is a cyclic quadrilateral.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Homothety at the centroid and the nine-point circle)

Let $h$ be the homothety with centre $Z$ and ratio $-\frac12$. It maps $A,B,C$ to the midpoints of $BC,CA,AB$, so it maps $\Gamma$ to the nine-point circle, which also passes through the foot $F$. Hence $Q'=h^{-1}(F)$ lies on $\Gamma$, on the line $ZF$, on the other side of $Z$ than $F$, with $ZQ'=2\,ZF$. Also $h(C)=M$, so $Z$ lies between $C$ and $M$ with $ZC=2\,ZM$.

$Z$ is inside $\Gamma$, so each line through $Z$ meets $\Gamma$ in two points, one on each side of $Z$. On line $ZF$ these are $Q'$ and $Q$ ($Q$ beyond $F$); on line $CM$ they are $C$ and $P$ ($P$ beyond $M$). By the power of the point $Z$, \[ZQ\cdot ZQ'=ZC\cdot ZP\quad\Longrightarrow\quad ZQ\cdot2\,ZF=2\,ZM\cdot ZP\quad\Longrightarrow\quad ZF\cdot ZQ=ZM\cdot ZP .\] Here $F,Q$ lie on one ray from $Z$ and $M,P$ on another, and the two rays are on different lines because $F\ne M$ (as $BC\ne AC$). By the converse of the power of a point theorem (the circle through $F,Q,M$ meets the ray $ZM$ again at the point $P'$ with $ZM\cdot ZP'=ZF\cdot ZQ=ZM\cdot ZP$, so $P'=P$), the points $F,M,P,Q$ are concyclic.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $M=(0,0)$, $A=(-1,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(f,h)$ with $h>0$; then $F=(f,0)$ and $Z=\bigl(\frac f3,\frac h3\bigr)$. The triangle is acute, so $-1<f<1$, and $BC>AC$ means $f<0$; in particular $f\ne0$, so $F\ne M$.

The circle $\Gamma$ is $x^2+y^2+Ey-1=0$ with $Eh=1-f^2-h^2$ (it passes through $A,B$, and through $C$). Points of the line $CM$ are $tC$; substituting gives $t^2(f^2+h^2)+tEh-1=0$, with roots $t=1$ (the point $C$) and, by Vieta, $t=-\frac1{f^2+h^2}$. So $P=-\frac{C}{f^2+h^2}$.

The circle $\omega$ through $M,F,P$ is $x^2+y^2-fx+\frac{1+f^2}{h}\,y=0$: it passes through $M$ and $F$ clearly, and at $P$ the value is $\frac{1}{f^2+h^2}+\frac{f^2}{f^2+h^2}-\frac{1+f^2}{f^2+h^2}=0$.

The line $ZF$. Its points beyond $F$ are $X(s)=F+s(F-Z)=\bigl(f(1+\tfrac{2s}3),\,-\tfrac{hs}3\bigr)$, $s>0$. Substituting into $\omega$ and dividing by $s/9$: \[(4f^2+h^2)s+3f^2-3=0,\qquad s_0=\frac{3(1-f^2)}{4f^2+h^2}>0 .\] Substituting into $\Gamma$ and multiplying by $9$: \[(4f^2+h^2)s^2+(15f^2+3h^2-3)s+9(f^2-1)=0 .\] The product of its roots is $\frac{9(f^2-1)}{4f^2+h^2}<0$, so it has exactly one positive root, which gives $Q$. With $K=4f^2+h^2$ and $u=1-f^2$, the value at $s_0$ is \[\frac{9u^2}{K}+\frac{3u(15f^2+3h^2-3)}{K}-9u=\frac{3u}{K}\bigl(3u+15f^2+3h^2-3-3K\bigr)=0,\] so $s_0$ is that root and $Q=X(s_0)$ lies on $\omega$. Hence $F,M,P,Q$ are concyclic.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

Consider the sequence $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ defined by \[(a_{n-1})^2=a_{n-2}a_n-2\] for all integers $n\ge 2$, with $a_0=a_1=1$. Prove that all terms of the sequence are integers.

Show the answer

proof (in fact $a_{n+1}=4a_n-a_{n-1}$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A hidden linear recurrence)

By induction all terms are positive: $a_n=\frac{a_{n-1}^2+2}{a_{n-2}}>0$. For $n\ge2$ we have \[a_{n-1}^2+2=a_{n-2}a_n\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_n^2+2=a_{n-1}a_{n+1}.\] Subtracting, $a_n^2-a_{n-1}^2=a_{n-1}a_{n+1}-a_{n-2}a_n$, i.e. $a_n(a_n+a_{n-2})=a_{n-1}(a_{n+1}+a_{n-1})$, so \[\frac{a_{n+1}+a_{n-1}}{a_n}=\frac{a_n+a_{n-2}}{a_{n-1}}\qquad(n\ge2).\] This ratio is therefore constant, equal to $\frac{a_2+a_0}{a_1}=\frac{3+1}{1}=4$ (as $a_2=\frac{1+2}{1}=3$). Hence $a_{n+1}=4a_n-a_{n-1}$ for all $n\ge1$, and since $a_0=a_1=1$ are integers, all terms are integers by induction ($1,1,3,11,41,153,\dots$).

Solution 2 (Start from the integer sequence)

Define integers $b_n$ by $b_0=b_1=1$ and $b_{n+1}=4b_n-b_{n-1}$. By induction $b_n\ge b_{n-1}>0$: indeed $b_{n+1}-b_n=3b_n-b_{n-1}>0$.

Let $D_n=b_n^2-b_{n-1}b_{n+1}$ for $n\ge1$. Then \[D_{n+1}-D_n=b_{n+1}(b_{n+1}+b_{n-1})-b_n(b_{n+2}+b_n)=b_{n+1}\cdot4b_n-b_n\cdot4b_{n+1}=0,\] so $D_n=D_1=1-1\cdot3=-2$ for all $n$, i.e. $b_{n-1}^2=b_{n-2}b_n-2$ for all $n\ge2$. Thus $(b_n)$ satisfies the given relation with the same starting values.

The given relation determines $a_n=\frac{a_{n-1}^2+2}{a_{n-2}}$ uniquely from the two previous terms (the divisor is never zero). Hence $a_n=b_n$ for all $n$ by induction, and all $a_n$ are integers.
