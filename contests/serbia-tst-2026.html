Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

The double sequence $\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}$ is defined by $a_{1,1}=1$ and \[a_{n,m}=\sum_{\substack{k\mid n,\ l\mid m\\ (k,l)\ne(n,m)}}a_{k,l}.\] Prove that for every two positive integers $m$ and $n$, there exists a positive integer $t$ such that $a_{n,m}=a_{1,t}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pairs of divisors are the divisors of one number)

The recurrence defines $a_{n,m}$ uniquely, by induction on $nm$. In particular, for $n=1$ it reads \[ a_{1,T}=\sum_{e\mid T,\ e\ne T}a_{1,e}\qquad(T>1). \tag{1} \]

Fix $n$ and $m$ and let $m=q_1^{\beta_1}\cdots q_v^{\beta_v}$ be the prime factorisation of $m$. Choose distinct primes $r_1,\dots,r_v$ that do not divide $n$. For a divisor $l=q_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots q_v^{\gamma_v}$ of $m$ put \[ l'=r_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots r_v^{\gamma_v}. \] Then $l\mapsto l'$ is a bijection from the divisors of $m$ to the divisors of $m'=r_1^{\beta_1}\cdots r_v^{\beta_v}$, and $l_1\mid l$ if and only if $l_1'\mid l'$.

Claim. $a_{k,l}=a_{1,\,kl'}$ for all $k\mid n$ and $l\mid m$.

We prove it by induction on $kl$. For $k=l=1$ both sides are $a_{1,1}$. Let $(k,l)\ne(1,1)$ and assume the claim for all pairs $(k_1,l_1)\ne(k,l)$ with $k_1\mid k$, $l_1\mid l$. Since $\gcd(k,l')=1$, every divisor of $kl'$ can be written in exactly one way as $k_1l_1'$ with $k_1\mid k$ and $l_1\mid l$. So $(k_1,l_1)\mapsto k_1l_1'$ is a bijection from the pairs occurring in the recurrence for $a_{k,l}$ onto the divisors of $kl'$ other than $kl'$ itself. Therefore \[ a_{k,l}=\sum_{\substack{k_1\mid k,\ l_1\mid l\\(k_1,l_1)\ne(k,l)}}a_{k_1,l_1} =\sum_{\substack{k_1\mid k,\ l_1\mid l\\(k_1,l_1)\ne(k,l)}}a_{1,\,k_1l_1'} =\sum_{e\mid kl',\ e\ne kl'}a_{1,e}=a_{1,\,kl'}, \] where the last step is (1). This proves the claim.

For $k=n$, $l=m$ we get $a_{n,m}=a_{1,t}$ with $t=n\,m'=n\,r_1^{\beta_1}\cdots r_v^{\beta_v}$.

Solution 2 (An explicit formula)

For $k\ge1$ let $d_k(n)$ be the number of ways to write $n$ as an ordered product of $k$ positive integers, and let $d_0(1)=1$, $d_0(n)=0$ for $n>1$. We need three facts.

  • (i) $d_{k+1}(n)=\sum_{e\mid n}d_k(e)$ for $k\ge0$ (choose the product $e$ of the first $k$ factors; the last factor is $n/e$).

  • (ii) If $n=p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_u^{\alpha_u}$ and $k\ge1$, then $d_k(n)=\prod_i\binom{\alpha_i+k-1}{k-1}$ (distribute the exponent of each prime over the $k$ factors). So $d_k(n)$ depends only on the exponents $\alpha_i$, and $d_k(xy)=d_k(x)d_k(y)$ when $\gcd(x,y)=1$; both statements also hold for $k=0$.

  • (iii) $d_k(n)\le k^{\alpha_1+\dots+\alpha_u}$, a polynomial bound in $k$, so the series below converge.

Formula. For all $n,m$, \[ a_{n,m}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d_k(n)\,d_k(m)}{2^{k+1}} . \tag{2} \] Proof. Call the right side $b_{n,m}$. First, $b_{1,1}=\sum_{k\ge0}2^{-k-1}=1$. Next, summing over all $e\mid n$, $f\mid m$ and using (i) twice, \[ \sum_{e\mid n,\ f\mid m}b_{e,f}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{d_{k+1}(n)\,d_{k+1}(m)}{2^{k+1}} =2\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{d_k(n)\,d_k(m)}{2^{k+1}}=2b_{n,m}-d_0(n)d_0(m). \] For $(n,m)\ne(1,1)$ the last term is $0$, so $\sum_{(e,f)\ne(n,m)}b_{e,f}=b_{n,m}$. Thus $b$ satisfies the recurrence and the initial value that define $a$, and $b=a$ by induction on $nm$. $\blacksquare$

Now let $m=q_1^{\beta_1}\cdots q_v^{\beta_v}$, choose distinct primes $r_1,\dots,r_v$ not dividing $n$, and put $m'=r_1^{\beta_1}\cdots r_v^{\beta_v}$ and $t=nm'$. By (ii), $d_k(m)=d_k(m')$ and $d_k(n)d_k(m')=d_k(t)$ for every $k\ge0$, and $d_k(1)=1$. Hence by (2) \[ a_{n,m}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{d_k(n)d_k(m')}{2^{k+1}}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{d_k(1)\,d_k(t)}{2^{k+1}}=a_{1,t}. \]

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Find all bounded sequences $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of positive integers such that for all positive integers $m$ and $n$, there exists a unique integer $i$, with $0\le i\le n-1$, such that $n\mid a_m+a_{m+1}+\dots+a_{m+i}$.

Show the answer

$a_1$ is an arbitrary positive integer and $a_n=1$ for all $n\ge2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Blocks of length a power of two)

Answer. $a_1=c$ is any positive integer and $a_n=1$ for all $n\ge2$.

Write $S(m,j)=a_m+a_{m+1}+\dots+a_{m+j-1}$ for the sum of $j$ consecutive terms starting at $a_m$. The condition says: for all $m,n$, exactly one of $S(m,1),S(m,2),\dots,S(m,n)$ is divisible by $n$.

The sequences work. If $m\ge2$, then $S(m,j)=j$ and only $j=n$ is divisible by $n$. If $m=1$, then $S(1,j)=c+j-1$ for $j=1,\dots,n$; these are $n$ consecutive integers, so exactly one is divisible by $n$.

Step 1: a claim about blocks. For every $k\ge0$ and every $l\ge2^k$: $2^k\mid S(l,2^k)$, and none of $S(l,1),\dots,S(l,2^k-1)$ is divisible by $2^k$.

The second part follows from the first by the uniqueness in the condition (with $n=2^k$, $m=l$). We prove the first part by induction on $k$; $k=0$ is trivial. Assume the claim for $k$.

(a) For $l\ge2^{k+1}$ the number $S(l,2^k)$ is divisible by $2^k$ but not by $2^{k+1}$. Let $m=l-2^k\ge2^k$, $A=S(m,2^k)$, $B=S(l,2^k)$; both are divisible by $2^k$. Apply the condition to $m$ and $n=2^{k+1}$. A sum $S(m,j)$ with $j\le2^{k+1}$ that is divisible by $2^{k+1}$ is divisible by $2^k$. For $j\le2^k$ this forces $j=2^k$ (claim for $m$). For $j=2^k+i$ with $1\le i\le2^k$ we have $S(m,j)=A+S(l,i)$, so $2^k\mid S(l,i)$ and this forces $i=2^k$ (claim for $l$). So the only candidates are $A$ and $A+B$, and exactly one of them is divisible by $2^{k+1}$. If $2^{k+1}\mid B$, then $A$ and $A+B$ would be both divisible or both not divisible by $2^{k+1}$. Hence $2^{k+1}\nmid B$.

(b) Let $l\ge2^{k+1}$. By (a), $S(l,2^k)$ and $S(l+2^k,2^k)$ are both odd multiples of $2^k$, so their sum $S(l,2^{k+1})$ is divisible by $2^{k+1}$. This is the claim for $k+1$.

Step 2: the sequence is eventually $1$. Let $a_n\le C$ for all $n$ and choose $k$ with $N=2^k>C$. For $l\ge N$, the numbers $S(l,N)$ and $S(l+1,N)$ are divisible by $N$, hence so is their difference $a_{l+N}-a_l$; as $|a_{l+N}-a_l|<N$, we get $a_{l+N}=a_l$ for all $l\ge N$. So from the index $N$ on the sequence has period $N$; let $\Sigma=S(l,N)$ $(l\ge N)$ be the sum over a period. By Step 1(a) (with $l\ge2N$), $\Sigma=N\cdot q$ with $q$ odd.

Suppose $q\ge3$. Take $n=\Sigma$ and $m=N$. Then $S(m,N)=\Sigma$, $S(m,2N)=2\Sigma$, …, $S(m,qN)=q\Sigma$ are all divisible by $n$, and $qN=\Sigma=n$, so these are $q\ge3$ sums among $S(m,1),\dots,S(m,n)$: a contradiction. Hence $q=1$: the $N$ positive integers of a period have sum $N$, so they are all $1$. Thus $a_n=1$ for all $n\ge N$.

Step 3: only $a_1$ may differ from $1$. Suppose some $a_l$ with $l\ge2$ is greater than $1$, and take the largest such $l$. Let $Z=a_1+\dots+a_l\ge3$, and apply the condition to $m=1$, $n=Z-1$. For $j<l$ we have $0<S(1,j)\le Z-a_l\le n-1$. For $j\ge l$ we have $S(1,j)=Z+(j-l)=n+1+(j-l)$, and for $j\le n$ this is at most $2n+1-l\le2n-1$. So each of $S(1,1),\dots,S(1,n)$ lies strictly between $0$ and $n$ or strictly between $n$ and $2n$; none is divisible by $n$. This contradicts the condition.

Hence $a_n=1$ for all $n\ge2$, and $a_1$ is arbitrary.

Solution 2 (Halving the sequence)

Answer. $a_1=c$ is any positive integer and $a_n=1$ for all $n\ge2$.

Let $S(m,j)=a_m+\dots+a_{m+j-1}$. A sequence of positive integers is called good if it is bounded and for all $m,n$ exactly one of $S(m,1),\dots,S(m,n)$ is divisible by $n$. The sequences $(c,1,1,1,\dots)$ are good: for $m\ge2$ the sums $S(m,j)=j$, $j\le n$, are divisible by $n$ only for $j=n$; for $m=1$ the sums $S(1,j)=c+j-1$, $j\le n$, are $n$ consecutive integers, exactly one of which is divisible by $n$. We show there are no other good sequences. Note that a good sequence stays good when its first term is deleted.

Step 1: $a_i$ is odd for $i\ge2$. Take $n=2$, $m=i-1$: exactly one of $a_{i-1}$ and $a_{i-1}+a_i$ is even, so $a_i$ is odd.

Step 2: halving. Let $(x_n)$ be a good sequence all of whose terms are odd. Then $y_n=\tfrac12(x_{2n-1}+x_{2n})$ is a good sequence.

The $y_n$ are positive integers and bounded. Fix $m,n$. In the sequence $x$ consider the sums starting at $x_{2m-1}$ and the modulus $2n$: exactly one of the first $2n$ sums is divisible by $2n$. A sum of an odd number of odd terms is odd, so that sum has an even number $2j$ of terms, $1\le j\le n$, and it equals $2(y_m+\dots+y_{m+j-1})$. Thus the sums of $x$ with an even number $2j$ of terms that are divisible by $2n$ correspond exactly to the indices $j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $n\mid y_m+\dots+y_{m+j-1}$. There is exactly one such $j$. So $y$ is good.

Step 3: a last large term is impossible. If $a$ is good, $l\ge2$, $a_l>1$ and $a_i=1$ for all $i>l$, we have a contradiction. Let $Z=a_1+\dots+a_l$, $n=Z-1\ge2$, $m=1$. For $j<l$: $0<S(1,j)\le Z-a_l<n$. For $l\le j\le n$: $n<S(1,j)=n+1+(j-l)\le2n-1$. So none of $S(1,1),\dots,S(1,n)$ is divisible by $n$.

Step 4: descent on the maximum. For a good sequence $a$ let $\mu(a)=\max_{i\ge2}a_i$. Suppose some good sequence has $\mu\ge2$, and choose a good sequence $a$ with $\mu(a)=M\ge2$ as small as possible. By Step 1, $M\ge3$. Let $c_i=a_{i+1}$; then $c$ is good, all its terms are odd and its largest term is $M$.

Two consecutive terms of $c$ cannot both equal $M$: if $c_p=c_{p+1}=M$, then for $m=p$ and $n=M$ the sums $S(p,1)=M$ and $S(p,2)=2M$ are both divisible by $n$.

Let $d_i=\tfrac12(c_{2i-1}+c_{2i})$. By Step 2, $d$ is good, and every $d_i$ is less than $M$. So $\mu(d)<M$, and by the choice of $M$ we get $\mu(d)\le1$: $d_i=1$ for all $i\ge2$. Then $c_{2i-1}=c_{2i}=1$ for $i\ge2$, i.e. $a_i=1$ for all $i\ge4$. Since $\mu(a)=M\ge3$, one of $a_2,a_3$ is greater than $1$; taking the larger such index as $l$, Step 3 gives a contradiction.

Hence every good sequence has $a_i=1$ for all $i\ge2$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 24: Cevians, Menelaus, and Projective-Flavored Ratios

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with altitudes $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$, and let $H$ be its orthocenter. The circle with diameter $AC$ intersects the altitude $BE$, outside triangle $ABC$, at the point $K$. Similarly, the circle with diameter $AB$ intersects the altitude $CF$, outside triangle $ABC$, at the point $L$. The circumcircle of triangle $DKL$ intersects the line $BC$ again at the point $G$. Prove that the line $BC$ is tangent to the circumcircle of triangle $GEF$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A circle centred at A, its polar structure, and two harmonic divisions)

Assume first $AB\ne AC$; the case $AB=AC$ is treated at the end. Let $X$ be the intersection of the lines $EF$ and $BC$.

Tools. Let $\omega$ be a circle with centre $A$ and radius $\varrho$. For a point $P\ne A$, the polar of $P$ with respect to $\omega$ is the line of all points $Y$ with $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AY}=\varrho^2$; it is perpendicular to $AP$. The condition is symmetric in $P$ and $Y$, so $Y$ lies on the polar of $P$ if and only if $P$ lies on the polar of $Y$. For four points $P,Q,R,S$ on a line, with signed lengths, the cross ratio is $(P,Q;R,S)=\dfrac{\overline{RP}}{\overline{RQ}}:\dfrac{\overline{SP}}{\overline{SQ}}$; the four points form a harmonic division if $(P,Q;R,S)=-1$. A central projection from a point onto another line preserves cross ratios. We use three standard facts.

Fact 1 (chord and polar). If a line through $P\ne A$ meets $\omega$ at $U,V$, then a point $Q$ of this line lies on the polar of $P$ if and only if $(U,V;P,Q)=-1$. Proof. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $UV$, so $AM\perp UV$, and use coordinates on the line with origin $M$: $U=-u$, $V=u$, $P=p$, $Q=q$. Then $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AQ}=AM^2+pq$ and $\varrho^2=AM^2+u^2$, so $Q$ is on the polar of $P$ if and only if $pq=u^2$. On the other hand $(U,V;P,Q)=\dfrac{(p+u)(q-u)}{(p-u)(q+u)}$, which equals $-1$ if and only if $2pq=2u^2$. $\square$

Fact 2 (Brokard's theorem: the diagonal triangle is self-polar). Let $K,K',L,L'$ be four points of $\omega$ such that $H=KK'\cap LL'$ lies inside $\omega$ and $W=KL\cap K'L'$, $G_0=KL'\cap K'L$ exist. Then the polar of $H$ is the line $WG_0$, and the polar of $W$ is the line $HG_0$. Proof. As the chords $KK'$ and $LL'$ cross, the points $K$ and $K'$ separate $L$ and $L'$ on $\omega$, so $W$ and $G_0$ are intersections of lines of opposite sides of a convex inscribed quadrilateral; they lie outside the segments $KL,K'L',KL',K'L$, outside $\omega$. Let $P_1$ and $P_2$ be the harmonic conjugates of $W$ with respect to $K,L$ and to $K',L'$; by Fact 1 the polar of $W$ is the line $P_1P_2$ (they are different points, as the lines $KL$ and $K'L'$ meet only at $W$). The projection from $G_0$ of the line $KL$ onto the line $K'L'$ sends $K\mapsto L'$, $L\mapsto K'$, $W\mapsto W$, hence $P_1\mapsto P_2$; so $G_0$ lies on $P_1P_2$. The projection from $H$ sends $K\mapsto K'$, $L\mapsto L'$, $W\mapsto W$, hence again $P_1\mapsto P_2$; so $H$ lies on $P_1P_2$. Thus the polar of $W$ is $HG_0$. In the same way, with the chords $KK'$, $LL'$ through $H$ and projections from $W$ and from $G_0$, the polar of $H$ is $WG_0$. $\square$

Fact 3 (harmonic property of a complete quadrangle) will be used for two quadrangles; in each case we prove it directly in Step 3.

Step 1: a circle centred at $A$. The points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, and $C,D,H,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $CH$. Hence \[ AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC=AH\cdot AD=:\varrho^2 . \] In the right triangle $AKC$ (right angle at $K$) the segment $KE$ is the altitude, so $AK^2=AE\cdot AC=\varrho^2$; similarly $AL^2=AF\cdot AB=\varrho^2$. Let $K'$ be the reflection of $K$ in the line $AC$ and $L'$ the reflection of $L$ in the line $AB$. Then $K'$ lies on the line $BE$, $L'$ lies on the line $CF$, and \[ K,\ K',\ L,\ L'\ \text{lie on the circle }\omega\text{ with centre }A\text{ and radius }\varrho . \] The chords $KK'$ and $LL'$ meet at $H$, and $H$ is inside $\omega$ because $AH<AD$ and $AH\cdot AD=\varrho^2$. For a point $Y$ of $BC$ we have $\overrightarrow{AH}\cdot\overrightarrow{AY}=\overrightarrow{AH}\cdot\overrightarrow{AD}=AH\cdot AD=\varrho^2$, as $DY\perp AH$. So the line $BC$ is the polar of $H$ with respect to $\omega$.

Step 2: the points $W$ and $G$. Let $W=KL\cap K'L'$ and $G_0=KL'\cap K'L$. These lines are not parallel. Indeed, if $KL\parallel K'L'$, the reflection in the common perpendicular bisector of these two chords (a line through $A$) exchanges $K$ with $L$ and $K'$ with $L'$, hence the line $KK'$ with the line $LL'$; so it fixes $H$, its axis is the line $AH=AD$, and it exchanges the lines $BE=KK'$ and $CF=LL'$ while fixing the line $BC\perp AD$. Then it exchanges $B$ and $C$, and $AB=AC$, which is excluded. The case $KL'\parallel K'L$ is the same, with $L$ and $L'$ exchanged.

By Fact 2 the polar $BC$ of $H$ is the line $WG_0$, so $W$ and $G_0$ lie on $BC$; and $G_0$ lies on the polar of $W$, which means $\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}=\varrho^2$.

Put coordinates on the line $BC$ with $D=0$, $B=-b$, $C=c$ $(b,c>0,\ b\ne c)$, $W=w$, $G_0=g$. As $AD\perp BC$, $\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}=AD^2+wg$, so \[ wg=\varrho^2-AD^2=AH\cdot AD-AD^2=-AD\cdot HD=-bc , \tag{1} \] because $DB\cdot DC=DH\cdot DA$ (the right triangles $BDH$ and $ADC$ are similar). In particular $W\ne D$ and $G_0\ne D$. Now, using $AD\perp BC$ once more, \[ \overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG_0}=\overrightarrow{WA}\cdot\overrightarrow{WG_0}=WA^2-\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}=WA^2-\varrho^2=\overline{WK}\cdot\overline{WL}, \] the last product being the power of $W$ with respect to $\omega$. The circle $DKL$ meets the line $BC$ at $D$ and at a second point $G'$ ($G'=D$ if it is tangent), and the power of $W$ with respect to it is $\overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG'}=\overline{WK}\cdot\overline{WL}=\overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG_0}$. As $W\ne D$, $G'=G_0$; as $G_0\ne D$, the circle is not tangent to $BC$, and $G=G_0$. So $g$ is the coordinate of $G$.

Step 3: two harmonic divisions. First, $(W,G;B,C)=-1$. Project the line $BC$ from $K'$ onto the line $LL'$: $W\mapsto L'$ (as $W\in K'L'$), $G\mapsto L$ (as $G\in K'L$), $B\mapsto H$ (as $K'B$ is the line $KK'$), $C\mapsto C$. Since $C$ lies on the polar $BC$ of $H$ and $LL'$ is a chord through $H$, Fact 1 gives $(L,L';H,C)=-1$, hence $(L',L;H,C)=-1$ and $(W,G;B,C)=-1$. (This is the harmonic property of the complete quadrangle $KK'LL'$.) In coordinates, $\dfrac{-b-w}{c-w}=-\dfrac{-b-g}{c-g}$, or \[ 2wg+(b-c)(w+g)-2bc=0 . \] With (1) this gives $\dfrac{w+g}{2}=\dfrac{2bc}{b-c}$.

Second, $(B,C;D,X)=-1$ (the harmonic property of the complete quadrangle $AFHE$). Indeed, Ceva's theorem for the cevians $AD,BE,CF$ and Menelaus' theorem for the line $XEF$ in triangle $ABC$ give \[ \frac{DB}{DC}\cdot\frac{EC}{EA}\cdot\frac{FA}{FB}=1=\frac{XB}{XC}\cdot\frac{EC}{EA}\cdot\frac{FA}{FB}, \] so $\dfrac{DB}{DC}=\dfrac{XB}{XC}$, with $D$ inside and $X$ outside the segment $BC$. With $X=x$ this reads $\dfrac{x+b}{x-c}=\dfrac bc$, so $x=\dfrac{2bc}{b-c}$.

Thus $X$ is the midpoint of $WG$.

Step 4: tangency. By Step 3 and (1), \[ XG^2=\Bigl(\frac{g-w}{2}\Bigr)^2=\Bigl(\frac{g+w}{2}\Bigr)^2-gw=x^2+bc,\qquad \overline{XB}\cdot\overline{XC}=(x+b)(x-c)=x^2+(b-c)x-bc=x^2+bc . \] Since $B,C,E,F$ are concyclic and $X$ lies on $BC$ and on $EF$, we get $XG^2=\overline{XB}\cdot\overline{XC}=\overline{XE}\cdot\overline{XF}$. By the converse of the tangent-secant theorem, $XG$ is tangent to the circle $GEF$. So $BC$ is tangent to the circumcircle of $GEF$ at $G$.

The case $AB=AC$. The figure is symmetric in $AD$, the circle $DKL$ touches $BC$ at $D$, so $G=D$, and the circle $DEF$ (the nine-point circle) is symmetric in $AD$ and passes through $D$; hence it touches $BC$ at $D$.

Solution 2 (Brokard, an orthocentre, and the Newton-Gauss line)

Assume first $AB\ne AC$; the case $AB=AC$ is treated at the end. Vectors and dot products are used freely; for a circle $\Gamma$ with diameter $UV$ and any point $Q$, the power of $Q$ with respect to $\Gamma$ equals $\overrightarrow{QU}\cdot\overrightarrow{QV}$.

Step 1: the circle $\omega$. The points $B,C,E,F$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$, and $C,D,H,E$ on the circle with diameter $CH$, so \[ AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC=AH\cdot AD=:\varrho^2 . \] The circle with diameter $AC$ is symmetric in the line $AC$, and $BE\perp AC$; so it meets the line $BE$ at $K$ and at the reflection $K'$ of $K$ in $AC$, and $E$ is the midpoint of $KK'$. In the right triangle $AKC$ the segment $KE$ is the altitude, so $AK^2=AE\cdot AC=\varrho^2$. In the same way the circle with diameter $AB$ meets $CF$ at $L$ and $L'$, $F$ is the midpoint of $LL'$, and $AL^2=\varrho^2$. So $K,K',L,L'$ lie on the circle $\omega$ with centre $A$ and radius $\varrho$; the chords $KK'$ and $LL'$ meet at $H$, which lies inside $\omega$ because $AH<AD$ and $AH\cdot AD=\varrho^2$.

For $P\ne A$, the polar of $P$ with respect to $\omega$ is the line of points $Y$ with $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AY}=\varrho^2$; it is perpendicular to $AP$. For $Y$ on $BC$, $\overrightarrow{AH}\cdot\overrightarrow{AY}=AH\cdot AD=\varrho^2$ (as $DY\perp AH$), so the polar of $H$ is the line $BC$.

Step 2: Brokard's theorem. Let $W=KL\cap K'L'$ and $G_0=KL'\cap K'L$. These lines are not parallel: if, say, $KL\parallel K'L'$, then the reflection in the common perpendicular bisector of these chords (a line through $A$) exchanges $K$ with $L$ and $K'$ with $L'$, so it exchanges the lines $KK'=BE$ and $LL'=CF$ and fixes $H$; its axis is then the line $AH=AD$, it fixes the line $BC\perp AD$, and it exchanges $B=BE\cap BC$ and $C=CF\cap BC$, so $AB=AC$. The case $KL'\parallel K'L$ is the same with $L,L'$ exchanged.

By Brokard's theorem (for four points on a circle the diagonal triangle $HWG_0$ is self-polar; a proof is given in Solution 1, Fact 2), the polar of $H$ is the line $WG_0$, and the polar of $W$ is the line $HG_0$. Hence:

  • $W$ and $G_0$ lie on $BC$, the polar of $H$;

  • $G_0$ lies on the polar of $W$, so $\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}=\varrho^2$ and $HG_0\perp AW$.

Step 3: $G_0=G$. As $AD\perp BC$ and $W,G_0\in BC$, \[ \overline{DW}\cdot\overline{DG_0}=\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}-AD^2=AH\cdot AD-AD^2=-AD\cdot HD<0 . \] So $W$ and $G_0$ are different from $D$ and lie on different sides of $D$; in particular $W\ne G_0$. Moreover \[ \overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG_0}=\overrightarrow{WA}\cdot\overrightarrow{WG_0}=WA^2-\overrightarrow{AW}\cdot\overrightarrow{AG_0}=WA^2-\varrho^2=\overline{WK}\cdot\overline{WL}. \] The circle $DKL$ meets $BC$ at $D$ and at a second point $G'$ ($G'=D$ in the tangent case), and the power of $W$ gives $\overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG'}=\overline{WK}\cdot\overline{WL}=\overline{WD}\cdot\overline{WG_0}$. Since $W\ne D$, $G'=G_0$; since $G_0\ne D$, this is the second intersection: $G=G_0$.

Step 4: $G$ is the orthocentre of triangle $WHA$. This triangle is not degenerate, because $W\in BC$, $W\ne D$, so $W$ is not on the line $AH=AD$. The line $WG$ is $BC$, which is perpendicular to $AH$; and $HG\perp AW$ by Step 2. So two altitudes of triangle $WHA$ pass through $G$, and $G$ is its orthocentre. With the origin at $G$ and vectors $\mathbf a,\mathbf h,\mathbf w$ for $A,H,W$, the two perpendicularities read $\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf a-\mathbf h)=0$ and $\mathbf h\cdot(\mathbf a-\mathbf w)=0$, so \[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf h=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf w=\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf w . \tag{1} \]

Step 5: the midpoint of $WG$ lies on $EF$ (Newton–Gauss line). The four lines $KL$, $K'L'$, $KL'$, $K'L$ form a complete quadrilateral: any two of them meet (at one of $K,K',L,L'$, or at $W$ or $G$ by Step 2), and no three pass through a point, as no three of $K,K',L,L'$ are collinear. Its six vertices form three pairs of opposite vertices (vertices not on a common line): $W$ and $G$, $K$ and $K'$, $L$ and $L'$. By the Newton–Gauss line theorem, the midpoints of the three segments $WG$, $KK'$, $LL'$ are collinear. The last two midpoints are $E$ and $F$ (Step 1). So the midpoint $P$ of $WG$ lies on the line $EF$; it also lies on $BC$, and $P\ne G$.

Step 6: a power computation. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle with diameter $AH$; it passes through $E$ and $F$ because $\angle AEH=\angle AFH=90^\circ$. With the origin at $G$, the point $P$ is $\tfrac12\mathbf w$, and by (1) \[ \operatorname{pow}(P,\Gamma)=\Bigl(\mathbf a-\tfrac12\mathbf w\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\mathbf h-\tfrac12\mathbf w\Bigr)=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf h-\tfrac12(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf w+\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf w)+\tfrac14|\mathbf w|^2=\tfrac14|\mathbf w|^2=PG^2 . \] As $P$ lies on the line $EF$, this power also equals $\overline{PE}\cdot\overline{PF}$. Hence $\overline{PE}\cdot\overline{PF}=PG^2>0$.

Step 7: tangency. The point $G$ is not on the line $EF$: otherwise $G$ and $P$ would be two different common points of the lines $EF$ and $BC$, which are different lines. So the circle $GEF$ exists. It meets the line $BC=PG$ at $G$ and at a second point $G''$ ($G''=G$ in the tangent case), and the power of $P$ with respect to it is $\overline{PG}\cdot\overline{PG''}=\overline{PE}\cdot\overline{PF}=PG^2$. Hence $\overline{PG''}=\overline{PG}$, $G''=G$, and $BC$ is tangent to the circle $GEF$ at $G$.

The case $AB=AC$. The figure is symmetric in $AD$, the circle $DKL$ touches $BC$ at $D$, so $G=D$, and the circle $DEF$ (the nine-point circle) is symmetric in $AD$ and passes through $D$; hence it touches $BC$ at $D$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABCD$ be a cyclic quadrilateral with circumcircle $\Omega$. A point $E$ lies on $\Omega$ such that $AE=AD$. Let $F$ be the intersection of the lines $BE$ and $CD$. The line $AF$ intersects $\Omega$ again at the point $G$. The line through $F$ parallel to $DE$ intersects the line $BC$ at the point $H$. Prove that the line $GH$ is tangent to $\Omega$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The point H has equal power to the circle and to the point F)

We use directed angles between lines modulo $180^\circ$ and assume the figure is not degenerate (in particular $F$ does not lie on $\Omega$). Let $O$ and $R$ be the centre and radius of $\Omega$, and let $\ell$ be the line $FH$.

Step 1: $HF^2=HB\cdot HC$. Since $FH\parallel DE$ and $B,C,D,E$ are concyclic, \[ \angle(FH,FB)=\angle(ED,EB)=\angle(CD,CB)=\angle(CF,CB). \] By the converse of the tangent-chord theorem, $FH$ is tangent to the circle $FBC$ at $F$. Hence $HF^2=\overline{HB}\cdot\overline{HC}$, which is the power of $H$ with respect to $\Omega$: \[ HF^2=HO^2-R^2. \tag{1} \]

Step 2: the tangent at $G$. As $AD=AE$, the radius $OA$ is perpendicular to $DE$, so the tangent $t_A$ to $\Omega$ at $A$ is parallel to $DE$ and to $\ell$. Let $t_G$ be the tangent at $G$. The tangents at the two ends of the chord $AG$ make equal angles with it: $\angle(t_A,AG)=\angle(AG,t_G)$. Hence \[ \angle(\ell,FG)=\angle(FG,t_G). \tag{2} \] $t_G$ is not parallel to $\ell$. Otherwise $t_G\parallel t_A$, $AG$ is a diameter, $F$ lies on the line $OA$ and $\ell\perp OF$ at $F$. Then for every point $P$ of $\ell$ we have $PO^2=PF^2+OF^2$, so $PF^2-(PO^2-R^2)=R^2-OF^2\ne0$, which contradicts (1) for $P=H$.

So $t_G$ meets $\ell$ at a point $H'$, and by (2) the triangle $H'FG$ has equal angles at $F$ and $G$: $H'F=H'G$. Since $H'G$ is a tangent, $H'G^2=H'O^2-R^2$. Thus \[ H'F^2=H'O^2-R^2. \tag{3} \]

Step 3: $H=H'$. By (1) and (3), both $H$ and $H'$ belong to the set of points $P$ with $PO^2-PF^2=R^2$. Here $O\ne F$ (otherwise the set would be empty), so this set is a line $r$ perpendicular to $OF$. The point $F$ is not on $r$ because $FO^2\ne R^2$. Hence $\ell\ne r$, and the two lines $\ell$ and $r$ have at most one common point. As $H$ and $H'$ are common points, $H=H'$. Therefore $H$ lies on the tangent $t_G$, i.e. $GH$ is tangent to $\Omega$.

Solution 2 (A cyclic quadrilateral through the second point of AH)

We use directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and assume the figure is not degenerate. Let the line $AH$ meet $\Omega$ again at $L$.

Step 1. From $FH\parallel DE$ and the circle $BCDE$, $\angle(FH,FB)=\angle(ED,EB)=\angle(CD,CB)=\angle(CF,CB)$, so $FH$ is tangent to the circle $FBC$ and \[ HF^2=\overline{HB}\cdot\overline{HC}=\overline{HL}\cdot\overline{HA}, \] the last product being the power of $H$ with respect to $\Omega$ computed on the line $HA$. By the converse of the tangent-secant theorem, $HF$ is tangent to the circle $AFL$ at $F$, hence \[ \angle(LA,LF)=\angle(FA,FH). \tag{1} \]

Step 2: $F,G,H,L$ are concyclic. The tangent $t_A$ to $\Omega$ at $A$ is parallel to $DE$ (because $AD=AE$), hence to $FH$. By the tangent-chord theorem in $\Omega$, \[ \angle(FH,FG)=\angle(t_A,AG)=\angle(LA,LG)=\angle(LH,LG). \] So $F$ and $L$ see the segment $HG$ under the same directed angle: $F,L,G,H$ are concyclic.

Step 3. From this circle and from (1), using that $A,F,G$ are collinear and $A,L,H$ are collinear, \[ \angle(GH,GF)=\angle(LH,LF)=\angle(LA,LF)=\angle(FA,FH)=\angle(FG,FH). \] So the triangle $HFG$ has equal angles at $G$ and $F$, and $HG=HF$. Then $HG^2=HF^2$ equals the power of $H$ with respect to $\Omega$, and $G$ lies on $\Omega$. If the line $HG$ met $\Omega$ at a second point $G'$, we would have $\overline{HG}\cdot\overline{HG'}=HG^2$, so $G'=G$. Hence $HG$ is tangent to $\Omega$.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Maja and Kosta play the following game. There are $2n$ holes in a row in front of them, numbered from $1$ to $2n$ from left to right. Initially, each hole with an odd number contains a hazelnut, while all other holes are empty. In one move, a player moves a hazelnut from some hole numbered $x$ to an empty hole numbered $y$ such that $y>x$. The players move alternately, and the player who cannot make a move loses. If Maja plays first, determine, depending on $n$, which player has a winning strategy.

Show the answer

Maja wins if $n\equiv1,2\pmod 4$; Kosta wins if $n\equiv0,3\pmod4$

Show the solution

Solution (A mirror strategy for Kosta and a reduction from n to n-2)

Answer. Maja has a winning strategy if $n\equiv1$ or $2\pmod4$, and Kosta has one if $n\equiv0$ or $3\pmod4$.

The game is finite, because every move increases the sum of the numbers of the occupied holes.

Kosta wins for $n\equiv0,3\pmod4$. Call hole $2n+1-i$ the mirror of hole $i$, and call a position symmetric if for every $i$ exactly one of the holes $i$ and $2n+1-i$ contains a hazelnut. The initial position is symmetric: $i$ is odd exactly when $2n+1-i$ is even. In a symmetric position let $c$ be the number of hazelnuts in the left half (holes $1,\dots,n$). At the start $c$ is the number of odd numbers up to $n$: $c=2k$ for $n=4k$ and $c=2k+2$ for $n=4k+3$. So $c$ is even.

Kosta keeps the following true before each move of Maja: the position is symmetric and $c$ is even. Suppose Maja moves a hazelnut from $i$ to $j$ $(i<j)$.

  • $i+j\ne2n+1$. Before her move hole $i$ was full and hole $j$ empty, so by symmetry hole $2n+1-i$ was empty and hole $2n+1-j$ full; these two holes are different from $i$ and $j$. Kosta moves the hazelnut from $2n+1-j$ to $2n+1-i$; this is a legal move since $2n+1-j<2n+1-i$. The position is symmetric again. Kosta's move crosses the middle (starts in the left half and ends in the right half) exactly when Maja's does, so $c$ changes by $0$ or by $-2$ and stays even.

  • $i+j=2n+1$. Then $i\le n<j$; the position is still symmetric after Maja's move, and $c$ has dropped by $1$, so $c$ is odd; in particular $c\ge1$. Kosta takes a hazelnut in some hole $k\le n$; by symmetry its mirror $2n+1-k>k$ is empty, and he moves the hazelnut there. The position remains symmetric and $c$ is even again.

So Kosta can always answer. Since the game is finite, Maja is the one who eventually cannot move.

Maja wins for $n\equiv1,2\pmod4$. For $n=1$ she moves the hazelnut from hole $1$ to hole $2$ and Kosta cannot move. For $n\ge2$ she moves the hazelnut from hole $1$ to hole $2n$. From now on:

  • holes $1$ and $2$ are empty and stay empty (hazelnuts only move to the right);

  • hole $2n$ stays full, so the hazelnut in hole $2n-1$ can never move, and no hazelnut can enter the holes $2n-1,2n$.

So the rest of the game is played in the holes $3,4,\dots,2n-2$ only, where the holes $3,5,\dots,2n-3$ are full and the others empty. After renumbering ($h\mapsto h-2$) this is the initial position of the game with $n-2$ in place of $n$, and it is Kosta's turn. Since $n-2\equiv3$ or $0\pmod4$ (for $n=2$ there are no holes left and Kosta cannot move), the player who moves second in this smaller game wins by the first part. That player is Maja.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

For an integer $x$, define $\operatorname{rad}(x)$ as the product of all distinct prime divisors of $x$, with $\operatorname{rad}(0)=0$, $\operatorname{rad}(1)=\operatorname{rad}(-1)=1$. Suppose that $P$ is a nonconstant monic polynomial with integer coefficients such that $\operatorname{rad}(P(n))\mid P(n^n)$ for every positive integer $n$. Determine all possible values of $P(0)$.

Show the answer

$P(0)\in\{-1,0,1\}$

Show the solution

Solution (All powers of a root are roots)

Answer. The possible values are $P(0)=\boxed{-1,\ 0,\ 1}$.

Examples. $P(x)=x$: $\operatorname{rad}(n)\mid n^n$. $P(x)=x-1$: for $n\ge2$, $\operatorname{rad}(n-1)\mid n-1\mid n^n-1$; for $n=1$, $\operatorname{rad}(0)=0$ divides $P(1)=0$. $P(x)=(x-1)^2$: $\operatorname{rad}((n-1)^2)=\operatorname{rad}(n-1)$ divides $n^n-1$, hence $(n^n-1)^2$. These have $P(0)=0,-1,1$.

Now let $P$ satisfy the condition. Note what it says for a prime $p$: if $p\mid P(n)$, then $p\mid P(n^n)$. (If $P(n)\ne0$, then $p\mid\operatorname{rad}(P(n))\mid P(n^n)$; if $P(n)=0$, then $0\mid P(n^n)$ means $P(n^n)=0$.)

Step 1. If a prime $p$ divides $P(n)$ for a positive integer $n$, then $p\mid P(n^l)$ for every $l\ge1$.

If $p\mid n$, then $n^l\equiv n\pmod p$ and $P(n^l)\equiv P(n)\equiv0$. Let $p\nmid n$. For $k\ge0$ put $N=n+kp$. Then $P(N)\equiv P(n)\equiv0\pmod p$, so $p\mid P(N^N)$. Now $N^N\equiv n^{\,n+kp}=n^{\,n+k}\cdot n^{k(p-1)}\equiv n^{\,n+k}\pmod p$ by Fermat's theorem, hence $p\mid P(n^{\,n+k})$ for all $k\ge0$. Given $l$, choose $k\ge0$ with $n+k\equiv l\pmod{p-1}$; then $n^{\,n+k}\equiv n^l\pmod p$ and $p\mid P(n^l)$.

Step 2. If $w$ is a complex root of $P$, then $P(w^l)=0$ for every $l\ge1$.

Let $R$ be the minimal polynomial of $w$ over $\mathbb Q$. It divides $P$, and since $P$ is monic with integer coefficients, Gauss's lemma shows that $R$ is monic with integer coefficients and $P=RS$ with $S\in\mathbb Z[x]$. Fix $l$ and let $Q(x)=P(x^l)$. Suppose $R\nmid Q$. As $R$ is irreducible, $R$ and $Q$ are coprime in $\mathbb Q[x]$, so there are $A,B\in\mathbb Z[x]$ and a positive integer $c$ with \[ A(x)Q(x)+B(x)R(x)=c . \] By Schur's theorem (see below) infinitely many primes divide some value $R(n)$ with $n$ a positive integer; take such a prime $p>c$ and such an $n$. Then $p\mid R(n)\mid P(n)$, so $p\mid P(n^l)=Q(n)$ by Step 1, and the identity at $x=n$ gives $p\mid c$, which is impossible. Hence $R\mid Q$, and $P(w^l)=Q(w)=0$.

Step 3. Let $w$ be a root of $P$. By Step 2 all the numbers $w,w^2,w^3,\dots$ are roots of $P$, which has finitely many roots; so $w^u=w^v$ for some $u<v$, and $w=0$ or $w^{v-u}=1$. Thus every root of $P$ has absolute value $0$ or $1$. Since $P$ is monic, $|P(0)|$ is the product of the absolute values of its roots, so $|P(0)|\in\{0,1\}$ and $P(0)\in\{-1,0,1\}$.

Schur's theorem. For a non-constant $R\in\mathbb Z[x]$, infinitely many primes divide a value $R(n)$, $n\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$. If $R(0)=0$, every prime $p$ divides $R(p)$. Let $R(0)=c_0\ne0$ and suppose only the primes $p_1,\dots,p_s$ occur; put $\Pi=p_1\cdots p_s$. Writing $R(x)=c_0+xR_1(x)$ we get $R(c_0\Pi t)=c_0\bigl(1+\Pi t\,R_1(c_0\Pi t)\bigr)=c_0\,T(t)$, where $T$ is a non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients and $T(t)\equiv1\pmod\Pi$. Choose an integer $t$ with $c_0t>0$ and $|T(t)|>1$ (all but finitely many $t$ satisfy the second condition). A prime factor of $T(t)$ is none of the $p_i$, and it divides $R(c_0\Pi t)$ with $c_0\Pi t$ a positive integer. Contradiction.
