Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

$a,b,c$ are pairwise distinct real numbers not equal to zero. If $a+\frac2b=b+\frac2c=c+\frac2a$, then find the value of $\left|a^2b+b^2c+c^2a\right|$.

Show the answer

$6\sqrt2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Eliminating with the common value)

Let $k$ be the common value, so $a+\frac2b=b+\frac2c=c+\frac2a=k$. Multiplying the three equations by $b$, $c$, $a$ respectively, \[ab=kb-2,\qquad bc=kc-2,\qquad ca=ka-2 .\] Finding $k$. Using these, \[abc=(ab)c=(kb-2)c=k\,bc-2c=k(kc-2)-2c=(k^2-2)c-2k,\] and cyclically $abc=(k^2-2)a-2k=(k^2-2)b-2k$. Since $a\ne c$, comparing the first two expressions forces $k^2=2$ (and then $abc=-2k$).

The cyclic sum. $a^2b=a\cdot ab=a(kb-2)=k\,ab-2a=k(kb-2)-2a=k^2b-2k-2a=2b-2a-2k$. Adding the three cyclic versions, \[a^2b+b^2c+c^2a=2(a+b+c)-2(a+b+c)-6k=-6k,\] so $|a^2b+b^2c+c^2a|=6|k|=6\sqrt2$.

Such numbers exist: $a=1$, $b=2+2\sqrt2$, $c=\sqrt2-2$ are distinct and nonzero, and $a+\frac2b=b+\frac2c=c+\frac2a=\sqrt2$.

The value is $\boxed{6\sqrt2}$.

Solution 2 (A Moebius map of order three)

Let $k$ be the common value. From $a+\frac2b=k$ we get $k-a=\frac2b\ne0$ and $b=\frac2{k-a}$; similarly $c=\frac2{k-b}$, $a=\frac2{k-c}$. So with $\mu(x)=\frac{2}{k-x}$ we have $b=\mu(a)$, $c=\mu(b)$, $a=\mu(c)$.

Composing. Linear fractional maps compose like their matrices; $\mu$ has matrix $M=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-1&k\end{pmatrix}$ and \[M^2=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2k\\-k&k^2-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad M^3=\begin{pmatrix}-2k&2k^2-4\\2-k^2&k^3-4k\end{pmatrix}.\] Hence $a=\mu(\mu(\mu(a)))=\frac{-2ka+2k^2-4}{(2-k^2)a+k^3-4k}$ (all intermediate values are defined, being $b$ and $c$). Clearing the denominator, \[(2-k^2)a^2+(k^3-2k)a+(4-2k^2)=0\iff (2-k^2)\,(a^2-ka+2)=0 .\] If $k^2\ne2$, then $a^2-ka+2=0$, i.e. $a(k-a)=2$, i.e. $\mu(a)=a$, so $b=a$, contradicting distinctness. Therefore $k^2=2$.

The cyclic sum. Multiply $a+\frac2b=k$ by $ab$: $a^2b+2a=kab$; adding the cyclic versions, $\sum a^2b=k(ab+bc+ca)-2(a+b+c)$. Multiply $a+\frac2b=k$ by $b$: $ab+2=kb$; adding, $ab+bc+ca=k(a+b+c)-6$. So \[a^2b+b^2c+c^2a=(k^2-2)(a+b+c)-6k=-6k,\] and $|a^2b+b^2c+c^2a|=6|k|=\boxed{6\sqrt2}$. (The example $a=1$, $b=2+2\sqrt2$, $c=\sqrt2-2$ with $k=\sqrt2$ shows the situation occurs.)

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Let $s(X)$ denote the sum of all numbers belonging to the set $X$. In how many ways can the numbers $2^1,2^2,\dots,2^{10}$ be split into two sets $A$ and $B$ such that the equation \[t^2-s(A)t+s(B)=0\] has at least one positive integer solution?

Show the answer

$1$ (the split $A=\{2,4,8,32,64\}$, $B=\{16,128,256,512,1024\}$); if $B=\varnothing$ were allowed there would be $2$

Show the solution

Solution (Vieta's formulas and the factorisation of 2047)

We read “split into two sets” in the usual way, as a partition into two nonempty sets. (Only $B=\varnothing$ would change the count: then $A$ is everything, the equation $t^2-2046t=0$ has the root $2046$, and the answer would be $2$; $A=\varnothing$ gives $t^2+2046=0$, which has no root.)

Note that $s(A)+s(B)=2+4+\dots+1024=2046$.

Let $t$ be a positive integer root. The other root is $t'=s(A)-t$, an integer, and $tt'=s(B)$. Then \[(t+1)(t'+1)=tt'+t+t'+1=s(B)+s(A)+1=2047=23\cdot89 .\] Since $t+1\ge2>0$, also $t'+1>0$, so $\{t+1,t'+1\}$ is $\{1,2047\}$ or $\{23,89\}$.

  • $\{1,2047\}$: as $t+1\ge2$, $t'=0$ and $s(B)=tt'=0$, i.e. $B=\varnothing$, which is not a split into two sets.

  • $\{23,89\}$: $s(A)=t+t'=22+88=110$ and $s(B)=22\cdot88=1936$; indeed $t^2-110t+1936=(t-22)(t-88)$.

Every subset of $\{2^1,\dots,2^{10}\}$ is determined by its sum (binary representation), and $110=2+4+8+32+64$. So the only split is $A=\{2,4,8,32,64\}$, $B=\{16,128,256,512,1024\}$, and the number of ways is $\boxed{1}$.

Remark. Equivalently, with $x=s(A)$ and $s(B)=2046-x$, a positive integer root $t$ satisfies $x(t+1)=t^2+2046$, i.e. $x=t-1+\frac{2047}{t+1}$; so $t+1\mid2047$, and $t\in\{22,88\}$ gives $x=110$, while $t=2046$ gives $x=2046$, i.e. $B=\varnothing$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

A convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ that is cyclic is given. Point $P$ is chosen on line $BC$ such that $CP=CA$ and point $B$ lies between $C$ and $P$. Point $Q$ is chosen on line $CD$ such that $CQ=CA$ and point $D$ lies between $C$ and $Q$. Prove that the incenter of triangle $ABD$ lies on line $PQ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two circles through the incentre)

Let $I$ be the incentre of $ABD$. We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ modulo $180^\circ$.

The circle centred at $K$. Let $K$ be the midpoint of the arc $AB$ of the circumcircle not containing $C$ and $D$. As $D$ lies on the other arc, the bisector $DI$ of $\angle ADB$ passes through $K$, and by the incentre lemma (trillium theorem) $KA=KB=KI$. As $C$ also lies on the other arc, $CK$ bisects $\angle ACB$. Since $B$ lies between $C$ and $P$, $\angle ACP=\angle ACB$, and in the isosceles triangle $ACP$ ($CA=CP$) the bisector of the apex angle is the perpendicular bisector of $AP$. So $KA=KP$. Hence \[A,\ B,\ I,\ P\ \text{lie on a circle with centre }K.\] The circle centred at $L$. In the same way, with $L$ the midpoint of the arc $AD$ not containing $B$ and $C$, the points $A$, $D$, $I$, $Q$ lie on a circle with centre $L$.

Collinearity. If $I=P$ or $I=Q$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, using the circle $(K)$ and that line $BP$ is line $BC$, \[\measuredangle(IP,IA)=\measuredangle(BP,BA)=\measuredangle(BC,BA),\] and using the circle $(L)$ and that line $DQ$ is line $DC$, \[\measuredangle(IA,IQ)=\measuredangle(DA,DQ)=\measuredangle(DA,DC).\] Adding, and using that $A,B,C,D$ are concyclic (so $\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\measuredangle(DC,DA)$), \[\measuredangle(IP,IQ)=\measuredangle(BC,BA)+\measuredangle(DA,DC)=\measuredangle(DC,DA)+\measuredangle(DA,DC)=0 .\] So the lines $IP$ and $IQ$ coincide: the incentre $I$ lies on line $PQ$.

Solution 2 (Vectors, Ptolemy and the law of sines)

Let $u$ and $v$ be the unit vectors from $C$ towards $B$ and towards $D$; they are not parallel. Then $P=C+CA\cdot u$ and $Q=C+CA\cdot v$, so a point $Z$ lies on line $PQ$ if and only if \[Z-C=s\,u+t\,v\qquad\text{with}\qquad s+t=CA .\tag{1}\] The incentre. With the side lengths of triangle $ABD$ as weights, $I=\dfrac{BD\cdot A+DA\cdot B+AB\cdot D}{AB+BD+DA}$, hence \[I-C=\frac{BD\,(A-C)+DA\,(B-C)+AB\,(D-C)}{AB+BD+DA},\qquad B-C=CB\,u,\quad D-C=CD\,v .\] Decomposing $A-C$. Write $A-C=x\,u+y\,v$. The triangle with vertices $C$, $C+xu$, $A$ has sides $x,y,CA$, with angle $\angle ACB$ at $C$, angle $\angle ACD$ at $A$ (alternate angles, since its side from $C+xu$ to $A$ is parallel to $v$), and angle $180^\circ-\angle BCD$ at $C+xu$. By the law of sines, \[x+y=CA\cdot\frac{\sin\angle ACD+\sin\angle ACB}{\sin\angle BCD}.\] As $ABCD$ is cyclic, $\angle ACD=\angle ABD$, $\angle ACB=\angle ADB$ and $\sin\angle BCD=\sin\angle BAD$; by the law of sines in triangle $ABD$, $\frac{\sin\angle ABD}{\sin\angle BAD}=\frac{AD}{BD}$ and $\frac{\sin\angle ADB}{\sin\angle BAD}=\frac{AB}{BD}$. Hence \[x+y=\frac{CA\,(AD+AB)}{BD}.\] Sum of the coefficients. The $u$- and $v$-coefficients of $I-C$ add up to \[\frac{BD\,(x+y)+DA\cdot CB+AB\cdot CD}{AB+BD+DA}=\frac{CA\,(AD+AB)+CA\cdot BD}{AB+BD+DA}=CA,\] where we used Ptolemy's theorem $AB\cdot CD+AD\cdot BC=AC\cdot BD$ for the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$. By (1), $I$ lies on line $PQ$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction

Let $A_n$ be the number of subsets of $\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ such that no two elements in the subset differ by exactly $1$. Let $B_n$ be the number of subsets of $\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ such that no two elements in the subset differ by exactly $2$. Prove that $A_{2025}<B_{1013}^2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Cutting the row into four blocks)

The numbers $A_n$ and $B_n$. Put $A_0=1$. A valid subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (no two elements differing by $1$) either avoids $n$ ($A_{n-1}$ choices) or contains $n$ and avoids $n-1$ ($A_{n-2}$ choices), so $A_n=A_{n-1}+A_{n-2}$ for $n\ge2$; in particular $A_n$ is increasing.

Two numbers differ by exactly $2$ only if they have the same parity and are neighbours among the numbers of that parity. The odd numbers of $\{1,\dots,1013\}$ are $1,3,\dots,1013$ ($507$ of them) and the even ones are $2,4,\dots,1012$ ($506$); choosing the odd part and the even part independently, each with no two neighbours, \[B_{1013}=A_{507}\,A_{506}.\] Cutting $\{1,\dots,2025\}$. Split it into four blocks of consecutive integers of lengths $507,506,506,506$ (note $507+3\cdot506=2025$). A valid subset of $\{1,\dots,2025\}$ meets each block in a valid subset of that block (a block of length $\ell$ behaves like $\{1,\dots,\ell\}$), and it is determined by these four parts. So $A_{2025}\le A_{507}A_{506}^3$. The inequality is strict: taking the last element $507$ of the first block and the first element $508$ of the second block gives four valid parts whose union is not valid. Finally $A_{506}\le A_{507}$, so \[A_{2025}<A_{507}A_{506}^3\le A_{507}^2A_{506}^2=B_{1013}^2 .\]

Solution 2 (Exponential bounds for Fibonacci numbers)

Identification. A valid subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (no two elements differing by $1$) either avoids $n$ or contains $n$ and avoids $n-1$; so $A_n=A_{n-1}+A_{n-2}$, with $A_1=2$, $A_2=3$. Hence $A_n=F_{n+2}$, where $F_1=F_2=1$, $F_{k+1}=F_k+F_{k-1}$. For $B_n$: a difference of exactly $2$ can only occur between neighbours of the same parity, and $\{1,\dots,1013\}$ has $507$ odd and $506$ even numbers, so $B_{1013}=A_{507}A_{506}=F_{509}F_{508}$. We must show $F_{2027}<(F_{508}F_{509})^2$.

Bounds. Let $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$, so $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$. We claim that for all $k\ge1$ \[\varphi^{k-2}\le F_k\le\varphi^{k-1},\qquad\text{with } F_k<\varphi^{k-1}\text{ for } k\ge3 .\] For $k=1,2$: $\varphi^{-1}\le F_1=1\le\varphi^{0}$ and $\varphi^{0}\le F_2=1<\varphi$. If the bounds hold for $k-1$ and $k$ (with $k\ge2$), then \[F_{k+1}=F_k+F_{k-1}\le\varphi^{k-1}+\varphi^{k-2}=\varphi^{k-2}(\varphi+1)=\varphi^{k},\qquad F_{k+1}\ge\varphi^{k-2}+\varphi^{k-3}=\varphi^{k-1},\] and the upper bound for $F_{k+1}$ is strict because the one for $F_k$ is ($F_2<\varphi$, and $F_k<\varphi^{k-1}$ for $k\ge3$ by induction).

Conclusion. \[A_{2025}=F_{2027}<\varphi^{2026}=\varphi^{507}\cdot\varphi^{506}\cdot\varphi^{507}\cdot\varphi^{506}\le(F_{509}F_{508})^2=B_{1013}^2 .\]

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

A grid of size $11\times11$ is given. Any $n$ cells of it can be colored black. Find the smallest such $n$ with the property that no matter which $n$ cells are colored black, there will always exist a $1\times5$ or $5\times1$ rectangle in the grid such that its 1st, 3rd, and 5th cells are black.

Show the answer

$82$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Four parity classes and row counting)

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,11$. Call a set of three cells bad if it consists of the 1st, 3rd and 5th cells of a $1\times5$ or $5\times1$ rectangle: cells $(r,c),(r,c+2),(r,c+4)$ or $(r,c),(r+2,c),(r+4,c)$. We find the largest number of black cells with no bad set; the answer is one more.

Reduction to four smaller grids. The cells of a bad set have the same row parity and the same column parity. Split the board into four classes by $(r\bmod 2,\ c\bmod2)$ and inside a class use the coordinates $(i,j)=(\lceil r/2\rceil,\lceil c/2\rceil)$. Odd indices give $6$ values and even indices $5$, so the classes are grids of sizes $6\times6$, $6\times5$, $5\times6$, $5\times5$; and a bad set is exactly a set of three consecutive cells in a row or column of one class grid. So we need, for each class, the maximum number $m$ of black cells with no three consecutive black cells in a line.

Lines. A line of $6$ cells splits into two triples, so it holds at most $4$ black cells. A line of $5$ cells splits into a triple and a pair, so at most $4$; and if it holds exactly $4$, its single white cell lies in each of the triples $\{1,2,3\},\{2,3,4\},\{3,4,5\}$, so it is the middle cell.

Upper bounds. $6\times6$: six rows, at most $24$. $6\times5$: five columns of length $6$, at most $20$; $5\times6$: likewise at most $20$. $5\times5$: at most $17$. Indeed, suppose at least $18$ black cells. As each row has at most $4$, at least three rows have exactly $4$ (else the total is at most $4+4+3+3+3=17$), and each of them is white in column $3$. So column $3$ has at most $2$ black cells, the other four columns have at least $16$, hence exactly $4$ each, and each of them is white in row $3$. Then row $3$ has at most one black cell, and the total is at most $4\cdot4+1=17$, a contradiction.

So at most $24+20+20+17=81$ black cells can avoid all bad sets.

A colouring with $81$ black cells. In the class (even row, even column) colour $(i,j)$ black iff $i+j\not\equiv1\pmod3$; in the other three classes colour $(i,j)$ black iff $i+j\not\equiv0\pmod3$. Along three consecutive cells of a line of a class, $i+j$ takes three consecutive values, one of which is excluded, so there is no bad set. The numbers of black cells are $36-12=24$, $30-10=20$, $30-10=20$ and $25-8=17$ (the excluded sums in the $5\times5$ class are $4,7,10$, occurring $3+4+1$ times): $81$ in total (see the figure).

Hence $81$ black cells need not contain a bad set, but $82$ always do: the answer is $\boxed{82}$.

Solution 2 (Packing disjoint bad sets)

Number rows and columns $1,\dots,11$; a bad set is three cells $(r,c),(r,c+2),(r,c+4)$ or $(r,c),(r+2,c),(r+4,c)$, i.e. the 1st, 3rd and 5th cells of a $1\times5$ or $5\times1$ rectangle.

82 black cells always contain a bad set. Split the board into the four classes of cells with given parities of row and column, and use in each class the coordinates $(i,j)=(\lceil r/2\rceil,\lceil c/2\rceil)$; the classes become grids of sizes $6\times6$, $6\times5$, $5\times6$, $5\times5$, and three consecutive cells in a row or column of a class grid form a bad set. Now cut every class into disjoint such triples:

  • $6\times6$: each row of $6$ into two triples ($12$ triples);

  • $6\times5$ and $5\times6$: each line of length $6$ into two triples ($10$ triples each);

  • $5\times5$: everything except the centre $(3,3)$, using the four rectangles $\{1,2\}\times\{1,2,3\}$, $\{1,2,3\}\times\{4,5\}$, $\{4,5\}\times\{3,4,5\}$, $\{3,4,5\}\times\{1,2\}$ (a pinwheel around the centre), each cut into two straight triples ($8$ triples).

This gives $40$ pairwise disjoint bad sets covering all cells but one. If no bad set is entirely black, each of the $40$ contains at most $2$ black cells, so there are at most $80+1=81$ black cells. Hence $82$ black cells force a bad set.

81 are not enough. Colour black, in the class (even row, even column), the cells with $i+j\not\equiv1\pmod 3$, and in the other three classes the cells with $i+j\not\equiv0\pmod3$. Three consecutive cells of a line in a class have three consecutive values of $i+j$, one of them excluded, so no bad set is black. Counting: $24+20+20+17=81$ black cells.

The smallest $n$ is $\boxed{82}$.
