Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms

For each positive integer $n$, consider the polynomial $P_n(x)=x^{3n}-3\cdot 4^{n-1}x^n-2^{3n-3}$.

  • (a) Prove that $P_n(x)$ has exactly one positive real zero, denoted by $a_n$.

  • (b) Let $b_n=\dfrac{2-a_n}{n}$ and $c_n=b_1+b_2+\cdots+b_n$. Prove that the sequence $(c_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ converges.

Show the answer

proof ($a_n=2\cos^{1/n}\frac{\pi}{9}$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A trigonometric cubic)

(a) Put $y=x^n$ and $y=2^{n-1}z$. Then \[P_n(x)=y^3-3\cdot4^{n-1}y-8^{n-1}=8^{n-1}\,(z^3-3z-1).\] For $z=2\cos\varphi$ we have $z^3-3z=2\cos3\varphi$, so the three roots of $z^3-3z-1$ are $2\cos\varphi$ with $\cos3\varphi=\frac12$, i.e. $\varphi=20^\circ,100^\circ,140^\circ$: the numbers $2\cos20^\circ>0$, $2\cos100^\circ<0$, $2\cos140^\circ<0$. A positive $x$ gives a positive $z$, and $x\mapsto z=x^n/2^{n-1}$ is a bijection of the positive reals. So $P_n$ has exactly one positive zero, \[a_n=\bigl(2^{n-1}\cdot2\cos\tfrac\pi9\bigr)^{1/n}=2\,c^{1/n},\qquad c=\cos\tfrac\pi9\in(0,1).\]

(b) $b_n=\frac{2(1-c^{1/n})}{n}>0$, so $(c_n)$ is increasing; we show it is bounded. By AM–GM applied to $\frac1c$ and $n-1$ ones, $\bigl(\frac1c\bigr)^{1/n}\le\frac{1/c+n-1}{n}=1+\frac{1/c-1}{n}$. Hence \[1-c^{1/n}=c^{1/n}\Bigl(\bigl(\tfrac1c\bigr)^{1/n}-1\Bigr)\le\frac{1/c-1}{n},\qquad b_n\le\frac{2(1/c-1)}{n^2}.\] Since $\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{k^2}\le1+\sum_{k=2}^n\bigl(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k\bigr)<2$, we get $c_n<4(\frac1c-1)$. An increasing bounded sequence converges.

Solution 2 (Descartes and Bernoulli)

(a) The coefficients of $P_n$ are $+1,\,-3\cdot4^{n-1},\,-2^{3n-3}$ (in decreasing degree): exactly one sign change. By Descartes' rule of signs $P_n$ has exactly one positive zero. (Directly: $P_n(x)=x^n\bigl(x^{2n}-3\cdot4^{n-1}\bigr)-2^{3n-3}$ is negative while $x^{2n}\le3\cdot4^{n-1}$, and for larger $x$ it is strictly increasing, since $x^n$ and $x^{2n}-3\cdot4^{n-1}$ are positive and increasing; it tends to $+\infty$.)

The value. With $z=a_n^n/2^{n-1}$ we get $z^3-3z-1=0$, $z>0$. As $f(z)=z^3-3z-1$ satisfies $f(1)=-3<0<1=f(2)$ and $f$ has only one positive zero, $z=z_0\in(1,2)$ does not depend on $n$, and $a_n=2q^{1/n}$ with $q=\frac{z_0}2\in(\frac12,1)$.

(b) By Bernoulli's inequality, $\bigl(1-\frac{1-q}{n}\bigr)^n\ge1-(1-q)=q$ (valid as $\frac{1-q}n<1$), so $q^{1/n}\ge1-\frac{1-q}n$. Therefore \[0<b_n=\frac{2(1-q^{1/n})}{n}\le\frac{2(1-q)}{n^2},\] so $(c_n)$ is increasing, and since $\sum_{k\le n}\frac1{k^2}<2$ it is bounded by $4(1-q)$; hence it converges.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

To explore space, scientists often need to observe distant objects such as comets, asteroids, and other astronomical phenomena. To this end, scientists design and launch observation satellites into orbit around the Earth. Most satellites do not move in perfect circles but have elliptical orbits, with the Earth located at one of the ellipse's foci. As the satellite moves in an elliptical orbit, the distance between it and the object being observed constantly changes. Generally, if the distance from the satellite to the object being observed is minimized, the sensors on the satellite will receive the best signal.

Consider a satellite (considered as a point particle) moving around the Earth in an elliptical orbit. In space with a rectangular coordinate system $Oxyz$ (the unit on each axis $Ox,Oy,Oz$ is $6400$ km), assume that the satellite moves on the plane $(Oxy)$ in an orbit that has the equation $x^2+3y^2=17$. The satellite needs to observe an object (also considered as a point particle) moving in space. According to research results, when the object moves to position $A\left(2,\dfrac{16}{\sqrt3},8\right)$, the observation of that object is the best. Determine the coordinates of point $C$ (on the elliptical orbit of the satellite) in space with the mentioned $Oxyz$ coordinate system so that the distance from position $C$ to position $A$ is minimized.

Show the answer

$C\left(1,\dfrac{4}{\sqrt3},0\right)$, with $CA=\sqrt{113}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Completing squares on the ellipse)

For $C=(x,y,0)$ on the orbit, $CA^2=(x-2)^2+\bigl(y-\frac{16}{\sqrt3}\bigr)^2+64$, so we minimise $g=(x-2)^2+\bigl(y-\frac{16}{\sqrt3}\bigr)^2$ under $x^2+3y^2=17$. Adding $x^2+3y^2-17=0$ to $g-49$: \[g-49=(x-2)^2+\Bigl(y-\tfrac{16}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)^2-49+(x^2+3y^2-17)=2(x-1)^2+4\Bigl(y-\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)^2\ge0\] (expand both sides: each equals $2x^2-4x+4y^2-\frac{32}{\sqrt3}y+\frac{70}{3}$). Equality holds only at $x=1$, $y=\frac4{\sqrt3}$, which is on the orbit ($1+3\cdot\frac{16}{3}=17$). So the minimum distance is $\sqrt{49+64}=\sqrt{113}$ (units of $6400$ km), attained only at \[\boxed{C\Bigl(1,\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3},0\Bigr)} .\]

Solution 2 (A supporting tangent line)

Let $A'=\bigl(2,\frac{16}{\sqrt3},0\bigr)$ be the projection of $A$ on the plane $Oxy$. For $C$ in that plane, $CA^2=CA'^2+64$, so we minimise $CA'$ over the ellipse.

At $C_0=\bigl(1,\frac4{\sqrt3}\bigr)$ the tangent line is $x+4\sqrt3\,y=17$. The whole ellipse lies in the half-plane $x+4\sqrt3y\le17$: by Cauchy–Schwarz, $x+4\sqrt3y=1\cdot x+4\cdot(\sqrt3y)\le\sqrt{17}\sqrt{x^2+3y^2}=17$, with equality only at $C_0$. The point $A'$ satisfies $2+4\sqrt3\cdot\frac{16}{\sqrt3}=66>17$, so it lies on the other side, and every point of the ellipse is at least as far from $A'$ as the line is: \[CA'\ge\frac{66-17}{\sqrt{1+48}}=7 .\] Moreover $\overrightarrow{C_0A'}=(1,4\sqrt3)$ is a normal vector of the line, so $C_0$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $A'$ and $C_0A'=7$. Equality needs $C$ on the line, i.e. $C=C_0$. Hence the answer is $\boxed{C\bigl(1,\frac4{\sqrt3},0\bigr)}$, with $CA=\sqrt{49+64}=\sqrt{113}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Let $n,a,b$ be positive integers such that $1<n^2<a<b<n^2+n+3$. Find all possible positive divisors in range $(n^2;n^2+n+3)$ of $ab$.

Show the answer

only $a$ and $b$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The cofactor is almost determined)

We read the question as: find all positive divisors of $ab$ that lie in the open interval $(n^2,\,n^2+n+3)$. Clearly $a$ and $b$ are such divisors. Suppose $d\mid ab$ with $n^2<d<n^2+n+3$. Write $a=n^2+x$, $b=n^2+y$, $d=n^2+z$ with $x,y,z\in\{1,\dots,n+2\}$, $x<y$, and let $e=ab/d$. Then \[e-n^2=\frac{(x+y-z)n^2+xy}{n^2+z}=x+y-z+\frac{(x-z)(y-z)}{n^2+z},\] so $k=\frac{(x-z)(y-z)}{n^2+z}$ is an integer. As $|x-z|,|y-z|\le n+1$, $|k|\le\frac{(n+1)^2}{n^2+1}<2$ for $n\ge2$ (and $n\ge2$ because $n^2>1$).

  • $k=0$: $z=x$ or $z=y$, i.e. $d=a$ or $d=b$.

  • $k=-1$: $z$ is strictly between $x$ and $y$ and $(z-x)(y-z)=n^2+z$; but $(z-x)+(y-z)\le n+1$ gives $(z-x)(y-z)\le\frac{(n+1)^2}{4}<n^2$. Impossible.

  • $k=1$, $z>y>x$: $(z-x)(z-y)\le(z-1)(z-2)=(z-2)^2+(z-2)\le n^2+z-2<n^2+z$ (as $z-2\le n$). Impossible.

  • $k=1$, $z<x<y$: with $u=x-z<v=y-z\le n+2-z$ we need $uv=n^2+z$. Since $uv\le(n+1-z)(n+2-z)$, we need $(n+1-z)(n+2-z)\ge n^2+z$, i.e. $z^2-(2n+4)z+3n+2\ge0$; for $z\ge2$ the left side is at most $4-4n-8+3n+2<0$ (it decreases on $[2,n+2]$), so $z=1$. Then $uv=n^2+1$ with $u<v\le n+1$, so $u\ge\frac{n^2+1}{n+1}>n-1$, i.e. $u\ge n$, $v=n+1$, and $n(n+1)=n^2+1$ forces $n=1$. Impossible.

So the only divisors of $ab$ in the interval $(n^2,n^2+n+3)$ are $\boxed{a\text{ and }b}$.

Solution 2 (Splitting d between a and b)

We look for the positive divisors of $ab$ in the open interval $(n^2,\,n^2+n+3)$; $a$ and $b$ are two of them. Let $d\mid ab$ with $n^2<d<n^2+n+3$ and $d\ne a,b$. Put $g=\gcd(d,a)$ and $h=\gcd(d,b)$. Since $\frac dg\mid\frac ag\cdot b$ and $\gcd(\frac dg,\frac ag)=1$, we get $\frac dg\mid b$, hence $\frac dg\mid h$ and $d\le gh$. Also $g\mid a-d\ne0$ and $h\mid b-d\ne0$, so \[n^2<d\le gh\le|a-d|\cdot|b-d| .\] All of $a,b,d$ lie in $\{n^2+1,\dots,n^2+n+2\}$, a set of $n+2$ consecutive integers.

  • If $d$ is between $a$ and $b$: $|a-d|+|b-d|=b-a\le n+1$, so the product is at most $\frac{(n+1)^2}4<n^2$.

  • If $d>b$: with $z=d-n^2\le n+2$, $(d-a)(d-b)\le(z-1)(z-2)=(z-2)^2+(z-2)\le n^2+z-2<d$.

  • If $d<a$: $(a-d)(b-d)\le(n^2+n+1-d)(n^2+n+2-d)$, and this is $\ge d$ only for $d=n^2+1$ (as in Solution 1, the quadratic in $z=d-n^2$ is negative for $z\ge2$). Then $(a-d)(b-d)\ge n^2+1$ forces $a=n^2+n+1$, $b=n^2+n+2$, so $g\mid n$, $h\mid n+1$ and $d=n^2+1$ divides $gh$, a divisor of $n^2+n$. But $n^2+1\mid n^2+n$ means $n^2+1\mid n-1$, impossible as $0<n-1<n^2+1$.

Hence no such $d$ exists: the only divisors in the interval are $\boxed{a\text{ and }b}$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics

An plays a game where he writes triples on a board from left to right. Initially, the triple $(1,1,1)$ is written on the board. In each turn, An performs one of the following two operations with the current triple $(x,y,z)$:

  • (i) Delete the triple $(x,y,z)$ and write on the board $(y,z,x+z)$,

  • (ii) Delete the triple $(x,y,z)$ and write on the board $(x+z+1,x+y+z+1,x+y+2z+1)$.

  • (a) Prove that An needs exactly $4$ turns to write on the board the triple $(a,b,6)$.

  • (b) Find the smallest natural number $k$ for which there is a way to play such that after $k$ turns, An can write on the board the triple $(c,d,129)$.

Show the answer

(a) proof; (b) $k=12$

Show the solution

Solution (A Narayana number system)

Let $N_1=N_2=N_3=1$ and $N_{j+3}=N_{j+2}+N_j$ (Narayana's sequence): $1,1,1,2,3,4,6,9,13,19,28,41,60,88,129,\dots$ Write triples as column vectors and let $\mathbf M(x,y,z)=(y,z,x+z)$, a linear map, and $\mathbf J=(1,1,1)$, the starting triple. Then \[\text{(i): }T\mapsto\mathbf MT,\qquad \text{(ii): }T\mapsto\mathbf M^3T+\mathbf J,\] since $\mathbf M^3(x,y,z)=(x+z,\,x+y+z,\,x+y+2z)$. Also $\mathbf M(N_j,N_{j+1},N_{j+2})=(N_{j+1},N_{j+2},N_{j+3})$, so $\mathbf M^e\mathbf J=(N_{e+1},N_{e+2},N_{e+3})$.

Shape of every triple. By induction on the number of turns, the triple on the board is $\sum_{j=1}^r\mathbf M^{e_j}\mathbf J$ with $e_1>e_2>\dots>e_r\ge0$ and $e_j-e_{j+1}\ge3$: at the start $r=1$, $e_1=0$; (i) adds $1$ to every exponent; (ii) adds $3$ to every exponent and appends a new exponent $0$. If the play has $u$ turns of type (i) and $w$ of type (ii), then $r=w+1$ and $e_1=u+3w$, so the number of turns is \[u+w=e_1-2(r-1).\tag{$*$}\] Its third entry is $z=\sum_jN_{m_j}$ with $m_j=e_j+3$: a sum of Narayana numbers with indices $\ge3$ and pairwise gaps $\ge3$.

Lemma. Such a sum with largest index $m$ is less than $N_{m+1}$. By induction: the rest has largest index $\le m-3$, so it is less than $N_{m-2}$ (or is $0$), and $N_m+N_{m-2}=N_{m+1}$.

Since $N_3<N_4<N_5<\cdots$, the Lemma shows that $m_1$ is the unique index with $N_{m_1}\le z<N_{m_1+1}$; repeating, the whole representation, hence $r$ and $e_1$, hence by $(*)$ the number of turns, is determined by $z$.

(a) $N_7=6<9=N_8$, so $m_1=7$ and the remainder is $0$: $r=1$, $e_1=4$, and every way to write a triple $(a,b,6)$ takes exactly $4$ turns. (It is possible: four turns of type (i) give $(3,4,6)$.)

(b) $129=N_{15}$, so $r=1$, $e_1=12$: the triple $(c,d,129)$ can only appear after exactly $12$ turns (twelve turns of type (i) give $(60,88,129)$). The smallest $k$ is $\boxed{12}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle $(AB<AC)$ with circumcenter $O$. Let $E,F$ be the reflection of $A$ across $B,C$ respectively. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. Points $I,P$ are chosen on line $AM$ such that triangle $AIE$, $APF$ are isosceles triangles with $IA=IE$, $PA=PF$. Let $T$ be the intersection of $IE$ and $PF$.

  • (a) Prove that $T$ lies on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$.

  • (b) Let $D$ be the intersection of lines $BI$ and $CP$. Let $J$ be the intersection of lines $TD$ and $IP$. Let $X$ be the intersection of line $OT$ and $BC$. Lines $AT$, $AX$ intersect $EF$ at $L,U$ respectively. Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $TAJ$, $TIP$, $TEF$, $TLU$ all have a common point other than $T$.

Show the solution

Solution (Reflections, an Apollonius circle and a Miquel point)

Part (a). Let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle and $A^*$ the antipode of $A$.

$I$ and $P$. $\angle ABA^*=90^\circ$, so the line $BA^*$ is the perpendicular to $AB$ at $B$, the midpoint of $AE$: it is the perpendicular bisector of $AE$. Hence $I=AM\cap BA^*$ and, likewise, $P=AM\cap CA^*$. (In particular $D=BI\cap CP=A^*$.) In the isosceles triangle $AIE$ the axis $BA^*$ is a line of symmetry, so line $IE$ is the reflection of line $AM$ in $BA^*$; similarly $PF$ is the reflection of $AM$ in $CA^*$.

An orthocenter on $AM$. Let $A'$ be the reflection of $A$ in $M$, a point of line $AM$. $ABA'C$ is a parallelogram, so $A'B\parallel AC\perp CA^*$ and $A'C\parallel AB\perp BA^*$: $A'$ is the orthocenter of triangle $A^*BC$. Let $H_1,H_2$ be the reflections of $A'$ in $A^*B$ and $A^*C$. As is well known, the reflections of the orthocenter in the sides lie on the circumcircle, here $\Omega$.

Angle count. Write $\ell=AM$, $\ell_1=IE$, $\ell_2=PF$ (so $H_1\in\ell_1$, $H_2\in\ell_2$). With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$ and the reflection rule $\angle(\ell_1,A^*B)=\angle(A^*B,\ell)$: \[\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)=\angle(\ell_1,A^*B)+\angle(A^*B,A^*C)+\angle(A^*C,\ell_2)=\angle(A^*B,\ell)+\angle(A^*B,A^*C)+\angle(\ell,A^*C)=2\angle(A^*B,A^*C).\] In the same way, since $A^*H_1$, $A^*H_2$ are the reflections of $A^*A'$ in $A^*B$, $A^*C$, $\angle(A^*H_1,A^*H_2)=2\angle(A^*B,A^*C)$. Hence for $T=\ell_1\cap\ell_2$, $\angle(TH_1,TH_2)=\angle(A^*H_1,A^*H_2)$, so $T$ lies on the circle through $A^*,H_1,H_2$, which is $\Omega$. (If $T$ coincides with $H_1$ or $H_2$ it is on $\Omega$ anyway.) This proves (a).

Part (b). Keep the notation of (a): $\ell=AM$, $\ell_1=IE$, $\ell_2=PF$, and $D=A^*$. The homothety $h$ with centre $A$ and ratio $2$ maps $B,C,M,O$ to $E,F,A',D$. So $A'$ is the midpoint of $EF$, $EF\parallel BC$, and $DE=DF=DA$, $DA'\perp EF$.

Step 1: the bisectors at $T$. The reflections in $DB$ and $DC$ fix $D$ and map $\ell$ to $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$, so $D$ is at the same distance from $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$. Hence $TD$ is a bisector of the angles between $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$. (Here $T\ne D$: $D\in\ell_1$ would give $D\in\ell$, i.e. $AB=AC$.) Since $AD$ is a diameter of $\Omega$ and $T\in\Omega$, $TA\perp TD$, so $TA$ is the other bisector.

Step 2: an Apollonius circle. $T\notin\ell$ (otherwise $T=I=P$, but $BD$ and $CD$ meet only at $D\notin\ell$). In triangle $TIP$ the two bisectors at $T$ meet line $IP$ at $A$ and $J$, so \[\frac{AI}{AP}=\frac{TI}{TP}=\frac{JI}{JP}=:\rho .\] $I\ne P$ lie on the ray $AM$, so $\rho\ne1$, and the locus of the points $Z$ with $ZI/ZP=\rho$ is the circle $\mathcal A$ with diameter $AJ$. As $\angle ATJ=90^\circ$, $\mathcal A$ is the circle $TAJ$.

Step 3: the Miquel point. The circles $TIP$ and $TEF$ are not tangent at $T$ (a homothety at $T$ taking one to the other would map $I\mapsto E$, $P\mapsto F$, forcing $IP\parallel EF$, but these lines meet at $A'$). Let $Q\ne T$ be their second common point. With directed angles, \[\angle(IQ,IE)=\angle(IQ,IT)=\angle(PQ,PT)=\angle(PQ,PF),\qquad \angle(EQ,EI)=\angle(EQ,ET)=\angle(FQ,FT)=\angle(FQ,FP),\] so triangles $QIE$ and $QPF$ are similar and $\frac{QI}{QP}=\frac{IE}{PF}=\frac{IA}{PA}=\rho$. Hence $Q\in\mathcal A$: the circles $TAJ$, $TIP$, $TEF$ pass through $Q$. It remains to prove that $Q$ lies on the circle $TLU$.

Step 4: the circle $\Gamma=(TEF)$. By (a), $\angle(TE,TF)=\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)=2\angle(DB,DC)$. The lines $DE$, $DF$ are the reflections of $DA$ in $DB$, $DC$, so the same computation gives $\angle(DE,DF)=2\angle(DB,DC)$. Hence $D\in\Gamma$. As $DE=DF$, the line $DA'$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$; let $D'$ be the antipode of $D$ on $\Gamma$. Then $D'E=D'F$, and $\angle DTD'=90^\circ=\angle DTA$ shows that $D'$ lies on line $AT$.

Step 5: $Q,D,J,A'$ are concyclic. Using $\Gamma$ (points $Q,D,T,D'$) and $\mathcal A$ (points $Q,T,A,J$), \[\angle(QD,QJ)=\angle(QD,QT)+\angle(QT,QJ)=\angle(D'D,D'T)+\angle(AT,AJ)=\angle(DA',AT)+\angle(AT,\ell)=\angle(A'D,A'J).\] Let $U_0$ be the point of line $EF$ with $JU_0\perp TD$ (so $JU_0\parallel AT$). Since $\angle DJU_0=\angle DA'U_0=90^\circ$, the circle $DJA'$ has diameter $DU_0$, and $Q$ lies on it: $\angle DQU_0=90^\circ$. Also $\angle DQD'=90^\circ$. Therefore $Q$, $U_0$, $D'$ are collinear.

Step 6: $U_0=U$. Let $J_1$ be the midpoint of $AJ$. Then $h^{-1}$ maps $J\mapsto J_1$ and $U_0$ to the point $X_0$ of $BC$ with $J_1X_0\parallel AT$. We show $XJ_1\parallel AT$; then $X=X_0$ and $U=h(X)=U_0$.

(i) The reflection $\ell_3$ of $\ell$ in $BC$ passes through $T$. Let $H_3$ be the reflection of $A'$ in $BC$; it lies on $\Omega$ and on $\ell_3=MH_3$. Let $T_3$ be the second intersection of $\ell_3$ with $\Omega$. As in (a), $\angle(T_3H_3,T_3H_1)=\angle(BH_3,BH_1)=2\angle(BC,BD)=\angle(\ell_3,\ell_1)$, so the line $T_3H_1$ is the line through $H_1$ parallel to $\ell_1$, which is $\ell_1$. Likewise $T_3\in\ell_2$, so $T_3=T$.

(ii) The tangents to $\Omega$ at $A$ and $T$ meet on $BC$. Let $N$ be the second intersection of $\ell$ with $\Omega$. The reflection in the perpendicular bisector $OM$ of $BC$ preserves $\Omega$ and maps $\ell$ to $\ell_3$ (the reflections of a line through $M$ in $BC$ and in $OM$ coincide), $A\mapsto H_3$, hence $N\mapsto T$ and $NT\parallel BC$. Projecting $\Omega$ from $N$ to line $BC$ sends $A,T,B,C$ to $M$, the point at infinity, $B$, $C$; as $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$, $(A,T;B,C)=-1$. So $ABTC$ is a harmonic quadrilateral and the tangents at $A$ and $T$ meet at a point $Y$ of line $BC$.

(iii) An orthic triangle. $OY$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AT$. In triangle $OXY$ (recall $X=OT\cap BC$), $YT\perp OX$ and $OM\perp XY$: $T$ and $M$ are the feet of the altitudes from $Y$ and $O$. Let $J'$ be the foot of the altitude from $X$. The circles with diameters $XY$ and $XO$ give $\angle(J'T,J'Y)=\angle(XT,XY)$ and $\angle(J'M,J'O)=\angle(XM,XO)$, i.e. $\angle(J'T,OY)=-\angle(J'M,OY)$: the lines $J'T$ and $J'M$ are symmetric in $OY$. The reflection of $T$ in $OY$ is $A$, so $A\in J'M$, i.e. $J'\in AM$. On the other hand $J_1\in AM$, and $J_1A=J_1T$ ($J_1$ is the circumcentre of the right triangle $ATJ$), so $J_1\in OY$. Since $A\notin OY$, $J'=J_1$. Hence $XJ_1\perp OY$, and $AT\perp OY$ gives $XJ_1\parallel AT$.

Step 7: conclusion. The lines $D'T$ (which is $AT$) and $D'Q$ meet line $EF$ at $L$ and $U$. Since $D'E=D'F$, the inversion with centre $D'$ and radius $D'E$ swaps $\Gamma$ and the line $EF$, hence $T\leftrightarrow L$ and $Q\leftrightarrow U$. So $D'T\cdot D'L=D'Q\cdot D'U$, and $T$, $L$, $U$, $Q$ are concyclic.

Thus the circles $TAJ$, $TIP$, $TEF$, $TLU$ all pass through $Q\ne T$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

  • (a) Find the number of distinct positive zeroes of the polynomial $Q(x)=x^3-5x^2+4x-1$.

  • (b) Let $a,b,c,d$ be integers such that the polynomial $P(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$ has three distinct, positive zeroes, none of which are rational. Find the minimal value of $S=|a|+|b|+|c|+|d|$.

Show the answer

(a) $1$; (b) $13$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Hoelder inequality at x = 1 and x = -1)

(a) $Q(x)=x(x-1)(x-4)-1$. For $x\le0$ and for $1\le x\le4$, $x(x-1)(x-4)\le0$, so $Q(x)<0$. For $0<x<1$, $x(1-x)\le\frac14$ and $4-x<4$, so $x(x-1)(x-4)=x(1-x)(4-x)<1$ and $Q(x)<0$. For $x>4$, $Q'(x)=3x^2-10x+4>0$, so $Q$ increases; $Q(4)=-1<0<19=Q(5)$. So $Q$ has exactly $\boxed{1}$ real zero, and it is positive.

(b) Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be the roots; we may assume $a>0$. By Vieta, $b<0$, $c>0$, $d<0$, so \[S=a-b+c-d=-P(-1)=a\prod(1+x_i).\] Also $|P(1)|=a\prod|1-x_i|\ge1$ (an integer, and $1$ is not a root since the roots are irrational) and $-d=a\prod x_i\ge1$. Using $(1+x)^2=(1-x)^2+4x$ and the inequality $\prod_{i=1}^3(u_i+v_i)\ge\bigl(\sqrt[3]{u_1u_2u_3}+\sqrt[3]{v_1v_2v_3}\bigr)^3$ for $u_i,v_i\ge0$ (Hoelder), \[S^2=a^2\prod\bigl((1-x_i)^2+4x_i\bigr)\ge\Bigl(|P(1)|^{2/3}+4a^{1/3}|d|^{1/3}\Bigr)^3\ge\bigl(1+4a^{1/3}\bigr)^3 .\] For $a\ge2$ this gives $S\ge(1+4\sqrt[3]2)^{3/2}>14$. For $a=1$ it gives $S>11$, so $S\ge12$; and $S=12$ would give $|P(1)|^{2/3}+4|d|^{1/3}\le144^{1/3}<5.25$, so $|d|^{1/3}<1.07$, i.e. $d=-1$, and then $|P(1)|^{2/3}<1.25$, i.e. $|P(1)|=1$. But with $a=1$, $d=-1$: $|P(1)|=|b+c|=|c-|b||$ and $|b|+c=10$, so $|P(1)|$ is even. Contradiction. Hence $S\ge13$.

$P(x)=x^3-5x^2+6x-1$ attains it: $P(0)=-1$, $P(1)=1$, $P(2)=-1$, $P(4)=7$ give three positive roots in $(0,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,4)$; they are irrational, since the only possible rational roots $\pm1$ are not roots. The minimum is $\boxed{13}$.

Solution 2 (Newton inequalities and casework)

(a) $Q'(x)=3x^2-10x+4$ vanishes at $x_\pm=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{13}}3$, and $Q$ has a local maximum at $x_-\approx0.465$. There $3x_-^2=10x_--4$, so $Q(x_-)=\frac{x_-}{3}(10x_--4)-5x_-^2+4x_--1=\frac{-5x_-^2+8x_--3}{3}=-\frac{(5x_--3)(x_--1)}{3}<0$, because $x_-<\frac35<1$ (as $\sqrt{13}>3.4$). So $Q<0$ on $(-\infty,x_+]$, and $Q$ increases on $[x_+,\infty)$ to $+\infty$: exactly $\boxed{1}$ real root, which is positive (as $Q(0)<0$).

(b) Take $a>0$ and write $P=ax^3-px^2+qx-r$ with $p,q,r$ positive integers (signs by Vieta), $S=a+p+q+r$. For three distinct positive roots, \[p^2>3aq\quad\text{and}\quad q^2>3pr,\] since $(\sum x_i)^2-3\sum x_ix_j=\frac12\sum(x_i-x_j)^2>0$ and $(\sum x_ix_j)^2-3x_1x_2x_3\sum x_i=\frac12\sum_{\text{cyc}}x_j^2(x_i-x_k)^2>0$, multiplied by $a^2$. Suppose $S\le12$.

$a\ge2$. From $q^2>3p$ and $p^2>6q$: $q^4>9p^2>54q$, so $q\ge4$, $p^2>24$, $p\ge5$, and $S\ge a+5+4+1$; for $a\ge3$ this exceeds $12$, and for $a=2$ it equals $12$. Equality forces $P=2x^3-5x^2+4x-1$, which has the root $1$.

$a=1$, $p+q+r\le11$. From $p^2>3q$, $q^2>3p$ we get $p,q\ge4$ (if $q\le3$ then $p\le2$, then $q\le1$, then $p<1$). Then: $p=4$: $q\in\{4,5\}$ (as $q<\frac{16}3$) and $r<\frac{q^2}{12}$: $(4,4,1)$ and $(4,5,2)$ have root $1$; $(4,5,1)$ has $P(1)=1$ and local minimum $P(\frac53)=\frac{23}{27}>0$, so only one real root. $p=5$: $q+r\le6$ and $q^2>15r$: $(5,4,1)$ is $Q$ from (a), one real root; $(5,5,1)$ has root $1$. $p\ge6$: $q\le4$ and $q^2>18r\ge18$ fail. So $S\ge13$. It is attained by $x^3-5x^2+6x-1$: its values $-1,1,-1,7$ at $x=0,1,2,4$ give three positive roots, in $(0,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,4)$, and they are irrational because the only possible rational roots $\pm1$ are not roots. The minimum is $\boxed{13}$.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

In a game, Mario needs to move forward by jumping, where in each move he can jump either $1$, $a$ or $b$ meters, where $a,b$ are positive integers such that $1<a<b$. Given a route to travel with length $m$ meters, Mario uses the following strategy to jump: First, Mario performs $b$-meter jumps (with the maximal number of jumps as long as Mario does not exceed the finish). If Mario has not reached the finish yet, Mario will then perform $a$-meter jumps (with the maximal number of jumps as long as Mario does not exceed the finish), and finally, if Mario still has not reached the finish, Mario will perform $1$-meter jumps. Mario wins if there is no way to move to the finish with the number of jumps fewer than the number of jumps in the above strategy, else Mario loses.

  • (a) With $a=6$, $b=14$, prove that there is a positive integer $m$ so that Mario loses.

  • (b) Find the number of pairs $(a,b)$ with $1<a<b\leq 100$ such that Mario wins with the above strategy for all positive integers $m$.

Show the answer

(a) proof ($m=18$); (b) $1086$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Exchanging one b-jump)

Write $b=qa+r$ with $q\ge1$, $0\le r<a$. For $R\ge0$ let $f(R)=\lfloor R/a\rfloor+(R\bmod a)$, the number of jumps when only $a$ and $1$ are used greedily; this is optimal among jumps of lengths $a$ and $1$ (with $\alpha$ jumps of length $a$ the count is $R-\alpha(a-1)$, smallest for the largest $\alpha$). A route of length $m$ with $\beta$ jumps of length $b$ needs at least $g(\beta)=\beta+f(m-\beta b)$ jumps, and Mario's strategy uses $g(\lfloor m/b\rfloor)$.

Key difference. For $R\ge b$ write $R=\alpha a+\gamma$, $0\le\gamma<a$. If $\gamma\ge r$, then $R-b=(\alpha-q)a+(\gamma-r)$ and $f(R)-f(R-b)=q+r$. If $\gamma<r$, then $\alpha\ge q+1$ and $R-b=(\alpha-q-1)a+(a+\gamma-r)$, so $f(R)-f(R-b)=q+r+1-a$.

Mario wins for all $m$ if $r=0$ or $q+r\ge a$. Then $f(R)-f(R-b)\ge1$ for all $R\ge b$ (the case $\gamma<r$ needs $r>0$, and then $q+r+1-a\ge1$). So $g(\beta)\ge g(\beta+1)$ whenever $(\beta+1)b\le m$: $g$ is non-increasing and its minimum is at $\beta=\lfloor m/b\rfloor$, which is Mario's count.

Mario loses for some $m$ if $r>0$ and $q+r<a$. Take $m=(q+1)a$. Then $m-b=a-r\in(0,a)$, so $b<m<2b$ and Mario makes one $b$-jump and $a-r$ jumps of length $1$, $1+a-r$ jumps in total; but $q+1$ jumps of length $a$ suffice, and $q+1<1+a-r$.

(a) $a=6$, $b=14=2\cdot6+2$: $q+r=4<6$; with $m=18$ Mario uses $14+1+1+1+1$ ($5$ jumps), while $6+6+6$ needs $3$.

(b) Count pairs $2\le a<b\le100$ with $a\mid b$ or $q+r\ge a$ (where $b=qa+r$). For fixed $a$ and $q$, the admissible $r$ are $0$ and $\max(1,a-q)\le r\le a-1$, i.e. $1+\min(q,a-1)$ values, subject to $a<b\le100$ (for $q=1$, $r=0$ gives $b=a$, so only $b=2a-1$ remains). Let $W(a)$ be the number of admissible $b$. Counting by hand, block by block:

$a$$W(a)$sum
$2$–$10$$98,96,93,89,84,78,71,63,54$$726$
$11$–$20$$44,36,31,27,22,20,18,14,14,14$$240$
$21$–$33$$9$ for $a=21,\dots,25$; $5$ for $a=26,\dots,33$$85$
$34$–$50$$3$ for $a=34$; $2$ for $a=35,\dots,50$$35$
$51$–$99$$0$$0$

For instance, for $35\le a\le50$ only $b=2a-1$ ($q=1$) and $b=2a$ ($q=2$, $r=0$) are at most $100$; $a=34$ also has $b=3a-2=100$; for $a\ge51$ even $2a-1>100$. In total $726+240+85+35=\boxed{1086}$ pairs. (A short program confirms the count, and the criterion by brute force over all $m\le3b+2a$.)

Solution 2 (A smallest losing distance)

Keep $b=qa+r$, $f$ and the key difference of Solution 1, and let $G(m)$ be Mario's count and $O(m)$ the optimum.

Criterion. Suppose Mario loses for some $m$, and take the smallest such $m$. An optimal route for $m$ uses no $b$-jump: otherwise removing it gives $O(m-b)\le O(m)-1<G(m)-1=G(m-b)$ (Mario's strategy on $m\ge b$ starts with a $b$-jump), so $m-b$ would be a smaller losing distance. So $O(m)=f(m)$, and $m\ge b$ (for $m<b$ Mario's count is $f(m)$). By minimality $G(m-b)=O(m-b)\le f(m-b)$, hence $f(m)=O(m)<G(m)=1+G(m-b)\le1+f(m-b)$, i.e. $f(m)-f(m-b)\le0$. By the key difference this means $\gamma<r$ and $q+r+1-a\le0$: so $r>0$ and $q+r<a$. Conversely, in that case $m=(q+1)a$ is a losing distance (Solution 1).

In particular, for $a=6$, $b=14$ ($q=r=2$, $q+r<a$) the distance $m=(q+1)a=18$ is losing, which answers (a).

Therefore Mario wins for all $m$ iff $a\mid b$ or $q+r\ge a$.

Counting. Fix $a$. The values $b\in(a,100]$ with $a\nmid b$ and $q+r<a$ are $b=qa+r$ with $1\le r\le a-1-q$, which requires $q\le a-2$. So the number of losing pairs for this $a$ is $\sum_{q\ge1}\#\{r:1\le r\le a-1-q,\ qa+r\le100\}$. Summing over $a=2,\dots,99$ gives $3765$ losing pairs, out of $\binom{99}{2}=4851$; so $4851-3765=\boxed{1086}$ pairs are winning for Mario.
