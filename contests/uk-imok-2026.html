Cayley

Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Substitution and Elimination

Cayley, Hamilton and Maclaurin each have an amount of money.

If Cayley gave Hamilton $1$ pound then Hamilton would have three times the amount that Cayley would have.

If instead Hamilton gave Maclaurin $1$ pound then Maclaurin would have twice the amount that Hamilton would have.

If instead Maclaurin gave Cayley $1$ pound then Cayley would have a quarter of the amount that Maclaurin would have.

If instead Cayley and Hamilton each gave Maclaurin $1$ pound, how many times the total of Cayley and Hamilton will Maclaurin have?

Show the answer

$\frac32$ times

Show the solution

Solution (Three equations)

Let Cayley, Hamilton and Maclaurin have $c$, $h$ and $m$ pounds. The three statements say \[h+1=3(c-1),\qquad m+1=2(h-1),\qquad c+1=\tfrac14(m-1).\] So $h=3c-4$, $m=2h-3$ and $m=4c+5$. Substituting the first into the second gives $m=2(3c-4)-3=6c-11$, and comparing with $m=4c+5$ gives $6c-11=4c+5$, so $c=8$. Then $h=20$ and $m=37$.

(Check: $21=3\cdot7$, $38=2\cdot19$ and $9=\tfrac14\cdot36$.)

If Cayley and Hamilton each give Maclaurin $1$ pound, then Cayley and Hamilton have $7+19=26$ pounds and Maclaurin has $39$ pounds. Since $39=\tfrac32\cdot26$, Maclaurin has $\boxed{\tfrac32}$ times the total of the other two.

Problem 2

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

A $3\times3$ grid has the middle square shaded out. The digits $1$ to $8$ are placed in the grid once each to form four three-digit numbers, two reading left-to-right and two reading top-to-bottom, in such a way that all the three-digit numbers are multiples of $4$. Find how many ways this can be done.

Show the answer

$192$

Show the solution

Solution (Last two digits)

Name the cells as in the figure. The four numbers are $\overline{atb}$, $\overline{cud}$ (rows) and $\overline{alc}$, $\overline{brd}$ (columns). A number is a multiple of $4$ exactly when the number formed by its last two digits is, so we need \[\overline{tb},\quad \overline{ud},\quad \overline{lc},\quad \overline{rd}\quad\text{all multiples of }4 .\] A two-digit multiple of $4$ ends in an even digit; more precisely, $\overline{pq}=10p+q$ with $10p\equiv2p\pmod4$, so

  • if the tens digit $p$ is odd, the units digit is $2$ or $6$;

  • if $p$ is even, the units digit is $4$ or $8$ (the digit $0$ is not available).

So $b$, $c$, $d$ are even. They use three of the four even digits $2,4,6,8$; call the fourth even digit $e$.

The corner $d$. If $d\in\{4,8\}$, both $u$ and $r$ would have to be even, but only one even digit ($e$) is left. So $d\in\{2,6\}$, and $u$, $r$ are odd.

The corners $b$ and $c$. If $b\in\{4,8\}$ then $t$ must be even, so $t=e$; if $c\in\{4,8\}$ then $l=e$. Both cannot happen. The digits $b$, $c$, $e$ are $4$, $8$ and the other digit $s$ of $\{2,6\}$ (the one not used by $d$). Since $b$ and $c$ are not both in $\{4,8\}$, one of $b$, $c$ is $s$ and the other is $4$ or $8$; the remaining one of $4$, $8$ is $e$, and it must sit at $t$ (if $b\in\{4,8\}$) or at $l$ (if $c\in\{4,8\}$). In every case the four odd digits fill the remaining four cells, and every condition is then satisfied:

  • $\overline{ud}$ and $\overline{rd}$: odd tens digit, units $d\in\{2,6\}$;

  • the corner equal to $s$ has an odd tens digit next to it;

  • the corner in $\{4,8\}$ has the even digit $e$ as its tens digit.

Counting. $2$ choices for $d$; $2$ choices for which of $b$, $c$ is $s$; $2$ choices for which of $4$, $8$ goes in that other corner (then $e$ and its cell are forced); and $4!=24$ ways to place the odd digits $1,3,5,7$ in the four remaining cells. Total: $2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot24=\boxed{192}$.

Problem 3

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Pythagorean Theorem

$BCDF$ is a rhombus with side length $x$. $E$ lies on $DF$ and $ABCE$ is a parallelogram. $AB$ has length $y$ and $AF$ has length $z$. The perimeter of trapezium $ABCD$ is $10\%$ more than the perimeter of $BCDF$. The perimeter of $ABCE$ is $10\%$ less than the perimeter of $BCDF$. Prove that $ABCE$ is, in fact, a rectangle.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The converse of Pythagoras)

The order of the points. Since $ABCE$ is a parallelogram, $AE$ is parallel to $BC$, has length $x$, and $E$ is reached from $A$ by moving in the direction from $B$ to $C$. Since the rhombus $BCDF$ is a parallelogram, $FD$ is parallel to $BC$, has length $x$, and $D$ is reached from $F$ in the same direction. As $E$ lies on $FD$, the points $A$, $F$, $E$, $D$ lie on one line parallel to $BC$. Measure positions on this line in the direction from $B$ to $C$, with $E$ at $0$: then $A$ is at $-x$, and if $F$ is at $f$ then $D$ is at $f+x$. Since $E$ lies on the segment $FD$, $f\le0\le f+x$, so $-x\le f\le 0$. Hence $A$, $F$, $E$, $D$ lie in this order, $F$ lies on the side $AE$, and $AD=AF+FD=z+x$.

In the parallelogram $ABCE$ we also have $CE=AB=y$.

The perimeters. The rhombus has perimeter $4x$. The trapezium $ABCD$ has perimeter $AB+BC+CD+DA=y+x+x+(z+x)=3x+y+z$, and the parallelogram $ABCE$ has perimeter $2x+2y$. So \[3x+y+z=\tfrac{110}{100}\cdot4x=\tfrac{22}{5}x,\qquad 2x+2y=\tfrac{90}{100}\cdot4x=\tfrac{18}{5}x .\] The second gives $y=\tfrac45x$, and then the first gives $z=\tfrac{22}{5}x-3x-\tfrac45x=\tfrac35x$.

A right angle. In triangle $ABF$ the sides are $AB=\tfrac45x$, $AF=\tfrac35x$ and $BF=x$ (a side of the rhombus). Since \[\Bigl(\tfrac45x\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(\tfrac35x\Bigr)^2=\tfrac{16+9}{25}x^2=x^2=BF^2,\] the converse of Pythagoras' theorem gives $\angle BAF=90^\circ$. As $F$ lies on the side $AE$, this is the angle $\angle BAE$ of the parallelogram. A parallelogram with a right angle has all its angles equal to $90^\circ$ (consecutive angles add up to $180^\circ$), so $ABCE$ is a rectangle.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

The order of the points. Since $ABCE$ is a parallelogram, $AE$ is parallel to $BC$, has length $x$, and $E$ is reached from $A$ by moving in the direction from $B$ to $C$. Since the rhombus $BCDF$ is a parallelogram, $FD$ is parallel to $BC$, has length $x$, and $D$ is reached from $F$ in the same direction. As $E$ lies on $FD$, the points $A$, $F$, $E$, $D$ lie on one line parallel to $BC$. Measure positions on this line in the direction from $B$ to $C$, with $E$ at $0$: then $A$ is at $-x$, and if $F$ is at $f$ then $D$ is at $f+x$. Since $E$ lies on the segment $FD$, $f\le0\le f+x$, so $-x\le f\le 0$. Hence $A$, $F$, $E$, $D$ lie in this order, $F$ lies on the side $AE$, and $AD=AF+FD=z+x$.

The perimeters give $y$ and $z$ in terms of $x$. The rhombus $BCDF$ has perimeter $4x$, the trapezium $ABCD$ has perimeter $AB+BC+CD+DA=y+x+x+(z+x)$, and the parallelogram $ABCE$ has perimeter $2(BC+AB)=2x+2y$. Hence $3x+y+z=\tfrac{22}{5}x$ and $2x+2y=\tfrac{18}{5}x$, so $y=\tfrac45x$ and $z=\tfrac35x$.

Choose the unit so that $x=5$; then $y=4$, $z=3$. Put $B=(0,0)$ and $C=(5,0)$. The line $ADEF$ is parallel to $BC$, at some height $h>0$, and $F=(p,h)$ with $BF=5$: \[p^2+h^2=25 .\] Since $A$ lies on the same horizontal line, $AF=3$ and $A$ comes before $F$ in the direction from $B$ to $C$, $A=(p-3,h)$, and $AB=4$ gives $(p-3)^2+h^2=16$. Subtracting the two equations, $p^2-(p-3)^2=9$, that is $6p-9=9$, so $p=3$ and $h=4$. Hence $A=(0,4)$ lies directly above $B$: the side $AB$ is perpendicular to $BC$.

So the parallelogram $ABCE$ has a right angle at $B$, and therefore it is a rectangle.

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Midsegments and Internal Frameworks

$D$ is a point on the side $BC$ of triangle $ABC$ such that $AB=AD=CD=2BD$. $F$ and $E$ are the midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$ respectively such that $FE$ is parallel to $BC$. Prove that triangle $DEF$ is isosceles.

Show the answer

proof ($DE=DF$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Feet of perpendiculars)

Let $BD=2a$, so $AB=AD=CD=4a$ and $BC=6a$. We show $DE=DF$.

Let $N$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$, and $F'$, $E'$ the feet of the perpendiculars from $F$, $E$.

Where the feet are. Triangle $ABD$ is isosceles with $AB=AD$, so the altitude $AN$ bisects the base: $BN=ND=a$. In triangle $ABN$, the segment $FF'$ is parallel to $AN$ and $F$ is the midpoint of $AB$, so $F'$ is the midpoint of $BN$ (midpoint theorem): $BF'=\tfrac a2$. In the same way, in triangle $ANC$, $E'$ is the midpoint of $NC$; since $NC=ND+DC=a+4a=5a$, we get $NE'=\tfrac{5a}2$.

Hence, measuring along $BC$, \[F'D=BD-BF'=2a-\tfrac a2=\tfrac{3a}2,\qquad DE'=NE'-ND=\tfrac{5a}2-a=\tfrac{3a}2 .\]

Equal heights. $FF'=\tfrac12AN=EE'$ (again by the midpoint theorem, or because $FE\parallel BC$).

So the right triangles $FF'D$ and $EE'D$ have equal legs, and by Pythagoras $DF=DE$. Thus triangle $DEF$ is isosceles. (In fact $DF^2=\tfrac94a^2+\tfrac14AN^2=\tfrac94a^2+\tfrac{15}4a^2=6a^2$, while $FE=\tfrac12BC=3a$, so $DE=DF\ne FE$.)

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Take $BD=1$, so $AB=AD=CD=2$. Put $B=(0,0)$, $D=(1,0)$, $C=(3,0)$. Since $AB=AD$, the point $A$ is on the perpendicular bisector $x=\tfrac12$ of $BD$, and $AB=2$ gives \[A=\Bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{2}\Bigr).\] The midpoints are \[F=\Bigl(\tfrac14,\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}4\Bigr),\qquad E=\Bigl(\tfrac{1/2+3}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}4\Bigr)=\Bigl(\tfrac74,\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}4\Bigr).\] Then \[DF^2=\Bigl(1-\tfrac14\Bigr)^2+\tfrac{15}{16}=\tfrac{9}{16}+\tfrac{15}{16}=\tfrac32,\qquad DE^2=\Bigl(\tfrac74-1\Bigr)^2+\tfrac{15}{16}=\tfrac32 .\] So $DF=DE$ and triangle $DEF$ is isosceles. (Geometrically: $F$ and $E$ are at the same height and their $x$-coordinates $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac74$ are symmetric about $x=1$, the vertical line through $D$.)

Problem 5

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Contradiction and Elimination

On an ordinary clock-face the numbers in adjacent positions always differ either by $1$ or $11$.

Is it possible to re-arrange the numbers $1$ to $12$ round the clock-face so that the two numbers in adjacent positions always differ either by $3$, $4$, or $5$?

Show the answer

No

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Small and large numbers must alternate)

Suppose such an arrangement exists. Call $1,2,3,10,11,12$ the outer numbers and $4,5,6,7,8,9$ the inner numbers.

Two outer numbers cannot be neighbours: two of $1,2,3$ (or two of $10,11,12$) differ by at most $2$, and a number from $\{1,2,3\}$ and one from $\{10,11,12\}$ differ by at least $7$. So each of the $6$ outer numbers has inner numbers on both sides. With $6$ outer and $6$ inner numbers on a circle of $12$ and no two outer numbers adjacent, the two kinds must alternate. Then every inner number also has outer numbers on both sides.

But the number $4$ can only be next to numbers differing from it by $3$, $4$ or $5$, that is, to $1$, $7$, $8$, $9$. Its only possible outer neighbour is $1$, so it cannot have two outer neighbours. This contradiction shows that the answer is No.

Solution 2 (Counting inner-outer neighbour pairs)

Call $1,2,3,10,11,12$ outer and $4,\dots,9$ inner. Two outer numbers differ by at most $2$ or by at least $7$, so in an arrangement of the required kind no two outer numbers are adjacent. Each outer number therefore has two inner neighbours, which makes $6\cdot2=12$ adjacent (outer, inner) pairs.

Now count the same pairs from the inner side. The possible outer neighbours of each inner number (difference $3$, $4$ or $5$) are: \[4:\{1\},\quad 5:\{1,2,10\},\quad 6:\{1,2,3,10,11\},\quad 7:\{2,3,10,11,12\},\quad 8:\{3,11,12\},\quad 9:\{12\}.\] Each number has only two neighbours on the circle, so $4$ and $9$ each have at most one outer neighbour and the others at most two. That allows at most $1+2+2+2+2+1=10$ (outer, inner) pairs, fewer than $12$. So such an arrangement is impossible: No.

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Search and Elimination

Find all triples of distinct primes $(p,q,r)$ satisfying the equation below. \[(p-5)(q-21)(r-26)=pqr\]

Show the answer

$(p,q,r)=(2,23,3)$ and $(7,2,19)$

Show the solution

Solution (Signs, then a short search)

Signs. No factor on the left is $0$ (that would need $p=5$, and then the left side is $0\ne pqr$; $21$ and $26$ are not prime). The right side is positive, so an even number of the factors $p-5$, $q-21$, $r-26$ is negative. They cannot all be positive: then $0<p-5<p$, $0<q-21<q$, $0<r-26<r$ and the left side would be smaller than $pqr$. So exactly two factors are negative. In each case we write the equation with the absolute values; the factor that stays positive is smaller than its prime, so the product of the other two absolute values must exceed the product of their primes.

Case 1: $p<5$ and $q<21$ (so $r>26$). Then $(5-p)(21-q)(r-26)=pqr$, and since $r-26<r$ we need $(5-p)(21-q)>pq$. Solving for $r$: $r=\dfrac{26K}{K-pq}$ with $K=(5-p)(21-q)$.

  • $p=2$: $3(21-q)>2q$ gives $q\le12$, so $q\in\{3,5,7,11\}$, with $(K,K-pq)=(54,48),(48,38),(42,28),(30,8)$ and $r=\tfrac{1404}{48},\tfrac{1248}{38},\tfrac{1092}{28}=39,\tfrac{780}{8}$: none is a prime.

  • $p=3$: $2(21-q)>3q$ gives $q\le8$, so $q\in\{2,5,7\}$, with $r=\tfrac{988}{32},\tfrac{832}{17},\tfrac{728}{7}=104$: none is a prime.

Case 2: $p<5$ and $r<26$ (so $q>21$). Now $(5-p)(q-21)(26-r)=pqr$, we need $(5-p)(26-r)>pr$, and $q=\dfrac{21K}{K-pr}$ with $K=(5-p)(26-r)$.

  • $p=2$: $3(26-r)>2r$ gives $r\le15$, $r\in\{3,5,7,11,13\}$, and $q=\tfrac{21\cdot69}{63}=23,\ \tfrac{21\cdot63}{53},\ \tfrac{21\cdot57}{43},\ \tfrac{21\cdot45}{23},\ \tfrac{21\cdot39}{13}=63$. Only $r=3$, $q=23$ works.

  • $p=3$: $2(26-r)>3r$ gives $r\le10$, $r\in\{2,5,7\}$, and $q=\tfrac{21\cdot48}{42}=24,\ \tfrac{21\cdot42}{27},\ \tfrac{21\cdot38}{17}$: none is a prime.

Case 3: $q<21$ and $r<26$ (so $p>5$, $p\ge7$). Now $(p-5)(21-q)(26-r)=pqr$, so \[p=\frac{5(21-q)(26-r)}{(21-q)(26-r)-qr}=\frac{5(21-q)(26-r)}{546-26q-21r}.\] If $q$ and $r$ are both odd, then $21-q$ is even while $546-26q-21r$ is odd, so $p$ would be even, impossible for a prime $p\ge7$. So $q=2$ or $r=2$.

  • $q=2$: with $s=26-r$ (odd), $p=\dfrac{95s}{21s-52}$. The condition $p\ge7$ means $95s\ge7(21s-52)$, i.e. $s\le7$, and $s=26-r$ with $r\le23$ prime gives $s\in\{3,7\}$: $s=3$ gives $\tfrac{285}{11}$, not an integer; $s=7$ gives $p=\tfrac{665}{95}=7$ with $r=19$.

  • $r=2$: with $t=21-q$ (even), $p=\dfrac{120t}{26t-42}=\dfrac{60t}{13t-21}$, and $p\ge7$ means $t\le4$, so $t\in\{2,4\}$, giving $\tfrac{120}{5}=24$ or $\tfrac{240}{31}$: no prime.

Answer. $\boxed{(p,q,r)=(2,23,3)\text{ or }(7,2,19)}$. Check: $(-3)(2)(-23)=138=2\cdot23\cdot3$ and $(2)(-19)(-7)=266=7\cdot2\cdot19$.

Hamilton

Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

A palindromic sum is a sum of two palindromic numbers. For example, $2024=2002+22$ and $2025=1551+474$.

  • (a) Find, with proof, the next integer after $2025$ which cannot be written as a palindromic sum.

  • (b) Find, with proof, the next integer after $2025$ which can be written as a palindromic sum in more than one way.

Show the answer

(a) $2028$; (b) $2035$

Show the solution

Solution (Working modulo 110)

Let $n$ be an integer with $2026\le n\le2035$, and suppose $n=P+Q$ with palindromes $P\ge Q$. Then $P\ge\tfrac n2>1000$, so $P$ is a four-digit palindrome at most $n$: either $P=2002$ or $P=\overline{1aa1}=1001+110a$ with a digit $a$. (Whether or not $0$ counts as a palindrome does not matter: $n$ itself is not a palindrome.)

$P=2002$. Then $Q=n-2002$ is between $24$ and $33$, and the only palindrome there is $33$; this happens only for $n=2035$.

$P=1001+110a$. Put $N=n-1001$ (so $1025\le N\le1034$). Then $Q=N-110a$, so $Q\equiv N\pmod{110}$, that is, $Q\equiv N\pmod{10}$ and $Q\equiv N\pmod{11}$. Conversely every palindrome $Q\le N$ with $Q\equiv N\pmod{110}$ and $Q\ge N-990$ gives a digit $a=\frac{N-Q}{110}$ and a representation. The palindrome $Q$ cannot have four digits (the only four-digit palindromes up to $1034$ are $1001$ and $1111$, and $Q=1001$ would need $N\equiv1001\pmod{110}$, i.e. $n=2002$). Let $d$ be the last digit of $N$, which is the last digit of $Q$.

  • One digit: $Q=d$, which needs $d\equiv N\pmod{11}$.

  • Two digits: $Q=11d$, which needs $N\equiv0\pmod{11}$.

  • Three digits: $Q=\overline{dyd}=101d+10y\equiv2d-y\pmod{11}$, so $y\equiv2d-N\pmod{11}$. This determines $y$ uniquely, and there is no such $Q$ if the remainder is $10$. All such $Q$ lie between $101$ and $999$, so the conditions $Q\le N$, $Q\ge N-990$ hold.

Since $1025=11\cdot93+2$, for $n=2026,2027,\dots,2035$ we have \[d=5,6,7,8,9,0,1,2,3,4,\qquad N\bmod 11=2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9,10,0 .\] The one-digit case never occurs ($d\not\equiv N$ in every column), and the two-digit case only for $n=2035$, with $Q=44$ (and $a=9$, $P=1991$). In the three-digit case, $y\equiv2d-N$ is: \[n=2026:\ y=8\ (Q=585),\qquad n=2027:\ y=9\ (Q=696),\qquad n=2028:\ y\equiv10,\ \text{none},\qquad n=2035:\ y=8\ (Q=484),\] and for $n=2029,\dots,2034$ there is at most this one three-digit candidate (when $d=0$, for $n=2031$, there is none, since a palindrome cannot end in $0$).

(a) $2026=1441+585$ and $2027=1331+696$ are palindromic sums, while $2028$ has no representation at all. The answer is $\boxed{2028}$.

(b) For $2026\le n\le2034$ the only possible representation is the single three-digit case, so there is at most one. For $n=2035$ there are three: \[2035=2002+33=1991+44=1551+484 .\] So the answer is $\boxed{2035}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Difference of Squares and Related Forms

Let $f(x)$ denote the fractional part of $x^2$. For example, $f(1.1)=0.21$. How many positive fractions, $x$, less than $1$ satisfy $f(20+x)=f(26-x)$?

Show the answer

$91$

Show the solution

Solution (A difference of squares)

Two numbers have the same fractional part exactly when their difference is an integer. So the condition is that \[(20+x)^2-(26-x)^2=\bigl((20+x)-(26-x)\bigr)\bigl((20+x)+(26-x)\bigr)=(2x-6)\cdot46=92x-276\] is an integer, that is, $92x$ is an integer. For $0<x<1$ this means $x=\tfrac{k}{92}$ with $k=1,2,\dots,91$, and each of these is a fraction that works. So there are $\boxed{91}$ such fractions.

(Equivalently: as $x$ runs from $0$ to $1$, the difference $92x-276$ increases from $-276$ to $-184$ and passes each of the $91$ integers $-275,\dots,-185$ exactly once.)

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Parity Arguments

Starting at $(0,0)$, a frog makes $99$ jumps of distance $\sqrt5$ between points on the lines $y=-1$, $y=0$ and $y=1$, increasing its $x$-coordinate by $1$ or $2$ each time. How many possibilities are there for the frog's final position?

Show the answer

$149$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting the long jumps)

A jump of length $\sqrt5$ that increases $x$ by $1$ or $2$ changes $(x,y)$ by $(1,\pm2)$ or $(2,\pm1)$, because $1^2+2^2=5$. Call these steep and flat jumps. Since $y$ stays in $\{-1,0,1\}$, a steep jump goes from $y=1$ to $y=-1$ or back, and a flat jump goes between $y=0$ and $y=\pm1$.

Let $b$ be the number of flat jumps; there are $99-b$ steep ones, and the final $x$-coordinate is $(99-b)+2b=99+b$. The first jump starts at $y=0$, so it is flat; hence $1\le b\le99$. A steep jump keeps $y$ odd, while a flat jump switches between $y=0$ and $y$ odd; so the final $y$ is $0$ if $b$ is even and $\pm1$ if $b$ is odd.

Every such position is reached: make one flat jump to $y=1$ (or to $y=-1$), then all $99-b$ steep jumps (which keep $y=\pm1$), then the remaining $b-1$ flat jumps, alternating between $y=0$ and $y=\pm1$. Choosing the sign of the first jump gives both $y=1$ and $y=-1$ when $b$ is odd (the steep jumps flip the sign $99-b$ times, an even number).

So the final positions are $(99+b,\pm1)$ for odd $b$ from $1$ to $99$ ($2\cdot50=100$ positions) and $(99+b,0)$ for even $b$ from $2$ to $98$ ($49$ positions): $\boxed{149}$ in total.

Solution 2 (A parity invariant)

Each jump changes $(x,y)$ by $(1,\pm2)$ or $(2,\pm1)$, since these are the only ways to move a distance $\sqrt5$ with $x$ increasing by $1$ or $2$. In all four cases $x+y$ changes by an odd number ($3$, $-1$, $3$ or $1$). So after $99$ jumps $x+y$ is odd.

The first jump starts on $y=0$, where $(1,\pm2)$ would leave the strip, so it increases $x$ by $2$; the other $98$ jumps increase $x$ by at least $1$ and at most $2$. Hence $100\le x\le198$. So the final position is one of

  • $(x,0)$ with $x$ odd, $101\le x\le197$: $49$ points;

  • $(x,\pm1)$ with $x$ even, $100\le x\le198$: $2\cdot50=100$ points.

All of them are reached. To end at $x=99+b$ with exactly $b$ jumps of type $(2,\pm1)$ ($1\le b\le 99$): first jump $(2,1)$ or $(2,-1)$, then $99-b$ jumps of type $(1,\mp2)$ bouncing between $y=1$ and $y=-1$, then $b-1$ jumps of type $(2,\pm1)$ between $y=\pm1$ and $y=0$. The final $y$ is then forced by the parity of $x+y$, and when it is $\pm1$ the sign is chosen by the first jump.

Hence there are $49+100=\boxed{149}$ possible final positions.

Problem 4

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Direct Similarity

$P$ is a point inside the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ satisfying both conditions below. \[\angle BPC=\angle CPD\] \[\angle DAC=\angle PCD=\angle PBC=\angle BCA\] Prove that $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two pairs of similar triangles)

Let $\alpha$ be the common value of the four equal angles and $\theta=\angle BPC=\angle CPD$.

$AD\parallel BC$. The equal angles $\angle DAC=\angle BCA$ are alternate angles for the lines $AD$, $BC$ and the transversal $AC$ (they lie on opposite sides of $AC$ in the convex quadrilateral), so $AD\parallel BC$.

Triangles $PBC$ and $PCD$. They have $\angle PBC=\alpha=\angle PCD$ and $\angle BPC=\theta=\angle CPD$, so they are similar, with $B\leftrightarrow C$, $P\leftrightarrow P$, $C\leftrightarrow D$. Their third angles are $\angle PCB=\angle PDC=180^\circ-\alpha-\theta$, and \[\frac{BC}{CD}=\frac{PC}{PD}. \tag{1}\]

Triangles $ACD$ and $CPD$. Since $P$ is inside the quadrilateral, $\angle BCD=\angle BCP+\angle PCD=(180^\circ-\alpha-\theta)+\alpha=180^\circ-\theta$. As $AD\parallel BC$, the co-interior angles give $\angle ADC=180^\circ-\angle BCD=\theta$. So triangle $ACD$ has $\angle DAC=\alpha$ and $\angle ADC=\theta$, just like triangle $CPD$, which has $\angle PCD=\alpha$ and $\angle CPD=\theta$. Hence triangles $ACD$ and $CPD$ are similar with $A\leftrightarrow C$, $D\leftrightarrow P$, $C\leftrightarrow D$, and \[\frac{AD}{CP}=\frac{CD}{PD}. \tag{2}\]

Conclusion. From (1) and (2), $BC=\dfrac{CD\cdot PC}{PD}=AD$. The quadrilateral $ABCD$ has a pair of opposite sides $AD$, $BC$ that are parallel and equal, so it is a parallelogram.

Solution 2 (The sine rule)

Let $\alpha$ be the common value of the four equal angles and $\theta=\angle BPC=\angle CPD$.

Since $\angle DAC=\angle BCA$ are alternate angles for the transversal $AC$, the sides $AD$ and $BC$ are parallel. It remains to show $AD=BC$.

In triangle $PBC$ the angles are $\alpha$ at $B$, $\theta$ at $P$ and $180^\circ-\alpha-\theta$ at $C$. In triangle $PCD$ they are $\alpha$ at $C$, $\theta$ at $P$, so $180^\circ-\alpha-\theta$ at $D$. The sine rule in these triangles gives \[BC=\frac{PC\sin\theta}{\sin\alpha},\qquad PC=\frac{CD\sin(\alpha+\theta)}{\sin\theta},\qquad\text{so}\qquad BC=\frac{CD\sin(\alpha+\theta)}{\sin\alpha}.\] In the quadrilateral, $\angle BCD=\angle BCP+\angle PCD=180^\circ-\theta$ (as $P$ lies inside), so $\angle ACD=\angle BCD-\angle BCA=180^\circ-\theta-\alpha$ (as the diagonal $CA$ lies inside the angle $BCD$). The sine rule in triangle $ACD$, with $\angle DAC=\alpha$, gives \[AD=\frac{CD\sin\angle ACD}{\sin\angle DAC}=\frac{CD\sin(\alpha+\theta)}{\sin\alpha}.\] Hence $AD=BC$. Together with $AD\parallel BC$, this shows that $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Structured Two-Variable Models

A pair of fractions, each with both numerator and denominator being positive integers, add up to $2$. The numerator of one, $x$, is the denominator of the other. If $x$ is increased by $10$, the sum is still $2$. Find all possible pairs of fractions for which this is true.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{20}{25}$ and $\dfrac{24}{20}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Subtracting the two equations)

Let the fractions be $\dfrac xa$ and $\dfrac bx$ with positive integers $a$, $b$, $x$. We are told \[\frac xa+\frac bx=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{x+10}{a}+\frac{b}{x+10}=2 .\] Subtracting the first equation from the second: \[\frac{10}{a}=\frac bx-\frac b{x+10}=\frac{10b}{x(x+10)},\qquad\text{so}\qquad ab=x(x+10).\] Multiplying the first equation by $ax$ gives $x^2+ab=2ax$, hence $x^2+x^2+10x=2ax$ and $a=x+5$. Then \[b=\frac{x(x+10)}{x+5}=\frac{(x+5)^2-25}{x+5}=x+5-\frac{25}{x+5}.\] So $x+5$ divides $25$; as $x+5\ge6$, we get $x+5=25$, $x=20$, $a=25$, $b=24$.

Check: $\tfrac{20}{25}+\tfrac{24}{20}=\tfrac45+\tfrac65=2$ and $\tfrac{30}{25}+\tfrac{24}{30}=\tfrac65+\tfrac45=2$. So the only pair is $\boxed{\tfrac{20}{25}\text{ and }\tfrac{24}{20}}$.

Solution 2 (Two roots of one quadratic)

Write the fractions as $\dfrac xa$ and $\dfrac bx$ with positive integers $a$, $b$, $x$. For a positive number $t$, \[\frac ta+\frac bt=2\iff t^2-2at+ab=0 .\] Both $t=x$ and $t=x+10$ satisfy this, and they are different, so they are the two roots of the quadratic. By Vieta's formulas, \[x+(x+10)=2a,\qquad x(x+10)=ab .\] So $a=x+5$ and $b=\dfrac{x(x+10)}{x+5}$. Since $x(x+10)=(x+5)^2-25$, the number $x+5$ must divide $25$, and $x+5\ge6$ forces $x+5=25$. Hence $x=20$, $a=25$, $b=24$, and the fractions are $\boxed{\tfrac{20}{25}\text{ and }\tfrac{24}{20}}$ (that is, $\tfrac45$ and $\tfrac65$ written with the common number $20$). Indeed $\tfrac{20}{25}+\tfrac{24}{20}=2$ and $\tfrac{30}{25}+\tfrac{24}{30}=2$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Worst-Case Thresholds

A padlock has two wheels, each of which has the digits $0$ to $9$ equally spaced. Together, the wheels show a two-digit code. Trying to open it with the wrong code locks it forever, but there is a probe to check if the code is close without locking the padlock forever. It is close if either (i) the code is correct or (ii) the correct code can be shown by rotating one wheel to an adjacent digit. For example, if the wheels show $02$, the probe will show it is close if the correct code is $01$, $02$, $03$, $12$ or $92$. Find the minimum number of times to use the probe to guarantee discovering the correct code.

Show the answer

$22$

Show the solution

Solution (A perfect tiling and a worst case)

Think of the codes as the $100$ points $(i,j)$ with $i,j\in\{0,\dots,9\}$, each coordinate taken modulo $10$ (the wheels are circular). A probe at $c$ answers “close” exactly when the correct code is in the cross $N(c)$ made of $c$ and its four neighbours $c\pm(1,0)$, $c\pm(0,1)$: five codes.

At least $22$ probes are needed. Suppose that the answer is “not close” for each of the first $19$ probes; this is possible because $19$ crosses cover at most $95$ codes, so some code is consistent with all these answers. After them at least $100-95=5$ codes are still possible. Each further probe has two answers, so with $m$ more probes at most $2^m$ codes can be told apart; $2^2=4<5$, so at least $3$ more probes are needed. Hence no method can guarantee success with fewer than $19+3=22$ probes.

$22$ probes suffice. The $20$ crosses centred at the points $(i,j)$ with $j\equiv2i\pmod5$ cover every code exactly once: the five codes of a cross centred at $(i,j)$ have values of $j-2i$ congruent to $0$, $\mp2$, $\pm1$ modulo $5$, all different, so each code $(u,v)$ belongs to exactly one cross, namely the one whose centre is the member of $N((u,v))$ with $j-2i\equiv0\pmod 5$. (Since $10$ is a multiple of $5$, this is consistent around the wheels.) There are $10\cdot2=20$ centres.

Probe these centres one by one. If the answer is “close” at some probe (at most the $19$th), or if the first $19$ answers are all “not close” (then the code is in the $20$th cross, without probing it), we know the code lies in one known cross $\{c,\ c+e_1,\ c+e_2,\ c-e_1,\ c-e_2\}$, where $e_1=(1,0)$, $e_2=(0,1)$. Now

  • probe $c+e_1+e_2$; its cross meets the known one in $\{c+e_1,c+e_2\}$. If “close”, probe $c+2e_1$, whose cross meets it only in $c+e_1$; this decides between $c+e_1$ and $c+e_2$.

  • If “not close”, the code is $c$, $c-e_1$ or $c-e_2$. Probe $c-e_1-e_2$ (its cross meets the known one in $\{c-e_1,c-e_2\}$): if “not close” the code is $c$; if “close”, probe $c-2e_1$ to decide between $c-e_1$ and $c-e_2$.

This takes at most $3$ probes, so in total at most $19+3=22$.

Hence the minimum is $\boxed{22}$.

Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization · Prime Factorization

From the set $\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}$ six different integers $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$ are to be chosen, so that the following equation is true for all values of $x$. \[A\bigl(x+B(x+C)\bigr)=D\bigl(x+E(x+F)\bigr)\]

  • (a) Show that for $n=9$ this is possible.

  • (b) Prove that this is not possible for $n=8$.

Show the answer

(a) e.g. $A,B,C,D,E,F=2,3,6,4,1,9$; (b) proof

Show the solution

Solution (Comparing coefficients, then a perfect square)

Expanding, \[A\bigl(x+B(x+C)\bigr)=A(1+B)\,x+ABC,\qquad D\bigl(x+E(x+F)\bigr)=D(1+E)\,x+DEF .\] Two linear expressions are equal for all $x$ exactly when the coefficients agree: \[A(1+B)=D(1+E)\qquad\text{and}\qquad ABC=DEF .\]

(a) Take $A=2$, $B=3$, $C=6$, $D=4$, $E=1$, $F=9$, six different numbers from $\{1,\dots,9\}$. Then $A(1+B)=8=D(1+E)$ and $ABC=36=DEF$; indeed \[2\bigl(x+3(x+6)\bigr)=8x+36=4\bigl(x+1\cdot(x+9)\bigr).\]

(b) Suppose six different numbers from $\{1,\dots,8\}$ work. From $ABC=DEF$, the product of all six numbers is $ABC\cdot DEF=(ABC)^2$, a perfect square.

The prime $5$ divides only one number in $\{1,\dots,8\}$, namely $5$. If $5$ were among the six numbers, it would be on one side of $ABC=DEF$, and $5$ would divide one side but not the other. So $5$ is not used; in the same way $7$ is not used. Hence the six numbers are exactly $1,2,3,4,6,8$, and their product is \[1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot6\cdot8=1152=2^7\cdot3^2,\] which is not a perfect square (the exponent of $2$ is odd). This contradiction shows that it is impossible for $n=8$.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Pythagorean Theorem

A square $ABCD$ has each side of length $1$. $X$ lies on $AB$ and $Y$ lies on $AD$ with $AX=x$, $AY=y$ and $x<y$. Triangle $AXY$ is removed to leave a pentagon $BCDYX$. The perimeter of $BCDYX$ is $\frac{29}{30}$ of the perimeter of $ABCD$. The area of $BCDYX$ is also $\frac{29}{30}$ of the area of $ABCD$. Find the length of $AX$.

Show the answer

$AX=\frac16$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sum and product of the legs)

Let $c=XY=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The pentagon has perimeter $(1-x)+1+1+(1-y)+c=4-x-y+c$ and area $1-\tfrac12xy$. The conditions are \[4-x-y+c=\tfrac{29}{30}\cdot4,\qquad 1-\tfrac12xy=\tfrac{29}{30},\] that is, \[x+y-c=\tfrac{2}{15},\qquad xy=\tfrac1{15}.\] Let $s=x+y$. Then $c=s-\tfrac2{15}$ and $c^2=x^2+y^2=s^2-2xy=s^2-\tfrac2{15}$. Hence \[s^2-\tfrac{4}{15}s+\tfrac{4}{225}=s^2-\tfrac{2}{15},\qquad \tfrac{4}{15}s=\tfrac{2}{15}+\tfrac{4}{225}=\tfrac{34}{225},\qquad s=\tfrac{17}{30}.\] So $x$ and $y$ are the roots of $t^2-\tfrac{17}{30}t+\tfrac1{15}=0$, i.e. $30t^2-17t+2=0$, $(6t-1)(5t-2)=0$. The roots are $\tfrac16$ and $\tfrac25$, and $x<y$ gives \[AX=x=\boxed{\tfrac16}.\] Check: $y=\tfrac25$, $c=\sqrt{\tfrac1{36}+\tfrac4{25}}=\tfrac{13}{30}$, and $x+y-c=\tfrac{5+12-13}{30}=\tfrac2{15}$.

Solution 2 (Factorising a difference of squares)

Let $c=XY$, the hypotenuse of the right triangle $AXY$. Removing the triangle replaces the path $X\to A\to Y$ (length $x+y$) by $XY$, so the perimeter drops by $x+y-c$, and the area drops by $\tfrac12xy$. Both drops are $\tfrac1{30}$ of the original values $4$ and $1$: \[x+y-c=\tfrac2{15},\qquad \tfrac12xy=\tfrac1{30}.\] By Pythagoras, $c^2=x^2+y^2$, so \[(x+y-c)(x+y+c)=(x+y)^2-c^2=2xy=\tfrac{2}{15}.\] Dividing by $x+y-c=\tfrac2{15}$ gives $x+y+c=1$: the removed triangle has perimeter $1$. Adding and subtracting, \[x+y=\tfrac12\Bigl(1+\tfrac2{15}\Bigr)=\tfrac{17}{30},\qquad c=\tfrac{13}{30}.\] With $xy=\tfrac1{15}=\tfrac{2}{30}$, the legs are the roots of $30t^2-17t+2=0$, namely $\tfrac16$ and $\tfrac25$. Since $x<y$, $AX=\boxed{\tfrac16}$.

Problem 3

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 27: Base Systems and Positional Arithmetic · Digit Patterns Across Bases

A number, $g$, is good if $g=2^a+2^b$ for two different non-negative integers $a$ and $b$. A number, $G$, is great if it is both good and the product of two different good numbers. How many numbers less than one million are great?

Show the answer

$16$

Show the solution

Solution (Odd parts)

Good numbers. If $a<b$ then $2^a+2^b=2^a(2^k+1)$ with $k=b-a\ge1$. So the good numbers are exactly the numbers $2^m(2^k+1)$ with $m\ge0$, $k\ge1$; the odd part of a good number is $2^k+1$ with $k\ge1$.

Which products are good? Let $g_1=2^m(2^k+1)$ and $g_2=2^{m'}(2^{k'}+1)$ be good. Their product is $2^{m+m'}(2^k+1)(2^{k'}+1)$, whose odd part is $(2^k+1)(2^{k'}+1)$. The product is good exactly when this odd part equals $2^K+1$ for some $K\ge1$, that is, when \[2^{k+k'}+2^k+2^{k'}=2^K .\]

  • If $k<k'$, the left side is $2^k\bigl(2^{k'}+2^{k'-k}+1\bigr)$, and the bracket is odd (two even terms plus $1$) and greater than $1$; so the left side is not a power of $2$.

  • If $k=k'$, the left side is $2^{2k}+2^{k+1}=2^{k+1}\bigl(2^{k-1}+1\bigr)$. For $k\ge2$ the bracket is odd and greater than $1$; for $k=1$ it equals $2$ and the left side is $8=2^3$.

So the product of two good numbers is good only when both have odd part $3$: $g_1=3\cdot2^m$, $g_2=3\cdot2^{m'}$, and then $g_1g_2=9\cdot2^{m+m'}=2^{m+m'+3}+2^{m+m'}$ is indeed good.

Great numbers. For $g_1\ne g_2$ we need $m\ne m'$, so $M=m+m'\ge1$; conversely every $M\ge1$ occurs ($m=0$, $m'=M$). Hence the great numbers are exactly $9\cdot2^M$ with $M\ge1$: $18,36,72,\dots$. The condition $9\cdot2^M<10^6$ means $2^M<111111.\overline{1}$, i.e. $M\le16$ (as $2^{16}=65536$ and $2^{17}=131072$). So there are $\boxed{16}$ great numbers below one million.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Process Termination and Reachability

Dennis has a $5\times5$ grid of unit squares, initially white, and an infinite quantity of red and black paint. As often as he likes, Dennis can pick any row or column and paint it red or black. How many different paintings can Dennis construct that contain $3$ red squares and $22$ black?

Show the answer

$500$

Show the solution

Solution (A forbidden 2 by 2 pattern)

Only the last painting of each row and each column matters: the colour of a square is the colour of whichever of its row and its column was painted later (white if neither was painted).

A forbidden pattern. Take two rows and two columns, meeting in four squares. If none of these four lines was painted, the squares are white. Otherwise, the one of the four lines painted last gives both of its two squares (among the four) its own colour, because the crossing lines were painted earlier or not at all. Hence in a painting without white squares, the four squares never look like \[\begin{matrix}\text{R}&\text{B}\\\text{B}&\text{R}\end{matrix}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\begin{matrix}\text{B}&\text{R}\\\text{R}&\text{B}\end{matrix}\] (each of the four lines would contain one R and one B). In other words: if two red squares are in different rows and different columns, at least one of the other two corners of their rectangle is red.

The possible shapes of three red squares. If some two red squares are in different rows and columns, the third red square must be one of the other two corners of their rectangle: the three red squares form an L, three corners of a rectangle. Otherwise every two red squares share a row or a column; then all three share one line (if $P$, $Q$ share a row and $P$, $R$ share a column, then $Q$, $R$ share neither). So the red squares are three squares of one row or column, or an L.

Both shapes can be made.

  • Three red squares in row $r$: paint the other four rows black, then row $r$ red, then the two columns that should be black in row $r$ black.

  • An L with red squares at $(r,c)$, $(r,c')$, $(r',c)$ and black at $(r',c')$: paint, in this order, row $r'$ black, column $c'$ black, column $c$ red, the three other rows black, row $r$ red, the three other columns black. Checking each square against the later of its row and column: $(r,c)$, $(r,c')$ get red from row $r$, $(r',c)$ gets red from column $c$, $(r',c')$ black from column $c'$, the other squares of row $r$ black from the last three columns, the other squares of column $c$ black from the three rows painted after it, and everything else black.

Counting. Three squares in one line: $10$ lines, $\binom53=10$ choices each, so $100$. L-shapes: choose two rows and two columns, $\binom52^2=100$ rectangles, and one of the $4$ corners to be black: $400$. In total $100+400=\boxed{500}$ paintings.

Problem 5

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Distance from Perpendicular Structure

Let $ABCD$ be an isosceles trapezium with $AD$ parallel to $BC$ and $\angle BAD=\angle CDA$. $E$ lies on $AD$ in such a way that the ratio below is satisfied. \[AE:EB:AB:ED:BC=1:3:k:7:9\] Find the value of $k$.

Show the answer

$k=\sqrt7$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Dropping a perpendicular)

Scale so that $AE=1$, $EB=3$, $AB=k$, $ED=7$, $BC=9$. Then $AD=AE+ED=8<9=BC$. In an isosceles trapezium the longer parallel side overhangs the shorter one equally at both ends, by $\tfrac{9-8}{2}=\tfrac12$.

Let $H$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to the line $AD$. Then $H$ lies on the extension of $DA$ beyond $A$, with $HA=\tfrac12$, so $HE=HA+AE=\tfrac32$. Let $h=BH$. Pythagoras in the right triangles $BHE$ and $BHA$ gives \[EB^2=h^2+\Bigl(\tfrac32\Bigr)^2,\qquad AB^2=h^2+\Bigl(\tfrac12\Bigr)^2 .\] Subtracting: $9-k^2=\tfrac94-\tfrac14=2$, so $k^2=7$ and \[k=\boxed{\sqrt7}.\] (Then $h^2=\tfrac{27}4$, a genuine trapezium.)

Solution 2 (The cosine rule)

Take $AE=1$, $EB=3$, $AB=k$, $ED=7$, $BC=9$, so $AD=8$. Since the trapezium is isosceles and $BC$ is longer than $AD$ by $1$, the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$ is at distance $\tfrac12$ from $B$. Hence the angle $\beta=\angle ABC$ satisfies $\cos\beta=\dfrac{1/2}{k}=\dfrac1{2k}$, and since $AD\parallel BC$, $\angle BAE=\angle BAD=180^\circ-\beta$, with $\cos\angle BAE=-\dfrac1{2k}$.

The cosine rule in triangle $ABE$: \[EB^2=AB^2+AE^2-2\cdot AB\cdot AE\cos\angle BAE=k^2+1+2\cdot k\cdot1\cdot\frac{1}{2k}=k^2+2 .\] So $9=k^2+2$ and $k=\boxed{\sqrt7}$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Extremal Geometric Configurations

One hundred points are arranged into a regular array of ten equally-spaced rows and columns. How many ways are there to choose ten of the points, one from each row and column, so that seven of the chosen points lie strictly inside (not on the perimeter of) the triangle formed by the other three?

Show the answer

$4$

Show the solution

Solution (Forcing the vertices)

Use coordinates $(x,y)$ with $x$ the column and $y$ the row, both in $\{0,1,\dots,9\}$. The ten chosen points have different $x$'s and different $y$'s. Call the three points forming the triangle its vertices; the other seven must be strictly inside.

Step 1: a corner is a vertex. The triangle lies in the square $[0,9]^2$, so a point strictly inside it lies strictly inside the square. Hence the chosen points in row $0$, row $9$, column $0$ and column $9$ are vertices. These are four roles for three vertices, so some vertex has two roles: it is a corner of the square. Two corners cannot both be chosen unless they are opposite (adjacent corners share a row or a column).

Step 2: not two opposite corners. Suppose $(0,0)$ and $(9,9)$ are vertices and $R$ is the third. The other eight chosen points ($R$ and the seven inside) use rows $1,\dots,8$ and columns $1,\dots,8$, so they are $(x,\sigma(x))$ for a rearrangement $\sigma$ of $1,\dots,8$. $R$ is not on the line $y=x$ (the triangle is not flat), and the inside of the triangle lies strictly on $R$'s side of this line. So either $\sigma(x)>x$ for all eight points or $\sigma(x)<x$ for all, which is impossible because $\sum\sigma(x)=\sum x$. The other pair of opposite corners is the same after a reflection.

Step 3: setting up. So exactly one corner is a vertex. Rotating the array, we may take it to be $O=(0,0)$ and multiply the final count by $4$ (each configuration has exactly one corner vertex). The other two vertices are the points in column $9$ and in row $9$: $P=(9,a)$ and $Q=(b,9)$ with $1\le a,b\le8$. The seven inside points use the columns $\{1,\dots,8\}\setminus\{b\}$ and rows $\{1,\dots,8\}\setminus\{a\}$. A point $(x,y)$ is strictly inside $OPQ$ exactly when \[9y>ax,\qquad 9x>by,\qquad (9-a)x+(9-b)y<81-ab,\] (the last being the side of the line $PQ$ containing $O$; one checks $P$ and $Q$ both satisfy $(9-a)x+(9-b)y=81-ab$).

Step 4: $a=b=8$. Suppose $a\ne8$. Then some inside point lies in row $8$, say $(x,8)$. From $9x>8b$ we get $x>\tfrac{8b}9\ge b-1$, so $x\ge b$, and $x\ne b$ (column $b$ is used by $Q$), so $x\ge b+1$. The third condition gives $(9-a)x<81-ab-8(9-b)=9+8b-ab$, hence \[(9-a)(b+1)<9+8b-ab,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad 9b+9-ab-a<9+8b-ab,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad b<a .\] By the same argument with rows and columns exchanged, if $b\ne8$ then the inside point in column $8$ forces $a<b$. If $a\ne8$ and $b\ne8$ we get both $b<a$ and $a<b$; if $a\ne8=b$ we get $8<a$; if $b\ne8=a$ we get $8<b$. All are impossible, so $a=b=8$.

Step 5: the inside points. Now $P=(9,8)$, $Q=(8,9)$, and the seven inside points use rows and columns $1,\dots,7$. For such a point, $9y>8x$ and $9x>8y$ give $|x-y|<\tfrac{\min(x,y)}{8}<1$, so $x=y$. Thus the inside points must be $(1,1),\dots,(7,7)$, and these are indeed strictly inside ($9k>8k$ and $2k<17$).

So for each corner there is exactly one configuration, and the answer is $\boxed{4}$.
