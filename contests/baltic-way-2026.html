Problem 1

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Turbo is jumping on the positive integers in such a way that at time $t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ Turbo is at the integer $a_t$. Turbo begins at $a_1$ and chooses the numbers they visit in such a way that \[\begin{array}{ll} a_t\cdot a_{t+1}\ge t+401 & \text{if $t$ is odd},\\ a_t\cdot a_{t+1}\le t+401 & \text{if $t$ is even} \end{array}\] for all positive integers $t$.

Given that no $410$ consecutive integers are all visited by Turbo, prove that they visit at least one of the numbers $2026$ and $2027$.

Show the solution – Even-indexed terms climb, odd-indexed terms settle

Write $E_k=a_{2k}$ for $k\ge1$ and $O_k=a_{2k+1}$ for $k\ge0$. The condition for the odd time $t=2k+1$ and for the even time $t=2k$ reads \[O_kE_{k+1}\ge 2k+402\quad(k\ge0),\qquad\qquad E_kO_k\le 2k+401\quad(k\ge1).\tag{1}\]

Step 1: the even-indexed terms strictly increase. For $k\ge1$, (1) gives $O_kE_{k+1}\ge 2k+402>2k+401\ge E_kO_k$, and dividing by $O_k>0$ we get $E_{k+1}>E_k$. Since $E_1\ge1$ and each step adds at least $1$, we have $E_k\ge k$ for all $k\ge1$.

Step 2: once an odd-indexed term is $1$, all later odd-indexed terms are $1$. If $O_k=1$, then (1) gives $E_{k+1}\ge 2k+402$, and then $O_{k+1}\le \dfrac{2k+403}{E_{k+1}}\le\dfrac{2k+403}{2k+402}<2$, so $O_{k+1}=1$.

Step 3: late odd-indexed terms are at most $2$. For $k\ge 402$, by (1) and Step 1, \[O_k\le\frac{2k+401}{E_k}\le\frac{2k+401}{k}=2+\frac{401}{k}<3 .\]

Now look at $a_{1625}=O_{812}$.

Case $O_{812}=1$. By Step 2 also $O_{813}=1$. Then (1) with $k=812$ gives $E_{813}\ge 1624+402=2026$, and (1) with $k=813$ gives $E_{813}=E_{813}O_{813}\le 1626+401=2027$. Hence $a_{1626}=E_{813}$ is $2026$ or $2027$.

Case $O_{812}\ge2$. By Step 2, no $O_k$ with $k\le 812$ equals $1$ (otherwise $O_{812}$ would be $1$), so $O_k\ge2$ for $k\le812$. Together with Step 3, $O_k=2$ for $402\le k\le 812$. For $402\le k\le 811$ both $O_k$ and $O_{k+1}$ equal $2$, so (1) gives \[2E_{k+1}\ge 2k+402\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2E_{k+1}\le 2(k+1)+401=2k+403,\] hence $k+201\le E_{k+1}\le k+201.5$, that is, $a_{2k+2}=E_{k+1}=k+201$. As $k$ runs through $402,403,\dots,811$, Turbo visits every integer from $603$ to $1012$: these are $410$ consecutive integers, which is excluded by the hypothesis.

So the first case occurs, and Turbo visits $2026$ or $2027$ (namely at time $1626$).

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Substitution and Special Inputs

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ such that \[f(x+2y+f(x))=y+f(y+2x)\] holds for all $x,y>0$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x$

Show the solution – Equal arguments, then unbounded growth

The answer is $\boxed{f(x)=x}$. It works: both sides of the equation equal $2x+2y$.

Conversely, let $f$ satisfy the equation.

Step 1: $f(x)\ge x$ for all $x>0$. Suppose $f(x_0)<x_0$ for some $x_0$. Put $x=x_0$ and $y=x_0-f(x_0)>0$. Then \[x+2y+f(x)=x_0+2x_0-2f(x_0)+f(x_0)=3x_0-f(x_0)=y+2x,\] so the equation becomes $f(z)=y+f(z)$ with $z=3x_0-f(x_0)$, that is, $y=0$, a contradiction.

Step 2: $f(x)\le x$ for all $x>0$. Let $g(x)=f(x)-x\ge0$. Writing $f(t)=t+g(t)$ on both sides, the left side is $(x+2y+f(x))+g(x+2y+f(x))=2x+2y+g(x)+g\bigl(2x+2y+g(x)\bigr)$ and the right side is $y+(y+2x)+g(y+2x)$. Hence \[g(2x+y)=g(x)+g\bigl(2x+2y+g(x)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }x,y>0.\tag{1}\] Let $x>0$ and $u>2x$, and put $y=u-2x>0$ in (1): \[g(u)=g(x)+g(w),\qquad w=2u-2x+g(x).\tag{2}\] Note that $w\ge 2u-2x>4x-2x=2x$.

We prove by induction on $N\ge0$: for all $x>0$ and all $u>2x$, $g(u)\ge N\,g(x)$. For $N=0$ this is $g\ge0$. If it holds for $N$, take $x$ and $u>2x$; in (2) we have $w>2x$, so $g(w)\ge N\,g(x)$ and $g(u)=g(x)+g(w)\ge (N+1)\,g(x)$.

Fix $x>0$ and take $u=3x$: then $g(3x)\ge N\,g(x)$ for every $N$, and since $g(3x)$ is a fixed real number and $g(x)\ge0$, this forces $g(x)=0$.

Therefore $f(x)=x$ for all $x>0$.

Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Let $n>1$ and let $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{n+1}$ be positive real numbers with $x_{n+1}=x_1$ satisfying \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i}>2n.\] Show that \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i^3+\frac{1}{x_i^3}\right)>5n.\]

Show solution 1 – AM-GM with three terms

For each $i$ apply the AM-GM inequality to the three positive numbers $x_{i+1}^3$, $\dfrac1{x_i^3}$ and $1$: \[x_{i+1}^3+\frac1{x_i^3}+1\ \ge\ 3\sqrt[3]{x_{i+1}^3\cdot\frac{1}{x_i^3}\cdot1}=3\,\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i}.\] Add these $n$ inequalities. Because $x_{n+1}=x_1$, the numbers $x_2,x_3,\dots,x_{n+1}$ are $x_1,\dots,x_n$ in another order, so $\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i+1}^3=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^3$. Therefore \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(x_i^3+\frac1{x_i^3}\Bigr)+n\ \ge\ 3\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i}\ >\ 3\cdot 2n=6n,\] and so $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(x_i^3+\frac1{x_i^3}\Bigr)>5n$.

Show solution 2 – Through the squares

For each $i$, $\bigl(x_{i+1}-\frac1{x_i}\bigr)^2\ge0$ gives \[\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i}\le\frac12\Bigl(x_{i+1}^2+\frac1{x_i^2}\Bigr).\] Adding over $i=1,\dots,n$ and using $x_{n+1}=x_1$ (so that $\sum x_{i+1}^2=\sum x_i^2$), we get \[\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(x_i^2+\frac1{x_i^2}\Bigr)\ \ge\ 2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i}\ >\ 4n.\tag{1}\] Now compare cubes with squares. For $t>0$ put $s=t+\frac1t\ge2$ and $q=t^2+\frac1{t^2}\ge2$. Then \[t^3+\frac1{t^3}=\Bigl(t+\frac1t\Bigr)\Bigl(t^2-1+\frac1{t^2}\Bigr)=s\,(q-1)\ \ge\ 2(q-1),\] because $s\ge2$ and $q-1\ge1>0$. Applying this to every $x_i$ and using (1): \[\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(x_i^3+\frac1{x_i^3}\Bigr)\ \ge\ 2\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(x_i^2+\frac1{x_i^2}\Bigr)-2n\ >\ 8n-2n=6n>5n.\] (This even shows that the sum exceeds $6n$.)

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Algebraic Forcing of Function Form

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ such that \[\bigl(f(x)+f(y)-1\bigr)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)\bigl(2f(x+y)-1\bigr)\] holds for all rational numbers $x,y$.

Show the answer

$f\equiv1$, and $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+c^{x}}$ for a constant $c>0$

Show the solution – Reduction to the exponential Cauchy equation

The solutions are $\boxed{f\equiv 1}$ and $\boxed{f(x)=\dfrac1{1+c^x}}$ for a constant $c>0$ (for $c=1$ this is $f\equiv\frac12$).

Reformulation. Since $f$ never vanishes, we may divide the equation by $f(x)f(y)f(x+y)$; the equation is equivalent to \[\frac1{f(y)}+\frac1{f(x)}-\frac1{f(x)f(y)}=2-\frac1{f(x+y)} .\] Put $k(x)=\dfrac1{f(x)}-1$, a real function on $\mathbb Q$ with $k(x)\ne-1$; conversely $f=\dfrac1{1+k}$. In terms of $k$ the left side is \[(1+k(x))+(1+k(y))-(1+k(x))(1+k(y))=1-k(x)k(y),\] and the right side is $2-(1+k(x+y))=1-k(x+y)$. So $f$ is a solution if and only if \[k(x+y)=k(x)\,k(y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb Q,\tag{1}\] with $k$ never equal to $-1$.

Solving (1). With $x=y=0$: $k(0)=k(0)^2$, so $k(0)\in\{0,1\}$.

If $k(0)=0$, then $k(x)=k(x+0)=k(x)k(0)=0$ for all $x$, which gives $f\equiv1$.

If $k(0)=1$: first $k(x)=k(\tfrac x2)^2\ge0$, and $k(x)k(-x)=k(0)=1$, so $k(x)>0$ for all $x$. Let $c=k(1)>0$. By induction on $N$, (1) gives $k(Nx)=k(x)^N$ for every positive integer $N$ and rational $x$. Hence $k(N)=c^N$ and $k(-N)=1/k(N)=c^{-N}$ for positive integers $N$, and $k(0)=1=c^0$. For a rational $p/q$ with $q\ge1$: $k(p/q)^q=k(p)=c^p$, and since $k(p/q)>0$ we get $k(p/q)=c^{p/q}$. So $k(x)=c^x$ and $f(x)=\dfrac1{1+c^x}$.

Check. The functions $k\equiv0$ and $k(x)=c^x$ ($c>0$) satisfy (1) and are never $-1$, so by the equivalence above $f\equiv1$ and $f(x)=\frac1{1+c^x}$ satisfy the original equation and take nonzero values.

Problem 5

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure · Symmetric and Cyclic Inequalities

Let $n\ge3$ be an integer. Determine the smallest real number $M$ such that \[\frac{x_1-x_2}{x_1+x_2}+\frac{x_2-x_3}{x_2+x_3}+\dots+\frac{x_n-x_1}{x_n+x_1}<M\] holds for all positive real numbers $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n$.

Show the answer

$M=n-2$

Show solution 1 – Rewrite each term with a single fraction

Indices are taken modulo $n$, so $x_{n+1}=x_1$, and $S$ denotes the left side. Since \[\frac{x_i-x_{i+1}}{x_i+x_{i+1}}=1-\frac{2x_{i+1}}{x_i+x_{i+1}},\qquad\text{we have}\qquad S=n-2T,\quad T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i+x_{i+1}}.\]

$S<n-2$ always. Let $X=x_1+\dots+x_n$. Because $n\ge3$, the sum $X$ contains at least one positive term besides $x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$, so $x_i+x_{i+1}<X$ and \[T>\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{x_{i+1}}{X}=\frac{X}{X}=1 .\] Hence $S=n-2T<n-2$.

$n-2$ cannot be lowered. Let $0<\varepsilon<1$ and $x_i=\varepsilon^{\,i-1}$. For $i=1,\dots,n-1$ the $i$-th term is $\dfrac{\varepsilon^{i-1}-\varepsilon^{i}}{\varepsilon^{i-1}+\varepsilon^{i}}=\dfrac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}$, and the last term is $\dfrac{\varepsilon^{n-1}-1}{\varepsilon^{n-1}+1}$. So \[S=(n-1)\,\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}-\frac{1-\varepsilon^{n-1}}{1+\varepsilon^{n-1}}\ \longrightarrow\ (n-1)-1=n-2\qquad(\varepsilon\to0^+).\] Thus for every $M<n-2$ some choice of the $x_i$ gives $S>M$.

The smallest $M$ is therefore $\boxed{n-2}$.

Show solution 2 – Counting the non-positive terms

Indices are taken modulo $n$, so $x_{n+1}=x_1$. Let $\varphi(a,b)=\dfrac{a-b}{a+b}$ for $a,b>0$ and $t_i=\varphi(x_i,x_{i+1})$, so the left side is $S=t_1+\dots+t_n$. We use three facts:

  • $-1<\varphi(a,b)<1$, and $\varphi(a,b)>0$ exactly when $a>b$;

  • $\varphi(a,b)=1-\dfrac{2b}{a+b}=\dfrac{2a}{a+b}-1$ increases in $a$ and decreases in $b$;

  • $\varphi(b,a)=-\varphi(a,b)$.

$S<n-2$ always. Not all $t_i$ are positive, since otherwise $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_n>x_1$.

If at least two of the $t_i$ are $\le0$, the other $n-2$ terms are each less than $1$, so $S<n-2$.

If exactly one $t_i$ is $\le0$, rotate the indices (this does not change $S$) so that it is $t_n$. Then $t_1,\dots,t_{n-1}>0$ means $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_n$. As $n\ge3$, we have $x_2>x_n$, hence \[t_1+t_n=\varphi(x_1,x_2)-\varphi(x_1,x_n)<0,\] because $\varphi(x_1,\cdot)$ is decreasing. The remaining $n-2$ terms $t_2,\dots,t_{n-1}$ are each less than $1$, so again $S<n-2$.

$n-2$ cannot be lowered. For $0<\varepsilon<1$ take $x_i=\varepsilon^{\,i-1}$. Then $t_1=\dots=t_{n-1}=\dfrac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}$ and $t_n=-\dfrac{1-\varepsilon^{n-1}}{1+\varepsilon^{n-1}}$, so $S\to (n-1)-1=n-2$ as $\varepsilon\to0^+$. Hence no $M<n-2$ works, and the answer is $\boxed{n-2}$.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Process Termination and Reachability

A $2027\times2027$ grid consists of unit squares. Some diagonals of the unit squares are drawn, we call them walls. Each unit square contains at most one wall and the top left square contains no walls.

A robot starts from the top left corner of the grid. It starts moving towards the bottom right corner, always moving in straight lines in the direction it is facing. If the robot hits the centre of a unit square containing a wall, it does the following:

  • If the wall is perpendicular to its movement direction, it chooses to turn $90$ degrees left or right.

  • If the wall is parallel to its direction, it keeps moving in the same direction.

The robot stops moving if it meets the edge of the grid.

A unit square is said to be reachable if, for some sequence of choices, the robot visits the interior of that square. Find the least number of walls so that at least half of the squares are reachable or prove that no such configuration of walls exists.

Show the answer

$4050$

Show the solution – Counting the ends of straight runs, and a spiral of walls

Number the rows $1,\dots,n$ from top to bottom and the columns $1,\dots,n$ from left to right, where $n=2027=2m+1$, $m=1013$; $(r,c)$ is the square in row $r$ and column $c$. Call $(r,c)$ dark if $r+c$ is even. A diagonal is a line $c-r=d$ (direction $\searrow$ or $\nwarrow$), an anti-diagonal a line $r+c=s$ (direction $\nearrow$ or $\swarrow$). A wall is of type $\diagdown$ (along a diagonal) or of type $\diagup$ (along an anti-diagonal). A robot moving along a diagonal passes a $\diagdown$ wall and must turn at a $\diagup$ wall onto the anti-diagonal; a robot moving along an anti-diagonal passes a $\diagup$ wall and must turn at a $\diagdown$ wall onto the diagonal. The answer is $\boxed{4050}$.

Only dark squares can be reached. The robot always moves at $45^\circ$. Between the centres of $(r,c)$ and $(r\pm1,c\pm1)$ it passes through the common corner of these two squares and is inside no other square; turns happen at centres. So $r+c$ keeps its parity, and only dark squares are visited. There are $\frac{n^2+1}{2}$ dark squares, while half of all squares is $\frac{n^2}{2}$, not an integer. Hence at least half of the squares are reachable exactly when every dark square is reachable.

At least $4050$ walls are needed. Call a run a maximal straight piece of motion: it starts at the start or at a wall where the robot turns, and it ends where the robot stops or turns. A run is determined by its first square and its direction, so the runs are the initial run and, for each wall, at most two runs (one in each direction along the wall). Call a run long if it contains at least two squares.

The dark squares on the border are: in row $1$, in row $n$, in column $1$ and in column $n$ the $m+1$ squares with odd index, the four corners counted twice; so there are $4(m+1)-4=4m=4052$ of them.

  • Let $Q\ne(1,1)$ be a dark border square, say in row $1$ (the other sides are alike). Both lines through $Q$ leave the grid upwards at $Q$, so the robot can only enter $Q$ coming up from row $2$, that is, at the end of a long run. Thus each of these $4051$ squares is the last square of some long run.

  • A wall at which the robot turns is the last square of a long run (the run on which the robot arrived).

  • For a wall on a border square, of the two directions along the wall at least one leaves the grid at once (the two directions are opposite, and one of them points out of the grid). So such a wall starts at most one long run.

Let $w_I$ and $w_B$ be the numbers of walls at which the robot can turn, inside the grid and on the border. The number of long runs is at most $1+2w_I+w_B$. Each long run has one last square, and we need a long run ending at each of the $4051$ border squares and at each of the $w_I$ inner turning walls: these are $4051+w_I$ different squares. Hence \[1+2w_I+w_B\ \ge\ 4051+w_I,\qquad\text{so}\qquad w_I+w_B\ \ge\ 4050 .\] Since $w_I+w_B$ is at most the number of walls, at least $4050$ walls are needed.

A configuration with $4050$ walls. Place

  • $\diagup$ at $(2,2)$ and $(3,3)$; $\diagdown$ at $(3,1)$ and $(n-1,n-1)$;

  • $\diagdown$ at $(1,2k+1)$ for $k=1,\dots,m-1$ (top row);

  • $\diagup$ at $(n-2k,n)$ for $k=1,\dots,m-1$ (right column);

  • $\diagdown$ at $(n,n-2k)$ for $k=2,\dots,m-1$ (bottom row);

  • $\diagup$ at $(2k,2)$ for $k=3,\dots,m$ (second column).

That is $4+2(m-1)+2(m-2)=4m-2=4050$ walls, all on dark squares. In terms of $d=c-r$ and $s=r+c$: the top wall $(1,2k+1)$ has $d=2k$, $s=2k+2$; the right wall $(n-2k,n)$ has $d=2k$, $s=2n-2k$; the bottom wall $(n,n-2k)$ has $d=-2k$, $s=2n-2k$; the wall $(2k,2)$ has $d=2-2k$, $s=2k+2$; and $(2,2),(3,3),(n-1,n-1)$ have $d=0$, $(3,1)$ has $d=-2$. In particular the top and right walls have $s\le n-1$ and $s\ge n+3$ respectively, the second-column walls have $s\le n+1$, and the bottom walls have $s\ge n+3$. Using this list one reads off which walls lie on each line below.

  • (a) From the start the robot moves $\searrow$ along $d=0$ and meets $(2,2)$. It may go $\nearrow$ or $\swarrow$ along $s=4$, whose squares are $(1,3),(2,2),(3,1)$.

  • (b) At $(3,1)$ it may turn $\searrow$ along $d=-2$, which carries no other wall, and runs to $(n,n-2)$.

  • (c) At a top wall $(1,2k+1)$, $1\le k\le m-1$, it turns $\searrow$ along $d=2k$; the only other wall there is $(n-2k,n)$, where it turns $\swarrow$ along $s=2n-2k$. The walls on this anti-diagonal are $(n-2k,n)$ and, for $k\ge2$, $(n,n-2k)$; for $k=1$ it is $(n-1,n-1)$.

  • (c1) For $k=1$: at $(n-1,n-1)$ it may go $\searrow$ to $(n,n)$, or $\nwarrow$ along $d=0$ to the next wall $(3,3)$, where it may go $\swarrow$ to $(5,1)$ or $\nearrow$ to the top wall $(1,5)$ (no other wall lies on $s=6$).

  • (c2) For $k\ge2$: at $(n,n-2k)$ it turns $\nwarrow$ along $d=-2k$, whose only other wall is $(2k+2,2)$. There it may go $\swarrow$ to $(2k+3,1)$, or $\nearrow$ along $s=2k+4$ to the top wall $(1,2k+3)$ if $k+1\le m-1$, or to the corner $(1,n)$ if $k+1=m$.

By (a), (c1), (c2) and induction on $k$, every top wall is reached, so all routes above can be travelled. Now every anti-diagonal is traversed completely: $s=2$ is the start; $s=4$ by (a); $s=6$ by (c1), from $(1,5)$ to $(5,1)$; $s=2k+4$ for $2\le k\le m-1$, that is $s=8,\dots,n+1$, by (c2), from $(1,2k+3)$ to $(2k+3,1)$; $s=2n-2k$ for $2\le k\le m-1$ by (c), between its end squares $(n-2k,n)$ and $(n,n-2k)$; $s=2n-2$, consisting of $(n-2,n),(n-1,n-1),(n,n-2)$, by (c) and (b); and $s=2n$ by (c1). Every dark square lies on one of these anti-diagonals, so every dark square is reachable.

Hence the least number of walls is $\boxed{4050}$. (The picture shows the same pattern for $n=9$; the green path is the spine of the spiral, and the runs leaving it to the border are not drawn.)

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n>1$ be an integer. There are $n$ cards, each of which has a distinct prime written on it. Ann does not know which primes are written on the cards. The cards are placed face down in a row, such that the numbers can not be seen. In a move, Ann picks a set of one or more consecutive cards and is then told the product of the primes on those cards. Determine the least number of moves she must make to determine what prime is written on each card.

Show the answer

$\left\lfloor \frac n2\right\rfloor+1$

Show the solution – Signatures of the cards and the gaps between them

Number the cards $1,\dots,n$ from left to right and let $p_i$ be the prime on card $i$. A product of distinct primes determines the set of these primes, so each move tells Ann the set of primes on an interval of cards. The answer is $\boxed{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}$.

When do the moves suffice? Let Ann's moves be the intervals $I_1,\dots,I_k$, and let the signature of card $i$ be $\sigma(i)=\{j: i\in I_j\}$. We claim that Ann knows every card if and only if all signatures are nonempty and pairwise different.

  • If $\sigma(i)=\sigma(i')$ for cards $i\ne i'$, exchanging the primes of cards $i$ and $i'$ changes no answer, since each interval contains both cards or neither. Even if Ann chooses her moves depending on earlier answers, after the exchange she receives the same first answer, hence makes the same second move, and so on; she cannot distinguish the two arrangements.

  • If $\sigma(i)=\varnothing$, replacing $p_i$ by a prime that is on no card changes no answer.

  • If the signatures are nonempty and distinct, let $P_j$ be the set of primes on $I_j$. The set $\bigcap_{j\in\sigma(i)}P_j\setminus\bigcup_{j\notin\sigma(i)}P_j$ contains $p_i$; it contains only primes on the cards (the intersection is over at least one $P_j$); and it contains $p_{i'}$ only if card $i'$ lies in exactly the same intervals as card $i$, that is, $\sigma(i')=\sigma(i)$, which forces $i'=i$. So this set is $\{p_i\}$, and Ann knows $p_i$.

At least $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ moves. Consider the $n+1$ gaps: gap $0$ before card $1$, gap $i$ between cards $i$ and $i+1$ ($1\le i\le n-1$), and gap $n$ after card $n$. Say that an interval from card $l$ to card $r$ uses the gaps $l-1$ and $r$. Every gap is used:

  • card $1$ has a nonempty signature, so some interval starts at card $1$ and uses gap $0$; likewise some interval ends at card $n$ and uses gap $n$;

  • for $1\le i\le n-1$, $\sigma(i)\ne\sigma(i+1)$, so some interval contains exactly one of the cards $i,i+1$; it either ends at card $i$ or starts at card $i+1$, and in both cases it uses gap $i$.

Each interval uses two gaps, so $2k\ge n+1$, that is, $k\ge\frac{n+1}2$, and $k\ge\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$.

$\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ moves suffice. Let $k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$; then $n\le 2k-1$ and $k\le n$ (as $n\ge2$). For $j=1,\dots,k$ let $I_j$ consist of the cards $j,j+1,\dots,\min(j+k-1,\,n)$. Card $i$ lies in $I_j$ exactly when $i-k+1\le j\le i$, so \[\sigma(i)=\{\,j:\ \max(1,\,i-k+1)\le j\le \min(k,\,i)\,\}.\] Because $i\le n\le 2k-1$, this is a nonempty block of consecutive integers. For $i\le k$ the blocks are $\{1,\dots,i\}$, all different; for $i\ge k$ they are $\{i-k+1,\dots,k\}$, all different; and a block of the second kind with $i>k$ does not contain $1$, so it differs from every block of the first kind. By the criterion above, Ann determines all cards with these $k$ moves.

Problem 8

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Linear Recurrences

A ring of $n>1$ dancers are dancing to an endless song. Every beat of the song, each dancer swings either their left or their right leg. Each beat, except the first one, they decide which leg to swing according to the following rule: they swing their right leg if their right neighbour swung the same leg as them last beat, and otherwise they swing their left leg. Determine the number of ways the dancers could have swung their legs the first beat if there exists a future beat when everyone swings the same leg as each other.

Note: Two configurations are considered different if there exists a dancer making a different swing in those two configurations.

Show the answer

$2^{2^a}$, where $2^a$ is the largest power of $2$ dividing $n$

Show solution 1 – The rule applied a power of two times

Number the dancers $0,1,\dots,n-1$ around the ring so that the right neighbour of dancer $i$ is dancer $i+1$ (indices modulo $n$). At a given beat let $x_i=0$ if dancer $i$ swings the right leg and $x_i=1$ if the left leg. The rule says that the new value is $0$ when $x_i=x_{i+1}$ and $1$ otherwise, that is, \[x_i'\equiv x_i+x_{i+1}\pmod 2 .\] Call this map $T$. Everyone swings the same leg exactly when the state is all $0$ or all $1$; the all-$1$ state goes to the all-$0$ state, which stays fixed. So a configuration is counted exactly when $T^jx=\mathbf 0$ for some $j\ge0$ (then every later beat is uniform; and a uniform later beat is $\mathbf0$ or is followed by $\mathbf 0$). Write $n=2^a u$ with $u$ odd. We show that this happens exactly when $x$ has period $2^a$, that is, $x_{i+2^a}=x_i$ for all $i$.

Applying $T$ a power of two times. For a shift $e$ let $T_e$ be the map $x\mapsto y$ with $y_i\equiv x_i+x_{i+e}$; so $T=T_1$. Applying $T_e$ twice gives \[x_i+x_{i+e}+x_{i+e}+x_{i+2e}\equiv x_i+x_{i+2e}\pmod 2,\] so $T_e\circ T_e=T_{2e}$, and by induction $T^{2^b}=T_{2^b}$: after $2^b$ beats, $x_i$ has become $x_i+x_{i+2^b}$.

Periodic configurations are counted. If $x$ has period $2^a$, then $T^{2^a}x=T_{2^a}x=\mathbf 0$.

Only periodic configurations are counted. Suppose $T^jx=\mathbf 0$. Choose $b\ge a$ with $2^b\ge j$. As $T$ maps $\mathbf0$ to $\mathbf0$, also $T^{2^b}x=\mathbf0$, that is, $x_{i+2^b}=x_i$ for all $i$. The set of integers $e$ with $x_{i+e}=x_i$ for all $i$ is closed under addition and subtraction and contains $n$ and $2^b$, so it contains $\gcd(n,2^b)=2^a$. Thus $x$ has period $2^a$.

A configuration of period $2^a$ is determined by $x_0,\dots,x_{2^a-1}$, which can be chosen freely. Hence the number of configurations is $\boxed{2^{2^a}}$, where $2^a$ is the largest power of $2$ dividing $n$.

Show solution 2 – Halving the ring

Number the dancers $0,1,\dots,n-1$ around the ring so that the right neighbour of dancer $i$ is dancer $i+1$ (indices modulo $n$), and write $x_i=0$ for the right leg, $x_i=1$ for the left leg. The rule is $x_i'\equiv x_i+x_{i+1}\pmod 2$; call this map $T$, and define it in the same way for a ring of any size $\ell\ge1$ (for $\ell=1$ the dancer is their own right neighbour and $T$ sends everything to $\mathbf 0$). The all-$1$ state goes to the all-$0$ state $\mathbf0$, which is fixed, so a configuration is counted exactly when $T^jx=\mathbf0$ for some $j\ge0$; call such an $x$ good, and let $N(\ell)$ be the number of good configurations on a ring of $\ell$ dancers. Note that $Tx=\mathbf0$ exactly when all $x_i$ are equal.

Odd rings: $N(\ell)=2$. The constant states $\mathbf0$ and $\mathbf1$ are good. Let $E$ be the set of states with an even number of $1$'s. Every $Tx$ lies in $E$, since $\sum_i(x_i+x_{i+1})=2\sum_ix_i$. If $Tx=Ty$ with $x,y\in E$, then $T(x+y)=\mathbf0$, so $x+y$ is constant; it lies in $E$, while $\mathbf1$ has $\ell$ (odd) ones, so $x+y=\mathbf0$ and $x=y$. Thus $T$ is injective on $E$, and since $T\mathbf0=\mathbf0$, it maps nonzero states of $E$ to nonzero states. Now let $x$ be non-constant: $Tx\ne\mathbf0$ and $Tx\in E$, so $T^2x,T^3x,\dots$ are all nonzero, and $x$ is not good.

Even rings: $N(\ell)=N(\ell/2)^2$. Applying $T$ twice gives $x_i''\equiv x_i+2x_{i+1}+x_{i+2}\equiv x_i+x_{i+2}$. So on the dancers $0,2,4,\dots,\ell-2$ (and separately on $1,3,\dots,\ell-1$) two beats act exactly like one beat of the rule on a ring of $\ell/2$ dancers. Since $\mathbf0$ is fixed, $x$ is good if and only if $T^{2j}x=\mathbf0$ for some $j$, if and only if both halves $(x_0,x_2,\dots)$ and $(x_1,x_3,\dots)$ are good for the ring of $\ell/2$ (take the larger of the two times). The halves can be chosen independently, so $N(\ell)=N(\ell/2)^2$.

Conclusion. Write $n=2^au$ with $u$ odd. Halving $a$ times gives $N(n)=N(u)^{2^a}=2^{2^a}$. The answer is $\boxed{2^{2^a}}$, where $2^a$ is the largest power of $2$ dividing $n$.

Problem 9

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Generalized Pigeonhole

In the Baltic Garden, every Beetle has a unique string of three digits written on its back. Two Beetles are enemies if the string formed by the first two digits on one is the same as the string formed by the last two digits on the other. A Beetle may be its own enemy.

What is the largest number of Beetles that can live in the garden if no Beetle has an enemy?

Show the answer

$330$

Show solution 1 – Grouping the strings by their digits

Call a set of strings peaceful if no string in it is an enemy of a string in it (itself included): no string's first two digits equal some string's last two digits. The answer is $\boxed{330}$.

A peaceful set with $330$ strings. Take all strings $abc$ with $a<b\ge c$. The first two digits $ab$ of such a string satisfy $a<b$, and the last two digits $bc$ of such a string satisfy $b\ge c$; a pair of digits cannot satisfy both, so the set is peaceful. For the middle digit $b$ there are $b$ choices of $a$ and $b+1$ choices of $c$, so the set has \[\sum_{b=0}^{9}b(b+1)=0+2+6+12+20+30+42+56+72+90=330\] strings.

No peaceful set has more than $330$ strings. Sort the $1000$ strings into classes according to the multiset of their digits.

  • Class $\{x,x,x\}$: the string $xxx$ is its own enemy, so it is not in a peaceful set. ($10$ classes.)

  • Class $\{x,x,y\}$ with $x\ne y$: the strings $xxy$, $xyx$, $yxx$. Here $xyx$ begins with $xy$, the end of $xxy$; $yxx$ begins with $yx$, the end of $xyx$; and $xxy$ begins with $xx$, the end of $yxx$. Any two of them are enemies, so a peaceful set contains at most one. ($10\cdot9=90$ classes.)

  • Class $\{x,y,z\}$ with distinct digits: six strings. For any string $abc$, the string $bca$ begins with $bc$, the end of $abc$, so they are enemies. Hence the three strings $xyz,\,yzx,\,zxy$ are pairwise enemies, and so are $xzy,\,zyx,\,yxz$. A peaceful set contains at most one string of each triple, so at most $2$ from the class. ($\binom{10}{3}=120$ classes.)

In total a peaceful set has at most $0+90\cdot1+120\cdot2=330$ strings, so the largest number of Beetles is $330$.

Show solution 2 – Prefixes, suffixes and a majorization bound

Call a set of strings peaceful if no string's first two digits equal some (possibly the same) string's last two digits. The answer is $\boxed{330}$.

Construction. All strings $abc$ with $a<b\ge c$: first two digits $ab$ have $a<b$, last two digits $bc$ have $b\ge c$, so no pair occurs as both, and the number of strings is $\sum_{b=0}^{9}b(b+1)=330$.

Upper bound. Let $B$ be peaceful, $P$ the set of two-digit strings that begin a string of $B$, and $Q$ the set of those that end a string of $B$; then $P\cap Q=\varnothing$. For a digit $b$ let $\alpha_b$ be the number of digits $a$ with $ab\in P$ and $\beta_b$ the number of digits $c$ with $bc\in Q$. A string $abc\in B$ has $ab\in P$ and $bc\in Q$, so \[|B|\ \le\ \sum_{b=0}^{9}\alpha_b\beta_b\ \le\ \sum_{b=0}^{9}h(u_b),\qquad u_b=\alpha_b+\beta_b,\quad h(u)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{u^2}{4}\Bigr\rfloor ,\] using $\alpha\beta\le\frac{(\alpha+\beta)^2}4$ for integers.

The $u_b$ cannot be too concentrated. Let $D$ be a set of $k$ digits. A two-digit string $xy$ contributes to $\sum_{b\in D}u_b$ at most once: if $xy\in P$ it is counted in $\alpha_y$ only, if $xy\in Q$ in $\beta_x$ only. It contributes only if $y\in D$ or $x\in D$, and there are $100-(10-k)^2=20k-k^2$ such strings. Order the $u_b$ as $u_{(1)}\ge u_{(2)}\ge\dots\ge u_{(10)}$ and put $v_j=21-2j$ (so $v=19,17,\dots,1$). Taking for $D$ the digits with the $k$ largest values, \[U_k:=u_{(1)}+\dots+u_{(k)}\ \le\ 20k-k^2=v_1+\dots+v_k=:V_k\qquad(k=1,\dots,10).\]

Convexity of $h$. Let $\delta(u)=h(u)-h(u-1)$ for $u\ge1$ and $\delta(0)=0$; one checks $\delta(u)=\lfloor u/2\rfloor$, which is nondecreasing. Hence for integers $u,v\ge0$: if $u\ge v$, then $h(u)-h(v)=\sum_{w=v+1}^{u}\delta(w)\le(u-v)\delta(u)$; if $u<v$, then $h(v)-h(u)=\sum_{w=u+1}^{v}\delta(w)\ge(v-u)\delta(u)$. In both cases \[h(u)-h(v)\le\delta(u)\,(u-v).\]

Summation. Let $c_j=\delta(u_{(j)})$; then $c_1\ge c_2\ge\dots\ge c_{10}\ge0$. Put $c_{11}=0$ and $U_0=V_0=0$. By Abel summation, \[\sum_{j=1}^{10}\bigl(h(u_{(j)})-h(v_j)\bigr)\le\sum_{j=1}^{10}c_j\bigl(u_{(j)}-v_j\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{10}(c_k-c_{k+1})(U_k-V_k)\le0,\] because $c_k-c_{k+1}\ge0$ and $U_k-V_k\le0$. Therefore \[|B|\le\sum_{j=1}^{10}h(v_j)=\sum_{j=1}^{10}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(21-2j)^2}{4}\Bigr\rfloor=\sum_{t=0}^{9}t(t+1)=330,\] since $\lfloor(2t+1)^2/4\rfloor=t^2+t$. So at most $330$ Beetles can live in the garden, and $330$ is possible.

Problem 10

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

Let $n$ be a positive integer. The top right square of a $2n\times2n$ grid of unit squares is white and all other squares are red. The Wolf performs the following operation some number of times:

  • The Wolf chooses a row or column with an odd number of red squares.

  • The colours in the chosen row or column are changed so that all red squares become white and all white squares become red.

In terms of $n$, determine the maximum possible number of white squares at the end of the process.

Show the answer

$4n^2-2n-1$

Show solution 1 – Parities of the lines and a corner invariant

Number the rows $1,\dots,2n$ from the top and the columns $1,\dots,2n$ from the left; the corner square $K=(1,2n)$ is the one that starts white. Every line has $2n$ squares, an even number, so a line has an odd number of red squares exactly when it has an odd number of white squares. The answer is $\boxed{4n^2-2n-1}$.

Which lines can be chosen. Flipping a line turns its $w$ white squares into $2n-w$, so its own parity does not change; flipping a column changes one square in every row, so it changes the parity of every row; similarly a row flip changes the parity of every column. Let $x$ and $y$ be the parities of the numbers of row moves and column moves made so far. Since at the start only row $1$ and column $2n$ are odd, at any moment

  • row $1$ is odd iff $y=0$, and every other row is odd iff $y=1$;

  • column $2n$ is odd iff $x=0$, and every other column is odd iff $x=1$.

Invariant: $K$ is white if and only if $x=y=0$. This holds at the start. A move on row $1$ needs $y=0$; it changes the colour of $K$ and the value of $x$, so the statement stays true. A move on another row needs $y=1$; it changes neither $K$ nor $y$, and with $y=1$ the statement says $K$ is red, as it was. Column moves are handled in the same way, with column $2n$ in place of row $1$.

Counting white squares at the end. Let $R$ be the set of rows and $C$ the set of columns that were flipped an odd number of times, $a=|R|$, $b=|C|$; then $a\equiv x$ and $b\equiv y\pmod 2$. A square $(i,j)\ne K$ is white at the end exactly when exactly one of $i\in R$, $j\in C$ holds. The number of squares $(i,j)$ (including $K$) with exactly one of these properties is \[a(2n-b)+(2n-a)b=4n^2-D,\qquad D=ab+(2n-a)(2n-b).\] For $K$ itself, starting white, $K$ is red exactly when exactly one of $1\in R$, $2n\in C$ holds, so $K$ is counted in $4n^2-D$ exactly when it is red. By the invariant, $K$ is white iff $a,b$ are both even. Hence the number of white squares is \[W=4n^2-D+1\ \text{ if $a,b$ are both even},\qquad W=4n^2-D-1\ \text{ otherwise}.\]

Upper bound. For fixed $a$, $D$ is linear in $b\in[0,2n]$, so $D\ge\min\bigl(D|_{b=0},D|_{b=2n}\bigr)=2n\min(2n-a,\,a)$.

  • If $a$ and $b$ are both even: $(a,b)=(2n,0)$ is impossible, since then $1\in R$, $2n\notin C$, so $K$ is red, while the invariant says it is white; likewise $(a,b)=(0,2n)$ is impossible. If $2\le a\le 2n-2$ then $D\ge 4n$; if $a=0$ then $b\le 2n-2$ and $D=2n(2n-b)\ge4n$; if $a=2n$ then $b\ge2$ and $D=2nb\ge4n$. So $W\le 4n^2-4n+1\le 4n^2-2n-1$.

  • Otherwise: if $1\le a\le 2n-1$ then $D\ge2n$; if $a\in\{0,2n\}$ then $a$ is even, so $b$ is odd, and $D=2n(2n-b)$ or $D=2nb$ is at least $2n$. So $W\le 4n^2-2n-1$.

The bound is reached. The Wolf flips row $1$ (allowed, $y=0$), then column $1$ (allowed, now $x=1$), then rows $2,3,\dots,2n-1$ (allowed, $y=1$), then column $1$ again (allowed, $x=1+(2n-2)$ is odd). Now rows $1,\dots,2n-1$ were flipped once and no column an odd number of times: row $1$ is white except $K$, rows $2,\dots,2n-1$ are white, row $2n$ is red. That is $(2n-1)+(2n-2)\cdot2n=4n^2-2n-1$ white squares.

Show solution 2 – Rectangles of four squares and counting the red squares

Number the rows $1,\dots,2n$ from the top and the columns $1,\dots,2n$ from the left; the corner square $K=(1,2n)$ is the one that starts white. A line is a row or a column. Every line has $2n$ squares, an even number, so a line has an odd number of red squares exactly when it has an odd number of white squares; call such a line odd. Let $\rho$ be the number of red squares at the end. The answer is $\boxed{4n^2-2n-1}$.

Four invariants.

  • The number of white squares stays odd. A move turns the $w$ white squares of its line into $2n-w$, an even change, and at the start there is $1$ white square. So $\rho$, which is $4n^2$ minus an odd number, is odd.

  • Parities of the lines. A move does not change the parity of its own line ($w$ becomes $2n-w$), and it changes exactly one square of every crossing line, so it changes the parity of every crossing line. Let $x$ and $y$ be the parities of the numbers of row moves and of column moves made so far. At the start only row $1$ and column $2n$ are odd, so at any moment row $1$ is odd iff $y=0$, every other row is odd iff $y=1$, column $2n$ is odd iff $x=0$, and every other column is odd iff $x=1$.

  • The corner: $K$ is white iff $x=y=0$. This holds at the start. A move on row $1$ needs $y=0$; it changes the colour of $K$ and the value of $x$, so the statement stays true. A move on another row needs $y=1$; it changes neither $K$ nor $y$, and with $y=1$ the statement says that $K$ is red, as it was. Column moves are handled in the same way, with column $2n$ in place of row $1$.

  • Rectangles. For rows $i\ne i'$ and columns $j\ne j'$, the parity of the number of white squares among $(i,j),(i,j'),(i',j),(i',j')$ never changes, because a move changes the colour of none or of exactly two of these four squares. At the start this number is $1$ if $K$ is one of the four squares and $0$ otherwise.

At the end there are at least $2n+1$ red squares.

Case $y=1$. The rows $2,\dots,2n$ are odd, so each contains a red square, and $\rho\ge 2n-1$. If $\rho=2n-1$, then row $1$ contains no red square, so $K$ is white; but $K$ is red when $y=1$. Hence $\rho\ge2n$, and as $\rho$ is odd, $\rho\ge2n+1$.

Case $x=1$. The same argument with the columns $1,\dots,2n-1$ and column $2n$ gives $\rho\ge2n+1$.

Case $x=y=0$. Now $K$ is white, and row $1$ and column $2n$ are odd. Take $i\ne1$ and $j\ne2n$. The rectangle with corners $(1,j),(1,2n),(i,j),(i,2n)$ contains $K$, so it has an odd number of white squares; since $K$ is white, the other three squares contain an even number of white squares. Hence $(i,j)$ is red exactly when $(1,j)$ and $(i,2n)$ have the same colour. Let $\alpha$ be the number of red squares in row $1$ and $\beta$ the number in column $2n$. Both lines are odd and $K$ is white, so $\alpha,\beta$ are odd and $1\le\alpha,\beta\le p$, where $p=2n-1$. The squares outside row $1$ and column $2n$ form a $p\times p$ block, and by the rule just proved it contains $\alpha\beta+(p-\alpha)(p-\beta)$ red squares. So \[\rho=\alpha+\beta+\alpha\beta+(p-\alpha)(p-\beta).\] For fixed $\beta$ the expression $\alpha\beta+(p-\alpha)(p-\beta)$ is linear in $\alpha$, and for fixed $\alpha$ it is linear in $\beta$, so on $1\le\alpha,\beta\le p$ its least value is taken at a corner. Its values there are $p$ at $(1,p)$ and $(p,1)$, $1+(p-1)^2\ge p$ at $(1,1)$ (because $(p-1)(p-2)\ge0$ for every positive integer $p$), and $p^2\ge p$ at $(p,p)$. Therefore $\rho\ge\alpha+\beta+p\ge p+2=2n+1$.

So in every case the number of white squares is $4n^2-\rho\le4n^2-2n-1$.

The bound is reached. The Wolf flips column $2n$, then row $2n$, then the columns $2,3,\dots,2n-1$, then row $2n$ again. Every move is allowed: column $2n$ is odd at the start ($x=0$); after it $y=1$, so row $2n$ is odd; after that $x=1$, so the columns $2,\dots,2n-1$ are odd, and each of them changes $y$; after these $1+(2n-2)$ column moves $y$ is odd, so row $2n$ is odd again. In the end the columns $2,\dots,2n$ have been flipped once and row $2n$ twice. Columns $2,\dots,2n-1$ are white, column $2n$ is white except $K$, and column $1$ is red: that is $(2n-2)\cdot2n+(2n-1)=4n^2-2n-1$ white squares.

Problem 11

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Transformation Chains in Geometry

In an acute triangle $ABC$ with $AB<AC$, point $D$ is chosen on side $BC$. The bisectors of the angles $BAD$ and $CAD$ intersect the circumcircles of triangles $ABD$ and $ACD$ again at points $X$ and $Y$, respectively. Let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ containing $A$ on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Prove that the points $X$, $A$, $N$, and $Y$ are concyclic.

Show solution 1 – Composing two rotations

Draw $BC$ horizontally with $B$ on the left and $A$ above $BC$. Write $\alpha=\angle BAC$, $\angle BAD=2u$ and $\angle DAC=2v$, so that $u+v=\alpha/2<90^\circ$. For lines $\ell,m$ let $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denote the angle through which $\ell$ must be turned counterclockwise to become parallel to $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Four points $P,Q,R,S$ with $P\ne Q$ and $\measuredangle(RP,RQ)=\measuredangle(SP,SQ)\neq0$ are concyclic.

The three isosceles triangles. The bisector of the inscribed angle $BAD$ passes through the midpoint of the arc $BD$ of the circle $ABD$ that does not contain $A$, so $XB=XD$, the point $X$ lies below $BC$, and $\angle BXD=180^\circ-\angle BAD=180^\circ-2u$ (the quadrilateral $ABXD$ is cyclic). In the same way $YD=YC$, $Y$ lies below $BC$ and $\angle DYC=180^\circ-2v$. Finally $NB=NC$, $N$ lies above $BC$ and $\angle BNC=\angle BAC=\alpha$.

Two rotations. Let $\rho_X$ be the rotation about $X$ through $180^\circ-2u$ clockwise. Since $XB=XD$, $\angle BXD=180^\circ-2u$, $X$ is below $BC$ and $B$ is to the left of $D$, it maps $B$ to $D$. Likewise the rotation $\rho_Y$ about $Y$ through $180^\circ-2v$ clockwise maps $D$ to $C$. Hence $\rho=\rho_Y\circ\rho_X$ maps $B$ to $C$.

We write both rotations as products of reflections. For a line $\ell$ let $\sigma_\ell$ be the reflection in $\ell$. If two lines $\ell,m$ meet at $Z$, then $\sigma_m\circ\sigma_\ell$ is the rotation about $Z$ through $2\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ counterclockwise. Let $g$ be the line $XY$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $X$ with $\measuredangle(\ell,g)=-(90^\circ-u)$ and $m$ the line through $Y$ with $\measuredangle(g,m)=-(90^\circ-v)$. Then $\rho_X=\sigma_g\circ\sigma_\ell$ and $\rho_Y=\sigma_m\circ\sigma_g$, so \[\rho=\sigma_m\circ\sigma_g\circ\sigma_g\circ\sigma_\ell=\sigma_m\circ\sigma_\ell .\] Here $\measuredangle(\ell,m)=-(180^\circ-u-v)\equiv u+v\pmod{180^\circ}$, which is not $0$, so $\ell$ and $m$ meet at a point $Z$, and $\rho$ is the rotation about $Z$ through $2(u+v)=\alpha$ counterclockwise.

The centre is $N$. Since $\rho(B)=C$, we get $ZB=ZC$ and $\angle BZC=\alpha$, and the ray $ZB$ turns counterclockwise onto the ray $ZC$. The points $Z$ with $ZB=ZC$ and $\angle BZC=\alpha$ are $N$ and its mirror image in $BC$; for the mirror image (below $BC$) the ray to $B$ turns clockwise onto the ray to $C$. Hence $Z=N$.

Conclusion. The lines $NX$ and $NY$ are $\ell$ and $m$ (note $N\ne X,Y$, as $N$ is above $BC$ and $X,Y$ below), so $\measuredangle(NX,NY)=\measuredangle(\ell,m)=u+v$. On the other hand, turning the ray $AB$ counterclockwise onto the ray $AC$ sweeps the angle $\alpha$ and passes $AX$, $AD$, $AY$ in this order, with $\angle XAD=u$ and $\angle DAY=v$; so $\measuredangle(AX,AY)=u+v$. Therefore $\measuredangle(NX,NY)=\measuredangle(AX,AY)\ne0$, and $X$, $A$, $N$, $Y$ are concyclic.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates on the line BC

Write $\angle BAD=2u$ and $\angle DAC=2v$, and put $w=u+v=\tfrac12\angle BAC$, so $0<w<90^\circ$. Write $s=\tan u$, $t=\tan v$ and $c=\cot w$. Then $s,t,c>0$ and \[c=\frac{1-st}{s+t}.\]

The points. Take coordinates with $B=(0,0)$, $D=(2p,0)$, $C=(2p+2q,0)$ ($p,q>0$) and $A$ above the $x$-axis.

  • $AX$ bisects the inscribed angle $BAD$ of the circle $ABD$, so $X$ is the midpoint of the arc $BD$ not containing $A$: $XB=XD$ and $X$ lies below $BC$. Moreover $\angle XBD=\angle XAD=u$ (inscribed angles on the arc $XD$). Hence $X=(p,\,-ps)$.

  • In the same way $YD=YC$, $Y$ lies below $BC$ and $\angle YCD=\angle YAD=v$, so $Y=(2p+q,\,-qt)$.

  • $N$ is above $BC$ on the perpendicular bisector $x=p+q$ of $BC$, and $\angle BNC=\angle BAC=2w$, so $\angle NBC=90^\circ-w$ and $N=(p+q,\,(p+q)c)$.

The angle $XNY$. We have \[\vec{NX}=\bigl(-q,\;-ps-(p+q)c\bigr),\qquad \vec{NY}=\bigl(p,\;-qt-(p+q)c\bigr).\] For vectors $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ let $\times$ denote $x_1y_2-y_1x_2$ and $\cdot$ the dot product. Then \[\vec{NX}\times\vec{NY}=q^2t+q(p+q)c+p^2s+p(p+q)c=p^2s+q^2t+(p+q)^2c,\] \[\vec{NX}\cdot\vec{NY}=-pq+\bigl(ps+(p+q)c\bigr)\bigl(qt+(p+q)c\bigr)=-pq+pqst+(p+q)c(ps+qt)+(p+q)^2c^2 .\] Subtracting $c$ times the first expression from the second, \[\vec{NX}\cdot\vec{NY}-c\,\bigl(\vec{NX}\times\vec{NY}\bigr)=-pq(1-st)+c\bigl[(p+q)(ps+qt)-p^2s-q^2t\bigr]=-pq(1-st)+c\,pq(s+t)=0,\] because $c(s+t)=1-st$. Hence the cross product is positive and the dot product equals $c$ times it, also positive. So the counterclockwise angle $\theta$ from $\vec{NX}$ to $\vec{NY}$ lies in $(0^\circ,90^\circ)$ and satisfies $\tan\theta=1/c=\tan w$, that is, $\theta=w$.

The angle $XAY$. Turning the ray $AB$ counterclockwise onto the ray $AC$ passes $AX$, $AD$, $AY$ in this order, and $\angle XAD=u$, $\angle DAY=v$. So the counterclockwise angle from $\vec{AX}$ to $\vec{AY}$ is also $u+v=w$.

Conclusion. The points $A$ and $N$ see the segment $XY$ under the same angle $w$, with the same orientation; hence they lie on the same arc of one circle through $X$ and $Y$. So $X$, $A$, $N$, $Y$ are concyclic.

  • In the right triangle $ADC$ the segment $DE$ is the altitude to the hypotenuse, so $AE\cdot AC=AD^2$; likewise $AF\cdot AB=AD^2$. Hence $AE\cdot AC=AF\cdot AB$ and $B$, $C$, $E$, $F$ are concyclic.

  • $AX\perp EF$: in $\omega$, $\angle AFE=\angle ADE=90^\circ-\angle DAC=\angle ACB$, while $\angle BAO=90^\circ-\angle ACB$ (the triangle is acute). So in the triangle formed by $A$, $F$ and the point $AX\cap EF$ the angles at $A$ and $F$ add up to $90^\circ$.

In particular $TB\cdot TC>0$, so $T$ lies on line $BC$ outside the segment $BC$, hence outside $\Gamma$ and outside the triangle $ABC$. Moreover $T$ is not on the line $AX$: the ray $AO$ runs inside the angle $BAC$, so the line $AX$ meets the line $EF$ at a point of the segment $EF$, which lies inside the triangle.

Step 2: $DX\perp AT$. Let the line $TA$ meet $\Gamma$ again at $Q$ ($Q\ne A$: otherwise $TA$ would be tangent to $\Gamma$ and $TA^2=TB\cdot TC=TD^2$, impossible because $TA>TD$ in the right triangle $ADT$). Then $TQ\cdot TA=TB\cdot TC=TD^2$, so $TD$ is tangent at $D$ to the circle $ADQ$. Also $Q\ne X$, because $T$ is not on the line $AX$. The centre of that circle lies on the perpendicular to $TD$ at $D$, which is the line $DA$; as $D$ and $A$ are both on the circle, $AD$ is a diameter and $\angle AQD=90^\circ$. Also $\angle AQX=90^\circ$ because $AX$ is a diameter of $\Gamma$. So $D$ and $X$ both lie on the line through $Q$ perpendicular to $AQ$, which means $DX\perp AT$.

Step 3: $L$ is the orthocentre of triangle $ATX$. The line $EF$ passes through $T$ and is perpendicular to $AX$; the line $DX$ passes through $X$ and is perpendicular to $AT$. They are two altitudes of triangle $ATX$ (a genuine triangle, as $T$ is not on the line $AX$), and they meet at $L$. Hence $AL\perp TX$.

First $K\ne X$: otherwise $L$ would lie on $AX$ and on $DX$; these lines are different ($D\notin AX$, since $AX\perp BC$ would force $AB=AC$), so $L=X$; but $L$ lies on $EF$, and $AX$ meets $EF$ at a point of the segment $EF$, which is inside $\Gamma$, while $X$ is on $\Gamma$. As $AX$ is a diameter, $\angle AKX=90^\circ$, so $XK\perp AL$. Both $XK$ and $XT$ are perpendicular to $AL$ through $X$, so they coincide.

Step 5. By the power of $T$ with respect to $\Gamma$, $TK\cdot TX=TB\cdot TC=TE\cdot TF$, with all lengths measured on the rays from $T$ (the point $T$ is outside both $\Gamma$ and the circle $BCEF$). The lines $TEF$ and $TKX$ are different ($X\notin EF$), so by the converse of the power of a point, $E$, $F$, $X$, $K$ are concyclic.

Show solution 2 – Inversion with radius AD

In the right triangles $ADC$ and $ADB$ the altitudes from $D$ give $AE\cdot AC=AD^2=AF\cdot AB$, so $B,C,E,F$ are concyclic. Moreover $AX\perp EF$, because $\angle AFE=\angle ADE=90^\circ-\angle DAC=\angle ACB$ and $\angle BAO=90^\circ-\angle ACB$. Let $P$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $EF$; it lies on $AX$. As $\angle AFE=\angle ACB\neq\angle ABC$ (because $AB<AC$), $EF$ meets $BC$ at a point $T$.

The inversion. Let $\Phi$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $AD$. As $AE\cdot AC=AF\cdot AB=AD^2$, it swaps $E\leftrightarrow C$ and $F\leftrightarrow B$. The circle $\Gamma$ passes through $A$, $B$, $C$, so $\Phi(\Gamma)$ is the line through $\Phi(B)=F$ and $\Phi(C)=E$, the line $EF$. Consequently:

  • $\Phi(L)$ lies on $\Gamma$ and on the ray $AL$, so $\Phi(L)=K$;

  • $\Phi(X)$ lies on $EF$ and on the ray $AX$, so $\Phi(X)=P$; that is, $AP\cdot AX=AD^2$.

The circle through $E,F,X$ does not pass through $A$: the circle $AEF$ is $\omega$, whose points are at distance at most $AD$ from $A$, while $AX=2R>AD$ because $D$ lies inside $\Gamma$. So $\Phi$ maps it to the circle through $C$, $B$, $P$, and \[E,F,X,K\ \text{concyclic}\iff B,C,P,L\ \text{concyclic}.\]

(Here $P\ne L$: otherwise $L$ would lie on $AX$ and $DX$, which meet only at $X$ because $D\notin AX$ (as $AB\ne AC$); but $P$ is on the segment $EF$, inside $\Gamma$, and $X$ is on $\Gamma$.)

Hence $TP\cdot TL=TB\cdot TC$ (signed lengths), and $B$, $C$, $P$, $L$ are concyclic. Applying $\Phi$, the points $E$, $F$, $X$, $K$ are concyclic.

Problem 14

  • Pressing button 2 displays the circle with centre $P$ through $Q$.

Show the answer

$3$

Show the solution – Locate X and Y, construct, then exclude two moves

Use coordinates with $M=(0,0)$, $A=(1,0)$, so $\Gamma$ is the unit circle and $B=(2,0)$.

The target points. $B$ is the reflection of $X$ over $Y$ exactly when $Y$ is the midpoint of $XB$. The homothety with centre $B$ and ratio $\tfrac12$ maps $\Gamma$ to the circle with centre $\tfrac12(M+B)=A$ and radius $\tfrac12$, so $Y$ lies on $\Gamma$ and on this circle: \[x^2+y^2=1,\quad (x-1)^2+y^2=\tfrac14\ \Longrightarrow\ x=\tfrac78,\ y=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}8 .\] Then $X=2Y-B=\bigl(-\tfrac14,\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}4\bigr)$, which is indeed on $\Gamma$. For $N$ use directed angles modulo $180^\circ$: from $DB=DN$ and $MB=MC$, \[\measuredangle(ND,NM)=\measuredangle(ND,NB)=\measuredangle(BN,BD)=\measuredangle(BM,BC)=\measuredangle(CB,CM)=\measuredangle(CD,CM),\] so $N$ lies on the circle $DCM$ as well.

The line $BL$ (not through $D$) goes to the circle $DBL$, which by Step 2 is the circle $ABD$; the line $NK$ goes to the circle $DNK$, which by Step 3 is the circle $DCM$. So $E^*\neq D$ is the second common point of the circles $ABD$ and $DCM$.

Step 5: $\angle DE^*O=90^\circ$. The circles $ABD$ and $DCM$ meet at $D$ and $E^*$. The line $BC$ through $D$ meets them again at $B$ and $C$, and the line $AM$ through $D$ meets them again at $A$ and $M$. By the standard lemma, $E^*$ is the centre of the spiral similarity taking $B\mapsto C$ and $A\mapsto M$; hence triangles $E^*BA$ and $E^*CM$ are directly similar, and then so are $E^*BC$ and $E^*AM$. So the spiral similarity $g$ with centre $E^*$ taking $B\mapsto A$ also takes $C\mapsto M$, hence $M_a\mapsto P$, and it turns every line through the angle $\measuredangle(BC,AM)$. Hence $E^*$ lies on the circle with diameter $OD$, and $OE^*\perp DE^*$ (or $E^*=O$).

Step 6: the polar of $E$. The polar of $E$ with respect to $\omega$ is the line through $E^*$ perpendicular to $DE$; by Step 5 it passes through $O$. By Brocard's theorem for the four points $B,N,K,L$ of $\omega$, the polar of $E=BL\cap NK$ passes through $F=BN\cap KL$. So the polar of $E$ is the line $OF$, and it is perpendicular to $DE$. Hence $ED\perp OF$.

Show solution 2 – Complex numbers centred at D

Let $\omega$ be the circle with centre $D$ and radius $DB$, and write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of triangle $ABC$.

Angles at $D$. In triangle $ABD$, $\angle ADB=180^\circ-\beta-\tfrac\alpha2$, so $\angle ADC=\beta+\tfrac\alpha2$.

  • The reflection $B_1$ of $B$ in $AD$ lies on ray $AC$ with $DB_1=DB$, so $B_1\in\{K,L\}$. If $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AC$ (the midpoint of $KL$), then by symmetry $AH$ is the distance from $A$ to the foot of the perpendicular from $D$ to $AB$, which lies strictly inside $AB$ (the angles $DAB$, $ABD$ are acute). So $AB_1=AB>AH$ and $B_1$ is the point farther from $A$: $K=B_1$. Then $\angle ADK=\angle ADB$ and $\angle KDC=\angle ADC-\angle ADK=2\beta+\alpha-180^\circ=\beta-\gamma$.

  • $\angle DKL=\angle DKA=\angle DBA=\beta$, so in the isosceles triangle $DKL$, $\angle KDL=180^\circ-2\beta$, and $L$ (closer to $A$) is reached from $K$ by turning towards $DA$: $\angle CDL=(\beta-\gamma)+(180^\circ-2\beta)=\alpha$.

  • $\angle DBN=\angle CBM=\tfrac\alpha2$ and $DB=DN$, so $\angle BDN=180^\circ-\alpha$, with $N$ below $BC$ (on the ray $BM$).

  • $DC/DB=AC/AB=\sin\beta/\sin\gamma$ (bisector theorem and law of sines).

Coordinates. Use complex numbers with $D=0$, radius $DB=1$, $C$ on the positive real axis and $A$ in the upper half-plane. Put $u=e^{i\beta}$, $v=e^{i\gamma}$; then $e^{i\alpha}=-1/(uv)$ and \[b=-1,\quad l=e^{i\alpha}=-\frac1{uv},\quad n=e^{-i\alpha}=-uv,\quad k=e^{i(\beta-\gamma)}=\frac uv,\quad c=\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin\gamma}=\frac{v(u^2-1)}{u(v^2-1)} .\] The circumcentre lies above the midpoint of $BC$ at height $\tfrac{BC}2\cot\alpha$, which gives \[o=\frac{c-1}2+\frac{i(1+c)}2\cdot\frac{\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{u^2v^2+uv-v^2+1}{(v^2-1)(uv+1)} .\] For points $p,q,r,s$ of the unit circle the lines $pq$ and $rs$ meet at $\dfrac{pq(r+s)-rs(p+q)}{pq-rs}$. Hence, with $\Pi=u^2v^2+uv+v^2-1$, \[e=BL\cap NK=-\frac{u\,\Pi}{v(u^3v+1)},\qquad f=BN\cap KL=\frac{u^2v^2-uv-v^2-1}{uv^3+1},\] and a direct subtraction gives \[f-o=-\frac{v(u+v)\,\Pi}{(v^2-1)(uv+1)(uv^3+1)} .\] ($\Pi\ne0$, since $e\ne0$: $D$ is not on the line $BL$.) Therefore \[\frac{e-0}{f-o}=\frac{u\,(v^2-1)(uv+1)(uv^3+1)}{v^2\,(u+v)(u^3v+1)} .\]

This quotient is purely imaginary. For unit complex numbers $z,w$ we have $z+w=\sqrt{zw}\,\bigl(\sqrt{z/w}+\sqrt{w/z}\bigr)$, a square root of $zw$ times a real number, and $v^2-1=v\,(v-\bar v)=v\cdot2i\sin\gamma$. Writing every factor this way, \[uv+1=\sqrt{uv}\cdot2\cos\tfrac{\beta+\gamma}2,\quad uv^3+1=\sqrt{uv^3}\cdot2\cos\tfrac{\beta+3\gamma}2,\quad u+v=\sqrt{uv}\cdot2\cos\tfrac{\beta-\gamma}2,\quad u^3v+1=\sqrt{u^3v}\cdot2\cos\tfrac{3\beta+\gamma}2,\] (with $\sqrt{u^av^b}=e^{i(a\beta+b\gamma)/2}$), the unimodular parts multiply to \[\frac{u\cdot v\cdot\sqrt{uv}\cdot\sqrt{uv^3}}{v^2\cdot\sqrt{uv}\cdot\sqrt{u^3v}}=u^{1+\frac12-\frac32}\,v^{1+\frac32-2-\frac12}=1,\] so $\dfrac{e}{f-o}=2i\sin\gamma\cdot(\text{real number})$ is purely imaginary. Hence the vector $\vec{DE}$ is perpendicular to the vector $\vec{OF}$, that is, $ED\perp OF$.

Problem 16

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Multiplicative Functions

Let $f:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ be a multiplicative function such that $f(n)\le n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$. A pair of positive integers $(a_1,d)$ is called Lithuanian if the sequence given by $a_{n+1}=f(a_n+d)$ is bounded.

Prove that every pair of positive integers is Lithuanian if and only if there are infinitely many prime numbers $p$ for which $f(p^{k_p})<p^{k_p}$ for some positive integer $k_p$.

Note: A function $f:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is multiplicative if $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ for all coprime $m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$. A sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots$ is bounded if there exists an $M$ such that $|a_n|<M$ for all $n$.

Show the solution – A window that cannot be crossed

Call a prime $p$ bad if $f(p^k)<p^k$ for some $k\ge1$, and good otherwise. Since $f$ is multiplicative, for pairwise coprime $m_1,\dots,m_r$ we have $f(m_1\cdots m_r)=f(m_1)\cdots f(m_r)$ (induction on $r$).

A basic estimate. If $p^k\,\|\,m$ (that is, $m=p^ks$ with $p\nmid s$) and $f(p^k)<p^k$, then \[f(m)=f(p^k)f(s)\le(p^k-1)s=m-\frac m{p^k}.\]

Infinitely many bad primes $\Rightarrow$ every pair is Lithuanian. Fix $(a_1,d)$. Choose $d$ distinct bad primes $p_1,\dots,p_d$ and exponents $k_i$ with $f(p_i^{k_i})<p_i^{k_i}$; put $q_i=p_i^{k_i}$. By the Chinese remainder theorem (the moduli $p_i^{k_i+1}$ are pairwise coprime) there is a $V$ with \[V+i-1\equiv q_i\pmod{p_i^{k_i+1}}\qquad(i=1,\dots,d),\] and adding a multiple of $\prod p_i^{k_i+1}$ we may also assume $V>a_1$ and $V\ge d\,q_i$ for all $i$. Then $p_i^{k_i}\,\|\,V+i-1$, so for every $m=V+i-1$ in the window $\{V,V+1,\dots,V+d-1\}$ the basic estimate gives \[f(m)\le m-\frac m{q_i}\le m-\frac V{q_i}\le m-d\le V-1 .\] Now we show by induction that $a_n<V$ for all $n$. This holds for $n=1$. If $a_n\le V-1$, then $a_n+d\le V+d-1$. Either $a_n+d<V$, and then $a_{n+1}=f(a_n+d)\le a_n+d<V$; or $a_n+d$ lies in the window, and then $a_{n+1}=f(a_n+d)\le V-1$. So the sequence is bounded and $(a_1,d)$ is Lithuanian.

Finitely many bad primes $\Rightarrow$ some pair is not Lithuanian. Let $Q$ be the product of the bad primes ($Q=1$ if there are none) and take $(a_1,d)=(1,Q)$. We claim $a_n=1+(n-1)Q$. It holds for $n=1$; if it holds for $n$, then $a_n+Q=1+nQ\ge2$ is coprime to every bad prime, so it is a product of powers of distinct good primes, each fixed by $f$, and $a_{n+1}=f(1+nQ)=1+nQ$. Hence $a_n\to\infty$ and $(1,Q)$ is not Lithuanian.

The two parts together prove the equivalence.

Problem 17

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

A positive integer $n$ is dangerous if it can be expressed as $n=a\cdot b$, where $a,b$ are positive integers such that $|a-b|<6$. Does there exist an infinite strictly increasing arithmetic progression of positive integers that contains no dangerous numbers?

Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – The progression 30k+7

Yes. We show that no number $n=30k+7$ ($k\ge0$) is dangerous. Suppose $n=ab$ with $a\ge b\ge1$ and $t=a-b\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$.

  • $n$ is odd, so $a$ and $b$ are odd and $t$ is even: $t\in\{0,2,4\}$.

  • $t=0$: $n=b^2$. Squares are $\equiv0,1,4\pmod5$, but $n\equiv2\pmod5$.

  • $t=2$: $n=b(b+2)$, so $n+1=(b+1)^2\equiv0,1,4\pmod5$, but $n+1\equiv3\pmod5$.

  • $t=4$: $n=b(b+4)$, so $n+4=(b+2)^2\equiv0,1\pmod3$, but $n+4=30k+11\equiv2\pmod3$.

Every case is impossible, so $7,37,67,97,\dots$ is an infinite strictly increasing arithmetic progression of positive integers without dangerous numbers. The answer is Yes.

Show solution 2 – The progression 60k+22

Yes. We show that no number $n=60k+22$ ($k\ge0$) is dangerous. Note $n\equiv2\pmod4$, $n\equiv1\pmod3$, $n\equiv2\pmod5$. Suppose $n=ab$ with $a\ge b\ge1$ and $t=a-b\in\{0,\dots,5\}$.

  • If $a,b$ were both odd, $n$ would be odd; if both were even, $4\mid n$. So $a$ and $b$ have different parities and $t$ is odd: $t\in\{1,3,5\}$.

  • $t=1$: $n=b(b+1)$; for $b\equiv0,1,2\pmod3$ this is $\equiv0,2,0\pmod3$, never $1$.

  • $t=3$: $n=b(b+3)$; for $b\equiv0,1,2,3,4\pmod5$ this is $\equiv0,4,0,3,3\pmod5$, never $2$.

  • $t=5$: $n=b(b+5)\equiv b^2\equiv0,1,4\pmod5$, never $2$.

So $22,82,142,\dots$ is an infinite strictly increasing arithmetic progression of positive integers without dangerous numbers, and the answer is Yes.

Problem 18

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Fermat and Euler Type Results

Determine all positive integers $k$ for which there exists a positive integer $n$ such that \[n+k\mid n!+1.\]

Show the answer

$k=1$ and every $k\ge4$

Show the solution – Wilson's theorem and the k-1 missing factors

We use Wilson's theorem: $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$ for every prime $p$.

Key identity. Let $p$ be a prime and $n=p-k\ge1$. Then \[(p-1)!=n!\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(p-j)\equiv n!\,(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\pmod p,\] so by Wilson \[n!\,(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p. \tag{$*$}\]

A first restriction. If $n+k\mid n!+1$, then every prime factor $q$ of $n+k$ is larger than $n$ (if $q\le n$, then $q\mid n!$ and $q\mid1$).

$k=1$ works. For a prime $p$ and $n=p-1$, Wilson gives $n+1=p\mid(p-1)!+1=n!+1$ (for instance $n=1$: $2\mid 2$).

$k\ge4$ works. Let $N=(k-1)!+(-1)^k\ge6-1>1$ and let $p$ be a prime factor of $N$. If $p\le k-1$, then $p\mid(k-1)!$ and $p\mid1$, impossible. If $p=k$, then $k$ is an odd prime and by Wilson $N\equiv-1-1=-2\not\equiv0\pmod k$, impossible. So $p>k$, and $n=p-k\ge1$. As $p\mid N$ we have $(k-1)!\equiv-(-1)^k=(-1)^{k-1}\pmod p$, and $(*)$ becomes $n!\equiv-1\pmod p$. Thus $n+k=p\mid n!+1$. (Examples: $k=4$, $p=7$, $n=3$: $7\mid 3!+1$; $k=6$, $p=11$, $n=5$: $11\mid 5!+1$.)

$k=2$ and $k=3$ fail. Suppose $n+k\mid n!+1$ with $k\in\{2,3\}$. If $n+k$ were composite, it would be a product of at least two primes, each at least $n+1$, so $n+k\ge(n+1)^2$, i.e. $n^2+n+1\le k\le3$; this forces $n=1$, $k=3$, and $4\nmid 1!+1$. So $p=n+k$ is a prime with $n=p-k\ge1$, and $(*)$ together with $n!\equiv-1$ gives $(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\equiv1\pmod p$:

  • $k=2$: $-1\equiv1$, so $p=2$ and $n=0$, impossible;

  • $k=3$: $2\equiv1$, impossible.

Hence the required $k$ are exactly $\boxed{k=1\text{ and all }k\ge4}$.

Problem 19

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments

The numbers $1,2,\dots,2026$ are written on a blackboard. A bad pair is a pair of integers $(a,b)$ such that $a<b$ and $a$ does not divide $b$. In a move, Bob chooses a bad pair $(a,b)$ from the blackboard, erases $a$ and $b$, and writes the remainder of $b$ when divided by $a$ on the blackboard.

Find the minimum number of moves that Bob must make to ensure there are no bad pairs on the blackboard.

Show the answer

$1008$

Show solution 1 – Counting the groups of merged numbers

A board has no bad pair exactly when any two different numbers on it divide one another (equal numbers are never a bad pair).

Lower bound. Follow every number through the process: a move takes two numbers and replaces them by one, so each number on the final board comes from a group of original numbers, and the groups partition $\{1,\dots,2026\}$. A group of $s$ original numbers costs exactly $s-1$ moves, so if the final board has $F$ numbers, Bob made $2026-F$ moves.

Groups of size $1$ are original numbers that were never touched. They are distinct and lie on the final board, so they form a chain $c_1<c_2<\dots<c_r$ with $c_i\mid c_{i+1}$. Then $c_{i+1}\ge2c_i$, so $2026\ge c_r\ge2^{r-1}$ and $r\le11$. The other $2026-r$ original numbers lie in groups of size at least $2$, so there are at most $\frac{2026-r}2$ such groups, and \[F\le r+\frac{2026-r}2=\frac{2026+r}2\le\frac{2037}2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad F\le1018.\] Hence Bob needs at least $2026-1018=1008$ moves.

1008 moves suffice. Keep the ten numbers $1,2,4,\dots,512$ and pair up all the other $2016$ numbers into pairs $(a,b)$ with $2\le a<b$ and $b\equiv1\pmod a$; such a pair is bad, and the move on it writes $1$. Since the pairs are disjoint, the $1008$ moves can be made in any order. The pairs:

  • for $i=1,\dots,8$ the numbers strictly between $2^i$ and $2^{i+1}$ are $2^i+1,\dots,2^{i+1}-1$; pair them as consecutive numbers $(2^i+1,2^i+2),(2^i+3,2^i+4),\dots$, leaving out $2^{i+1}-1$;

  • the leftovers $3,7,15,31,63,127,255,511$ are paired as $(3,7),(15,31),(63,127),(255,511)$; here $2^{j+1}-1=2(2^j-1)+1$;

  • the numbers $513,514,\dots,2026$ (an even count, $1514$, including $1024$) are paired as consecutive numbers $(513,514),\dots,(2025,2026)$.

For consecutive numbers $(a,a+1)$ with $a\ge2$ the remainder is $1$. The final board is $1,2,4,\dots,512$ together with $1008$ ones, in which any two different numbers divide one another. So the minimum is $\boxed{1008}$.

Show solution 2 – Counting erased original numbers

A board has no bad pair exactly when any two different numbers on it divide one another.

Lower bound. Call the numbers $1,\dots,2026$ written at the start original. The original numbers that are never erased are still on the final board; they are distinct, so any two of them divide one another: they form a chain $c_1<\dots<c_r$ with $c_i\mid c_{i+1}$, hence $c_{i+1}\ge 2c_i$ and $2026\ge c_r\ge 2^{r-1}$, so $r\le11$. Therefore at least $2026-11=2015$ original numbers are erased. A move erases two numbers, so at most two original ones. Hence the number of moves is at least $2015/2$, that is, at least $1008$.

A construction with 1008 moves. Leave $1,2,4,8,\dots,512$ untouched. Every other number is used in exactly one move on a pair $(a,b)$ with $2\le a<b$ and $b\equiv1\pmod a$ (so $a\nmid b$ and the remainder written is $1$):

  • $(3,7)$, $(15,31)$, $(63,127)$, $(255,511)$, since $2(2^j-1)+1=2^{j+1}-1$;

  • for $i=2,\dots,8$, the pairs $(2^i+1,2^i+2),(2^i+3,2^i+4),\dots,(2^{i+1}-3,2^{i+1}-2)$ of consecutive numbers;

  • the pairs $(513,514),(515,516),\dots,(2025,2026)$ of consecutive numbers.

These pairs are disjoint and use every number from $3$ to $2026$ other than $4,8,\dots,512$: that is $2016$ numbers, in $1008$ pairs. The moves can be made in any order, and the final board consists of $1,2,4,\dots,512$ and $1008$ ones, which has no bad pair. The minimum number of moves is $\boxed{1008}$.

Problem 20

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

Let $n\ge3$. The positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ are arranged in a circle so that each integer divides the sum of its two neighbours. Let $m$ be the smallest among $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$. Determine all possible values of \[\frac{1}{a_1a_2}+\frac{1}{a_2a_3}+\dots+\frac{1}{a_na_1}\] in terms of $n$ and $m$.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{k}{m^2}$ for $k=1,2,\dots,n$

Show the solution – Removing the largest number

Write $S(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\sum\frac1{a_ia_{i+1}}$ (indices mod $n$). Call a circle of positive integers good if each number divides the sum of its two neighbours. We show that the possible values of $S$ are exactly $\dfrac k{m^2}$, $k=1,2,\dots,n$.

Insertion and removal. Let $x,y$ be neighbours on a circle and $z=x+y$.

  • Removal. Suppose a good circle with at least $4$ numbers contains $x,z,y$ consecutively with $z=x+y$. Delete $z$. The new neighbour sum of $x$ is the old one minus $z$ plus $y$, i.e. the old one minus $x$, still a multiple of $x$; similarly for $y$; nothing else changes. So the new circle is good. Moreover \[\frac1{xz}+\frac1{zy}=\frac{x+y}{xyz}=\frac1{xy},\] so $S$ does not change.

  • Insertion. Conversely, inserting $z=x+y$ between neighbours $x,y$ of a good circle gives a good circle (the neighbour sums of $x$ and $y$ grow by $x$ and $y$, and $z\mid x+y$), with the same value of $S$.

Every value is $k/m^2$ with $1\le k\le n$. Let a good circle with $n\ge 3$ numbers and minimum $m$ be given. Repeat the following while the circle has at least $4$ numbers and they are not all equal: take a largest number $z$ that has a smaller neighbour (it exists: a block of largest numbers that is not the whole circle has a smaller number next to it). Its neighbours $x,y$ satisfy $0<x+y<2z$ and $z\mid x+y$, so $z=x+y$, and we remove $z$. The minimum stays $m$ (we removed a number larger than one of its neighbours), and $S$ is unchanged. We stop at one of the following circles.

  • All numbers equal, with $j$ numbers ($3\le j\le n$): they all equal $m$, and $S=j/m^2$.

  • Three numbers, not all equal: the largest one, $c$, has a smaller neighbour, so as above $c=a+b$ for the other two numbers $a\le b$. Then $a\mid b+c=a+2b$ gives $a\mid 2b$, and $b\mid a+c=2a+b$ gives $b\mid2a$. Since $b\mid 2a$ and $a\le b$, the quotient $2a/b$ is $1$ or $2$, i.e. $b=2a$ or $b=a$; also $m=a$. Then \[S=\frac1{ab}+\frac1{bc}+\frac1{ca}=\frac1{ab}+\frac{a+b}{abc}=\frac2{ab},\] which is $\dfrac1{m^2}$ if $b=2a$ and $\dfrac2{m^2}$ if $b=a$.

So $S=\dfrac k{m^2}$ with $k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}$.

Every such value occurs. For $1\le k\le n-1$ take $k$ copies of $m$ followed by $2m,3m,\dots,(n-k+1)m$. It is good: two adjacent copies of $m$ have neighbour sums that are multiples of $m$; $jm$ has neighbours $(j-1)m$ and $(j+1)m$ with sum $2jm$; the last number $(n-k+1)m$ has neighbours $(n-k)m$ and $m$ with sum $(n-k+1)m$. The minimum is $m$, and \[S\,m^2=(k-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\frac1{j(j+1)}+\frac1{n-k+1}=(k-1)+\Bigl(1-\frac1{n-k+1}\Bigr)+\frac1{n-k+1}=k .\] (For $k=n-1$ the circle is $m,\dots,m,2m$.) For $k=n$ take all numbers equal to $m$: $S=n/m^2$.

Hence the possible values are $\boxed{\dfrac{k}{m^2}\ \ (k=1,2,\dots,n)}$.
