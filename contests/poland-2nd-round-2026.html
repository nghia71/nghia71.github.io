Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Determine all positive integers $n$ such that there exists positive integer $k$ and primes $p_1,p_2,\dots,p_n,q$ (not necessarily distinct) that satisfy \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{k}{p_i}=\frac{q}{q^2+1}.\]

Show the answer

$n=1$ and $n=2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A prime factor of $q^2+1$ must be one of the $p_i$)

Examples. $n=1$: $k=2$, $p_1=5$, $q=2$ gives $\frac25=\frac{2}{2^2+1}$. $n=2$: $k=1$, $p_1=p_2=5$, $q=2$ gives $\frac15+\frac15=\frac25$.

No other $n$. Let $\Pi=p_1p_2\cdots p_n$. Multiplying the equation by $\Pi(q^2+1)$ gives \[k(q^2+1)\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\Pi}{p_i}=q\,\Pi .\] Let $r$ be any prime factor of $q^2+1$. Then $r$ divides $q\Pi$ and $r\nmid q$ (as $q\mid q^2$, a common factor would divide $1$), so $r\mid\Pi$: every prime factor of $q^2+1$ is one of the $p_i$. Also note that the right-hand side satisfies $\frac q{q^2+1}<\frac1q\le\frac12$.

If $q$ is odd, then $2\mid q^2+1$, so some $p_i=2$ and the left side is at least $\frac k2\ge\frac12$, a contradiction. Hence $q=2$, the right side is $\frac25$, and $5$ is among the $p_i$; say it occurs $c\ge1$ times. Then $\frac{kc}5\le\frac25$, so $kc\in\{1,2\}$.

  • $kc=2$: the $5$'s alone already give $\frac25$, so there is no other prime and $n=c$; this gives $n=1$ ($k=2$) or $n=2$ ($k=1$).

  • $kc=1$: then $k=c=1$ and the other primes $p_i\ne5$ (there are $n-1$ of them) satisfy $\sum\frac1{p_i}=\frac15$. Multiplying by their product $\Pi'$ gives $5\sum\frac{\Pi'}{p_i}=\Pi'$, so $5\mid\Pi'$, which is impossible. (If $n=1$ there are no other primes, the sum is $0\ne\frac15$.)

Therefore the possible values are exactly $n\in\boxed{\{1,2\}}$.

Remark (a variant). Odd $q$ can also be ruled out by size instead of parity. Let $r$ be a prime factor of $q^2+1$; it is one of the $p_i$, so $\frac q{q^2+1}\ge\frac kr\ge\frac1r$, i.e. $r\ge\frac{q^2+1}q>q$, so $r\ge q+1$. If $q^2+1$ had two prime factors (counted with multiplicity), it would be at least $(q+1)^2>q^2+1$. Hence $q^2+1$ is itself a prime. For odd $q$ the number $q^2+1\ge10$ is even, so it is not prime; thus $q=2$ and $q^2+1=5$, and the proof finishes as above.

Solution 2 (The exact denominator of the sum, then a size argument)

Examples. $n=1$: $k=2$, $p_1=5$, $q=2$ gives $\frac25=\frac{2}{2^2+1}$. $n=2$: $k=1$, $p_1=p_2=5$, $q=2$ gives $\frac15+\frac15=\frac25$.

No other $n$. Let $r_1<r_2<\dots<r_m$ be the distinct primes among $p_1,\dots,p_n$, and let $c_j\ge1$ be the number of indices $i$ with $p_i=r_j$. Grouping equal terms, the equation reads \[\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{kc_j}{r_j}=\frac{q}{q^2+1}.\] The right-hand side is less than $1$ (as $q<q^2+1$), so every term on the left is less than $1$: $kc_j<r_j$, hence $r_j\nmid kc_j$.

The reduced denominator. Put $R=r_1r_2\cdots r_m$. Multiplying by $R$, the left-hand side becomes the integer \[N=\sum_{j=1}^m kc_j\cdot\frac{R}{r_j},\] so the sum equals $\frac NR$. For each $j$, every summand of $N$ except the $j$-th contains the factor $r_j$, while the $j$-th is $kc_j\cdot\frac R{r_j}$, a product of numbers not divisible by the prime $r_j$. Hence $r_j\nmid N$ for every $j$, so $\gcd(N,R)=1$ and $\frac NR$ is in lowest terms. The fraction $\frac q{q^2+1}$ is also in lowest terms, since a common divisor of $q$ and $q^2+1$ divides $q^2+1-q\cdot q=1$. Two equal fractions in lowest terms with positive denominators have equal denominators, so \[r_1r_2\cdots r_m=q^2+1 .\]

Only one prime occurs. Write $D=q^2+1$. If $m\ge2$, then $r_1^2<r_1r_2\le D$, so $r_1<\sqrt D$. The first group alone gives \[\frac{q}{D}\ \ge\ \frac{kc_1}{r_1}\ \ge\ \frac1{r_1}\ >\ \frac1{\sqrt D},\] that is, $q>\sqrt D=\sqrt{q^2+1}$, which is false. Hence $m=1$ and $r_1=q^2+1$ is a prime.

Finishing. If $q$ were odd, $q^2+1$ would be even and at least $10$, not a prime. So $q=2$ and $r_1=5$: all $p_i$ equal $5$, $c_1=n$, and the equation becomes $\frac{kn}5=\frac25$, i.e. $kn=2$. Thus $n=1$ (with $k=2$) or $n=2$ (with $k=1$), and the possible values are exactly $n\in\boxed{\{1,2\}}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Integer $n\ge 2$ is given. Real numbers $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ satisfy

  • $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k^2=n$,

  • $\dfrac{1}{k}\le a_k\le k$  for all $k=1,2,\dots,n$.

Prove that \[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k^2+1}{k}a_k>2n.\]

Show the solution

Solution (A quadratic bound for each term, then the equality case)

For each $k$ we have $\frac1k\le a_k\le k$, hence $(k-a_k)\bigl(a_k-\frac1k\bigr)\ge0$. Expanding, \[\Bigl(k+\frac1k\Bigr)a_k-a_k^2-1\ge0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{k^2+1}{k}\,a_k\ge a_k^2+1,\] with equality if and only if $a_k\in\{k,\frac1k\}$. Summing over $k$ and using $\sum a_k^2=n$: \[\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{k^2+1}{k}a_k\ \ge\ \sum_{k=1}^n a_k^2+n=2n .\] It remains to exclude equality. Equality would force $a_k\in\{k,\frac1k\}$ for every $k$, in particular $a_1=1$. Let $T$ be the set of $k\in\{2,\dots,n\}$ with $a_k=\frac1k$ and $T'$ the set with $a_k=k$. Then \[n=\sum a_k^2=1+\sum_{k\in T'}k^2+\sum_{k\in T}\frac1{k^2}.\] If $T=\emptyset$, the right side is $1+\sum_{k=2}^nk^2>1+(n-1)=n$ (as $n\ge2$), a contradiction. If $T\ne\emptyset$, then \[0<\sum_{k\in T}\frac1{k^2}\le\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac1{k^2}<\sum_{k=2}^n\frac1{k(k-1)}=1-\frac1n<1,\] so the right side is an integer plus a number strictly between $0$ and $1$, and cannot equal the integer $n$. Hence equality is impossible and \[\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{k^2+1}{k}a_k>2n.\]

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

Pyramid $ABCDS$ with a convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ as the base is given. Assume that the bisectors of plane angles $\angle BAD$, $\angle BSD$, $\angle BCD$ are concurrent. Prove that the incenters of $ABS$, $BCS$, $CDS$, $DAS$ lie on a common plane.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Weighted averages (vectors))

The hypothesis. The bisectors of $\angle BAD$ and $\angle BCD$ lie in the base plane $\pi$, and the bisector of $\angle BSD$ lies in the plane $SBD$, which meets $\pi$ only along the line $BD$ (as $S\notin\pi$). So the common point $K$ lies on line $BD$. The bisector of $\angle BAD$ meets line $BD$ only at the foot of the bisector, and similarly for the other two angles; by the angle bisector theorem \[\frac{AB}{AD}=\frac{SB}{SD}=\frac{CB}{CD}=\frac{BK}{KD}=:\lambda .\]

Incentres as weighted averages. For a triangle $UVW$ with opposite sides $u,v,w$ the incentre is $\frac{uU+vV+wW}{u+v+w}$ (position vectors). Put $x=SA$, $y=SB$, $z=SC$, $w=SD$, $e_1=AB$, $e_2=BC$, $e_3=CD$, $e_4=DA$; the hypothesis says $e_1=\lambda e_4$, $y=\lambda w$, $e_2=\lambda e_3$. With $N_j=t_jI_j$, where $t_j$ is the perimeter of the corresponding face, \[N_1=yA+xB+e_1S=\lambda(wA+e_4S)+xB,\qquad N_4=wA+xD+e_4S,\] \[N_2=zB+yC+e_2S=\lambda(wC+e_3S)+zB,\qquad N_3=wC+zD+e_3S .\] Hence \[\lambda N_4-N_1=x(\lambda D-B),\qquad \lambda N_3-N_2=z(\lambda D-B),\] and therefore \[-z\,t_1I_1+x\,t_2I_2-x\lambda\,t_3I_3+z\lambda\,t_4I_4=z(\lambda N_4-N_1)-x(\lambda N_3-N_2)=0 .\] The same computation with each point replaced by the number $1$ (i.e. comparing total weights) gives $\lambda t_4-t_1=x(\lambda-1)$ and $\lambda t_3-t_2=z(\lambda-1)$, so the coefficients sum to $z\,x(\lambda-1)-x\,z(\lambda-1)=0$.

So we have a relation $c_1I_1+c_2I_2+c_3I_3+c_4I_4=0$ with $c_1+c_2+c_3+c_4=0$ and not all $c_j=0$ (indeed $c_1=-zt_1\ne0$). Then $c_2(I_2-I_1)+c_3(I_3-I_1)+c_4(I_4-I_1)=0$ with $(c_2,c_3,c_4)\ne(0,0,0)$ (otherwise $c_1=0$ too), so the vectors $I_2-I_1$, $I_3-I_1$, $I_4-I_1$ are linearly dependent and the four incentres lie in one plane.

Solution 2 (Menelaus twice: the lines $I_1I_4$ and $I_2I_3$ meet on $BD$)

The hypothesis. The common point of the three bisectors lies in the base plane and in the plane $SBD$, hence on line $BD$; it is the foot of each bisector, and the angle bisector theorem gives $\frac{AB}{AD}=\frac{SB}{SD}=\frac{CB}{CD}=\lambda$.

Case $\lambda=1$. Then $A$, $S$, $C$ are equidistant from $B$ and $D$, so they lie in the perpendicular bisector plane $\Sigma$ of $BD$. The reflection in $\Sigma$ fixes $A,S,C$ and swaps $B,D$, so it maps triangle $ABS$ to $ADS$ and $CBS$ to $CDS$, hence $I_1\leftrightarrow I_4$ and $I_2\leftrightarrow I_3$. These points are not in $\Sigma$ (an interior point of triangle $ABS$ is not in $\Sigma$, which meets that triangle only along $AS$), so both lines $I_1I_4$ and $I_2I_3$ are perpendicular to $\Sigma$, hence parallel, and the four points are coplanar.

Case $\lambda\ne1$. Let $K'$ be the point of line $BD$ outside segment $BD$ with $\frac{K'B}{K'D}=\lambda$. We show that line $I_1I_4$ passes through $K'$; by the same argument (with $C$ in place of $A$) so does $I_2I_3$. Then $I_1I_4$ and $I_2I_3$ meet at $K'$ and the four points are coplanar.

In triangle $ABS$ let the bisector from $S$ meet $AB$ at $E_1$, and in triangle $ADS$ let the bisector from $S$ meet $AD$ at $E_4$. Then $\frac{AE_1}{E_1B}=\frac{SA}{SB}$, $\frac{AE_4}{E_4D}=\frac{SA}{SD}$, and the incentres divide the bisectors as $\frac{SI_1}{I_1E_1}=\frac{SA+SB}{AB}$, $\frac{SI_4}{I_4E_4}=\frac{SA+SD}{AD}$.

Line $E_1E_4$ passes through $K'$. By Menelaus in triangle $ABD$ for the line $E_1E_4$ (in the plane $\pi$), it meets line $BD$ at the point $R$ with $\frac{RB}{RD}=\frac{E_1B}{AE_1}\cdot\frac{AE_4}{E_4D}=\frac{SB}{SA}\cdot\frac{SA}{SD}=\lambda$, outside the segment (the line crosses the sides $AB$, $AD$ internally). So $R=K'$. Menelaus in triangle $AE_1E_4$ for the line $BDK'$ gives \[\frac{E_1K'}{K'E_4}=\frac{BE_1}{AB}\cdot\frac{AD}{DE_4}=\frac{SB}{SA+SB}\cdot\frac{SA+SD}{SD}. \tag{1}\]

Line $I_1I_4$ meets line $E_1E_4$ at the same point. The points $I_1\in SE_1$, $I_4\in SE_4$ lie in the plane $SE_1E_4$; by Menelaus in triangle $SE_1E_4$, line $I_1I_4$ meets line $E_1E_4$ outside the segment at the point $R'$ with \[\frac{E_1R'}{R'E_4}=\frac{E_1I_1}{I_1S}\cdot\frac{SI_4}{I_4E_4}=\frac{AB}{SA+SB}\cdot\frac{SA+SD}{AD}=\lambda\,\frac{SA+SD}{SA+SB}.\] Since $\lambda=\frac{SB}{SD}$, this equals the ratio (1). On a line, the external point dividing $E_1E_4$ in a given ratio $\ne1$ is unique, so $R'=K'$ (the ratio is $\ne1$ because $\frac{SB(SA+SD)}{SD(SA+SB)}=1$ would mean $SB=SD$, i.e. $\lambda=1$).

Hence $K'\in I_1I_4$, likewise $K'\in I_2I_3$, and $I_1,I_2,I_3,I_4$ lie in the plane through these two intersecting lines. (If the two lines coincided, the points would be collinear, which is also coplanar.)

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing

Non-isosceles triangle $ABC$ with circumcircle $\Omega$ and circumcenter $O$ is given. Point $M$ is the midpoint of the arc $BC$ of $\Omega$ that doesn't contain $A$. Circumcircle of the triangle $AOM$ meets lines $AB$ and $AC$ at $P\ne A$ and $Q\ne A$, respectively. Assume that $A,B,P$ lie on the line $AB$ in that order and that $Q,A,C$ lie on the line $AC$ in that order. Prove that the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, the line perpendicular to $BC$ passing through point $A$ meet on $\Omega$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Directed angles)

We use directed angles $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circle $AOMPQ$ and let $A_1$ be the second intersection of the altitude from $A$ with $\Omega$; we show that the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$ is the line $MA_1$. Note $M\ne A_1$ and $M\ne A$: either equality would put $M$ on the altitude, so the bisector $AM$ would be perpendicular to $BC$ and $AB=AC$.

Step 1: $MP=MQ$. In $\omega$, the chords $MP$ and $MQ$ are seen from $A$ under the angles between line $AM$ and lines $AB$, $AC$. Since $AM$ bisects $\angle BAC$, these angles are both $\frac{\angle A}2$ (as angles between lines), so $MP=2\rho\sin\frac{\angle A}2=MQ$, where $\rho$ is the radius of $\omega$. Thus $M$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, and it suffices to prove $MA_1\perp PQ$.

Step 2: $OQ\perp CM$. Let $A^*$ be the antipode of $A$ on $\Omega$. Since $OA=OM$, $\measuredangle(MA,MO)=\measuredangle(AO,AM)$, and \[\measuredangle(AO,AM)=\measuredangle(CA^*,CM)=\measuredangle(CA^*,CA)+\measuredangle(CA,CM)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(CA,CM).\] As $A,O,M,Q$ lie on $\omega$ (and $Q\ne A$; also $Q\ne O$, since $O$ is not on line $AC$ outside segment $AC$, while $Q$ is), $\measuredangle(AC,QO)=\measuredangle(QA,QO)=\measuredangle(MA,MO)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(CA,CM)$, hence $\measuredangle(CM,QO)=90^\circ$.

Step 3: the isogonal lines. $\measuredangle(AB,AA_1)=\measuredangle(AO,AC)$. Indeed $\measuredangle(AB,AA_1)=\measuredangle(AB,BC)+90^\circ$ since $AA_1\perp BC$, while $\measuredangle(AO,AC)=\measuredangle(AA^*,AC)=\measuredangle(BA^*,BC)=\measuredangle(BA^*,BA)+\measuredangle(BA,BC)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(BA,BC)$.

Step 4: $MA_1\perp PQ$. Using $P\in AB$, $P\ne O$ (as for $Q$) and the circle $\omega$, then Step 2, then the circle $\Omega$: \[\measuredangle(PQ,AB)=\measuredangle(PQ,PA)=\measuredangle(OQ,OA)=\measuredangle(OQ,CM)+\measuredangle(CM,OA)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(CM,OA),\] \[\measuredangle(AB,MA_1)=\measuredangle(AB,AA_1)+\measuredangle(A_1A,A_1M)=\measuredangle(AO,AC)+\measuredangle(CA,CM).\] Adding, $\measuredangle(PQ,MA_1)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(CM,OA)+\measuredangle(AO,AC)+\measuredangle(CA,CM)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(CM,CM)=90^\circ$.

So the line through $M$ perpendicular to $PQ$, which is the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$ by Step 1, is the line $MA_1$. It meets the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$ at $A_1$ (the lines differ, since $M$ is not on the altitude), and $A_1\in\Omega$.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers)

Let $\Omega$ be the unit circle, $O=0$, and choose complex numbers $x,y,z$ of modulus $1$ with $A=a=x^2$, $B=b=y^2$, $C=c=z^2$. The two midpoints of the arcs $BC$ are the square roots $\pm yz$ of $bc$; replacing $z$ by $-z$ if necessary we may assume $M=m=-yz$. For points $u,v$ on the unit circle, the line $uv$ consists of the $w$ with $w+uv\bar w=u+v$, and chords $uv$, $u'v'$ are perpendicular iff $uv=-u'v'$. In particular the second intersection of the altitude from $A$ with $\Omega$ is $A_1=-\frac{bc}a$.

The circle $\omega=(AOM)$. Its centre is $o=\frac{am}{a+m}$ (note $a+m\ne0$: otherwise $A=-m$ would be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ containing $A$, i.e. $AB=AC$): indeed $|o|=\frac{1}{|a+m|}$, $|o-a|=\bigl|\frac{-a^2}{a+m}\bigr|=\frac1{|a+m|}$, $|o-m|=\frac1{|a+m|}$.

The points $P,Q$. The circle is $w\bar w=\bar o w+o\bar w$. Substituting $\bar w=\frac{a+b-w}{ab}$ (line $AB$) gives the quadratic \[w^2-(a+b-ab\bar o+o)\,w+o(a+b)=0,\] whose roots are $a$ and $p$; by Vieta $p=\frac{o(a+b)}a=\frac{m(a+b)}{a+m}$. Likewise $q=\frac{m(a+c)}{a+m}$.

$A_1P=A_1Q$. Using $m=-yz$, \[A_1-p=-\frac{y^2z^2}{x^2}+\frac{yz(x^2+y^2)}{x^2-yz}=\frac{yz\,N_P}{x^2(x^2-yz)},\qquad N_P=x^4+x^2y^2-x^2yz+y^2z^2,\] \[A_1-q=\frac{yz\,N_Q}{x^2(x^2-yz)},\qquad N_Q=x^4+x^2z^2-x^2yz+y^2z^2 .\] Since $\bar x=1/x$ etc., $\overline{N_P}=\frac1{x^4}+\frac1{x^2y^2}-\frac1{x^2yz}+\frac1{y^2z^2}=\frac{N_Q}{x^4y^2z^2}$, so $|N_P|=|N_Q|$ and $|A_1-p|=|A_1-q|$. Thus $A_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$; it also lies on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $BC$ and on $\Omega$. The two lines are different (the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$ also contains $M$, as $|m-p|=|m-q|$ follows from $m-p=\frac{m(m-b)}{a+m}$, $m-q=\frac{m(m-c)}{a+m}$ and $|m-b|=|m-c|$; and $M$ is not on the altitude because $AB\ne AC$), so they meet exactly at $A_1\in\Omega$.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Integer $n\ge 2$ and points $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_n,B_1,B_2,\dots,B_n$ on a plane are given. Assume that

  • $A_iB_i>101$  for all $i=1,2,\dots,n$,

  • $A_iB_j\le 100$  for all $1\le i<j\le n$.

Prove that $n\le 10^6$.

Show the answer

proof (in fact $n\le 40402$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The points $B_j$ are far apart; compare areas)

Key observation. For $i<j$ the triangle inequality gives \[B_iB_j\ \ge\ A_iB_i-A_iB_j\ >\ 101-100=1 .\] So any two of the points $B_1,\dots,B_n$ are more than $1$ apart. Moreover $A_1B_j\le100$ for all $j\ge2$.

Area count. The open discs of radius $\frac12$ around $B_2,\dots,B_n$ are pairwise disjoint (their centres are more than $1$ apart), and each lies inside the disc of radius $100+\frac12$ around $A_1$. Comparing areas, \[(n-1)\cdot\pi\Bigl(\frac12\Bigr)^2\le\pi\Bigl(100\tfrac12\Bigr)^2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad n-1\le 4\cdot100.5^2=40401 .\] Hence $n\le 40402<10^6$.

Solution 2 (Pigeonhole in a grid)

For $i<j$, the triangle inequality in triangle $A_iB_iB_j$ gives $B_iB_j\ge A_iB_i-A_iB_j>101-100=1$. Thus the points $B_2,\dots,B_n$ are pairwise more than $1$ apart, and they all lie in the closed disc of radius $100$ around $A_1$, hence in the square of side $200$ centred at $A_1$ with sides parallel to fixed axes.

Divide this square into $283\times283$ closed squares of side $\frac{200}{283}$. Each point $B_j$ ($j\ge2$) lies in at least one of them (a point on a common edge lies in several); assign it to one such small square. Two points in the same closed small square are at distance at most its diagonal $\frac{200\sqrt2}{283}<1$, since $283>200\sqrt2\approx282.84$. So no small square is assigned two of the points $B_2,\dots,B_n$, and \[n-1\le283^2=80089,\qquad n\le80090<10^6 .\]

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Integer $n\ge 2$ and positive integers $a_1<a_2<\dots<a_n$ are given. Assume that \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_i}{a_i}=1\] for some $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\in\{1,2\}$. Prove that $a_i\le(2n)^{2^{i-1}}$ for all $i=1,2,\dots,n$.

Show the solution

Solution (The remainder is at least $1/(a_1\cdots a_{i-1})$)

We prove $a_i\le(2n)^{2^{i-1}}$ by strong induction on $i$.

Fix $i$ and put \[r_i=1-\sum_{j<i}\frac{x_j}{a_j}=\sum_{j\ge i}\frac{x_j}{a_j}.\] The right-hand form shows $r_i>0$. The left-hand form shows that $r_i\cdot a_1a_2\cdots a_{i-1}$ is an integer; being positive, it is at least $1$, so $r_i\ge\dfrac1{a_1\cdots a_{i-1}}$ (for $i=1$: $r_1=1$, empty product). On the other hand $a_j\ge a_i$ and $x_j\le2$ for $j\ge i$, so \[r_i\le\sum_{j=i}^n\frac2{a_i}=\frac{2(n-i+1)}{a_i}\le\frac{2n}{a_i}.\] Combining, $a_i\le 2n\cdot a_1a_2\cdots a_{i-1}$. By the induction hypothesis $a_j\le(2n)^{2^{j-1}}$ for $j<i$, hence \[a_i\le 2n\cdot(2n)^{1+2+\dots+2^{i-2}}=(2n)^{1+(2^{i-1}-1)}=(2n)^{2^{i-1}} .\] For $i=1$ this reads $a_1\le2n$, which is the case of the empty product. This completes the induction.
