Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · also AMC 10A Problem 2

A sign in a parking lot states that the total price includes a $27\%$ tax. To the nearest whole number, what percentage of the amount a customer pays is this tax?

(A)$21\%$(B)$22\%$(C)$27\%$(D)$37\%$(E)$73\%$
Show the answer

(A) $21\%$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pick a base price)

Suppose the price before tax is $100$. The tax is $27$ and the customer pays $127$. The tax is \[\frac{27}{127}\approx0.2126\] of the amount paid, that is, about $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Solution 2 (Bound the fraction without dividing)

The tax is $\frac{27}{127}$ of the amount paid. To round to the nearest percent we only need to know whether it is above or below $20.5\%$ and $21.5\%$: \[0.205\cdot127=26.035<27\qquad\text{and}\qquad0.215\cdot127=27.305>27.\] So $20.5\%<\frac{27}{127}<21.5\%$, which rounds to $21\%$. \[\boxed{21\%}\]

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry · also AMC 10A Problem 3

Square $ABCD$ has side length $24$. Let $E$ be the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$, and let $F$ be the intersection of $\overline{AE}$ and $\overline{BD}$. What is the area of $\triangle BEF$?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$45$(D)$48$(E)$52$
Show the answer

(D) $48$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Similar triangles)

Since $BE\parallel AD$, triangles $FBE$ and $FDA$ are similar (alternate angles), with ratio $BE:DA=12:24=1:2$. So $F$ divides the diagonal $BD$ in the ratio $BF:FD=1:2$, and its distance from line $BC$ is $\frac13$ of the distance from $D$ to $BC$, namely $\frac13\cdot24=8$. Hence \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot BE\cdot8=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $B=(0,0)$, $C=(24,0)$, $A=(0,24)$, $D=(24,24)$, so $E=(12,0)$. Line $BD$ is $y=x$ and line $AE$ is $y=24-2x$. They meet where $x=24-2x$, so $F=(8,8)$. Triangle $BEF$ has base $BE=12$ on the $x$-axis and height $8$: \[[BEF]=\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot8=\boxed{48}.\]

Solution 3 (Area ratios)

$[ABE]=\frac12\cdot AB\cdot BE=\frac12\cdot24\cdot12=144$. As in Solution 1, $AF:FE=DF:FB=2:1$, so $FE=\frac13 AE$. Triangles $BEF$ and $BEA$ share the vertex $B$ and have bases $FE$ and $AE$ on the same line, so \[[BEF]=\tfrac13[ABE]=\tfrac13\cdot144=\boxed{48}.\]

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 24: Quadrilaterals and Polygon Structure · also AMC 10A Problem 4

Half of the internal angles of a polygon measure $150^\circ$ each, and the other half of its internal angles measure $190^\circ$ each. How many sides does the polygon have?

(A)$36$(B)$40$(C)$60$(D)$72$(E)$180$
Show the answer

(A) $36$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Exterior angles)

Walking around the polygon, the turning (exterior) angle at a vertex is $180^\circ$ minus the interior angle: $30^\circ$ at a $150^\circ$ vertex and $-10^\circ$ at a $190^\circ$ (reflex) vertex. The turns add up to one full turn, $360^\circ$. With $n/2$ vertices of each kind, \[\tfrac n2\cdot30-\tfrac n2\cdot10=10n=360,\qquad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Solution 2 (Interior angle sum)

The interior angles of an $n$-gon add up to $180(n-2)$ degrees. Here they add up to $\frac n2\cdot150+\frac n2\cdot190=170n$. So \[170n=180n-360\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=\boxed{36}.\]

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · also AMC 10A Problem 5

Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ be a sequence with $a_1 = 94$, $a_2 = 190$, and $a_n$ equal to the arithmetic mean of the previous $n-1$ terms for $n \ge 3$. What is the value of $a_{10}$?

(A)$47$(B)$95$(C)$142$(D)$284$(E)$564$
Show the answer

(C) $142$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Adding the mean keeps the mean)

$a_3=\frac{94+190}2=142$. Now the first three terms have mean $142$: adding a number equal to the mean of a list does not change the mean. So $a_4=142$, and the mean is still $142$; the same happens at every step. Hence every term from $a_3$ on is $142$, and $a_{10}=\boxed{142}$.

Solution 2 (Track the partial sums)

Let $S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n$. For $n\ge2$ we have $a_{n+1}=S_n/n$, so \[S_{n+1}=S_n+\frac{S_n}{n}=\frac{n+1}{n}S_n,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{S_{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{S_n}{n}.\] So $S_n/n$ is constant for $n\ge2$: it equals $S_2/2=\frac{284}2=142$. Therefore $a_{10}=S_9/9=\boxed{142}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · also AMC 10A Problem 7

How many ordered triples of positive integers $(x,y,z)$ satisfy both of the following equations? \[x+y+z=31,\qquad x+3y+3z=41\]

(A)$4$(B)$5$(C)$6$(D)$7$(E)$8$
Show the answer

(A) $4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Eliminate $x$)

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $2y+2z=10$, so $y+z=5$ and then $x=31-5=26$. The positive solutions of $y+z=5$ are $(y,z)=(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$, and each gives a valid triple with $x=26$. There are $\boxed{4}$ triples.

Solution 2 (Treat $y+z$ as one unknown)

Let $s=y+z$. Then $x+s=31$ and $x+3s=41$, a $2\times2$ system with $s=5$, $x=26$. The number of ways to write $5$ as an ordered sum of two positive integers is $5-1=4$ (choose $y\in\{1,2,3,4\}$). Answer: $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · also AMC 10A Problem 10

For what positive integer $n$ is the mean of $1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots, n^2$ exactly $32\%$ greater than its median?

(A)$19$(B)$39$(C)$49$(D)$89$(E)$169$

Same problem as AMC 10A Problem 10, except that "odd" is left out. The solutions below treat odd $n$; this note completes them. For even $n$ the median is $\frac{(n/2)^2+(n/2+1)^2}2=\frac{n^2+2n+2}4$, and $\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6=\frac{33}{25}\cdot\frac{n^2+2n+2}4$ becomes $n^2-48n-148=0$, whose discriminant $2896$ is not a perfect square. So no even $n$ works and the answer is still $49$.

Show the answer

(C) $49$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Formulas for mean and median)

For odd $n$ the median is the middle square $\left(\frac{n+1}2\right)^2$. The mean is \[\frac{1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2}{n}=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6.\] Their ratio is \[\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)/6}{(n+1)^2/4}=\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac{132}{100}=\frac{33}{25}.\] So $50(2n+1)=99(n+1)$, that is $100n+50=99n+99$, and $n=\boxed{49}$.

Solution 2 (Check against the choices)

Using the ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}$ from Solution 1, the answer must make it exactly $1.32$. For $n=49$: $\frac{2\cdot99}{3\cdot50}=\frac{198}{150}=1.32$. Directly: the median is $25^2=625$ and the mean is $\frac{50\cdot99}{6}=825=1.32\cdot625$. The ratio $\frac{2(2n+1)}{3(n+1)}=\frac43-\frac{2}{3(n+1)}$ increases with $n$, so no other $n$ works. Answer $\boxed{49}$.

Problem 7

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool

What is the value of $(1+i)^{20}+(1-i)^{20}$, where $i=\sqrt{-1}$?

(A)$-2048$(B)$-1024$(C)$0$(D)$1024$(E)$2048$
Show the answer

(A) $-2048$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Square first)

$(1+i)^2=2i$ and $(1-i)^2=-2i$. So \[(1+i)^{20}=(2i)^{10}=2^{10}i^{10}=-1024,\qquad(1-i)^{20}=(-2i)^{10}=2^{10}i^{10}=-1024.\] The sum is $\boxed{-2048}$.

Solution 2 (Polar form)

$1\pm i=\sqrt2\,e^{\pm i\pi/4}$, so $(1\pm i)^{20}=2^{10}e^{\pm5\pi i}$ and the sum is $2^{10}\cdot2\cos5\pi=2^{11}\cdot(-1)=\boxed{-2048}$.

Problem 8

Algebra · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations

At Euclid High School, $25$ students registered to take the AMC 10 and $75$ students registered to take the AMC 12. (No student registered to take both.) However, not all $100$ students showed up on test day. To compute the percentage of those students who actually took one of the exams, Euclid High School performed the following incorrect two-step process:

  • Step 1: The school computed both the percentage of AMC 10 registrants who actually took that exam and the percentage of AMC 12 registrants who actually took that exam.
  • Step 2: The school took the average of the previous two numbers to obtain a final percentage.

Suppose that Euclid High School's process yielded an answer of $60\%$. What is the minimum number of students who might have actually taken one of the exams?

(A)$30$(B)$35$(C)$40$(D)$50$(E)$60$
Show the answer

(C) $40$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Set up the equation)

Let $x$ AMC 10 students and $y$ AMC 12 students take the tests. The school's number is \[\frac12\left(\frac{x}{25}+\frac{y}{75}\right)=\frac{60}{100}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad3x+y=90.\] We want to minimise $x+y=90-2x$, so $x$ should be as large as possible: $x=25$, $y=15$. Then $x+y=\boxed{40}$ (and $y=15\le75$ is fine).

Solution 2 (Weights)

In the school's average each AMC 10 student counts $\frac1{50}$ of the total and each AMC 12 student counts $\frac1{150}$, three times less. Reaching $60\%=\frac{90}{150}$ "costs" $90$ units, where an AMC 10 student is worth $3$ units and an AMC 12 student $1$ unit. To use the fewest students, use the expensive ones first: all $25$ AMC 10 students give $75$ units, and $15$ AMC 12 students give the remaining $15$. Total $\boxed{40}$ students.

Problem 9

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

How many ordered pairs $(x,y)$ of real numbers satisfy the following system of equations? \[|x^2-y^2|=4,\qquad\frac{16x}{y}=xy\]

(A)$2$(B)$4$(C)$6$(D)$8$(E)$10$
Show the answer

(E) $10$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor the second equation)

We need $y\ne0$, and then the second equation is $16x=xy^2$, i.e. $x(16-y^2)=0$.

  • $x=0$: $|{-y^2}|=4$, $y=\pm2$. Two pairs.
  • $y=\pm4$: $|x^2-16|=4$, so $x^2=20$ or $x^2=12$: four values of $x$, each with $y=4$ or $y=-4$. Eight pairs.

The cases do not overlap, so there are $\boxed{10}$ pairs.

Solution 2 (Picture it)

The first equation is the pair of hyperbolas $x^2-y^2=\pm4$; the second (with $y\ne0$) is the union of the line $x=0$ and the lines $y=\pm4$. The line $x=0$ meets only $y^2-x^2=4$, at $(0,\pm2)$. Each horizontal line $y=\pm4$ meets $x^2-y^2=4$ twice ($x=\pm\sqrt{20}$) and $y^2-x^2=4$ twice ($x=\pm\sqrt{12}$). Total $2+2\cdot4=\boxed{10}$.

Problem 10

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 42: Recursion, Paths, and Dynamic Counting · also AMC 10A Problem 15

The tree diagram below has nodes labeled with rational numbers. The top node, at level $0$, is $\frac12$. Each node $\frac ab$ sprouts two nodes in the level below it, $\frac{a}{a+b}$ and $\frac{b}{a+b}$. For example, level $1$ has nodes $\frac{1}{1+2}=\frac13$ and $\frac{2}{1+2}=\frac23$. At what level does the node $\frac{20}{27}$ appear in the diagram?

(A)$5$(B)$8$(C)$12$(D)$26$(E)$\text{The node }\frac{20}{27}\text{ does not appear in the diagram.}$
Show the answer

(B) $8$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Climb back up the tree)

Every entry is less than $1$, and every entry is in lowest terms: the root is, and $\gcd(a,a+b)=\gcd(b,a+b)=\gcd(a,b)=1$. So a child $\frac pq$ of $\frac ab$, written in lowest terms, has $q=a+b$ and $p=a$ or $p=b$, so its parent is $\frac{p}{q-p}$ or $\frac{q-p}{p}$; only the one that is less than $1$ can be in the tree. Going up from $\frac{20}{27}$: \[\tfrac{20}{27}\to\tfrac{7}{20}\to\tfrac{7}{13}\to\tfrac{6}{7}\to\tfrac16\to\tfrac15\to\tfrac14\to\tfrac13\to\tfrac12.\] That is $8$ steps up to row $0$, so $\frac{20}{27}$ is in row $\boxed{8}$.

Solution 2 (Continued fractions)

For an entry $\frac ab$ write $\frac ba$ as a continued fraction $[c_0;c_1,\dots,c_m]$. Moving to a child adds exactly $1$ to the sum $c_0+c_1+\cdots+c_m$: the left child $\frac{a}{a+b}$ turns $\frac ba$ into $\frac{a+b}a=1+\frac ba$, which raises $c_0$ by $1$, and the right child $\frac b{a+b}$ turns $\frac ba$ into $\frac{a+b}b=1+\frac ab$, which puts a new $1$ in front of the expansion. (One checks that the representations stay valid.) The root $\frac12$ has $\frac21=[2]$, sum $2$, so an entry in row $r$ has sum $r+2$.

$\frac{27}{20}=1+\frac{7}{20}$, $\frac{20}{7}=2+\frac67$, $\frac76=1+\frac16$, so $\frac{27}{20}=[1;2,1,6]$ with sum $10$. The fraction does occur: undoing the moves (a leading $1$ is removed by taking the reciprocal of the rest, a leading term $c_0\ge2$ is lowered by $1$) walks $[1;2,1,6]\to[2;1,6]\to[1;1,6]\to[1;6]\to[6]\to[5]\to[4]\to[3]\to[2]$, which is exactly the chain of Solution 1. The row is $10-2=\boxed{8}$.

Problem 11

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · also AMC 10A Problem 16

An urn contains $6$ red balls, $4$ black balls, and $4$ white balls. Suppose $7$ balls are drawn at random without replacement and a score is computed as follows:

  • $1$ point is given for each red ball up to a maximum of $3$ points.
  • $1$ point is given for each black ball up to a maximum of $2$ points.
  • $1$ point is given for each white ball up to a maximum of $2$ points.

For example, drawing $1$ red ball, $4$ black balls, and $2$ white balls would earn a score of $1+2+2=5$. What is the probability of getting the lowest possible score?

(A)$\dfrac{1}{858}$(B)$\dfrac{5}{3432}$(C)$\dfrac{1}{312}$(D)$\dfrac{2}{429}$(E)$\dfrac{5}{264}$
Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac{2}{429}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Find the lowest score, then count)

Let the draw have $r$ red, $k$ black and $w$ white balls, $r+k+w=7$.

If $r\ge3$, red gives $3$ points and $k+w=7-r\ge1$ gives at least $1$ more, so the score is at least $4$; it is exactly $4$ only when $k+w=1$, i.e. $r=6$: $(6,1,0)$ or $(6,0,1)$.

If $r=0$: $k+w=7$ with $k,w\le4$, so $\{k,w\}=\{4,3\}$ and the score is $2+2=4$: $(0,4,3)$ or $(0,3,4)$.

If $r=1$: $k+w=6$ with $k,w\le4$, so both are at least $2$ and the score is $1+2+2=5$.

If $r=2$: $k+w=5$, so both are at least $1$ and one is at least $3$; the score is at least $2+2+1=5$.

So the lowest score is $4$. The number of ways: $2\binom66\binom41+2\binom44\binom43=8+8=16$, out of $\binom{14}7=3432$: \[\frac{16}{3432}=\boxed{\tfrac{2}{429}}.\]

Solution 2 (Think about what stays in the urn)

The $7$ balls left in the urn determine the draw. Points are lost only through "extra" balls: each red beyond $3$, each black or white beyond $2$. The score is $7$ minus the number of extras, so we want the most extras possible. Count them by the number $r$ of reds:

  • $r=6$: $3$ red extras, and the seventh ball is not an extra: $3$ extras.
  • $3\le r\le5$: $r-3\le2$ red extras, and the $7-r\le4$ others give at most $\max(0,7-r-2)$, in total at most $2$.
  • $r=0,1,2$: the black and white counts add up to $7,6,5$; with each at most $4$ they have at most $3,2,2$ extras, and $3$ only for counts $4$ and $3$.

So at most $3$ extras (the lowest score is $4$), reached exactly by all $6$ reds plus one other ball, or no reds with $4$ of one of black/white and $3$ of the other. Counting as in Solution 1 gives $16$ draws out of $3432$: $\boxed{\tfrac2{429}}$.

Problem 12

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Ethan writes down five congruences: \[x\equiv0\pmod2,\quad x\equiv0\pmod3,\quad x\equiv1\pmod4,\quad x\equiv5\pmod6,\quad x\equiv7\pmod{12}.\] How many positive integers less than or equal to $2026$ satisfy at least one of these five congruences?

(A)$1688$(B)$1856$(C)$1857$(D)$2025$(E)$2026$
Show the answer

(E) $2026$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Work modulo 12)

All five moduli divide $12$, so look at the residue of $x$ mod $12$:

  • $x\equiv0\pmod2$: $0,2,4,6,8,10$;
  • $x\equiv0\pmod3$: $0,3,6,9$;
  • $x\equiv1\pmod4$: $1,5,9$;
  • $x\equiv5\pmod6$: $5,11$;
  • $x\equiv7\pmod{12}$: $7$.

Together they cover all twelve residues $0,1,\dots,11$. So every integer satisfies at least one congruence, and the answer is $\boxed{2026}$.

Solution 2 (Look only at what the first two miss)

An integer that is divisible by neither $2$ nor $3$ is $\equiv1,5,7,11\pmod{12}$. Then $1$ is $\equiv1\pmod4$, $5$ is $\equiv1\pmod4$, $7$ is caught by the last congruence, and $11\equiv5\pmod6$. So nothing escapes: all $\boxed{2026}$ numbers qualify.

Problem 13

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value

A board has $4$ spaces in a row, which can be filled with numbers. The sides of a fair, $8$-sided die are numbered $1$ through $8$. David rolls the die $4$ times. After each roll, he randomly chooses an empty spot to write the number down on the die. What is the probability that the numbers on the board form a strictly increasing sequence when read from left to right?

(A)$\dfrac{1}{128}$(B)$\dfrac{5}{432}$(C)$\dfrac{35}{2048}$(D)$\dfrac{35}{1024}$(E)$\dfrac{1}{24}$
Show the answer

(C) $\tfrac{35}{2048}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The board is four independent rolls)

The random placement does not change the distribution: the four numbers on the board, read left to right, are four independent fair rolls. (The rolls are independent and identically distributed, and putting them in a random order keeps them so.) There are $8^4=4096$ equally likely boards, and a strictly increasing one is determined by its set of $4$ different numbers: $\binom84=70$. The probability is $\frac{70}{4096}=\boxed{\tfrac{35}{2048}}$.

Solution 2 (Distinct first, then order)

The four rolls are all different with probability $\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5}{8^4}=\frac{1680}{4096}$. Given that, the placement puts them in one of $4!=24$ orders, equally likely, and exactly one is increasing. So the probability is $\frac{1680}{4096}\cdot\frac1{24}=\frac{70}{4096}=\boxed{\tfrac{35}{2048}}$.

Problem 14

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

Three identical boxes, each with one open face, are made from $1$-inch-thick wood. The outer dimensions of each box are $a$, $b$, and $c$ inches, where $a<b<c$, and each open face appears on a face with outer dimensions $b\times c$. It happens that if the open faces of the two boxes are held together, then the space inside can exactly contain the (rotated) third box, with no room to spare. What is $a+b+c$?

(A)$20$(B)$22$(C)$24$(D)$26$(E)$28$
Show the answer

(C) $24$

Show the solution

Solution (Compare sizes)

Inside one box the space is $(a-1)\times(b-2)\times(c-2)$: one wall at the back, two on each other side. Two boxes held open face to open face make a closed space $(2a-2)\times(b-2)\times(c-2)$. This must be exactly $a\times b\times c$ in some order: $\{2a-2,\,b-2,\,c-2\}=\{a,b,c\}$.

The largest of $a,b,c$ is $c$, and $c-2\ne c$, so $2a-2=c$. Then $\{b-2,c-2\}=\{a,b\}$, and since $b-2<b$ we get $b-2=a$, $c-2=b$. So $b=a+2$, $c=a+4=2a-2$, giving $a=6$, $b=8$, $c=10$ and $a+b+c=\boxed{24}$.

Problem 15

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Suppose that $\sin a=\frac14$ and $\sin b=\frac18$ with $0\le b<a\le\frac\pi2$. What is the greatest integer less than $\log_2\bigl(\sin(a+b)\cdot\sin(a-b)\bigr)$?

(A)$-5$(B)$-4$(C)$0$(D)$1$(E)$5$
Show the answer

(A) $-5$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Expand the product)

\[\sin(a+b)\sin(a-b)=\sin^2a\cos^2b-\cos^2a\sin^2b=\sin^2a(1-\sin^2b)-(1-\sin^2a)\sin^2b=\sin^2a-\sin^2b.\] So the product is $\frac1{16}-\frac1{64}=\frac3{64}$, and $\log_2\frac3{64}=\log_2 3-6$. Since $1<\log_23<2$, the value lies between $-5$ and $-4$, and the greatest integer below it is $\boxed{-5}$.

Solution 2 (Product to sum)

$\sin(a+b)\sin(a-b)=\frac12\bigl(\cos2b-\cos2a\bigr)=\frac12\bigl((1-2\sin^2b)-(1-2\sin^2a)\bigr)=\sin^2a-\sin^2b=\frac3{64}$. Now $\frac2{64}<\frac3{64}<\frac4{64}$, i.e. $2^{-5}<\frac3{64}<2^{-4}$, so the logarithm is between $-5$ and $-4$: answer $\boxed{-5}$.

Problem 16

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · also AMC 10A Problem 21

Gerry is playing the Triples Game. Her task is to partition the $3\times3$ grid of squares shown on the left into three sections, each containing three adjoining squares. (Three squares are adjoining if each shares an edge with at least one of the other two.) One point will be awarded for each section containing more X's than O's. Shown on the right is a sample partition that will earn one point in total. A partition that earns two or more points will win the game. What fraction of the possible partitions of this particular grid will result in a win for Gerry?

(A)$\dfrac{3}{10}$(B)$\dfrac13$(C)$\dfrac25$(D)$\dfrac49$(E)$\dfrac12$
Show the answer

(C) $\tfrac25$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count all partitions, then the wins)

All partitions. The sections are straight or L-shaped trominoes. Every partition has a straight tromino along one edge of the grid (this can be checked by looking at the section that holds a corner square; Solution 2 lists all partitions). There is the all-rows partition, the all-columns partition, and for each of the $4$ edges, the remaining $2\times3$ rectangle can be split into two L's in $2$ ways. Total $2+4\cdot2=10$.

Wins. There are $4$ X's. A section scores with $2$ or $3$ X's, so a win needs two sections with exactly $2$ X's each, and the third section has no X: it is a tromino made of O's. The O's are at the middle of the top row, the right of the middle row, and the whole bottom row; the top O touches no other O. So the O-section is the bottom row or the L made of the middle-right, bottom-middle and bottom-right squares.

  • Bottom row: the top $2\times3$ splits as two rows (XOX, XXO: win) or two L's: one split gives XOX and XXO (win), the other gives XXX and OXO (only one point).
  • The L: the leftover squares split in $2$ ways: top row (XOX) $+$ middle-left, centre, bottom-left (XXO), or left column (XXO) $+$ top-middle, top-right, centre (OXX). Both win.

So $4$ wins out of $10$: $\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Solution 2 (List all ten by the bottom-left square)

Name the squares TL, TM, TR / ML, C, MR / BL, BM, BR (top, middle, bottom rows). The section containing BL is the bottom row, the left column, or one of the three L's inside the bottom-left $2\times2$ block. Listing the partitions in each case (X/O contents in brackets, $\checkmark$ for a scoring section):

  • Bottom row (OOO): the top two rows split as TL TM TR (XOX$\checkmark$) + ML C MR (XXO$\checkmark$): win; TL TM ML (XOX$\checkmark$) + TR C MR (XXO$\checkmark$): win; TL ML C (XXX$\checkmark$) + TM TR MR (OXO): $1$ point.

Problem 17

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · also AMC 10A Problem 22

An engineer is designing a closely packed field of $7$ wind turbines for electricity production. Each turbine, whose center is marked by a labeled dot in the figure, occupies one of $7$ congruent elliptical regions. Turbines in the same row have centers that are $4$ units apart, and turbines in the same column have centers that are $9$ units apart, as shown in the figure below. The major axis of each ellipse is $m\sqrt p$, where $m$ and $p$ are positive integers and $p$ is not divisible by the square of any prime. What is $m+p$?

(A)$6$(B)$8$(C)$9$(D)$10$(E)$12$
Show the answer

(A) $6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangency at the midpoint)

Let each ellipse have horizontal semi-axis $a$ and vertical semi-axis $b$. Two congruent ellipses that are translates of each other and touch are symmetric about the midpoint of their centres, so they touch at that midpoint.

Neighbours in a row ($C$, $O$, $F$) have centres $4$ apart horizontally and touch at the midpoint, $2$ units from each centre along the horizontal axis: $a=2$.

$O$ and $A$: $A$ is $2$ units right and $\frac92$ units up from $O$ (the rows are $\frac92$ apart, since $B$ and $D$ in one column are $9$ apart). They touch at the midpoint $(1,\frac94)$ relative to $O$, which lies on $O$'s ellipse: \[\frac{1}{4}+\frac{(9/4)^2}{b^2}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^2=\frac{81}{16}\cdot\frac43=\frac{27}4,\quad b=\frac{3\sqrt3}2.\] The major axis is $2b=3\sqrt3$, so $m+p=3+3=\boxed{6}$.

Solution 2 (Squash the ellipses into circles)

Shrink the picture vertically by the factor $\frac ab$. The ellipses become congruent circles of radius $a$, still touching in the same pattern; since a row has three touching circles, $a=2$ and neighbouring centres are $4$ apart. The centres $O$ and $A$, originally $(0,0)$ and $(2,\frac92)$, become $(0,0)$ and $(2,\frac92\cdot\frac2b)$, and these circles touch, so \[2^2+\left(\frac9b\right)^2=4^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{81}{b^2}=12,\quad b=\frac{3\sqrt3}2.\] Major axis $2b=3\sqrt3$: $m+p=\boxed{6}$.

Problem 18

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

A sphere with center $O$ and radius $9$ cuts three mutually perpendicular planes that meet at point $P$, forming three circular cross-sections on the planes, which have radii $7$, $4$, and $3$. What is $OP$?

(A)$10$(B)$11$(C)$12$(D)$13$(E)$14$
Show the answer

(D) $13$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Put $P$ at the origin with the three planes as the coordinate planes, and let $O=(u,v,w)$. The distance from $O$ to the plane $x=0$ is $|u|$, and the cross-section there has radius $\sqrt{81-u^2}$; similarly for the others. So \[81-u^2,\ 81-v^2,\ 81-w^2\ \text{ are }\ 49,16,9\ \text{in some order},\] and $OP^2=u^2+v^2+w^2=3\cdot81-(49+16+9)=243-74=169$. Hence $OP=\boxed{13}$.

Solution 2 (A box with $OP$ as diagonal)

Drop perpendiculars from $O$ to the three planes. Together with $P$ they are corners of a rectangular box with $OP$ as a space diagonal, whose edge lengths $d_1,d_2,d_3$ are the distances from $O$ to the planes. By Pythagoras in each plane, $d_i^2=9^2-r_i^2$: $81-49=32$, $81-16=65$, $81-9=72$. So $OP^2=32+65+72=169$ and $OP=\boxed{13}$.

Problem 19

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

How many values of $x$ between $0$ and $2\pi$, inclusive, satisfy the equation \[\sin x+\sin(2x)+\sin(3x)=1+\cos x+\cos(2x)\ ?\]

(A)$1$(B)$2$(C)$4$(D)$6$(E)$8$
Show the answer

(D) $6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor both sides)

Left: $\sin x+\sin3x=2\sin2x\cos x$, so the left side is $\sin2x\,(1+2\cos x)$. Right: $1+\cos2x=2\cos^2x$, so the right side is $\cos x\,(2\cos x+1)$. The equation becomes \[(1+2\cos x)\bigl(2\sin x\cos x-\cos x\bigr)=0\iff(1+2\cos x)\cos x\,(2\sin x-1)=0.\] In $[0,2\pi]$: $\cos x=-\frac12$ gives $\frac{2\pi}3,\frac{4\pi}3$; $\cos x=0$ gives $\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2$; $\sin x=\frac12$ gives $\frac\pi6,\frac{5\pi}6$. These six values are different, so there are $\boxed{6}$ solutions.

Solution 2 (Polynomial in $c=\cos x$, $s=\sin x$)

Write everything with $s=\sin x$, $c=\cos x$: $\sin2x=2sc$, $\sin3x=s(4c^2-1)$, $\cos2x=2c^2-1$. The equation becomes $s(1+2c+4c^2-1)=c+2c^2$, i.e. $2sc(1+2c)=c(1+2c)$, so $c(1+2c)(2s-1)=0$, the same three factors as in Solution 1, with $2+2+2=\boxed{6}$ solutions in $[0,2\pi]$.

Problem 20

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · also AMC 10A Problem 24

How many polynomials $f(x)$ of degree $3$ satisfying \[f\left(3+\sqrt5\right)=f\left(3-\sqrt5\right)\] are there that have leading coefficient equal to $1$ and all of whose coefficients are integers between $-10$ and $10$, inclusive?

(A)$21$(B)$42$(C)$63$(D)$84$(E)$105$
Show the answer

(D) $84$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor $f(r)-f(s)$)

Let $f(x)=x^3+bx^2+cx+d$, $r=3+\sqrt5$, $s=3-\sqrt5$, so $r+s=6$, $rs=4$. Then \[f(r)-f(s)=(r-s)\left(r^2+rs+s^2+b(r+s)+c\right)=(r-s)(32+6b+c),\] using $r^2+rs+s^2=(r+s)^2-rs=32$. Since $r\ne s$ the condition is $c=-32-6b$. With $-10\le c\le10$: $-42\le6b\le-22$, so $b\in\{-7,-6,-5,-4\}$, giving $c=10,4,-2,-8$, all allowed. The constant $d$ is free: $21$ values. Total $4\cdot21=\boxed{84}$.

Solution 2 (Conjugates)

Compute $f(3+\sqrt5)$ in the form $P+Q\sqrt5$ with integers $P,Q$: $(3+\sqrt5)^2=14+6\sqrt5$ and $(3+\sqrt5)^3=72+32\sqrt5$, so \[f(3+\sqrt5)=(72+14b+3c+d)+(32+6b+c)\sqrt5.\] Replacing $\sqrt5$ by $-\sqrt5$ gives $f(3-\sqrt5)$. The two values are equal exactly when the $\sqrt5$ part vanishes: $32+6b+c=0$. Counting as in Solution 1: $4$ pairs $(b,c)$ and $21$ values of $d$, $\boxed{84}$ polynomials.

Problem 21

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement

Six dogs find a large cookie in the shape of a regular hexagon $ABCDEF$. To divide the cookie, the dogs agree to choose point $P$ uniformly at random in the interior of the hexagon. The dogs will then cut out the segments $\overline{PA}$, $\overline{PB}$, $\overline{PC}$, $\overline{PD}$, $\overline{PE}$, and $\overline{PF}$, dividing the cookie into six triangles, one for each dog. A dog will be happy if and only if its piece has at least $\frac1{10}$ of the total area of the hexagon. What is the probability that all six dogs will be happy?

(A)$\dfrac{\sqrt3}{50}$(B)$\dfrac1{25}$(C)$\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{50}$(D)$\dfrac4{25}$(E)$\dfrac25$

The AoPS transcription has the typo "dividing the cooking"; we write "cookie".

Show the answer

(D) $\tfrac4{25}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Distances to the sides)

Let the side be $1$ and the apothem (centre-to-side distance) $h=\frac{\sqrt3}2$. The piece on side $AB$ has area $\frac12\cdot1\cdot d$, where $d$ is the distance from $P$ to line $AB$; the whole hexagon has area $6\cdot\frac12\cdot1\cdot h=3h$. The dog is happy iff $\frac d2\ge\frac{3h}{10}$, i.e. $d\ge\frac35h$.

The points with $d\ge\frac35h$ for all six sides form a regular hexagon with the same centre whose sides are $\frac35h$ inside the original ones, so its apothem is $h-\frac35h=\frac25h$. It is similar to the original with ratio $\frac25$, so the probability is $\left(\frac25\right)^2=\boxed{\tfrac4{25}}$.

Solution 2 (Opposite pieces)

For opposite sides, say $AB$ and $DE$, the distances from $P$ add up to $2h$, so the two opposite pieces together always make $\frac13$ of the hexagon: the pieces on $AB$ and $DE$ have areas $\frac{H}6+t$ and $\frac{H}6-t$ where $H$ is the hexagon's area and $t$ measures how far $P$ is from the centre line between them. Both are at least $\frac H{10}$ iff $|t|\le\frac H6-\frac H{10}=\frac H{15}$, which (since $t$ is proportional to the signed distance) means $P$ lies in the central strip whose width is $\frac{H/15}{H/6}=\frac25$ of the hexagon's width. The three strips (one for each pair of opposite sides) cut out a central hexagon scaled by $\frac25$, of area ratio $\frac4{25}$: $\boxed{\tfrac4{25}}$.

Problem 22

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction

A flock of $8$ birds is flying in a formation, led by a single leader bird, which has exactly $2$ followers immediately behind. Each non-leader bird follows exactly one bird, and has $0$, $1$, or $2$ followers immediately behind. Two possible formations are shown in the picture below. (When a bird has two followers, swapping its left and right subflocks can produce a different formation, as shown in the figure.) What is the total number of possible formations?

(A)$48$(B)$52$(C)$76$(D)$132$(E)$196$
Show the answer

(C) $76$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Recursion on the size of a subflock)

Let $M_n$ be the number of formations of $n$ birds in which every bird (the leader included) has $0$, $1$ or $2$ followers, where two followers are ordered left/right and a single follower is not. Looking at the leader: \[M_1=1,\qquad M_n=M_{n-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}M_iM_{n-1-i}\ (n\ge2).\] This gives $M_1,\dots,M_7=1,1,2,4,9,21,51$. In our flock the leader has exactly two followers, with subflocks of sizes $i$ and $7-i$: \[\sum_{i=1}^{6}M_iM_{7-i}=2(M_1M_6+M_2M_5+M_3M_4)=2(21+9+8)=\boxed{76}.\]

Solution 2 (All formations minus the wrong ones)

Continuing the recursion of Solution 1 gives $M_8=M_7+\sum_{i=1}^6M_iM_{7-i}=51+76=127$. So there are $127$ formations of $8$ birds without the condition on the leader. The leader cannot have $0$ followers, and the formations where it has exactly $1$ follower correspond to formations of the other $7$ birds: $M_7=51$. Hence the answer is $127-51=\boxed{76}$. (The numbers $1,1,2,4,9,21,51,127$ are the Motzkin numbers.)

Problem 23

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

There is a unique real number $x$ between $0$ and $1$ such that $x^3+x^2=1$. For that $x$, there exist unique integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ such that the infinite product \[(x+1)(x^2+1)(x^4+1)(x^8+1)(x^{16}+1)\cdots\] equals $ax^2+bx+c$. What is $a+b+c$?

(A)$2$(B)$3$(C)$4$(D)$5$(E)$6$
Show the answer

(D) $5$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Telescope, then invert $1-x$)

Multiplying by $1-x$ telescopes: $(1-x)(1+x)(1+x^2)\cdots(1+x^{2^{k}})=1-x^{2^{k+1}}\to1$, since $0<x<1$. So the product equals $\frac1{1-x}$.

Now use $x^3+x^2=1$. Dividing $x^3+x^2-1$ by $1-x$: $(1-x)(-x^2-2x-2)=x^3+x^2-2$, so \[x^3+x^2-1=(1-x)(-x^2-2x-2)+1.\] At our $x$ the left side is $0$, so $(1-x)(x^2+2x+2)=1$ and $\frac1{1-x}=x^2+2x+2$. Thus $(a,b,c)=(1,2,2)$ and $a+b+c=\boxed{5}$.

Solution 2 (Undetermined coefficients)

As in Solution 1 the product is $\frac1{1-x}$. We want $(1-x)(ax^2+bx+c)=1$, i.e. $-ax^3+(a-b)x^2+(b-c)x+c=1$. Replace $x^3$ by $1-x^2$: \[(2a-b)x^2+(b-c)x+(c-a)=1.\] The polynomial $x^3+x^2-1$ has no rational root ($\pm1$ fail), so $x$ is irrational of degree $3$ and $1,x,x^2$ are linearly independent over the rationals. Comparing coefficients: $2a=b$, $b=c$, $c-a=1$, so $a=1$, $b=c=2$ and $a+b+c=\boxed{5}$.

Problem 24

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation

Trapezoid $ABCD$ has $AB=4$, $CD=5$, and $\angle DAB=\angle ABC=90^\circ$. Circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ lie inside the trapezoid as follows: $\omega_1$ is tangent to $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{AD}$, and $\overline{DC}$; $\omega_2$ is tangent to $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{BC}$, and $\overline{DC}$; and $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are tangent to each other. The distance between the centers of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ is $m-n\sqrt p$, where $m$, $n$, and $p$ are positive integers such that $p$ is not divisible by the square of any prime. What is $m+n+p$?

(A)$30$(B)$47$(C)$53$(D)$62$(E)$74$
Show the answer

(D) $62$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(4,0)$, with $AD$ and $BC$ vertical. Since $CD=5$ and the horizontal gap is $4$, the vertical gap is $3$; say $D=(0,h+3)$, $C=(4,h)$. Line $DC$ is $3x+4y=4h+12$.

$\omega_1$ (radius $r$) touches $AB$ and $AD$, so its centre is $(r,r)$; touching $DC$: $\frac{4h+12-7r}5=r$, so $r=\frac{h+3}3$. $\omega_2$ (radius $s$) has centre $(4-s,s)$; touching $DC$: $\frac{4h-s}{5}=s$, so $s=\frac{2h}3$.

The circles touch: $(4-r-s)^2+(r-s)^2=(r+s)^2$, i.e. $(4-r-s)^2=4rs$. With $r+s=h+1$ and $rs=\frac{2h(h+3)}9$: \[9(3-h)^2=8h(h+3)\iff h^2-78h+81=0\iff h=39\pm12\sqrt{10}.\] The circles must fit inside, so $h=39-12\sqrt{10}\approx1.05$. The distance between the centres is $r+s=h+1=40-12\sqrt{10}$, and $m+n+p=40+12+10=\boxed{62}$.

Solution 2 (Tangent lengths and half-angles)

Reflecting the figure in the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ swaps $A,B$, swaps $D,C$ and swaps the circles, without changing the distance we want; so we may assume $AD$ is the longer leg, $AD-BC=3$. Let the radii be $r$ (at $A$) and $s$ (at $B$). Along $AB$ the tangency points are $r$ from $A$ and $s$ from $B$, and two externally touching circles on a common line have tangency points $\sqrt{(r+s)^2-(r-s)^2}=2\sqrt{rs}$ apart: \[r+2\sqrt{rs}+s=4.\] Since $AD-BC=3$ and $CD=5$, $\cos\angle D=\frac35$, so $\tan\frac{\angle D}2=\frac{\sin D}{1+\cos D}=\frac12$ and $\tan\frac{\angle C}2=2$ ($\angle C=180^\circ-\angle D$). The centre of $\omega_1$ is on the bisector at $D$, so the tangent length from $D$ is $2r$: $AD=r+2r=3r$. Similarly the tangent length from $C$ to $\omega_2$ is $\frac s2$: $BC=\frac32s$. From $AD-BC=3$: $s=2r-2$. Substituting, \[3r-2+2\sqrt{r(2r-2)}=4\ \Longrightarrow\ (6-3r)^2=4(2r^2-2r)\ \Longrightarrow\ r^2-28r+36=0,\] so $r=14-4\sqrt{10}\approx1.35$ (the other root exceeds $4$; at this root $s=2r-2>0$ and $r+s<4$, so the equation before squaring holds too). The distance between the centres is $r+s=3r-2=40-12\sqrt{10}$: $\boxed{62}$.

Problem 25

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Let $m$ be a positive integer such that there are at least $50$ positive integers $x$ less than $m$ for which $x^2-1$ is a multiple of $m$. What is the sum of the digits of the least possible value of $m$?

(A)$12$(B)$15$(C)$16$(D)$18$(E)$22$
Show the answer

(B) $15$

Show the solution

Solution (Count square roots of 1 by CRT)

For $m\ge2$ we are counting the solutions of $x^2\equiv1\pmod m$ among $1,\dots,m-1$, i.e. all solutions mod $m$ ($x=0$ is never one). By the Chinese remainder theorem the count is a product over the prime powers in $m$:

  • an odd prime power $p^k$: $2$ solutions ($p^k\mid(x-1)(x+1)$ and $p$ cannot divide both factors);
  • $2$: $1$ solution; $4$: $2$; $2^k$ with $k\ge3$: exactly $4$. (A solution is odd; of $x-1$ and $x+1$ one is $\equiv2\pmod4$, so the other is divisible by $2^{k-1}$. Hence $x\equiv\pm1\pmod{2^{k-1}}$, which gives the four residues $\pm1$, $2^{k-1}\pm1$, and all four work.)

So the count is a power of $2$, and "at least $50$" means at least $64=2^6$. Each odd prime contributes a factor $2$ and the power of $2$ at most a factor $4$ (needing $8\mid m$); a single factor $2$ in $m$ adds nothing, so it only makes $m$ larger. The cheapest ways to reach $2^6$: $8\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11=9240$; $4\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13=60060$; $3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17=255255$. The least is $m=9240$, with digit sum $\boxed{15}$.
