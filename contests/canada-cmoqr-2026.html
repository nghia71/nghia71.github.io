Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry

Do there exist four lines in the same plane, no three concurrent and no two parallel, so that four particles can travel at (possibly different) constant non-zero speeds along these lines and such that at every point in time, the positions of the four particles are concyclic?

Show the answer

Yes

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A spiral similarity applied to a fixed circle)

The answer is yes.

Identify the plane with $\mathbb{C}$. Put $a_1=1$, $a_2=i$, $a_3=2+i$, $a_4=1+2i$ and let particle $j$ be at \[z_j(t)=a_j(1+it)\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}).\] Its velocity is the constant $ia_j\ne0$, so each particle moves along a line with constant non-zero speed $|a_j|$. In coordinates, \[z_1=(1,\,t),\quad z_2=(-t,\,1),\quad z_3=(2-t,\,1+2t),\quad z_4=(1-2t,\,2+t),\] so the four lines are \[\ell_1:\ x=1,\qquad \ell_2:\ y=1,\qquad \ell_3:\ 2x+y=5,\qquad \ell_4:\ x+2y=5 .\]

The lines. Their slopes are $\infty,0,-2,-\tfrac12$, so no two are parallel. The six pairwise intersection points are \[\ell_1\cap\ell_2=(1,1),\ \ell_1\cap\ell_3=(1,3),\ \ell_1\cap\ell_4=(1,2),\ \ell_2\cap\ell_3=(2,1),\ \ell_2\cap\ell_4=(3,1),\ \ell_3\cap\ell_4=\bigl(\tfrac53,\tfrac53\bigr).\] If three of the lines passed through one point, the three intersection points of those lines would coincide; all six points are distinct, so no three lines are concurrent.

The circle. The numbers $a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4$ are four distinct points of the circle $|z-(1+i)|=1$. For a fixed $t$, multiplication by $w=1+it\ne0$ is a spiral similarity about $0$ (a rotation followed by a dilation), so it maps this circle onto the circle with centre $(1+i)w=(1-t)+(1+t)i$ and radius $|w|=\sqrt{1+t^2}$. One can also check this directly: for instance \[\bigl(1-(1-t)\bigr)^2+\bigl(t-(1+t)\bigr)^2=t^2+1,\qquad \bigl(2-t-(1-t)\bigr)^2+\bigl(1+2t-(1+t)\bigr)^2=1+t^2,\] and similarly for $z_2$ and $z_4$. Hence at every time $t$ the four (distinct) particles lie on one circle.

Solution 2 (A configuration symmetric about an axis)

The answer is yes. The idea: if two pairs of points are mirror images in the same axis, the four points lie on a circle centred on the axis.

Let the particles be at \[P_1(t)=(1+t,\,t),\quad P_2(t)=(-1-t,\,t),\quad P_3(t)=(2t,\,1+t),\quad P_4(t)=(-2t,\,1+t).\] Each moves along a line with constant non-zero velocity; the lines are \[\ell_1:\ y=x-1,\qquad \ell_2:\ y=-x-1,\qquad \ell_3:\ y=\tfrac x2+1,\qquad \ell_4:\ y=-\tfrac x2+1,\] with slopes $1,-1,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12$, so no two are parallel. The pairwise intersections are \[(0,-1),\ (0,1),\ (4,3),\ \bigl(\tfrac43,\tfrac13\bigr),\ \bigl(-\tfrac43,\tfrac13\bigr),\ (-4,3)\] (for $\ell_1\ell_2$, $\ell_3\ell_4$, $\ell_1\ell_3$, $\ell_1\ell_4$, $\ell_2\ell_3$, $\ell_2\ell_4$), all distinct, so no three lines are concurrent.

$P_2$ is the reflection of $P_1$ and $P_4$ the reflection of $P_3$ in the $y$-axis, so every point $(0,c)$ of the $y$-axis is equidistant from $P_1,P_2$ and from $P_3,P_4$. Choose $c$ so that $(0,c)$ is equidistant from $P_1$ and $P_3$: \[(1+t)^2+(t-c)^2=(2t)^2+(1+t-c)^2\iff t^2-2tc=4t^2-2(1+t)c\iff c=\tfrac32t^2 .\] Hence at time $t$ all four particles lie on the circle with centre $\bigl(0,\tfrac32t^2\bigr)$ through $P_1(t)$, for every $t$.

Remark. At $t=-1$ and $t=0$ two of the particles meet on the axis (then the four positions are three distinct points, still on that circle).

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms

Do there exist positive real numbers $a$, $b$, $c$, not all equal, such that $a^b=b^c=c^a$?

Show the answer

No

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Comparing products of logarithms)

The answer is no. Suppose $a^b=b^c=c^a$ and take logarithms: \[b\ln a=c\ln b=a\ln c=K .\] Since $a,b,c>0$, the numbers $\ln a,\ln b,\ln c$ all have the sign of $K$.

If $K=0$, then $a=b=c=1$. Otherwise, the system is unchanged by the cyclic shift $a\to b\to c\to a$, so we may assume that $a$ is the largest of the three; then either $a\ge b\ge c$ or $a\ge c\ge b$.

Case $K>0$. Then $a,b,c>1$ and $x=\ln a$, $y=\ln b$, $z=\ln c$ are positive and ordered like $a,b,c$. If $a\ge b\ge c$, then $x\ge y>0$ and $b\ge c>0$, so $bx\ge cy$, with equality only if $b=c$ and $x=y$. As $bx=cy$, we get $a=b=c$. If $a\ge c\ge b$, then $a\ge c>0$ and $z\ge y>0$, so $az\ge cy$, with equality only if $a=c$ and $z=y$; again $a=b=c$.

Case $K<0$. Then $a,b,c<1$ and $X=-\ln a$, $Y=-\ln b$, $Z=-\ln c$ are positive and ordered opposite to $a,b,c$; also $bX=cY=aZ=-K$. If $a\ge b\ge c$, then $Z\ge X>0$ and $a\ge b>0$, so $aZ\ge bX$ with equality only if $a=b$ and $Z=X$, i.e. $a=b=c$. If $a\ge c\ge b$, then $Y\ge X>0$ and $c\ge b>0$, so $cY\ge bX$ with equality only if $c=b$ and $Y=X$, i.e. $a=b=c$.

In every case $a=b=c$, so no such numbers exist.

Solution 2 (A chain of strict inequalities)

The answer is no. We use two monotonicity facts: for an exponent $e>0$ the map $u\mapsto u^e$ is increasing on $(0,\infty)$; and for a base $u>1$ (resp. $0<u<1$) the map $e\mapsto u^e$ is increasing (resp. decreasing).

Suppose $a^b=b^c=c^a=V$. If one of the numbers is $1$, say $a=1$ (the other cases are the same up to the cyclic shift), then $V=1$, so $b^c=1$ gives $b=1$ and $c^a=c=1$. Otherwise $\ln a,\ln b,\ln c$ have the sign of $\ln V\ne0$ (because $b\ln a=\ln V$ etc.), so all three numbers are $>1$ or all are $<1$.

Suppose $a\ne b$; by the cyclic symmetry this covers every case in which the numbers are not all equal.

All numbers $>1$. If $a>b$: from $b^c=a^b>b^b$ we get $c>b$; from $c^a=b^c<c^c$ we get $a<c$; from $a^b=c^a>a^a$ we get $b>a$, a contradiction. If $a<b$, all inequalities reverse: $b^c=a^b<b^b$ gives $c<b$, then $c^a=b^c>c^c$ gives $a>c$, then $a^b=c^a<a^a$ gives $b<a$, a contradiction.

All numbers $<1$. Now comparing exponents reverses each conclusion. If $a>b$: $b^c=a^b>b^b$ gives $c<b$; $c^a=b^c>c^c$ gives $a<c$; so $a<c<b$, contradicting $a>b$. If $a<b$: $b^c=a^b<b^b$ gives $c>b$; $c^a=b^c<c^c$ gives $a>c$; so $a>c>b$, contradicting $a<b$.

Hence $a=b$, and then $a^a=a^c$ with $a\ne1$ gives $c=a$. So the numbers are all equal, and the answer is no.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let point $P$ be outside circle $\Gamma$. The tangents from $P$ to $\Gamma$ hit $\Gamma$ at $A$ and $B$. A third line through $P$ hits $\Gamma$ at $C$ and $D$, such that $C$ is between $P$ and $D$. Point $Q$ is on chord $CD$ such that $\angle DAQ=\angle PBC$. Prove that $\angle DBQ=\angle PAC$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (The harmonic ratio and two pairs of similar triangles)

Since $C$ lies between $P$ and $D$, the point $C$ is on the arc $AB$ facing $P$ and $D$ is on the other arc, so $A,C,B,D$ lie on $\Gamma$ in this order. By the tangent-chord angle and inscribed angles, \[\angle PBC=\angle BAC=\angle BDC,\qquad \angle PAC=\angle ABC=\angle ADC. \tag{1}\]

Step 1: $AC\cdot BD=AD\cdot BC$. Triangles $PAC$ and $PDA$ share the angle at $P$, and $\angle PAC=\angle ADC=\angle PDA$ by (1). So they are similar and $\dfrac{AC}{DA}=\dfrac{PA}{PD}$. In the same way $\dfrac{BC}{DB}=\dfrac{PB}{PD}$. Since $PA=PB$, we get $\dfrac{AC}{AD}=\dfrac{BC}{BD}$.

Step 2: the length $DQ$. In triangles $ADQ$ and $ABC$ we have $\angle DAQ=\angle PBC=\angle BAC$ and $\angle ADQ=\angle ADC=\angle ABC$ by (1). So they are similar, and \[DQ=\frac{AD\cdot BC}{AB}=\frac{AC\cdot BD}{AB}\qquad\text{(by Step 1)}.\]

Step 3. Compare triangle $DQB$ (angle at $D$) with triangle $ACB$ (angle at $A$): $\angle QDB=\angle CDB=\angle CAB$ by (1), and by Step 2 \[\frac{DQ}{DB}=\frac{AC}{AB}.\] By SAS similarity, triangles $DQB$ and $ACB$ are similar with $D\leftrightarrow A$, $Q\leftrightarrow C$, $B\leftrightarrow B$. Therefore $\angle DBQ=\angle ABC=\angle PAC$ by (1), as required.

Solution 2 ($Q$ is the midpoint of $CD$)

Since $C$ lies between $P$ and $D$, the point $C$ is on the arc $AB$ facing $P$ and $D$ is on the other arc, so $A,C,B,D$ lie on $\Gamma$ in this order. By the tangent-chord angle, $\angle PBC=\angle BAC$ and $\angle PAC=\angle ABC$. Let $O$ be the centre of $\Gamma$ and $M$ the midpoint of $CD$, so $OM\perp CD$ (if $O$ is on line $CD$, then $M=O$). We show that $Q=M$.

Let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $OP$. Since $\angle OAP=\angle OBP=\angle OMP=90^\circ$, the points $A,B,M$ lie on $\omega$. The line $PD$ meets the inside of $\Gamma$, so it lies inside the angle $APB$; hence $M$, like $O$, is on the same side of line $PA$ as $B$. Thus $\angle AMP$ and $\angle AOP$ are inscribed angles of $\omega$ on the same side of chord $AP$, and \[\angle AMP=\angle AOP=\tfrac12\angle AOB=\angle ADB,\] because $OP$ bisects $\angle AOB$ and $D$ lies on the major arc $AB$ of $\Gamma$. As $M$ lies between $P$ and $D$ and $ACBD$ is cyclic, \[\angle AMD=180^\circ-\angle AMP=180^\circ-\angle ADB=\angle ACB .\] Also $\angle ADM=\angle ADC=\angle ABC$. So triangles $AMD$ and $ACB$ are similar, and $\angle DAM=\angle BAC=\angle PBC$.

As a point $X$ moves along chord $CD$ from $C$ to $D$, the ray $AX$ turns steadily from $AC$ to $AD$, so $\angle DAX$ strictly decreases. Hence exactly one point of the chord satisfies $\angle DAX=\angle PBC$, and therefore $Q=M$.

Exchanging the roles of $A$ and $B$ in the argument above (now using $\angle BMP=\angle BOP=\angle ADB$, so $\angle BMD=180^\circ-\angle ADB=\angle BCA$, and $\angle BDM=\angle BDC=\angle BAC$), triangles $BMD$ and $BCA$ are similar, so \[\angle DBQ=\angle DBM=\angle CBA=\angle PAC .\]

Solution 3 (The Law of Sines and a decreasing cotangent)

Since $C$ lies between $P$ and $D$, the points $A,C,B,D$ lie on $\Gamma$ in this order, so $ACBD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral with $A,B$ opposite. Put \[\gamma=\angle CBD,\qquad \delta=\angle CAD,\qquad \gamma+\delta=180^\circ,\] and $\theta=\angle DAQ=\angle PBC$, $\varphi=\angle DBQ$, $\psi=\angle PAC$. We must show $\varphi=\psi$. As $Q$ lies inside the segment $CD$, the ray $AQ$ is inside the angle $CAD$ and the ray $BQ$ inside the angle $CBD$, so $\angle CAQ=\delta-\theta$ and $\angle CBQ=\gamma-\varphi$.

Step 1: $CQ/DQ$ seen from $A$. The angles $\angle AQC$ and $\angle AQD$ are supplementary, so they have equal sines, and the Law of Sines in triangles $ACQ$ and $ADQ$ gives \[\frac{CQ}{DQ}=\frac{AC\sin(\delta-\theta)}{AD\sin\theta}.\] Since $C$ is between $P$ and $D$, the ray $BC$ lies inside the angle $PBD$, so $\angle PBD=\theta+\gamma$ and $\sin(\delta-\theta)=\sin(180^\circ-\gamma-\theta)=\sin\angle PBD$. The Law of Sines in triangles $PBD$ and $PBC$ gives \[\sin\angle PBD=\frac{PD\sin\angle PDB}{PB},\qquad \sin\theta=\sin\angle PBC=\frac{PC\sin\angle PCB}{PB}.\] Here $\angle PDB=\angle CDB$ and $\sin\angle PCB=\sin(180^\circ-\angle DCB)=\sin\angle DCB$, and in triangle $BCD$ the Law of Sines gives $\sin\angle CDB/\sin\angle DCB=BC/BD$. Therefore \[\frac{CQ}{DQ}=\frac{AC}{AD}\cdot\frac{PD\cdot BC}{PC\cdot BD}. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $CQ/DQ$ seen from $B$. In the same way, from triangles $BCQ$ and $BDQ$, \[\frac{CQ}{DQ}=\frac{BC\sin(\gamma-\varphi)}{BD\sin\varphi}. \tag{2}\]

Step 3: the angle $\psi$. The ray $AC$ lies inside the angle $PAD$, so $\angle PAD=\psi+\delta$ and $\sin\angle PAD=\sin(180^\circ-\psi-\delta)=\sin(\gamma-\psi)$. The Law of Sines in triangles $PAD$ and $PAC$ (they share the side $PA$), and then in triangle $ACD$, gives \[\frac{\sin(\gamma-\psi)}{\sin\psi}=\frac{\sin\angle PAD}{\sin\angle PAC}=\frac{PD\sin\angle PDA}{PC\sin\angle PCA}=\frac{PD\sin\angle CDA}{PC\sin\angle DCA}=\frac{PD\cdot AC}{PC\cdot AD}. \tag{3}\] (Again $\sin\angle PCA=\sin\angle DCA$, as the two angles are supplementary.)

Conclusion. Comparing (1), (2) and (3), \[\frac{\sin(\gamma-\varphi)}{\sin\varphi}=\frac{\sin(\gamma-\psi)}{\sin\psi}.\] The function $g(x)=\dfrac{\sin(\gamma-x)}{\sin x}=\sin\gamma\cot x-\cos\gamma$ is strictly decreasing on $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$, because $\sin\gamma>0$ and $\cot$ is strictly decreasing there. Both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ lie in this interval, so $\varphi=\psi$, that is, $\angle DBQ=\angle PAC$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control

Fix a prime $p$ and natural numbers $k\ge n$. Consider choosing $k$ vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ uniformly at random (possibly with repetition) from the set $\{(x_1,\dots,x_n)\mid x_i\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}\}$. Also, let $v_{k+i}$ be the vector with value $p$ in the $i$-th coordinate and $0$ elsewhere for $1\le i\le n$.

These $k+n$ vectors are said to generate $\mathbb{Z}^n$ if for any vector of integers $v=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$, there are some integers $c_1,\dots,c_{k+n}$ such that $v=\sum_{i=1}^{k+n}c_iv_i$. Let $P(p,n,k)$ be the probability that these $k+n$ vectors generate $\mathbb{Z}^n$.

Then, there is some constant $c$, depending on $p$ and $n$, such that $p^k\bigl(1-P(p,n,k)\bigr)-c$ approaches $0$ as $k\to\infty$. Determine $c$ in terms of $p$ and $n$.

Show the answer

$\boxed{c=\dfrac{p^n-1}{p-1}=1+p+\dots+p^{n-1}}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Hyperplanes and Bonferroni inequalities)

Reduction mod $p$. Let $\bar v$ denote the reduction of an integer vector modulo $p$, an element of $\mathbb{F}_p^n$. We claim that the $k+n$ vectors generate $\mathbb{Z}^n$ if and only if $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$ span $\mathbb{F}_p^n$. If they generate $\mathbb{Z}^n$, write each standard basis vector $e_j$ as an integer combination and reduce mod $p$; the terms with $v_{k+i}=pe_i$ vanish, so $e_j$ is in the span of $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$. Conversely, if these span $\mathbb{F}_p^n$, then for any integer vector $y$ there are integers $c_1,\dots,c_k$ with $y\equiv\sum c_iv_i\pmod p$; the difference $y-\sum c_iv_i$ has all coordinates divisible by $p$, so it is an integer combination of $pe_1,\dots,pe_n$. The $v_i$ are independent and uniform on $\{0,\dots,p-1\}^n$, i.e. $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$ are independent and uniform in $\mathbb{F}_p^n$.

Hyperplanes. A set of vectors fails to span $\mathbb{F}_p^n$ exactly when it lies in some hyperplane (a subspace of dimension $n-1$). A hyperplane is the kernel of a non-zero linear form, and two forms give the same kernel iff they are proportional, so there are \[H=\frac{p^n-1}{p-1}\] hyperplanes. For a hyperplane $W$ let $E_W$ be the event that all $\bar v_i$ lie in $W$; then $\Pr(E_W)=(p^{n-1}/p^n)^k=p^{-k}$. Two different hyperplanes meet in a subspace of dimension $n-2$, so $\Pr(E_W\cap E_{W'})=p^{-2k}$.

Bonferroni. Since $1-P(p,n,k)=\Pr\bigl(\bigcup_W E_W\bigr)$, the first two inclusion-exclusion bounds give \[Hp^{-k}-\binom H2p^{-2k}\le 1-P(p,n,k)\le Hp^{-k}.\] Multiplying by $p^k$: \[H-\binom H2p^{-k}\le p^k\bigl(1-P(p,n,k)\bigr)\le H,\] and the left side tends to $H$ as $k\to\infty$. Therefore \[c=\boxed{\dfrac{p^n-1}{p-1}=1+p+\dots+p^{n-1}}.\]

Solution 2 (An exact formula via the rows)

Reduction mod $p$. Write $\bar v$ for the reduction of an integer vector modulo $p$. The $k+n$ vectors generate $\mathbb{Z}^n$ exactly when $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$ span $\mathbb{F}_p^n$: if they generate, reducing integer combinations that give $e_1,\dots,e_n$ shows that the $\bar e_j$ lie in the span of the $\bar v_i$ (the vectors $pe_i$ reduce to $0$); conversely, if the $\bar v_i$ span, then any integer vector $y$ satisfies $y\equiv\sum c_iv_i\pmod p$ for some integers $c_i$, and $y-\sum c_iv_i$ is an integer combination of $pe_1,\dots,pe_n$. The vectors $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$ are independent and uniform in $\mathbb{F}_p^n$.

Put $\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_k$ as the columns of an $n\times k$ matrix $A$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. Its $nk$ entries are independent and uniform, so its rows $r_1,\dots,r_n$ are independent uniform vectors of $\mathbb{F}_p^k$. The columns span $\mathbb{F}_p^n$ iff $\operatorname{rank}A=n$ iff the rows are linearly independent.

Choose the rows one at a time. If $r_1,\dots,r_i$ are independent, they span a subspace with $p^i$ elements, and $r_{i+1}$ keeps the rows independent iff it avoids that subspace, which happens with probability $1-p^i/p^k$, whatever $r_1,\dots,r_i$ are. Hence \[P(p,n,k)=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl(1-p^{i}x\bigr),\qquad x=p^{-k}.\] Expanding the product, $P=1-(1+p+\dots+p^{n-1})x+x^2R(x)$, where $R$ is a polynomial whose coefficients depend only on $p$ and $n$. So \[p^k(1-P)=(1+p+\dots+p^{n-1})-xR(x)\longrightarrow 1+p+\dots+p^{n-1}\quad(k\to\infty,\ x\to0).\] Thus $c=\boxed{1+p+\dots+p^{n-1}=\dfrac{p^n-1}{p-1}}$.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Determine whether there exists a surjective function $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}$ such that for any positive numbers $a<b$, the image of the open interval $(a,b)$ under $f$ is a set of the form $\{1,2,\dots,N\}$ for some finite positive integer $N$.

Recall that a function $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}$ is surjective if for every natural number $M$, there exists some positive real number $r$ such that $f(r)=M$.

Show the answer

Yes

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting binary digits)

The answer is yes.

A sufficient condition. Suppose $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}$ satisfies

  • (i) $f$ is bounded on every interval $(a,b)$ with $0<a<b$, and

  • (ii) for every $x$ with $f(x)=v\ge2$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $y$ with $|y-x|<\varepsilon$ and $f(y)=v-1$.

Then the image of $(a,b)$ is a non-empty finite set; let $N$ be its maximum. If $v\ge2$ is in the image, say $v=f(x)$ with $x\in(a,b)$, then (ii) with $\varepsilon$ so small that $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subset(a,b)$ shows that $v-1$ is in the image. Going down from $N$, the image is exactly $\{1,\dots,N\}$.

The construction. For a dyadic rational $x>0$ (one of the form $u/2^j$) let $s(x)$ be the number of digits $1$ in its (finite) binary expansion; $s(x)\ge1$. Every $x\in(0,1)$ lies in exactly one interval $[2^{-m-1},2^{-m})$, $m=0,1,2,\dots$; call $m=m(x)$. Define \[f(x)=\begin{cases}\max\bigl(1,\ m(x)+2-s(x)\bigr), & x\in(0,1)\ \text{dyadic},\\ 1, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\]

Surjective. $f(2^{-m-1})=m+2-1=m+1$ for $m\ge0$, so every positive integer is a value.

(i). If $x\in(a,b)$, $x<1$, then $2^{-m(x)}>x>a$, so $m(x)<\log_2(1/a)$; and $f(x)\le m(x)+1$. So $f$ is bounded on $(a,b)$.

(ii). Let $f(x)=v\ge2$; then $x\in(0,1)$ is dyadic and $v=m+2-s$ with $m=m(x)$, $s=s(x)$. For large $J$ put $y=x+2^{-J}$. If $J$ exceeds every exponent appearing in the binary expansion of $x$, then $s(y)=s+1$; and since $x<2^{-m}$, for large $J$ also $y<2^{-m}$, so $m(y)=m$. Then $f(y)=\max(1,v-1)=v-1$ and $|y-x|=2^{-J}$ is as small as we like.

By the sufficient condition, $f$ has the required property.

Solution 2 (A recursive block construction)

The answer is yes. It suffices to build a surjective $f$ that is bounded on every $(a,b)$, $0<a<b$, and such that every $x$ with $f(x)=v\ge2$ has points with value $v-1$ arbitrarily close to it (then the image of $(a,b)$ is finite and closed under $v\mapsto v-1$ for $v\ge2$, hence equal to $\{1,\dots,N\}$).

Lemma. For each $d\ge1$ and each interval $I=[\alpha,\beta)$ there is $g:I\to\{1,\dots,d\}$ with $g(\alpha)=d$ such that for every $x\in I$ with $g(x)=v\ge2$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $y\in I$, $x<y<x+\varepsilon$, with $g(y)=v-1$.

Proof. Induction on $d$. For $d=1$ take $g\equiv1$. For the step from $d$ to $d+1$, cut $(\alpha,\beta)$ into the intervals \[I_i=\bigl[\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)2^{-i},\ \alpha+(\beta-\alpha)2^{-i+1}\bigr),\qquad i=1,2,\dots,\] put on each $I_i$ the function given by the case $d$, and set $g(\alpha)=d+1$. Every left endpoint of an $I_i$ has value $d$, and these endpoints approach $\alpha$ from the right; so the condition holds at $\alpha$. At a point $x\in I_i$ it holds inside $I_i$ by the induction hypothesis. $\square$

Now on each block $[\tfrac1{j+1},\tfrac1j)$, $j=1,2,\dots$, let $f$ be the function of the lemma with $d=j$, and let $f=1$ on $[1,\infty)$. Then $f(\tfrac1{j+1})=j$, so $f$ is surjective. An interval $(a,b)$ with $a>0$ meets only the blocks with $\tfrac1j>a$, on which $f\le j<\tfrac1a$, so $f$ is bounded there. Finally, the lemma gives, next to each point of value $v\ge2$, points of value $v-1$ as close as we like (to its right, inside the same block). Hence $f$ works.

Solution 3 (Dense colour classes capped by the ceiling)

The answer is yes.

Colour classes. For each integer $j\ge2$ let $D_j=\{j\sqrt2+q: q\in\mathbb{Q}\}$. Each $D_j$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ (it is a translate of $\mathbb{Q}$). The sets $D_j$ are pairwise disjoint, and disjoint from $\mathbb{Q}$: if $j\sqrt2+q=j'\sqrt2+q'$ with $j\ne j'$, then $\sqrt2=\frac{q'-q}{j-j'}$ would be rational, and $j\sqrt2+q\in\mathbb{Q}$ would also make $\sqrt2$ rational. Give every point of $D_j$ the colour $j$, and every other positive real the colour $1$; in particular all positive rationals have colour $1$, so colour $1$ is dense too. Thus every non-empty open interval contains points of every colour.

The function. For $x>0$ define \[f(x)=\min\bigl(\text{colour of }x,\ \lceil x\rceil\bigr),\] a positive integer.

Surjective. $f(\tfrac12)=1$. For $j\ge2$, pick a point $x\in D_j$ with $j<x<j+1$; then $\lceil x\rceil=j+1$ and $f(x)=\min(j,j+1)=j$.

Images of intervals. Let $0<a<b$ and $I=(a,b)$. For $x\in I$ we have $f(x)\le\lceil x\rceil\le\lceil b\rceil$, so $f(I)$ is a non-empty finite set; let $N$ be its largest element and pick $x_0\in I$ with $f(x_0)=N$. Then $\lceil x_0\rceil\ge N$, so $x_0>N-1$, and the set $I'=I\cap(N-1,\infty)$ is a non-empty open interval. For each $j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$, choose a point $y\in I'$ of colour $j$. Since $y>N-1$, we have $\lceil y\rceil\ge N\ge j$, so $f(y)=j$. Hence $f(I)\supseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$, and since $N$ is the largest value, $f(I)=\{1,2,\dots,N\}$.

So $f$ is surjective and has the required property.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

A set of positive integers $T$ is given. For a positive integer $n$, Alice and Bob play a game, where there are initially $n$ stones in a pile, they take turns taking $m$ stones from the pile, where $m\in T$, $m\le n$, and whoever takes the last stone wins. If at some point the number of stones in the pile is less than $\min T$, then the game is a draw. Call a number $n$ good if Alice can guarantee a win, bad if Bob can guarantee a win, and neutral if neither Alice nor Bob can guarantee a win.

For example, if $T$ is the set of prime numbers, then $n=4$ is neutral and $n=5$ is good; in the first game, Alice can guarantee a draw by taking $3$ stones on her first move, and cannot do better, and in the second game, she can take all the stones on her first move to win.

  • (a) Prove that if $T$ is finite, then the set of good numbers is eventually periodic: that is, there exist some positive integers $N$ and $p$ such that for all positive integers $n>N$, we have that $n$ is good if and only if $n+p$ is good.

  • (b) Construct an infinite set $T$ such that the set of good numbers is not eventually periodic: that is, for every pair of positive integers $N$ and $p$, there exists some $n>N$ such that exactly one of $n$ and $n+p$ is good.

Show the answer

proof and construction; for example $T=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus\{10^j,\ 10^j-10^i: j>i\ge1\}$ (good numbers: all non-powers of $10$), or $T=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus\{j!\}$ (good numbers: all non-factorials)

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (States of a finite window; a construction without draws)

Outcomes. Describe a position by the number $n\ge0$ of stones left, with some player to move, and give it a label: $\mathsf W$ if the player to move can force a win, $\mathsf L$ if the opponent can, $\mathsf D$ otherwise. Since every move removes stones, the game is finite and the labels are given by backward induction:

  • $n=0$ is $\mathsf L$ (the opponent has just taken the last stone); $1\le n<\min T$ is $\mathsf D$;

  • for $n\ge\min T$: $n$ is $\mathsf W$ if some move $m\in T$, $m\le n$, leads to an $\mathsf L$ position; otherwise $\mathsf D$ if some move leads to a $\mathsf D$ position; otherwise (all moves lead to $\mathsf W$) $\mathsf L$.

A number $n$ is good, bad or neutral according as its label is $\mathsf W$, $\mathsf L$ or $\mathsf D$.

(a) Let $T$ be finite with maximum $M$. For $n\ge M$ every $m\in T$ is a legal move, so the label of $n$ is a fixed function $\Phi$ of the window \[S_n=\bigl(\text{label}(n-1),\text{label}(n-2),\dots,\text{label}(n-M)\bigr).\] And $S_{n+1}$ is determined by $S_n$: it is $S_n$ shifted by one place with $\Phi(S_n)$ put in front. There are at most $3^M$ windows, so $S_i=S_j$ for some $M\le i<j$. Put $p=j-i$. By induction on $n$, $S_{n+p}=S_n$ for all $n\ge i$ (if it holds for $n$, apply the same map to both sides). Hence $\text{label}(n+p)=\Phi(S_{n+p})=\Phi(S_n)=\text{label}(n)$ for all $n\ge i$. In particular, for $n>N=i$, $n$ is good iff $n+p$ is good.

(b) Let $S=\{0,10,100,1000,\dots\}$ and let $\Delta$ be the set of positive differences of elements of $S$: \[\Delta=\{10^j: j\ge1\}\cup\{10^j-10^i: j>i\ge1\}.\] In decimal, the elements of $\Delta$ are $10\ldots0$ or $9\ldots90\ldots0$. Let $T=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus\Delta$; it is infinite and $1\in T$, so $\min T=1$ and there are no draws.

Claim: the bad numbers (label $\mathsf L$) are exactly the elements of $S$. Strong induction on $n$; $n=0$ is $\mathsf L$.

  • $n\in S$, $n\ge1$: a move $m\in T$ to $n-m\in S$ would make $m=n-(n-m)$ a positive difference of two elements of $S$, i.e. $m\in\Delta$, which is impossible. So every move leads outside $S$, to a $\mathsf W$ position by induction, and $n$ is $\mathsf L$.

  • $n\notin S$, $n\notin\Delta$: then $n\in T$, and taking all $n$ stones wins. So $n$ is $\mathsf W$.

  • $n\in\Delta\setminus S$: then $n=10^j-10^i$ with $j>i\ge1$. Take $m=n-10^i=10^i(10^{j-i}-2)$. In decimal $m$ is $j-i-1$ nines, then an $8$, then $i$ zeros, so $m\notin\Delta$, $m\in T$, and $0<m<n$. This move leads to $10^i\in S$, which is $\mathsf L$ by induction; so $n$ is $\mathsf W$.

So the good numbers are the positive integers that are not powers of $10$. This set is not eventually periodic: given $N$ and $p$, choose $j$ with $10^{j-1}>N+p$ and let $n=10^j-p$. Then $n>10^j-10^{j-1}>N$ and $10^{j-1}<n<10^j$, so $n$ is not a power of $10$ and is good, while $n+p=10^j$ is not good.

Solution 2 (Forcing draws with a set that has no long gaps)

(a) Give each number $n\ge0$ of stones, with some player to move, its outcome: win, loss or draw for the player to move ($n=0$ is a loss: the opponent took the last stone). Let $M=\max T$. For $n\ge M$ every $m\in T$ is a legal move, and the outcome of $n$ is a win if some move reaches a loss, otherwise a draw if some move reaches a draw, otherwise a loss; so it is a fixed function of the outcomes of $n-1,\dots,n-M$. Hence the block of outcomes of $n-1,\dots,n-M$ determines the next block (of $n,\dots,n-M+1$). There are at most $3^M$ blocks, so the blocks at two positions $i<j$ (both $\ge M$) coincide, and then by induction the outcomes satisfy $o(n+j-i)=o(n)$ for all $n\ge i$. As $n$ is good exactly when its outcome is a win, the good numbers are eventually periodic, with $N=i$ and $p=j-i$.

(b) Here we let draws do the work. Take \[T=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus\{j!: j\ge1\}=\{3,4,5,7,8,\dots\}.\] It is infinite, $\min T=3$, and among any three consecutive positive integers at least one lies in $T$ (the factorials $1,2,6,24,\dots$ never contain three consecutive integers).

Claim: $n$ is good iff $n\in T$; every $n\notin T$ is neutral.

  • If $n\in T$, Alice takes all $n$ stones and wins.

  • Let $n\notin T$. If $n\le2$ no move is possible and the game is a draw at once. If $n\ge3$, one of $n-1$, $n-2$ is in $T$ (as $n\notin T$); Alice takes that many stones and leaves $1$ or $2$ stones, fewer than $\min T$, so the game is a draw. Hence Bob cannot force a win.

  • Alice cannot force a win from $n\notin T$ either. Whatever she takes, she cannot take all $n$ stones (since $n\notin T$), so Bob faces $r\ge1$ stones. If $r\le2$ the game is drawn; if $r\in T$ Bob takes everything and wins; if $r\ge3$ and $r\notin T$, Bob is in exactly Alice's situation above and can force a draw by leaving $1$ or $2$ stones. In no case does Alice win against best play.

So the good numbers are exactly the positive integers that are not factorials.

Not eventually periodic. Given $N$ and $p$, choose $j\ge3$ with $(j-1)!\,(j-1)>p$ and $j!-p>N$, and put $n=j!-p$. Then $(j-1)!<n<j!$, so $n$ is not a factorial and is good, while $n+p=j!$ is not good.

Solution 3 (Freeze a bad position followed by a growing run of good ones)

Outcomes. Describe a position by the number $n\ge0$ of stones left, with some player to move. Label it $\mathsf W$ if the player to move can force a win, $\mathsf L$ if the opponent can, and $\mathsf D$ otherwise. Every move removes stones, so the labels are determined by backward induction: $n=0$ is $\mathsf L$ (the opponent has just taken the last stone); $1\le n<\min T$ is $\mathsf D$; for $n\ge\min T$, the position $n$ is $\mathsf W$ if some move $m\in T$, $m\le n$, leads to an $\mathsf L$ position, otherwise $\mathsf D$ if some move leads to a $\mathsf D$ position, and otherwise $\mathsf L$. A number $n$ is good, bad or neutral according as its label is $\mathsf W$, $\mathsf L$ or $\mathsf D$.

(a) Let $T$ be finite with largest element $M$. For $n\ge M$ every $m\in T$ is a legal move, so the label of $n$ is a fixed function of the window $S_n=(\text{label}(n-1),\dots,\text{label}(n-M))$, and therefore $S_{n+1}$ is a fixed function of $S_n$. There are at most $3^M$ windows, so $S_i=S_j$ for some $M\le i<j$. With $p=j-i$, induction on $n$ gives $S_{n+p}=S_n$ for all $n\ge i$, hence $\text{label}(n+p)=\text{label}(n)$ for all $n\ge i$. So for $n>N=i$, $n$ is good if and only if $n+p$ is good.

(b) Two facts.

Fact 1. The label of $n$ depends only on the elements of $T$ that are at most $n$. (Induction on $n$: the rule uses only moves $m\le n$ and labels of smaller positions.)

Fact 2. If $T$ is finite and $1\in T$, then there are bad numbers larger than any given bound. Indeed, with $1\in T$ there is always a legal move from $n\ge1$, so no label is $\mathsf D$. Suppose every $n\ge n_0$ (with $n_0\ge1$) were good, and let $n=n_0+\max T$. Every move from $n$ leads to a position between $n_0$ and $n-1$, which is good, i.e. labelled $\mathsf W$; so $n$ is $\mathsf L$, a contradiction.

Construction. Let $T_0=\{1\}$ and $f_0=0$. For $j=1,2,3,\dots$ in turn: by Fact 2 choose a number $b_j>f_{j-1}$ that is bad for the finite set $T_{j-1}$, and put \[T_j=T_{j-1}\cup\{b_j+1,\,b_j+2,\,\dots,\,b_j+j\},\qquad f_j=b_j+j .\] Let $T=T_0\cup T_1\cup T_2\cup\cdots$; it is infinite.

Claim: for $T$, every $b_j$ is bad and $b_j+1,\dots,b_j+j$ are good. All elements of $T_j$ are at most $f_j$, while an element added at a later stage $i>j$ exceeds $b_i>f_{i-1}\ge f_j$. So the elements of $T$ that are at most $f_j$ are exactly those of $T_j$, and by Fact 1 every $n\le f_j$ has the same label for $T$ as for $T_j$. In the game with $T_j$, the elements at most $b_j$ are those of $T_{j-1}$ (the new ones exceed $b_j$), so by Fact 1 the number $b_j$ is bad, as it is for $T_{j-1}$. Each of $b_j+1,\dots,b_j+j$ belongs to $T_j$, so the player to move takes all the stones and wins: these numbers are good.

Not eventually periodic. Let $N$ and $p$ be positive integers. The numbers $b_j$ increase without bound ($b_j>f_{j-1}\ge b_{j-1}+j-1$), so we can choose $j\ge p$ with $b_j>N$. Then $n=b_j>N$ is not good, while $n+p=b_j+p$ with $1\le p\le j$ is good. So the set of good numbers is not eventually periodic.

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Determine the least positive integer that is not a perfect square that can be written in the form $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}$ for some rational numbers $a$ and $b$ such that $ab\ne-1$.

Show the answer

$\boxed{10}$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Integer form and a prime dividing $k\pm2$)

The non-squares below $10$ are $2,3,5,6,7,8$. We show none of them is a value, and that $10$ is.

$10$ is a value: for $a=\tfrac12$, $b=\tfrac{13}2$ we have $ab+1=\tfrac{17}4\ne0$ and $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}=\dfrac{170/4}{17/4}=10$.

Setting up. Suppose $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}=k$ with rational $a,b$. Write $a=\tfrac xz$, $b=\tfrac yz$ with integers $x,y,z$, $z>0$, $\gcd(x,y,z)=1$. Then \[x^2-kxy+y^2=kz^2, \tag{$*$}\] and the left side can be written as $(x-y)^2-(k-2)xy$ or $(x+y)^2-(k+2)xy$.

$k=2$: $(x-y)^2=2z^2$ with $z>0$ would make $\sqrt2$ rational. Impossible.

$k=6$: modulo $3$, $(*)$ gives $x^2+y^2\equiv0$. Squares are $0$ or $1$ mod $3$, so $3\mid x$ and $3\mid y$. Then $9$ divides the left side, so $9\mid 6z^2$ and $3\mid z$, contradicting $\gcd(x,y,z)=1$.

$k=3,5,7,8$: take \[\begin{array}{c|cccc} k&3&5&7&8\\\hline p&5&3&5&3\\ e&k+2=5&k-2=3&k-2=5&k-2=6\\ w&x+y&x-y&x-y&x-y\end{array}\] so that $x^2-kxy+y^2=w^2-e\,xy$, $p\mid e$ but $p^2\nmid e$, and $k$ is a quadratic non-residue mod $p$ ($k\equiv3$ mod $5$; $k\equiv2$ mod $3$; $k\equiv2$ mod $5$; $k\equiv2$ mod $3$; the squares are $0,1,4$ mod $5$ and $0,1$ mod $3$). Modulo $p$, $(*)$ gives $w^2\equiv kz^2$. If $p\nmid z$, then $(wz^{-1})^2\equiv k$, impossible; so $p\mid z$, and then $p\mid w$. Now modulo $p^2$: $w^2\equiv0$ and $kz^2\equiv0$, so $p^2\mid e\,xy$, and since $p^2\nmid e$, $p\mid xy$. So $p$ divides $x$ or $y$, and as $p\mid w=x\pm y$ it divides both. Together with $p\mid z$ this contradicts $\gcd(x,y,z)=1$.

Hence the least non-square value is $\boxed{10}$.

Solution 2 (Diagonalising with $a=s+t$, $b=s-t$)

Put $s=\tfrac{a+b}2$, $t=\tfrac{a-b}2$, so $a=s+t$, $b=s-t$, $a^2+b^2=2(s^2+t^2)$ and $ab=s^2-t^2$. The equation $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}=k$ becomes $2s^2+2t^2=k(s^2-t^2+1)$, that is, \[(k+2)t^2-(k-2)s^2=k. \tag{$**$}\] Rational $a,b$ correspond to rational $s,t$.

$k=10$: $(**)$ reads $12t^2-8s^2=10$; $t=-3$, $s=\tfrac72$ works ($108-98=10$), giving $a=\tfrac12$, $b=\tfrac{13}2$ (and $ab+1=\tfrac{17}4\ne0$). So $10$ is a value.

$k=2$: $4t^2=2$ has no rational solution.

For $k=3,5,6,7,8$ write $t=\tfrac TZ$, $s=\tfrac SZ$ with integers, $Z>0$, $\gcd(S,T,Z)=1$. Recall the squares are $0,1$ mod $3$ and $0,1,4$ mod $5$; so $u^2\equiv2v^2$ (mod $3$ or mod $5$) forces both $u,v$ to be divisible by the modulus, and so does $u^2+v^2\equiv0\pmod 3$.

  • $k=3$: $5T^2-S^2=3Z^2$. Mod $5$: $S^2\equiv2Z^2$, so $5\mid S,Z$; then $25\mid5T^2$, so $5\mid T$.

  • $k=5$: $7T^2-3S^2=5Z^2$. Mod $3$: $T^2\equiv2Z^2$, so $3\mid T,Z$; then $9\mid3S^2$, so $3\mid S$.

  • $k=6$: $8T^2-4S^2=6Z^2$, i.e. $4T^2-2S^2=3Z^2$. Mod $3$: $T^2+S^2\equiv0$, so $3\mid T,S$; then $9\mid3Z^2$, so $3\mid Z$.

  • $k=7$: $9T^2-5S^2=7Z^2$. Mod $5$: $4T^2\equiv2Z^2$, i.e. $Z^2\equiv2T^2$, so $5\mid T,Z$; then $25\mid5S^2$, so $5\mid S$.

  • $k=8$: $10T^2-6S^2=8Z^2$, i.e. $5T^2-3S^2=4Z^2$. Mod $3$: $2T^2\equiv Z^2$, so $3\mid T,Z$; then $9\mid3S^2$, so $3\mid S$.

Each case contradicts $\gcd(S,T,Z)=1$. So $2,3,5,6,7,8$ are not values, and the answer is $\boxed{10}$.

Solution 3 ($p$-adic valuations with $p\mid k$)

The answer is $10$: for $a=\tfrac{13}2$, $b=\tfrac12$ we have $ab+1=\tfrac{17}4\ne0$ and $\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}=\dfrac{170/4}{17/4}=10$. The non-squares below $10$ are $2,3,5,6,7,8$; we show that for these $k$ the equation \[a^2+b^2=k(ab+1)\] has no rational solution. If $a=0$ it reads $b^2=k$, impossible for rational $b$ as $k$ is not a square; the same holds if $b=0$. So let $a,b$ be non-zero rationals.

Valuations. For a prime $p$ and a non-zero rational $r$, let $v_p(r)$ be the exponent of $p$ in $r$ (negative if $p$ divides the denominator). Then $v_p(rs)=v_p(r)+v_p(s)$, and if $r+s\ne0$ then $v_p(r+s)\ge\min(v_p(r),v_p(s))$, with equality when $v_p(r)\ne v_p(s)$.

Lemma. For $p=3$ or $p=7$, $v_p(a^2+b^2)=2\min(v_p(a),v_p(b))$. Indeed, let $m=\min(v_p(a),v_p(b))$ and write $a=p^m\tfrac ud$, $b=p^m\tfrac wd$ with integers $u,w,d$, $p\nmid d$, and $u,w$ not both divisible by $p$. The squares modulo $3$ are $0,1$ and modulo $7$ are $0,1,2,4$, so $-1$ is not a square modulo $p$. Hence $p\nmid u^2+w^2$: if $p\nmid w$, then $u^2\equiv-w^2$ would make $-1\equiv(uw^{-1})^2$ a square; if $p\mid w$, then $p\nmid u$ and $u^2+w^2\equiv u^2\not\equiv0$. So $v_p(a^2+b^2)=2m+v_p(u^2+w^2)-2v_p(d)=2m$.

$k=3,6,7$. Take $p=3,3,7$ respectively, so that $v_p(k)=1$, and write $v=v_p$, $m=\min(v(a),v(b))$. By the lemma, $2m=v(a^2+b^2)=1+v(ab+1)$, so $v(ab+1)=2m-1$ is odd.

  • If $v(ab)>0$, then $v(ab+1)=v(1)=0$, which is even.

  • If $v(ab)<0$, then $v(ab+1)=v(ab)=v(a)+v(b)\ge2m$, contradicting $v(ab+1)=2m-1$.

  • If $v(ab)=0$, then $v(ab+1)\ge0$, and being odd it is at least $1$; so $2m\ge2$, both $v(a),v(b)\ge1$ and $v(ab)\ge2$, a contradiction.

$k=5,8$. The equation is equivalent to \[(a-b)^2=(k-2)ab+k ,\] where $k-2\in\{3,6\}$ has $v_3(k-2)=1$ and $k\equiv2\pmod 3$. Write $v=v_3$ and $c=(k-2)ab$, so $v(c)=1+v(ab)$.

  • If $v(ab)\ge0$: then $v(c)\ge1>0=v(k)$, so $v\bigl((a-b)^2\bigr)=v(c+k)=0$ and $a-b=\tfrac ud$ with integers $u,d$ prime to $3$. From $u^2-kd^2=cd^2$ and $v(cd^2)\ge1$ we get $u^2\equiv kd^2\equiv2d^2\pmod 3$, so $2\equiv(ud^{-1})^2$ would be a square modulo $3$. Impossible.

  • If $v(ab)\le-1$ and $v(ab)$ is odd: then $v(a)\ne v(b)$; the equation is symmetric in $a,b$, so let $v(a)<v(b)$. Then $a\ne b$, $v(a-b)=v(a)$, and $2v(a)\le v(a)+v(b)-1=v(ab)-1$. On the right, $v(c)=1+v(ab)\le0=v(k)$, so $v(c+k)\ge1+v(ab)$. Thus $v\bigl((a-b)^2\bigr)\le v(ab)-1<v(ab)+1\le v(c+k)$, a contradiction.

  • If $v(ab)\le-2$ and $v(ab)$ is even: then $v(c)=1+v(ab)<0=v(k)$, so $c+k\ne0$ and $v(c+k)=1+v(ab)$ is odd, while $v\bigl((a-b)^2\bigr)=2v(a-b)$ is even. A contradiction.

$k=2$. The equation becomes $(a-b)^2=2$, impossible for rational $a,b$ because $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

So none of $2,3,5,6,7,8$ is a value, and the least non-square value is $\boxed{10}$.
