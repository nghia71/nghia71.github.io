Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Let $\mathbb N_{\ge1}$ denote the set of strictly positive integers. Let $f:\mathbb N_{\ge1}\to\mathbb N_{\ge1}$ be a function such that:

  • $f(f(n))$ divides a power of $n$ for every integer $n\ge1$,

  • $f(mn)\in\{f(m),f(n)\}$ for all integers $m\ge1$ and $n\ge1$, and

  • $f(k)=2026$ for at least one integer $k\ge1$.

Determine all possible values of $f(2026)$.

Show the answer

$f(2026)=1$

Show the solution

Solution (Following the value 2026 back to a prime)

Lemma. If $n_1,\dots,n_k\ge1$, then $f(n_1n_2\cdots n_k)\in\{f(n_1),\dots,f(n_k)\}$. This follows from the second condition by induction on $k$. In particular, for a prime $p$ and $j\ge1$ we get $f(p^j)=f(p)$, and for every $n>1$ there is a prime $p\mid n$ with $f(n)=f(p)$.

Let $k$ be an integer with $f(k)=2026$.

Case $k=1$. Then $f(2026)=f(f(1))$ divides a power of $1$, so $f(2026)=1$.

Case $k>1$. By the lemma there is a prime $p\mid k$ with $f(p)=2026$. Then $f(2026)=f(f(p))$ divides a power of $p$, so $f(2026)=p^j$ for some $j\ge0$. Suppose $j\ge1$. Then \[ f\bigl(f(2026)\bigr)=f(p^j)=f(p)=2026 . \] On the other hand $2026=2\cdot1013$, so by the lemma $f(2026)=f(q)$ for some $q\in\{2,1013\}$, and then $f(f(2026))=f(f(q))$ divides a power of the prime $q$. But $2026$ has two different prime factors, so it divides no power of a single prime. This contradiction shows $j=0$, that is, $f(2026)=1$.

The value $1$ occurs. Define $f(n)=1$ if $n=1$ or $\gcd(n,2026)>1$, and $f(n)=2026$ otherwise. Then:

  • $f(f(n))\in\{f(1),f(2026)\}=\{1\}$, and $1$ divides $n^1$;

  • if $\gcd(mn,2026)>1$, one of the primes $2$, $1013$ divides $m$ or $n$, so $f(m)=1$ or $f(n)=1$, as is $f(mn)$; if $mn=1$ then $m=n=1$; otherwise $mn>1$ is coprime to $2026$, $f(mn)=2026$, and the factor among $m,n$ that exceeds $1$ also has value $2026$;

  • $f(3)=2026$.

So all conditions hold, and the only possible value is $f(2026)=\boxed{1}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Albert has just finished writing a book, but unfortunately, his printer has shuffled the order of the $N$ pages of his book. Brigitte offers to help him put the pages back in the correct order. Brigitte only allows herself the following two operations, which she can perform in any order and repeatedly:

  • Reverse the order of all pages by placing the first page in the last position, the second in the second-to-last position, and so on, until the last page is placed in the first position.

  • If the first page bears the number $M$, then Brigitte sorts the first $M$ pages in descending order.

For example, if the order of the pages was $3,1,2,5,4$, the first operation would give $4,5,2,1,3$, while the second operation would give $3,2,1,5,4$.

Show that Brigitte can always put the pages of Albert's book back in the correct order using these two operations.

Show the solution

Solution (The first page can only grow)

Call the two operations $R$ (reverse all pages) and $S$ (if the first page is $M$, sort the first $M$ pages in descending order). The target order is $1,2,\dots,N$.

Observation. If the first page is $M$, then after $S$ the first page is the largest of the first $M$ pages, which is at least $M$. So $S$ never decreases the number on the first page.

Stable positions. Suppose we apply $S$ and the first page does not change, say it stays $m$. Then the largest of the first $m$ pages is $m$; these are $m$ distinct numbers from $\{1,\dots,m\}$, so they are exactly $1,\dots,m$, and after this $S$ the arrangement begins \[ m,\ m-1,\ \dots,\ 2,\ 1 . \] Call such an arrangement $m$-stable.

Strategy. Starting from any arrangement, apply $S$ repeatedly. The first page never decreases and is at most $N$, so it can increase only finitely many times; at the first application of $S$ that does not change it we have an $m$-stable arrangement for some $m$.

  • If $m=N$, the pages are $N,N-1,\dots,1$; one application of $R$ gives $1,2,\dots,N$ and we are done.

  • If $m<N$, the arrangement is $m,\dots,1,x_{m+1},\dots,x_N$ with $\{x_{m+1},\dots,x_N\}=\{m+1,\dots,N\}$. Apply $R$: we get $x_N,\dots,x_{m+1},1,2,\dots,m$, whose first page $x_N$ exceeds $m$. Now repeat the strategy. Since the first page never decreases under $S$, the next stable arrangement is $m'$-stable with $m'\ge x_N>m$.

Each round strictly increases $m$, and $m\le N$, so after at most $N$ rounds we reach an $N$-stable arrangement, and the final $R$ puts the book in the correct order.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle and $P$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$. The bisector of the angle $\angle ABC$ intersects the segment $[PC]$ at $D$ and intersects the circle with diameter $[AD]$ at $D$ and $E$. Show that if the line $(EC)$ is tangent to the circle with diameter $[AD]$, then the line $(PE)$ is the bisector of the angle $\angle APC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates along the bisector)

Let $\beta=\tfrac12\angle ABC$, $a=BC$, $c=BA$; since the triangle is acute, $0<2\beta<90^\circ$. Put $B$ at the origin with the bisector of $\angle ABC$ as the positive $x$-axis: \[ A=c(\cos\beta,\sin\beta),\qquad C=a(\cos\beta,-\sin\beta),\qquad u=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta), \] so $u$ is the unit vector along $BA$.

The points. $P$ is the projection of $C$ on $AB$: $P=(C\cdot u)\,u=a\cos2\beta\;u$. The point $D$ lies on the $x$-axis and on the line through $C$ perpendicular to $u$, so $D=(d,0)$ with $d\cos\beta=C\cdot u=a\cos2\beta$. Since $\angle APD=90^\circ$, $P$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with diameter $AD$; and $E\in\Gamma$ lies on the bisector with $\angle AED=90^\circ$, so $E$ is the foot of $A$ on the $x$-axis: $E=(c\cos\beta,0)$.

The tangency condition. The centre of $\Gamma$ is $O=\tfrac12(A+D)$, and $EC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ iff $(E-O)\cdot(C-E)=0$. With $2(E-O)=(c\cos\beta-d,\,-c\sin\beta)$ and $C-E=((a-c)\cos\beta,\,-a\sin\beta)$ this reads \[ (c\cos\beta-d)(a-c)\cos\beta+ac\sin^2\beta=0 . \] Using $d\cos\beta=a\cos2\beta=a\cos^2\beta-a\sin^2\beta$, the left side equals \[ \bigl((c-a)\cos^2\beta+a\sin^2\beta\bigr)(a-c)+ac\sin^2\beta=a^2\sin^2\beta-(a-c)^2\cos^2\beta . \] So the tangency is equivalent to $|a-c|=a\tan\beta$, i.e. $c=a(1+\tan\beta)$ or $c=a(1-\tan\beta)$.

The second case is not acute. Put $t=\tan\beta\in(0,1)$. If $c=a(1-t)$, then \[ BP=a\cos2\beta=a\,\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}>a(1-t)=c=BA, \] because this is equivalent to $(1-t)(1+t)>(1-t)(1+t^2)$, i.e. $t>t^2$. Then $A$ lies strictly between $B$ and the foot $P$ of the altitude from $C$, and $\angle BAC$ is obtuse, a contradiction. Hence $c=a(1+\tan\beta)$.

The bisector. Let $u^{\perp}=(\sin\beta,-\cos\beta)$, the unit vector perpendicular to $u$ on the side of $C$ ($C\cdot u^{\perp}=a\sin2\beta>0$). Then $A-P=(c-a\cos2\beta)\,u$ is a positive multiple of $u$ (as $c>a$), and $C-P$ is a positive multiple of $u^{\perp}$. So $PE$ is the bisector of $\angle APC$ iff the components of $E-P$ along $u$ and $u^{\perp}$ are equal and positive. They are \[ (E-P)\cdot u=c\cos^2\beta-a\cos2\beta,\qquad (E-P)\cdot u^{\perp}=c\sin\beta\cos\beta>0 . \] They are equal iff $c\cos\beta(\cos\beta-\sin\beta)=a(\cos\beta-\sin\beta)(\cos\beta+\sin\beta)$, i.e. (as $\beta<45^\circ$) iff $c\cos\beta=a(\cos\beta+\sin\beta)$, i.e. iff $c=a(1+\tan\beta)$, which we have proved. Hence $PE$ bisects $\angle APC$.

Solution 2 (Power of a point and a $45^\circ$ angle)

Let $\beta=\tfrac12\angle ABC$ and let $C_0$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to the bisector $BD$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle with diameter $AD$; it passes through $P$ (as $\angle APD=90^\circ$) and through $E$, and $\angle AED=90^\circ$.

Step 1: $CD\cdot CP=2\,CC_0^2$. The line $CP$ is perpendicular to $AB$ and the bisector makes the angle $\beta$ with $AB$, so the acute angle between $CP$ and $BD$ is $90^\circ-\beta$; hence in the right triangle $CC_0D$ the angle at $C$ is $\beta$ and $CD=CC_0/\cos\beta$. In the right triangles $BC_0C$ and $BPC$ we have $CC_0=BC\sin\beta$ and $CP=BC\sin2\beta$. Therefore \[ CD\cdot CP=\frac{BC\sin\beta}{\cos\beta}\cdot 2BC\sin\beta\cos\beta=2BC^2\sin^2\beta=2\,CC_0^2 . \]

Step 2: the tangency gives a $45^\circ$ angle. The line $CP$ meets $\Gamma$ at $D$ and $P$, and $C$ lies outside the segment $DP$, so the power of $C$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is $CD\cdot CP$. Thus $EC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ iff $CE^2=CD\cdot CP=2CC_0^2$. Since $CE^2=CC_0^2+C_0E^2$, this means $C_0E=CC_0$: the right triangle $CC_0E$ is isosceles, and the lines $CE$ and $BD$ make an angle of $45^\circ$.

Step 3: $EA=ED$. By the tangent-chord angle theorem, the two angles between the tangent $EC$ and the chord $ED$ are $\angle EAD$ and $180^\circ-\angle EAD$. One of them is $45^\circ$ (Step 2), and $\angle EAD<90^\circ$ because $\angle AED=90^\circ$. Hence $\angle EAD=45^\circ$, the right triangle $AED$ is isosceles and $EA=ED$.

Step 4: $E$ and $P$ lie on opposite sides of $AD$. In triangle $BDC$ the angle at $D$ is $180^\circ-\angle PDB=90^\circ+\beta$, which is obtuse, so the foot $C_0$ lies on ray $BD$ beyond $D$: $BD<BC_0$. By Step 2, $BE=BC_0\pm CC_0$. If $BE=BC_0-CC_0$, then (using $BE=BA\cos\beta$ from the right triangle $AEB$, $BC_0=BC\cos\beta$, $CC_0=BC\sin\beta$) we get $BA=BC(1-t)$ with $t=\tan\beta\in(0,1)$, while $BP=BC\cos2\beta=BC\,\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}>BC(1-t)$ because $1+t>1+t^2$; so $BP>BA$, the foot $P$ lies beyond $A$ on ray $BA$, and $\angle BAC$ would be obtuse. Hence $BE=BC_0+CC_0>BD$, so $D$ lies between $B$ and $E$, and $B$, $E$ are on opposite sides of line $AD$. The point $P$ lies on segment $AB$, hence on the same side of $AD$ as $B$.

Conclusion. The chords $EA$ and $ED$ of $\Gamma$ are equal and $P\in\Gamma$ lies on the arc $AD$ not containing $E$, so the inscribed angles $\angle APE$ and $\angle EPD$ are equal. Since $D$ lies on segment $PC$, $\angle EPD=\angle EPC$, and $PE$ bisects $\angle APC$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Determine the number of triplets $(a,b,c)$ of strictly positive integers such that \[a+b+2c=28^{29}\quad\text{and}\quad ab=c^2.\]

Show the answer

$2^{29}\cdot 7^{14}-1$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Parametrising $ab=c^2$)

Write $N=28^{29}=2^{58}\cdot7^{29}$.

Parametrisation. Let $(a,b,c)$ be a solution, $g=\gcd(a,b)$, $a=gx$, $b=gy$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$. From $g^2xy=c^2$ we get $g^2\mid c^2$, so $g\mid c$ and $xy=(c/g)^2$ is a perfect square; as $x,y$ are coprime, both are squares: $x=m^2$, $y=n^2$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Then $c=gmn$ and the first equation becomes \[ g(m^2+2mn+n^2)=g(m+n)^2=N . \] Conversely, every triple $(g,m,n)$ of positive integers with $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and $g(m+n)^2=N$ gives the solution $(gm^2,gn^2,gmn)$, and different triples give different solutions, because $g=\gcd(a,b)$ and $m^2=a/g$, $n^2=b/g$ are recovered from $(a,b,c)$.

Counting. So the answer is the number of coprime pairs $(m,n)$ of positive integers with $(m+n)^2\mid N$. For a fixed $s=m+n\ge2$, $\gcd(m,n)=\gcd(m,s)$, so there are exactly $\varphi(s)$ such pairs ($m=1,\dots,s-1$ coprime to $s$). Also $s^2\mid 2^{58}7^{29}$ iff $s\mid L$, where $L=2^{29}\cdot7^{14}$. Using Gauss's identity $\sum_{s\mid L}\varphi(s)=L$, the number of triples is \[ \sum_{\substack{s\mid L\\ s\ge2}}\varphi(s)=L-\varphi(1)=\boxed{2^{29}\cdot7^{14}-1}. \]

Solution 2 (A quadratic in $a$ and its discriminant)

Write $N=28^{29}=7L^2$ with $L=2^{29}\cdot7^{14}$ (indeed $L^2=2^{58}7^{28}$).

Fix $c\ge1$. Then $a,b$ are the roots of $t^2-(N-2c)t+c^2=0$, with discriminant \[ \Delta=(N-2c)^2-4c^2=N(N-4c)=7L^2(N-4c). \] The roots are positive integers iff $\Delta=k^2$ for an integer $k\ge0$ with $k\equiv N-2c\pmod 2$ and $N-2c>0$; then they are $\tfrac12(N-2c\pm k)$, positive because their product $c^2$ and their sum are positive. Each such $c$ gives two ordered triples if $k>0$ and one if $k=0$.

$7L^2(N-4c)$ is a square iff $7(N-4c)$ is a square, iff $N-4c=7j^2$ for some integer $j\ge0$ (the prime $7$ must divide the square root). Since $4\mid N$, we need $4\mid 7j^2$, i.e. $j=2i$ even; then \[ c=\frac N4-7i^2=7\Bigl(\bigl(\tfrac L2\bigr)^2-i^2\Bigr),\qquad k=7Lj=14Li , \] and the parity condition holds ($N$, $2c$, $k$ are even), as does $N-2c>0$. The condition $c\ge1$ means $0\le i\le \tfrac L2-1$.

So $i=0$ gives one triple ($a=b$) and each of the $\tfrac L2-1$ values $i\ge1$ gives two, for a total of \[ 1+2\Bigl(\frac L2-1\Bigr)=L-1=\boxed{2^{29}\cdot7^{14}-1}. \]

Solution 3 (Equal squarefree parts)

Write $N=28^{29}=2^{58}\cdot7^{29}=7L^2$ with $L=2^{29}\cdot7^{14}$.

Squarefree parts. Every positive integer can be written uniquely as $du^2$ with $d$ squarefree (a product of distinct primes) and $u\ge1$: $d$ is the product of the primes that occur to an odd power. Let $(a,b,c)$ be a solution and write $a=du^2$, $b=ev^2$ in this way. Since $ab=de(uv)^2=c^2$ is a square, $de$ is a square. Every prime appears in $d$ and in $e$ with exponent $0$ or $1$, and its exponent in $de$ is even, so it appears in both or in neither: $d=e$.

The equation. Now $c^2=d^2u^2v^2$ and $c>0$, so $c=duv$, and \[ N=a+b+2c=d(u^2+2uv+v^2)=d(u+v)^2 . \] So $d(u+v)^2=7L^2$. By uniqueness of the form $du^2$ with $d$ squarefree, $d=7$ and $u+v=L$.

Conversely, for all positive integers $u,v$ with $u+v=L$, the triple $(a,b,c)=(7u^2,7v^2,7uv)$ consists of positive integers with $ab=(7uv)^2=c^2$ and $a+b+2c=7(u+v)^2=7L^2=N$. Different pairs $(u,v)$ give different triples, since $u$ is determined by $a=7u^2$.

Counting. The solutions are therefore in bijection with the pairs of positive integers $(u,v)$ with $u+v=L$, namely $u=1,2,\dots,L-1$. The number of triples is \[ L-1=\boxed{2^{29}\cdot7^{14}-1}. \]

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Find all polynomials $P$ with integer coefficients such that, for all integers $a\ge1$ and $b\ge1$, the number $P(a)-P(b)$ is divisible by $a+2b$ or by $2a+b$.

Show the answer

exactly the constant polynomials

Show the solution

Solution (Reducing modulo $a+2b$ and $2a+b$)

Constant polynomials work, since then $P(a)-P(b)=0$. Conversely, let $P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d c_ix^i$ satisfy the condition. We use that $u-v\mid P(u)-P(v)$ for integers $u,v$.

Fix $b\ge1$ and put \[ A_b=P(-2b)-P(b),\qquad B_b=2^dP(-b/2)-2^dP(b)=\sum_{i=0}^d c_i2^{d-i}(-b)^i-2^dP(b), \] both integers.

  • Since $a\equiv-2b\pmod{a+2b}$, we have $P(a)\equiv P(-2b)$, so $a+2b\mid P(a)-P(b)$ iff $a+2b\mid A_b$.

  • Since $2a\equiv-b\pmod{2a+b}$, we have $2^dP(a)=\sum c_i2^{d-i}(2a)^i\equiv\sum c_i2^{d-i}(-b)^i\pmod{2a+b}$, so $2^d\bigl(P(a)-P(b)\bigr)\equiv B_b$. Hence $2a+b\mid P(a)-P(b)$ implies $2a+b\mid B_b$.

If $A_b\ne0$ and $B_b\ne0$, take $a>|A_b|+|B_b|$. Then $a+2b>|A_b|$ and $2a+b>|B_b|$, so neither divisibility can hold (a nonzero integer is not divisible by a larger positive integer), contradicting the hypothesis. Hence for every $b\ge1$, \[ P(-2b)=P(b)\qquad\text{or}\qquad P(-b/2)=P(b). \] One of the two alternatives holds for infinitely many $b$, so one of the polynomial identities $P(-2x)=P(x)$, $P(-x/2)=P(x)$ holds (the difference has infinitely many roots). Comparing the coefficients of $x^i$ gives $c_i\bigl((-2)^i-1\bigr)=0$, respectively $c_i\bigl((-\tfrac12)^i-1\bigr)=0$, so $c_i=0$ for all $i\ge1$.

Therefore the polynomials are exactly the constant polynomials.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Judith has gathered her $2026$ friends in a circle, so that each of her friends has a person to their left and a person to their right. Judith wishes to explain to them her clever solution to an Olympiad problem that can be told in a few seconds.

At 17:00, Judith chooses one of her friends and tells them her solution. Thereafter, every minute starting from 17:01, two things happen:

  • 1. First, every friend who knows the solution tells it to their two neighbors.

  • 2. Then, Judith chooses one of her friends and tells them her solution.

Determine the smallest integer $n$ such that it is possible for all of Judith's friends to know her solution after $n$ minutes.

Show the answer

$n=45$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Each first listener informs an arc)

Call the friend told at 17:00 the $0$-th seed and the friend told by Judith during minute $k$ ($1\le k\le n$) the $k$-th seed. The $k$-th seed is told after the spreading of minute $k$, so it takes part in the spreading of minutes $k+1,\dots,n$.

Lower bound. By induction on $t$: at the end of minute $t$, every friend who knows the solution is at distance at most $t-k$ (measured around the circle) from the $k$-th seed for some $k\le t$. Indeed, a seed is at distance $0$ from itself, and spreading moves the solution one step. Hence after $n$ minutes the informed friends lie in the union of the arcs centred at the seeds with $2(n-k)+1$ friends, $k=0,\dots,n$, and their number is at most \[ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigl(2(n-k)+1\bigr)=1+3+5+\dots+(2n+1)=(n+1)^2 . \] For $n\le44$ this is at most $45^2=2025<2026$. So $n\ge45$.

Construction for $n=45$. Number the friends by the residues modulo $2026$ in circular order, and write $[x,y]$ for the arc of friends $x,x+1,\dots,y$. A seed told during minute $k$ informs, by the end of minute $45$, the whole arc of radius $45-k$ around it. Choose the seeds

  • $k=0$: friend $0$, informing $[-45,45]$;

  • $k=1$: friend $2$, informing $[-42,46]$;

  • $k=2$: friend $89$, informing $[46,132]$;

  • $k=3,\dots,44$: the centres of consecutive arcs of $91-2k$ friends placed one after another starting at friend $133$;

  • $k=45$: anybody.

The arcs for $k=3,\dots,44$ contain $85+83+\dots+3=43^2-1=1848$ friends, so they fill exactly $[133,1980]$. Altogether the friends $-45,-44,\dots,1980$ are informed, which are $2026$ consecutive residues, that is, everybody.

Hence the smallest possible value is $\boxed{45}$. (If the state “after $n$ minutes” is read before Judith's telling of minute $n$, the bound becomes $(n+1)^2-1=2024$ for $n=44$, and the construction above does not use minute $45$'s telling, so the answer is the same.)

Solution 2 (Counting new listeners minute by minute)

Let $N_t$ be the number of informed friends at the end of minute $t$ (after Judith's telling), so $N_0=1$.

At any moment the informed friends form blocks of consecutive friends (arcs). Every block contains a friend told directly by Judith: a friend informed by spreading is adjacent to an earlier informed friend, so following earlier and earlier informants inside the block we reach someone told by Judith. At the end of minute $t$ Judith has told $t+1$ friends, so there are at most $t+1$ blocks.

During minute $t+1$ the spreading adds at most two friends per block (its two outer neighbours) and Judith adds at most one. Therefore \[ N_{t+1}\le N_t+2(t+1)+1=N_t+2t+3, \] and by summation \[ N_n\le 1+\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(2t+3)=1+n(n-1)+3n=(n+1)^2 . \] For $n\le44$ this gives $N_n\le2025<2026$, so at least $45$ minutes are needed.

Conversely, after $45$ minutes a friend told during minute $k$ has informed an arc of $2(45-k)+1=91-2k$ friends centred at him. Judith chooses the friends told at minutes $k=0,1,\dots,36$ as the centres of arcs of $91,89,\dots,19$ friends laid one after another around the circle. These arcs have $37\cdot55=2035\ge2026$ friends in total, so they cover the whole circle (the last ones may overlap the first) and everybody knows the solution after $45$ minutes, whoever Judith tells in minutes $37$ to $45$. Hence the answer is $\boxed{45}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle such that $AB\ne AC$. Let $H$ be the orthocenter of $ABC$ and $M$ be the midpoint of $[BC]$. Let $D$ and $E$ be the orthogonal projections of $H$ onto the internal and external bisectors, respectively, of the angle $\angle BAC$. Show that the points $D$, $E$, and $M$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A rectangle and isogonal lines)

Let $O$ be the circumcentre and $N$ the midpoint of $AH$.

The rectangle. The internal and external bisectors at $A$ are perpendicular, $HD\perp AD$ and $HE\perp AE$, so $ADHE$ is a rectangle. It is not degenerate: $H$ lies on the internal bisector only if the altitude from $A$ is the bisector, i.e. $AB=AC$, and $H$ lies on the external bisector only if that bisector is perpendicular to $BC$, i.e. the internal bisector is parallel to $BC$, which is impossible. The diagonals $AH$ and $DE$ of the rectangle bisect each other, so $N$ is also the midpoint of $DE$.

Direction of $DE$. The reflection in the line $\ell$ through $N$ parallel to $AD$ maps the rectangle to itself, exchanging $A\leftrightarrow E$ and $D\leftrightarrow H$; so it maps the line $AH$ to the line $DE$. Reflecting a direction in a line parallel to the bisector $AD$ is the same as reflecting it in $AD$. The reflection of line $AH$ in the bisector of $\angle BAC$ is the line $AO$ (the classical fact $\angle BAH=90^\circ-\angle B=\angle OAC$: the first equality from the right triangle formed by $A$, $B$ and the foot of the altitude, the second because $OA=OC$ and the central angle $AOC$ equals $2\angle B$ in the acute triangle). Hence $DE\parallel AO$.

Direction of $NM$. It is classical that $\vec{AH}=2\,\vec{OM}$ (both are perpendicular to $BC$; for instance the homothety with centre the centroid and ratio $-\tfrac12$ maps $A\mapsto M$ and $H\mapsto O$). Hence \[ \vec{NM}=\vec{AM}-\tfrac12\vec{AH}=\vec{AM}-\vec{OM}=\vec{AO}, \] so $NM\parallel AO$, and $N\ne M$.

The lines $DE$ and $NM$ both pass through $N$ and are parallel to $AO$, so they coincide. Hence $D$, $E$, $M$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Coordinates with the bisectors as axes)

Put $A$ at the origin, the internal bisector of $\angle BAC$ as the $x$-axis and the external bisector as the $y$-axis. Let $\alpha=\tfrac12\angle BAC$, $b=AC$, $c=AB$ (so $b\ne c$), $k=\cos2\alpha=\cos\angle BAC\ne0$, and \[ B=c(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha),\qquad C=b(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha). \] Orthocentre. $H=(x,y)$ satisfies $(H-B)\cdot C=0$ and $(H-C)\cdot B=0$, i.e. $H\cdot C=H\cdot B=B\cdot C=bck$. Dividing by $b$ and $c$: \[ x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha=ck,\qquad x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha=bk, \] so $x=\dfrac{(b+c)k}{2\cos\alpha}$ and $y=\dfrac{(b-c)k}{2\sin\alpha}$, both nonzero.

The points. The projections of $H$ on the axes are $D=(x,0)$ and $E=(0,y)$, so the line $DE$ is $\dfrac Xx+\dfrac Yy=1$. The midpoint of $BC$ is $M=\bigl(\tfrac{b+c}2\cos\alpha,\ \tfrac{c-b}2\sin\alpha\bigr)$, and \[ \frac{M_x}{x}+\frac{M_y}{y}=\frac{\cos^2\alpha}{k}+\frac{(c-b)\sin^2\alpha}{(b-c)k}=\frac{\cos^2\alpha-\sin^2\alpha}{k}=1 . \] So $M$ lies on line $DE$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

Show that there exists an integer $n\ge1$ for which there exist at least $2026$ triplets $(a,b,c)$ of strictly positive integers such that \[a^2+b^3+c^4=n.\]

Show the solution

Solution (Too many triples for too few values)

Let $t=3\cdot2026=6078$ and consider the triples $(a,b,c)$ with \[ 1\le a\le t^6,\qquad 1\le b\le t^4,\qquad 1\le c\le t^3 . \] There are $t^6\cdot t^4\cdot t^3=t^{13}$ of them. For each, $n=a^2+b^3+c^4$ satisfies $3\le n\le t^{12}+t^{12}+t^{12}=3t^{12}$, so these triples produce at most $3t^{12}$ different values of $n$. By the pigeonhole principle some value $n$ is produced by at least \[ \frac{t^{13}}{3t^{12}}=\frac t3=2026 \] triples. Different triples are different representations, so this $n$ has at least $2026$ representations $a^2+b^3+c^4=n$ in positive integers.
