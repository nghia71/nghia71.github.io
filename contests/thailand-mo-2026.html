Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $P$ be a point outside a circle $\Gamma$ with center $O$. A line through $P$ intersects $\Gamma$ at $A$ and $B$, where $\angle BOP$ is a right angle. Let $PC$ be a tangent to $\Gamma$ at $C$, where $\angle BOC$ is obtuse. Let the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $A$ intersect the line $PO$ at $X$. Let $Y$ be a point on the line $PO$ such that $\angle CYP$ is a right angle. Prove that \[\frac{XY}{YO} = \frac{PA}{AB}.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two pairs of inverse points)

Let $r$ be the radius of $\Gamma$ and let $A'$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to the line $PO$.

Order of the points. Both intersection points lie on the same side of the outside point $P$, and by the power of the point $P$ and Pythagoras in the right triangle $POB$, $PA\cdot PB=PO^2-r^2<PO^2+r^2=PB^2$. Hence $PA<PB$: $A$ lies between $P$ and $B$, and therefore its projection $A'$ lies between $O$ and $P$.

Two right triangles. In triangle $OCP$ the angle at $C$ is right (tangent) and $CY$ is the altitude to the hypotenuse, so \[OY\cdot OP=OC^2=r^2 .\] In triangle $OAX$ the angle at $A$ is right ($XA$ is tangent) and $AA'$ is the altitude to the hypotenuse $OX$, so \[OA'\cdot OX=OA^2=r^2 .\] (The condition that $\angle BOC$ is obtuse only selects one of the two tangents from $P$; both tangent points have the same foot $Y$.)

Positions on the ray $OP$. All of $Y,A',X,P$ lie on the ray $OP$. From the two products, $OY=r^2/OP$ and $OX=r^2/OA'$; since $OA'<OP$, we get $OX>OY$, so $XY=OX-OY$.

The ratio $PA/AB$. The lines $AA'$ and $BO$ are both perpendicular to $OP$, hence parallel, so by the intercept theorem in triangle $PBO$, \[\frac{PA}{AB}=\frac{PA'}{A'O}=\frac{OP-OA'}{OA'}=\frac{OP}{OA'}-1=\frac{OP\cdot OX}{r^2}-1 .\] The ratio $XY/YO$. \[\frac{XY}{YO}=\frac{OX-OY}{OY}=\frac{OX}{OY}-1=\frac{OX\cdot OP}{r^2}-1 .\] The two right-hand sides are equal, so $\dfrac{XY}{YO}=\dfrac{PA}{AB}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Choose the unit of length so that $\Gamma$ is the unit circle with centre $O=(0,0)$, and put $P=(p,0)$ with $p>1$. As $\angle BOP=90^\circ$ we may take $B=(0,1)$.

The point $A$. The points of line $PB$ are $(p(1-t),\,t)$, with $t=0$ at $P$ and $t=1$ at $B$. Substituting into $x^2+y^2=1$: \[p^2(1-t)^2+t^2=1\iff (p^2+1)t^2-2p^2t+(p^2-1)=0\iff (t-1)\bigl((p^2+1)t-(p^2-1)\bigr)=0 .\] Hence $A$ has parameter $t_A=\frac{p^2-1}{p^2+1}\in(0,1)$, so $A$ lies between $P$ and $B$, $A=\bigl(\frac{2p}{p^2+1},\frac{p^2-1}{p^2+1}\bigr)$, and \[\frac{PA}{AB}=\frac{t_A}{1-t_A}=\frac{p^2-1}{2}.\] The point $X$. The tangent at $A=(x_A,y_A)$ is $x_Ax+y_Ay=1$; it meets the $x$-axis at $X=\bigl(\frac1{x_A},0\bigr)=\bigl(\frac{p^2+1}{2p},0\bigr)$.

The point $Y$. A tangent point $C=(x_C,y_C)$ satisfies $OC\perp PC$, i.e. $x_C(x_C-p)+y_C^2=0$, i.e. $x_C^2+y_C^2=px_C$, so $x_C=\frac1p$. (The two tangent points are $\bigl(\frac1p,\pm\frac{\sqrt{p^2-1}}{p}\bigr)$; the obtuse angle $BOC$ picks the minus sign, but both give the same foot.) Hence $Y=\bigl(\frac1p,0\bigr)$.

Conclusion. Since $\frac{p^2+1}{2p}>\frac1p$, \[\frac{XY}{YO}=\frac{\frac{p^2+1}{2p}-\frac1p}{\frac1p}=\frac{p^2-1}{2}=\frac{PA}{AB}.\]

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ be positive real numbers such that $a+b+c+d = 4$. Find the minimum value of \[\frac{a}{(bc+1)^2} + \frac{b}{(cd+1)^2} + \frac{c}{(da+1)^2} + \frac{d}{(ab+1)^2}.\]

Show the answer

$1$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Cauchy-Schwarz, then AM-GM)

Write $S=\frac{a}{(bc+1)^2}+\frac{b}{(cd+1)^2}+\frac{c}{(da+1)^2}+\frac{d}{(ab+1)^2}$. For $a=b=c=d=1$ we get $S=4\cdot\frac14=1$. We show $S\ge1$ always.

Step 1 (Cauchy-Schwarz). With weights $a,b,c,d$ and the numbers $u_1=\frac1{bc+1}$, $u_2=\frac1{cd+1}$, $u_3=\frac1{da+1}$, $u_4=\frac1{ab+1}$, \[(a+b+c+d)\,(au_1^2+bu_2^2+cu_3^2+du_4^2)\ \ge\ (au_1+bu_2+cu_3+du_4)^2,\] so $S\ge \frac14\,T^2$ where $T=\frac{a}{bc+1}+\frac{b}{cd+1}+\frac{c}{da+1}+\frac{d}{ab+1}$.

Step 2 (AM-GM). Since $bc+1\ge 2\sqrt{bc}$, \[\frac{a}{bc+1}=a-\frac{abc}{bc+1}\ \ge\ a-\frac{abc}{2\sqrt{bc}}=a-\frac{a\sqrt{bc}}2\ \ge\ a-\frac{a(b+c)}4 .\] Adding the four analogous inequalities, \[T\ \ge\ 4-\tfrac14\bigl(a(b+c)+b(c+d)+c(d+a)+d(a+b)\bigr)=4-\tfrac14\bigl((a+c)(b+d)+2ac+2bd\bigr).\] Step 3. With $x=a+c$ and $y=b+d$ (so $x+y=4$) we have $2ac\le\frac{x^2}2$ and $2bd\le\frac{y^2}2$, hence \[(a+c)(b+d)+2ac+2bd\le xy+\frac{x^2+y^2}2=\frac{(x+y)^2}2=8 .\] Therefore $T\ge 4-2=2$, and $S\ge\frac14\cdot 2^2=1$.

So the minimum value is $\boxed{1}$, attained at $a=b=c=d=1$.

Solution 2 (A one-variable tangent bound)

Lemma. For every $s\ge0$, \[\frac1{(1+s^2)^2}\ \ge\ \frac34-\frac s2 .\] Proof. Multiply by $4(1+s^2)^2>0$; the claim becomes $4-(3-2s)(1+s^2)^2\ge0$. Expanding, \[4-(3-2s)(1+s^2)^2=2s^5-3s^4+4s^3-6s^2+2s+1=(s-1)^2\,(2s^3+s^2+4s+1),\] which is clearly nonnegative for $s\ge0$. $\square$

(The right-hand side is the tangent line at $s=1$ of the left-hand side, viewed as a function of $s=\sqrt{bc}$; this is why equality occurs at $s=1$.)

Apply the lemma with $s=\sqrt{bc}$ and then AM-GM $\sqrt{bc}\le\frac{b+c}2$: \[\frac{a}{(bc+1)^2}\ \ge\ \frac34a-\frac a2\sqrt{bc}\ \ge\ \frac34a-\frac{a(b+c)}4 .\] Adding the four cyclic versions, \[\sum\frac{a}{(bc+1)^2}\ \ge\ \frac34(a+b+c+d)-\frac14\bigl((a+c)(b+d)+2ac+2bd\bigr)\ \ge\ 3-\frac14\cdot 8=1,\] where the last step uses, with $x=a+c$ and $y=b+d$ (so $x+y=4$), the bounds $2ac\le\frac{x^2}2$ and $2bd\le\frac{y^2}2$ (AM-GM): \[(a+c)(b+d)+2ac+2bd\le xy+\frac{x^2+y^2}2=\frac{(x+y)^2}2=8 .\] Equality holds for $a=b=c=d=1$, so the minimum is $\boxed{1}$.

Solution 3 (Weighted Jensen and opposite pairs)

Let $\varphi(t)=\frac1{(1+t)^2}$ for $t\ge0$. Then $\varphi''(t)=\frac6{(1+t)^4}>0$, so $\varphi$ is convex, and it is decreasing.

Jensen. The weights $\frac a4,\frac b4,\frac c4,\frac d4$ are positive with sum $1$, so \[\frac{a}{(bc+1)^2}+\frac{b}{(cd+1)^2}+\frac{c}{(da+1)^2}+\frac{d}{(ab+1)^2} =4\Bigl(\tfrac a4\varphi(bc)+\tfrac b4\varphi(cd)+\tfrac c4\varphi(da)+\tfrac d4\varphi(ab)\Bigr)\ \ge\ 4\,\varphi\!\Bigl(\frac S4\Bigr),\] where \[S=a\cdot bc+b\cdot cd+c\cdot da+d\cdot ab=abc+bcd+cda+dab .\] Bounding $S$. Put $x=a+c$ and $y=b+d$, so $x+y=4$. Grouping the terms, \[S=ac(b+d)+bd(a+c)=ac\,y+bd\,x .\] By AM-GM, $ac\le\frac{x^2}4$ and $bd\le\frac{y^2}4$, hence \[S\le\frac{x^2y+y^2x}4=\frac{xy(x+y)}4=xy\le\Bigl(\frac{x+y}2\Bigr)^2=4 .\] Conclusion. As $\varphi$ is decreasing and $\frac S4\le1$, the sum is at least $4\varphi(1)=4\cdot\frac14=1$. For $a=b=c=d=1$ every term equals $\frac14$ and the sum is $1$. Hence the minimum value is $\boxed{1}$.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

The organizing committee of the Thailand Mathematical Olympiad wants to print identification cards for $2569$ participants using a printer with the following rules:

  • One must choose two finite groups of integers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k$ and $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_\ell$, where the numbers need not be distinct, and $k, l \ge 1$.

  • The printer can print a number $s$ if and only if there exist $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k \in \{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_l\}$ such that \[s = a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_kx_k.\]

Determine the smallest value of $k+l$ for which the printer can print every number from $1$ to $2569$.

Show the answer

$10$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting the printable numbers, base-5 construction)

Upper bound on what can be printed. Let $V$ be the set of distinct values among $b_1,\dots,b_l$; then $|V|\le l$. A printable number is $a_1x_1+\dots+a_kx_k$ with $(x_1,\dots,x_k)\in V^k$, so at most $|V|^k\le l^k$ different numbers can be printed. To print all of $1,2,\dots,2569$ we need \[l^k\ \ge\ 2569 .\] This forces $k+l\ge10$. If $l=1$ then $l^k=1$. For $l\ge2$, the value $l^k$ grows with $k$, so for $k+l\le9$ we have $l^k\le l^{9-l}$, and \[\max_{2\le l\le 8} l^{\,9-l}=\max(2^7,3^6,4^5,5^4,6^3,7^2,8^1)=\max(128,729,1024,625,216,49,8)=1024<2569 .\] So $k+l\le 9$ is impossible.

$k+l=10$ is enough. Take $k=l=5$, $a_i=5^{\,i-1}$ $(i=1,\dots,5)$ and $b_1,\dots,b_5=0,1,2,3,4$. Every integer $s$ with $0\le s\le 5^5-1=3124$ has a base-5 expansion $s=x_1+5x_2+25x_3+125x_4+625x_5$ with digits $x_i\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, so the printer prints every number from $1$ to $2569$.

The smallest value of $k+l$ is $\boxed{10}$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Find all positive integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ with $\gcd(a,b,c) = 1$ such that each of the following numbers is an integer: \[\frac{a^2+b^2}{b+c}, \qquad \frac{b^2+c^2}{c+a}, \qquad \frac{c^2+a^2}{a+b}.\] Here $\gcd(a,b,c)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of $a$, $b$, and $c$.

Show the answer

$(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Symmetric divisibilities, parity, and an lcm bound)

Clearly $(1,1,1)$ works. Let $(a,b,c)$ be any solution.

Step 1: a symmetric set of conditions. Modulo $b+c$ we have $b\equiv -c$, so $b^2\equiv c^2$; hence $b+c\mid a^2+b^2$ implies $b+c\mid a^2+c^2$ as well. In the same way $c+a\mid b^2+c^2$ gives $c+a\mid b^2+a^2$, and $a+b\mid c^2+a^2$ gives $a+b\mid c^2+b^2$. So \[b+c\mid a^2+b^2,\ a^2+c^2;\qquad c+a\mid b^2+c^2,\ b^2+a^2;\qquad a+b\mid c^2+a^2,\ c^2+b^2 .\tag{$*$}\] For each pair of the variables, their sum divides the sum of squares of the third variable and either of the two. This system is symmetric in $a,b,c$, and from now on we only use $(*)$ and $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$.

Step 2: $a,b,c$ are all odd. If two of them, say $u,v$, have the same parity and the third $w$ has the other parity, then $u+v$ is even while $w^2+u^2$ is odd, contradicting $u+v\mid w^2+u^2$. So all three have the same parity, and since $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$ they are all odd.

Step 3: $\gcd(b+c,\,c+a)=2$ (and similarly for the other pairs). Both numbers are even. An odd square is $\equiv1\pmod 8$, so $a^2+b^2\equiv2\pmod 8$, and $b+c\mid a^2+b^2$ gives $4\nmid b+c$; so $4$ does not divide the gcd. Now let $p$ be an odd prime dividing both $b+c$ and $c+a$. Then $p\mid (b+c)-(c+a)=b-a$, so $a\equiv b\equiv -c\pmod p$, and from $b+c\mid a^2+c^2$ we get $p\mid a^2+c^2\equiv 2c^2$, so $p\mid c$, and then $p\mid a$, $p\mid b$, contradicting $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. Hence $\gcd(b+c,c+a)=2$.

Step 4: the bound. By $(*)$, both $b+c$ and $c+a$ divide $a^2+b^2$, so their least common multiple $\frac{(b+c)(c+a)}2$ divides the positive number $a^2+b^2$, whence \[(b+c)(c+a)\ \le\ 2(a^2+b^2).\] The same reasoning works for every pair, so by symmetry we may assume $a\le b\le c$. Then \[(b+c)(c+a)\ \ge\ (b+b)(b+a)=2b^2+2ab\ \ge\ 2b^2+2a^2 ,\] with equality only if $c=b$ (first step) and $a=b$ (second step). Comparing with the previous display, equality must hold throughout, so $a=b=c$, and $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$ gives $a=b=c=1$.

The only solution is $\boxed{(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)}$.

Solution 2 (Half-sums and the roots of a quadratic)

Clearly $(1,1,1)$ works: each quotient equals $\frac22=1$. Let $(a,b,c)$ be any solution.

Step 1: $a,b,c$ are odd. Suppose two of them have the same parity and the third has the other parity. If $b,c$ are the pair, then $b+c$ is even while $a^2+b^2$ is odd, so $\frac{a^2+b^2}{b+c}$ is not an integer. In the same way, if $c,a$ are the pair then $c+a$ is even and $b^2+c^2$ is odd, and if $a,b$ are the pair then $a+b$ is even and $c^2+a^2$ is odd. So $a,b,c$ all have the same parity, and as $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$ they are all odd.

Step 2: half-sums. Put \[x=\frac{a+b}2,\qquad y=\frac{b+c}2,\qquad z=\frac{c+a}2 ,\] positive integers with $a=x+z-y$, $b=x+y-z$, $c=y+z-x$. Since $a,b,c>0$, each of $x,y,z$ is less than the sum of the other two.

Now $a^2+b^2=\bigl(x+(z-y)\bigr)^2+\bigl(x-(z-y)\bigr)^2=2x^2+2(z-y)^2$, and $2y=b+c$ divides it; so $y\mid x^2+(z-y)^2$, and hence $y\mid x^2+z^2$. In the same way $b^2+c^2=2y^2+2(x-z)^2$ is divisible by $2z$, so $z\mid x^2+y^2$, and $c^2+a^2=2z^2+2(y-x)^2$ is divisible by $2x$, so $x\mid y^2+z^2$.

Step 3: $x,y,z$ are pairwise coprime. If a prime $p$ divides $x$ and $y$, then $p\mid y\mid x^2+z^2$ gives $p\mid z^2$, so $p\mid z$, and then $p$ divides $a=x+z-y$, $b$ and $c$, contradicting $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. The other two pairs are handled in the same way. Each of $x,y,z$ divides $x^2+y^2+z^2$ (for instance $x\mid y^2+z^2$), and they are pairwise coprime, so $xyz\mid x^2+y^2+z^2$: \[x^2+y^2+z^2=k\,xyz\qquad\text{for a positive integer }k.\] Step 4: the smallest two are equal. Everything so far is symmetric in $x,y,z$, so we may assume $x\le y\le z$. First, \[kx=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{yz}>2,\qquad\text{because } x^2+y^2+z^2-2yz=x^2+(y-z)^2>0 ;\] as $kx$ is an integer, $kx\ge3$. Consider the quadratic \[F(t)=t^2-kxy\,t+x^2+y^2 ,\qquad F(z)=0 .\] Suppose $x<y$. Then \[F(y)=(2-kx)\,y^2+x^2\le x^2-y^2<0 .\] As $F$ is a quadratic with positive leading coefficient, $y$ lies strictly between its two roots; the root $z\ge y$ is therefore the larger root, and every $t>y$ with $F(t)<0$ satisfies $t<z$. Take $t=x+y$: \[F(x+y)=2(x^2+xy+y^2)-kxy(x+y)\le 2(x^2+xy+y^2)-3y(x+y)=2x^2-xy-y^2=(x-y)(2x+y)<0 .\] Hence $x+y<z$, contradicting Step 2. So $x=y$.

Step 5: conclusion. As $x=y$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$, we get $x=y=1$. By Step 2, $z<x+y=2$, so $z=1$. Then $a=b=c=1$.

The only solution is $\boxed{(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with circumcenter $O$. Let $ABD$ and $ACE$ be isosceles triangles lying outside triangle $ABC$ so that $\angle BAD = \angle CAE = 90^\circ$. Suppose that the circumcircle of triangle $AED$, whose center is $Q$, intersects the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$ at $Z \ne A$.

Suppose that $AO$ intersects $DE$ at $X$ and $AQ$ intersects $BC$ at $Y$.

Show that the circumcircles of triangles $AXY$ and $OQZ$ are tangent.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Complex coordinates and two homotheties)

Set-up. Use complex numbers with $A=0$. Write $\beta=B$, $\gamma=C$, $c=AB=|\beta|$, $b=AC=|\gamma|$, and label the triangle counterclockwise, so that its area is $S=\frac12\operatorname{Im}(\bar\beta\gamma)>0$. Rotating $AB$ by $-90^\circ$ and $AC$ by $+90^\circ$ about $A$ moves them away from the triangle, so \[D=-i\beta,\qquad E=i\gamma .\] For points $U,V$ (with $0,U,V$ not collinear), $W$ is the circumcentre of $0,U,V$ exactly when $|W|^2=|W-U|^2=|W-V|^2$, i.e. $2\operatorname{Re}(W\bar U)=|U|^2$ and $2\operatorname{Re}(W\bar V)=|V|^2$.

Step 1: $O$ and $Q$. We claim \[O=\frac{i\,(b^2\beta-c^2\gamma)}{4S},\qquad Q=-\frac{b^2\beta+c^2\gamma}{4S}.\] Indeed, $O\bar\beta=\frac{i(b^2c^2-c^2\gamma\bar\beta)}{4S}$ and $\operatorname{Re}(-i\,\gamma\bar\beta)=\operatorname{Im}(\gamma\bar\beta)=2S$, so $2\operatorname{Re}(O\bar\beta)=c^2$; similarly $O\bar\gamma=\frac{i(b^2\beta\bar\gamma-b^2c^2)}{4S}$ with $\operatorname{Re}(i\beta\bar\gamma)= \operatorname{Im}(\bar\beta\gamma)=2S$, so $2\operatorname{Re}(O\bar\gamma)=b^2$. For $Q$: $Q\bar D=Q\cdot i\bar\beta=-\frac{i(b^2c^2+c^2\gamma\bar\beta)}{4S}$ has real part $\frac{c^2\cdot 2S}{4S}$, so $2\operatorname{Re}(Q\bar D)=c^2=|D|^2$, and $Q\bar E=Q\cdot(-i\bar\gamma)=\frac{i(b^2\beta\bar\gamma+b^2c^2)}{4S}$ gives $2\operatorname{Re}(Q\bar E)=b^2=|E|^2$.

Step 2: $X=-\kappa O$ and $Y=-\kappa Q$, where $\kappa=\dfrac{4S}{b^2+c^2}$. Note $0<\kappa<1$, because $4S=2bc\sin A<b^2+c^2$. The point \[-\kappa O=\frac{-i(b^2\beta-c^2\gamma)}{b^2+c^2}=\frac{b^2(-i\beta)+c^2(i\gamma)}{b^2+c^2}=\frac{b^2D+c^2E}{b^2+c^2}\] lies on segment $DE$ and on line $AO$ (a real multiple of $O$), so it is $X$. Likewise $-\kappa Q=\frac{b^2\beta+c^2\gamma}{b^2+c^2}$ lies on segment $BC$ and on line $AQ$, so it is $Y$.

Hence the homothety $h(z)=-\kappa z$ with centre $A$ maps $O\mapsto X$, $Q\mapsto Y$, $A\mapsto A$, and therefore maps the circle $\omega=(AOQ)$ onto the circle $(AXY)$.

Step 3: the circle $(OQZ)$. Since $OA=OZ$ and $QA=QZ$, the point $Z$ is the reflection of $A$ in the line $OQ$, so $\omega'=(OQZ)$ is the reflection of $\omega$ in $OQ$. Let $M=\frac{O+Q}2$ and let $\sigma(z)=2M-z$ be the half-turn about $M$. The half-turn is the reflection in $OQ$ followed by the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of $OQ$; the latter fixes $\omega$ (its centre lies on that bisector). Hence $\sigma(\omega)=\omega'$ and $\sigma(\omega')=\omega$.

Step 4: a point of $\omega'$. Let $K$ be the centre of $\omega$. Because $0\in\omega$, a point $z\ne0$ lies on $\omega$ iff $|z|^2=2\operatorname{Re}(z\bar K)$. Applying this to $O$ and $Q$ and adding gives $2\operatorname{Re}(M\bar K)=\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}2$. So, for real $\lambda\ne0$, \[\lambda M\in\omega\iff \lambda^2|M|^2=\lambda\,\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}{2}\iff\lambda=\frac{2(|O|^2+|Q|^2)}{|O+Q|^2}.\tag{1}\] Now compute, using $\beta\bar\gamma-\bar\beta\gamma=-2i\operatorname{Im}(\bar\beta\gamma)=-4iS$: \[O\bar Q=\frac{-i(b^2\beta-c^2\gamma)(b^2\bar\beta+c^2\bar\gamma)}{16S^2}=\frac{-i\bigl(b^4c^2-b^2c^4+b^2c^2(\beta\bar\gamma-\bar\beta\gamma)\bigr)}{16S^2}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}(O\bar Q)=-\frac{b^2c^2}{4S},\] \[|O|^2+|Q|^2=\frac{|b^2\beta-c^2\gamma|^2+|b^2\beta+c^2\gamma|^2}{16S^2}=\frac{2(b^4c^2+c^4b^2)}{16S^2}=\frac{b^2c^2(b^2+c^2)}{8S^2}.\] Therefore \[\frac{-2\operatorname{Re}(O\bar Q)}{|O|^2+|Q|^2}=\frac{b^2c^2}{2S}\cdot\frac{8S^2}{b^2c^2(b^2+c^2)}=\kappa, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{|O+Q|^2}{|O|^2+|Q|^2}=1-\kappa .\] By (1) with $\lambda=\frac2{1-\kappa}$, the point $T^*=\frac{2M}{1-\kappa}$ lies on $\omega$, and so \[T=\sigma(T^*)=2M-\frac{2M}{1-\kappa}=-\frac{2\kappa M}{1-\kappa}\quad\text{lies on }\omega'.\] Step 5: tangency. Consider $\varphi=h\circ\sigma$, i.e. $\varphi(z)=-\kappa(2M-z)=\kappa z-2\kappa M$. It is a homothety with ratio $\kappa>0$, and its centre (fixed point) is the solution of $z=\kappa z-2\kappa M$, which is exactly $T$. Moreover $\varphi(\omega')=h(\sigma(\omega'))=h(\omega)=(AXY)$. Since $T\in\omega'$, we get $T=\varphi(T)\in(AXY)$. A homothety with centre $T$ maps the tangent line of $\omega'$ at $T$ to a parallel line through $T$, that is, to itself; and this image is the tangent line of $\varphi(\omega')=(AXY)$ at $T$. So the circles $(AXY)$ and $(OQZ)$ pass through $T$ and have the same tangent line there. As $\kappa\ne1$ they are different circles, hence they are tangent at $T$ (internally, since the ratio is positive). $\blacksquare$

Remark. The proof shows more: $XY\parallel OQ$, the point of tangency lies on the line through $A$ and the midpoint of $OQ$, and $AQ$ is the $A$-symmedian of $ABC$ (since $Y$ divides $BC$ in the ratio $c^2:b^2$).

Solution 2 (Vecten, symmedians, a homothety and a half-turn)

Write $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, let $M_a$ be the midpoint of $BC$, $m_a=AM_a$ the $A$-median, $H_a$ the foot and $h_a=AH_a$ the length of the altitude from $A$, and put \[\kappa=\frac{2bc\sin A}{b^2+c^2}=\frac{2ah_a}{b^2+c^2}\qquad(\text{as } bc\sin A=2[ABC]=ah_a).\] Then $0<\kappa<1$, since $2bc\sin A<2bc\le b^2+c^2$. We have $AD=c$, $AE=b$, and the four angles at $A$ add up to $360^\circ$, so $\angle DAE=180^\circ-A$. If $b=c$, then $X$, $Y$ lie on the axis of symmetry and triangle $AXY$ does not exist; so $b\ne c$, and $H_a\ne M_a$.

Step 1 (Vecten). The points $D$ and $E$ arise from $B$ and $C$ by quarter-turns about $A$ in opposite senses. So if $\rho$ is the quarter-turn about $A$ with $\rho(D)=B$, then also $\rho(C)=E$, and $\rho(E)=\rho(\rho(C))=C'$, the reflection of $C$ in $A$. In triangle $BCC'$ the segment $AM_a$ joins the midpoints of $C'C$ and $CB$, so $BC'=2m_a$ and $BC'\parallel AM_a$. As $\rho$ turns every line through $90^\circ$, we get $DE=BC'=2m_a$ and $DE\perp AM_a$. Likewise, $\rho$ takes the midpoint of $DE$ to the midpoint $N'$ of $BC'$, and $AN'$ joins the midpoints of $C'C$ and $C'B$, so $AN'=\frac a2$ and $AN'\parallel BC$. Hence the $A$-median of triangle $ADE$ has length $\frac a2$ and is perpendicular to $BC$: it lies on the line $AH_a$.

Step 2 (isogonal lines). Since $AD\perp AB$ and $AE\perp AC$, the angles $BAC$ and $DAE$ have the same bisector line $b_A$ (the bisector of $\angle DAE$ is the extension beyond $A$ of the bisector of $\angle BAC$). The reflection in $b_A$ swaps the lines $AB$, $AC$ and also the lines $AD$, $AE$, so two lines through $A$ are isogonal for the angle $BAC$ exactly when they are isogonal for the angle $DAE$. Recall that in any triangle the line from a vertex to the circumcentre is isogonal to the altitude from that vertex, and the symmedian is the reflection of the median in the bisector.

  • Line $AO$ is isogonal to the altitude line $AH_a$ of $ABC$, which by Step 1 is the median line of $ADE$. So $AX$ is the $A$-symmedian of triangle $ADE$ ($X$ lies on segment $DE$).

  • Line $AQ$ is isogonal to the altitude from $A$ of $ADE$, which is perpendicular to $DE$, hence (Step 1) is the median line $AM_a$ of $ABC$. So $AY$ is the $A$-symmedian of triangle $ABC$.

The angles $BAC$ and $DAE$ do not overlap. The rays $AO$ and $AY$ lie inside $\angle BAC$ ($O$ is inside the acute triangle, $Y$ on side $BC$). The rays $AX$ and $AQ$ lie inside $\angle DAE$: $X$ is on side $DE$, and as $\angle ADE+\angle AED=180^\circ-\angle DAE=A<90^\circ$, both are acute, so $Q$ is on the same side of $AD$ as $E$ and on the same side of $AE$ as $D$. Hence $A$ lies between $O$ and $X$, and between $Q$ and $Y$.

Step 3 (two equal ratios). Lemma. If a triangle has sides $p,q$ at a vertex $V$, the median from $V$ has length $m$, and the symmedian from $V$ meets the opposite side at $K$, then $VK=\frac{2pq\,m}{p^2+q^2}$. Indeed, let $\theta$ and $\eta$ be the angles of the median with the sides $q$ and $p$. The median halves the area $S$ of the triangle: $\frac12pm\sin\eta=\frac12qm\sin\theta=\frac S2$. The symmedian makes the angle $\theta$ with side $p$ and $\eta$ with side $q$, so $\frac12p\,VK\sin\theta+\frac12q\,VK\sin\eta=S$, i.e. $VK\bigl(\frac{pS}{qm}+\frac{qS}{pm}\bigr)=2S$, which is the claim. $\square$

By the lemma and Step 1, \[AY=\frac{2bc\,m_a}{b^2+c^2},\qquad AX=\frac{2bc\cdot\frac a2}{b^2+c^2}=\frac{abc}{b^2+c^2}.\] By the law of sines, $AO=\frac a{2\sin A}$ and $AQ=\frac{DE}{2\sin\angle DAE}=\frac{2m_a}{2\sin A}=\frac{m_a}{\sin A}$. Therefore \[\frac{AX}{AO}=\frac{AY}{AQ}=\frac{2bc\sin A}{b^2+c^2}=\kappa .\] With Step 2, the homothety $h$ with centre $A$ and ratio $-\kappa$ maps $O\mapsto X$, $Q\mapsto Y$, $A\mapsto A$, so it maps the circle $\omega=(AOQ)$ onto the circle $(AXY)$.

Step 4 (the half-turn). Since $OA=OZ$ and $QA=QZ$, the point $Z$ is the reflection of $A$ in the line $OQ$, so $\omega'=(OQZ)$ is the reflection of $\omega$ in $OQ$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $OQ$ and $\sigma$ the half-turn about $N$. It is the composition of the reflections in $OQ$ and in the perpendicular bisector of $OQ$, and the latter fixes $\omega$ (its centre lies on that line). So $\sigma(\omega)=\omega'$ and $\sigma(\omega')=\omega$.

Take $A$ as the origin of vectors, so that $h(\vec z)=-\kappa\vec z$ and $\sigma(\vec z)=2\vec{AN}-\vec z$. Then $\varphi=h\circ\sigma$ is the map $\vec z\mapsto\kappa\vec z-2\kappa\,\vec{AN}$, a homothety with ratio $\kappa$ whose centre $T$ is the solution of $\vec z=\kappa\vec z-2\kappa\vec{AN}$: \[\vec{AT}=-\frac{2\kappa}{1-\kappa}\,\vec{AN},\qquad\text{and}\qquad \varphi(\omega')=h(\omega)=(AXY).\] Reduction. Suppose $T\in\omega'$. Then $T=\varphi(T)\in(AXY)$. The homothety $\varphi$ with centre $T$ maps the tangent line of $\omega'$ at $T$ to a parallel line through $T$, i.e. to itself, and this image is the tangent line of $\varphi(\omega')=(AXY)$ at $T$. So the two circles pass through $T$ and have the same tangent there; their radii differ by the factor $\kappa\ne1$, so they are different circles, and therefore they are tangent at $T$.

It remains to prove $T\in\omega'$, i.e. $\sigma(T)\in\sigma(\omega')=\omega$. Now $\vec{A\sigma(T)}=2\vec{AN}-\vec{AT}=\frac2{1-\kappa}\vec{AN}$: we must show that the point $T^*$ on ray $AN$ with $AT^*=\frac{2AN}{1-\kappa}$ lies on $\omega$.

Step 5 (the metric fact). The point $N$, the midpoint of the chord $OQ$, is inside $\omega$, so ray $AN$ meets $\omega$ again at a point $T'$ beyond $N$. The power of $N$ gives $AN\cdot NT'=NO\cdot NQ=\frac{OQ^2}4$, and the median formula in triangle $AOQ$ gives $4AN^2=2AO^2+2AQ^2-OQ^2$. Hence \[AT'=AN+\frac{OQ^2}{4AN}=\frac{4AN^2+OQ^2}{4AN}=\frac{AO^2+AQ^2}{2AN}.\] So $T^*=T'$ if and only if $(1-\kappa)(AO^2+AQ^2)=4AN^2$. As $4AN^2=|\vec{AO}+\vec{AQ}|^2=AO^2+AQ^2+2\,AO\cdot AQ\cos\angle OAQ$, this is \[\kappa\,(AO^2+AQ^2)=-2\,AO\cdot AQ\cos\angle OAQ .\tag{$*$}\] By Step 2 the reflection in $b_A$ maps the rays $AH_a$, $AM_a$ (inside $\angle BAC$) to the rays $AO$, $AY$, so $\angle OAY=\angle H_aAM_a$, and since $Q$ is on the ray opposite to $AY$, $\angle OAQ=180^\circ-\angle H_aAM_a$. In the right triangle $AH_aM_a$, $\cos\angle H_aAM_a=\frac{h_a}{m_a}$, so $\cos\angle OAQ=-\frac{h_a}{m_a}$. Substituting $AO=\frac a{2\sin A}$, $AQ=\frac{m_a}{\sin A}$ and $\kappa=\frac{2ah_a}{b^2+c^2}$ into $(*)$ and multiplying by $\frac{\sin^2A}{ah_a}$, the condition becomes \[\frac{2}{b^2+c^2}\Bigl(\frac{a^2}4+m_a^2\Bigr)=1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad b^2+c^2=\frac{a^2}2+2m_a^2 ,\] which is the median formula in triangle $ABC$. So $T^*\in\omega$, hence $T\in\omega'$, and the circles $(AXY)$ and $(OQZ)$ are tangent at $T$. $\blacksquare$

Solution 3 (Inversion at A, then the distance of the centres)

Put $A$ at the origin and treat points as vectors; $U\cdot V$ is the dot product.

Set-up. Let $J$ be the rotation through $90^\circ$ about $A$ with $J(B)=D$. Label $ABC$ counterclockwise. As $D$ lies on the other side of $AB$ from $C$, it is obtained from $B$ by the clockwise quarter-turn about $A$; as $E$ lies on the other side of $AC$ from $B$, it is obtained from $C$ by the counterclockwise quarter-turn. So $J$ is the clockwise quarter-turn and $E=J^{-1}(C)=-J(C)$. The rotation $J$ preserves lengths and dot products, and $J(J(U))=-U$, so $U\cdot J(V)=-J(U)\cdot V$ for all $U,V$.

$A$, $O$, $Q$ are not collinear. If $O=Q$, the two circles would have the same centre and both pass through $A$, so they would coincide and $D$ would lie on the circle $ABC$; then $\angle BAD=90^\circ$ makes $BD$ a diameter and $O$ the midpoint of $BD$, which lies on the side of line $AB$ containing $D$. But $O$ is inside the acute triangle, on the side of $AB$ containing $C$, which is the other side. So $O\ne Q$. The line $OQ$ is the perpendicular bisector of the common chord $AZ$ and $Z\ne A$, so $A\notin OQ$.

Step 1: inversion. For $U\ne0$ put $U'=\frac{U}{|U|^2}$. A circle through $A$ with centre $W$ has equation $|U|^2=2\,W\cdot U$, so a point $U\ne0$ lies on it if and only if $W\cdot U'=\frac12$. A line not through $A$ has an equation $L\cdot U=1$, and then $|U'|^2=\frac1{|U|^2}=L\cdot U'$ for each of its points $U$. Also $J(U)'=J(U')$.

Since $B$, $C$ lie on the circle with centre $O$, and $D=J(B)$, $E=-J(C)$ lie on the circle with centre $Q$, \[O\cdot B'=O\cdot C'=\tfrac12,\qquad Q\cdot J(B')=\tfrac12,\qquad Q\cdot J(C')=-\tfrac12 .\] Let $M=\frac{B'+C'}2$ and $H=\frac{B'-C'}2$. Then $O\cdot M=\frac12$, $O\cdot H=0$, $Q\cdot J(M)=0$ and $Q\cdot J(H)=\frac12$.

  • $Q\cdot J(M)=0$ means $J(Q)\cdot M=0$, so $M$ is a multiple of $Q$, say $M=sQ$. Then $s\,(O\cdot Q)=O\cdot M=\frac12$, so $t:=O\cdot Q\ne0$ and $M=\frac{Q}{2t}$.

  • $O\cdot H=0$ means $H=r\,J(O)$ for a real $r$. Then $\frac12=Q\cdot J(H)=r\,Q\cdot J(J(O))=-rt$, so $H=-\frac{J(O)}{2t}$.

For $D'=J(B')$ and $E'=-J(C')$ we get $\frac{D'+E'}2=J(H)=\frac{O}{2t}$ and $\frac{D'-E'}2=J(M)=\frac{J(Q)}{2t}$. Hence \[|B'|^2+|C'|^2=2|M|^2+2|H|^2=\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}{2t^2}=|D'|^2+|E'|^2 .\] Step 2: $X$ and $Y$. Let $L\cdot U=1$ be the equation of line $BC$. Adding $|B'|^2=L\cdot B'$ and $|C'|^2=L\cdot C'$ gives \[\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}{2t^2}=L\cdot(B'+C')=\frac{L\cdot Q}{t},\qquad\text{so}\qquad L\cdot Q=\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}{2t}\ne0 .\] The point $\lambda Q$ of line $AQ$ lies on $BC$ exactly when $\lambda\,L\cdot Q=1$; therefore \[Y=\lambda Q,\qquad \lambda=\frac{2t}{|O|^2+|Q|^2}.\] The same computation for line $DE$, with $D',E'$ in place of $B',C'$ (their sum is $\frac{O}{t}$ and their squared lengths have the same sum), gives $X=\lambda O$ with the same $\lambda$. Since $|O|^2+|Q|^2\pm2t=|O\pm Q|^2>0$ (the vectors $O$, $Q$ are not parallel), we have $-1<\lambda<1$, and $\lambda\ne0$. So the homothety with centre $A$ and ratio $\lambda$ maps $A,O,Q$ to $A,X,Y$: if $\omega$ is the circle $AOQ$, with centre $W$ and radius $R$, then the circle $AXY$ has centre $\lambda W$ and radius $|\lambda|R$.

Step 3: coordinates. Choose Cartesian axes with origin $A$ and line $OQ$ horizontal (a rotation of axes does not change dot products): \[O=(o,h),\qquad Q=(q,h),\qquad h\ne0,\quad o\ne q .\] Then $t=oq+h^2$ and $|O|^2+|Q|^2=o^2+q^2+2h^2$. The centre $W$ of $\omega$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=m$ of $OQ$, where $m=\frac{o+q}2$; writing $W=(m,u)$, the condition $|W|^2=|W-O|^2$ reads $2mo+2uh=o^2+h^2$, so \[u=\frac{o^2+h^2-o(o+q)}{2h}=\frac{h^2-oq}{2h},\qquad R^2=m^2+u^2 .\] Since $OA=OZ$ and $QA=QZ$, the point $Z$ is the reflection of $A$ in line $OQ$ (the line $y=h$), and the circle $OQZ$ is the reflection of $\omega$ in that line: its centre is $W^*=(m,\,2h-u)$ and its radius is $R$.

Step 4: the distance of the centres. We compute \begin{align*} |W^*-\lambda W|^2-(1+\lambda)^2R^2&=(1-\lambda)^2m^2+\bigl(2h-(1+\lambda)u\bigr)^2-(1+\lambda)^2(m^2+u^2)\\ &=-4\lambda m^2+4h^2-4(1+\lambda)hu=4\bigl[h(h-u)-\lambda\,(hu+m^2)\bigr]. \end{align*} Here $h(h-u)=h^2-\frac{h^2-oq}2=\frac{h^2+oq}2=\frac t2$ and $hu+m^2=\frac{h^2-oq}2+\frac{(o+q)^2}4=\frac{2h^2+o^2+q^2}4=\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}4$. So the bracket equals $\frac t2-\lambda\cdot\frac{|O|^2+|Q|^2}4=\frac t2-\frac t2=0$, and the distance between the centres of the circles $AXY$ and $OQZ$ is $(1+\lambda)R>0$.

Conclusion. The radii are $|\lambda|R$ and $R$. If $\lambda>0$, the distance of the centres $(1+\lambda)R$ is the sum of the radii, and the circles are externally tangent. If $\lambda<0$, it is $R-|\lambda|R$, the difference of the radii, and as it is positive the circles are distinct and internally tangent. In either case the circles $AXY$ and $OQZ$ are tangent. $\blacksquare$

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Let $n \ge 3$ be a positive integer, and let $d(n)$ denote the number of positive divisors of $n$. Prove that there exist distinct positive integers $a$ and $b$, both divisors of $n$, such that $d(n) \mid a-b$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The residue class of 0)

Let $d=d(n)$. Suppose, for contradiction, that no two distinct divisors of $n$ are congruent modulo $d$. Then the $d$ divisors of $n$ lie in $d$ different residue classes modulo $d$, so each residue class modulo $d$ contains exactly one divisor of $n$.

In particular exactly one divisor of $n$ is a multiple of $d$. Such a divisor exists, say $q$, so $d\mid q\mid n$. The divisors of $n$ that are multiples of $d$ are exactly the numbers $d\,r$ with $r\mid \frac nd$; there are $d\!\left(\frac nd\right)$ of them. Hence $d\!\left(\frac nd\right)=1$, i.e. $\frac nd=1$, so \[d(n)=n .\] But for $n\ge3$ the number $n-1$ does not divide $n$ (as $1<n-1<n$ and $n-(n-1)=1$), so not all of $1,2,\dots,n$ are divisors of $n$ and $d(n)\le n-1<n$. This contradiction shows that two distinct divisors $a,b$ of $n$ with $d(n)\mid a-b$ exist.

Solution 2 (Counting divisors in residue classes modulo a prime)

Let $d=d(n)\ge2$ and suppose, for contradiction, that the divisors of $n$ are pairwise incongruent modulo $d$; then they form a complete residue system modulo $d$.

Claim. If $p$ is a prime dividing $d$, then exactly $\frac dp$ divisors of $n$ are divisible by $p$.

Indeed, among the residues $0,1,\dots,d-1$ exactly $\frac dp$ are multiples of $p$, and (as $p\mid d$) a divisor is divisible by $p$ iff its residue modulo $d$ is. $\square$

Let $p\mid d$. By the claim, some divisor of $n$ is divisible by $p$, so $p\mid n$; write $n=p^em$ with $e\ge1$, $p\nmid m$. The divisors of $n$ divisible by $p$ are $p^jt$ with $1\le j\le e$, $t\mid m$, so there are $e\,d(m)$ of them, while $d=(e+1)d(m)$. The claim gives \[e\,d(m)=\frac{(e+1)\,d(m)}p\iff pe=e+1\iff e(p-1)=1,\] so $p=2$ and $e=1$. Consequently $d$ has no odd prime factor, i.e. $d=2^k$ with $k\ge1$, and $2\,\|\,n$.

Finally, the complete residue system contains a divisor $q\equiv0\pmod d$, so $2^k=d\mid q\mid n$; with $2\,\|\,n$ this forces $k=1$, i.e. $d(n)=2$ and $n$ is a prime. An even prime is $2$, contradicting $n\ge3$. Hence two distinct divisors of $n$ are congruent modulo $d(n)$.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $a$, $b$, and $c$ be positive integers. An $a \times b \times c$ rectangular box is partitioned into $abc$ unit cubes colored either blue or yellow. In every row of unit cubes parallel to any edge of the rectangular box, there is at most one yellow cube.

Determine the maximum possible number of yellow cubes.

Show the answer

the product of the two smallest of $a,b,c$, i.e. $\min(ab,\,bc,\,ca)$

Show the solution

Solution (Projection bound and a cyclic construction)

By symmetry we may assume $a\le b\le c$ and must show that the maximum is $ab$. Give the unit cubes coordinates $(x,y,z)$ with $0\le x<a$, $0\le y<b$, $0\le z<c$; a row parallel to an edge is a set of cubes in which two of the coordinates are fixed.

Upper bound. The $ab$ rows parallel to the $z$-edge (fixed $x,y$) partition the box, and each contains at most one yellow cube. Hence there are at most $ab$ yellow cubes.

Construction. Colour $(x,y,z)$ yellow exactly when $z\equiv x+y\pmod b$ and $0\le z<b$ (possible since $b\le c$). Check the three kinds of rows:

  • fixed $(x,y)$: exactly one $z\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}$ with $z\equiv x+y\pmod b$;

  • fixed $(x,z)$: $y\equiv z-x\pmod b$ has exactly one solution with $0\le y<b$;

  • fixed $(y,z)$: $x\equiv z-y\pmod b$ has at most one solution with $0\le x<a$, because $a\le b$.

So every row contains at most one yellow cube, and there are exactly $ab$ yellow cubes (one for each pair $(x,y)$).

The maximum is $\boxed{\min(ab,\,bc,\,ca)}$, the product of the two smallest side lengths.

Problem 8

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool

Find all functions $f : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ satisfying \[f(u+(i+\sqrt{3})z) + f(u+(i-\sqrt{3})z) = 2f(u) + 2\operatorname{Re}(z)\] for all complex numbers $u$ and $z$, where $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ denotes the real part of $z$ and $i = \sqrt{-1}$.

Show the answer

$f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)+C$ for an arbitrary constant $C\in\mathbb C$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A cube root of unity)

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Since $i+\sqrt3=2e^{i\pi/6}$ and $i-\sqrt3=2e^{5i\pi/6}$, we have $i-\sqrt3=\omega(i+\sqrt3)$. As $z$ runs over $\mathbb C$, so does $w=(i+\sqrt3)z$, and $z=\frac{w}{\sqrt3+i}$. So the equation says: for all $u,w\in\mathbb C$, \[f(u+w)+f(u+\omega w)=2f(u)+L(w),\qquad L(w)=2\operatorname{Re}\frac{w}{\sqrt3+i}.\tag{1}\] Note that $L$ is additive: $L(w_1+w_2)=L(w_1)+L(w_2)$.

Fix $u,w$ and put $P=f(u+w)$, $R=f(u+\omega w)$, $T=f(u+\omega^2w)$. Applying (1) to $w$, $\omega w$, $\omega^2w$ (and using $\omega^3=1$): \[P+R=2f(u)+L(w),\qquad R+T=2f(u)+L(\omega w),\qquad T+P=2f(u)+L(\omega^2w).\] Adding and using $L(w)+L(\omega w)+L(\omega^2w)=L\bigl((1+\omega+\omega^2)w\bigr)=L(0)=0$ gives $P+R+T=3f(u)$, and subtracting the second equation, \[f(u+w)=P=f(u)-L(\omega w).\] Since $\frac{\omega}{\sqrt3+i}=\frac{e^{2\pi i/3}}{2e^{i\pi/6}}=\frac i2$, we get $-L(\omega w)=-\operatorname{Re}(iw)=\operatorname{Im}(w)$. Thus $f(u+w)=f(u)+\operatorname{Im}(w)$ for all $u,w$; with $u=0$, \[f(w)=\operatorname{Im}(w)+C,\qquad C=f(0).\] Check. For $f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)+C$: \[f(u+(i+\sqrt3)z)+f(u+(i-\sqrt3)z)=2\operatorname{Im}(u)+\operatorname{Im}(2iz)+2C=2f(u)+2\operatorname{Re}(z).\] So the solutions are exactly $\boxed{f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)+C}$ with $C\in\mathbb C$ arbitrary.

Solution 2 (Subtract the imaginary part, then use equilateral triangles)

Since $\operatorname{Im}$ is additive and real-linear, \[\operatorname{Im}\bigl(u+(i+\sqrt3)z\bigr)+\operatorname{Im}\bigl(u+(i-\sqrt3)z\bigr)=2\operatorname{Im}(u)+\operatorname{Im}(2iz)=2\operatorname{Im}(u)+2\operatorname{Re}(z),\] so $f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)$ is one solution. Put $g(z)=f(z)-\operatorname{Im}(z)$; subtracting the two equations, \[g\bigl(u+(i+\sqrt3)z\bigr)+g\bigl(u+(i-\sqrt3)z\bigr)=2g(u)\qquad\text{for all }u,z\in\mathbb C.\] Let $\alpha=\sqrt3+i=2e^{i\pi/6}$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$; then $i-\sqrt3=2e^{5i\pi/6}=\omega\alpha$. As $z$ runs over $\mathbb C$, so does $w=\alpha z$, hence \[g(u+w)+g(u+\omega w)=2g(u)\qquad\text{for all }u,w\in\mathbb C.\] Fix $u,w$. The points $P_1=u+w$, $P_2=u+\omega w$, $P_3=u+\omega^2w$ are the vertices of an equilateral triangle with centre $u$. Using the last equation with $w$, $\omega w$ and $\omega^2w$ in place of $w$ (and $\omega^3=1$), \[g(P_1)+g(P_2)=g(P_2)+g(P_3)=g(P_3)+g(P_1)=2g(u).\] The first two give $g(P_1)=g(P_3)$, the last two give $g(P_2)=g(P_1)$; so all three values are equal, and each equals $g(u)$. In particular $g(u+w)=g(u)$ for all $u,w$, so $g$ is a constant $C$, and $f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)+C$. Conversely every such $f$ works, because $f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)$ works (first display) and a constant $C$ adds $2C$ to both sides.

The solutions are $\boxed{f(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)+C}$ with $C\in\mathbb C$ arbitrary.

Problem 9

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle, and let $P$ be a point on side $BC$ distinct from $B$ and $C$. Let $N_1$ and $N_2$ be the centers of the nine-point circles of triangles $ABP$ and $ACP$, respectively.

Prove that $N_1N_2 \ge \frac{1}{4}BC$, and determine all positions of $P$ for which equality holds.

Show the answer

$N_1N_2\ge\frac14BC$, with equality exactly when $P$ is the foot of the altitude from $A$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two nine-point circles with a common chord)

Let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ (it lies inside segment $BC$ because the triangle is acute), $M$ the midpoint of $AP$, and $K_1$, $K_2$ the midpoints of $BP$ and $CP$. Call the two nine-point circles $\nu_1$ (of $ABP$) and $\nu_2$ (of $ACP$).

Common points. The nine-point circle of a triangle passes through the midpoints of its sides and the feet of its altitudes. In triangle $ABP$ the altitude from $A$ is perpendicular to line $BP=BC$, so its foot is $D$. Hence $\nu_1$ passes through $M$, $K_1$ and $D$; likewise $\nu_2$ passes through $M$, $K_2$ and $D$.

Projection of $N_1N_2$ onto $BC$. The points $D$ and $K_1$ are both on $\nu_1$ and on line $BC$, so the projection of the centre $N_1$ onto $BC$ is the midpoint of $DK_1$ (if $D=K_1$, line $BC$ is tangent at $D$ and the projection is $D$ itself). Similarly $N_2$ projects to the midpoint of $DK_2$. The distance between these two projections is \[\Bigl|\frac{D+K_1}2-\frac{D+K_2}2\Bigr|=\frac{K_1K_2}2=\frac12\Bigl(\frac{BP}2+\frac{PC}2\Bigr)=\frac{BC}4 .\] A segment is at least as long as its projection, so \[N_1N_2\ \ge\ \frac{BC}4 .\] Equality. Equality holds iff $N_1N_2\parallel BC$. Since the projections differ, $N_1\ne N_2$; the circles $\nu_1,\nu_2$ meet at $M$ and $D$ (and $M\ne D$ because $M$ is not on $BC$), so the line of centres $N_1N_2$ is perpendicular to the common chord $MD$. Hence equality holds iff $MD\perp BC$, i.e. iff $D$ is the projection of $M$ onto $BC$. As $A$ projects to $D$ and $P$ to itself, $M$ projects to the midpoint of $DP$; this equals $D$ iff $P=D$.

Therefore $N_1N_2\ge\frac14BC$, and equality holds iff $\boxed{P \text{ is the foot of the altitude from } A}$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Let $D$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$ and use coordinates with $D=(0,0)$, $A=(0,h)$ $(h>0)$, $B=(u,0)$, $C=(v,0)$, $P=(p,0)$, where $u<0<v$ (the triangle is acute, so $D$ is inside $BC$) and $u<p<v$.

Nine-point centre. For any triangle with vertices $V_1,V_2,V_3$ and circumcentre $O'$, the orthocentre is $V_1+V_2+V_3-2O'$ (as vectors), and the nine-point centre is the midpoint of the circumcentre and the orthocentre, namely $\frac12(V_1+V_2+V_3-O')$.

Triangle $ABP$. Its circumcentre $O_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=\frac{u+p}2$ of $BP$; writing $O_1=(\frac{u+p}2,y)$, the condition $O_1A^2=O_1B^2$ reads $\frac{(u+p)^2}4+(y-h)^2=\frac{(p-u)^2}4+y^2$, i.e. $y=\frac{h^2+up}{2h}$. Hence \[N_1=\tfrac12\bigl(A+B+P-O_1\bigr)=\Bigl(\frac{u+p}4,\ \frac{h^2-up}{4h}\Bigr),\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad N_2=\Bigl(\frac{v+p}4,\ \frac{h^2-vp}{4h}\Bigr).\] Distance. \[N_1N_2^2=\frac{(v-u)^2}{16}+\frac{(v-u)^2p^2}{16h^2}=\frac{BC^2}{16}\Bigl(1+\frac{p^2}{h^2}\Bigr)\ \ge\ \frac{BC^2}{16},\] so $N_1N_2\ge\frac14BC$, with equality iff $p=0$, i.e. iff $P=D$. (In fact $N_1N_2=\frac{BC}{4\sin\angle APB}$, since $\sqrt{1+p^2/h^2}=AP/AD$.) Hence equality holds iff $\boxed{P \text{ is the foot of the altitude from } A}$.

Problem 10

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

In a province with $n$ villages, there are $n$ species of pomelo ($n \ge 2$). In a recent year, the productivities of the pomelos satisfy the following.

  • Each village has a non-negative integer productivity for each species.

  • In each village, the total productivity of all $n$ species is exactly $n^2+1$ pomelo.

  • For each species, the total productivity of that species over all $n$ villages is exactly $n^2+1$ pomelo.

Show that for each village there exist another village and $2$ different species such that the total productivity of these $2$ species in these $2$ villages is at least $4n+2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two largest entries and averaging)

Write the productivities as an $n\times n$ table of nonnegative integers $p_{v,s}$ (village $v$, species $s$); every row sum and every column sum equals $N=n^2+1$. Fix a village $v$. Let $s,t$ be species with the two largest entries of row $v$, and put $m=p_{v,s}+p_{v,t}$.

Averaging over the other villages. The columns $s$ and $t$ have total $2N$, of which $m$ lies in row $v$; the remaining $2N-m$ is spread over the other $n-1$ villages. So some village $w\ne v$ has $p_{w,s}+p_{w,t}\ge\frac{2N-m}{n-1}$, and for this choice \[T:=p_{v,s}+p_{v,t}+p_{w,s}+p_{w,t}\ \ge\ m+\frac{2N-m}{n-1}=\frac{(n-2)\,m+2N}{n-1}.\tag{1}\] Case $n=2$. Then (1) gives $T\ge 2N=10=4n+2$.

Case $n\ge3$. The two largest of the $n$ entries of row $v$ have average at least the row average $\frac Nn$, so $m\ge\frac{2N}n=2n+\frac2n>2n$; as $m$ is an integer, $m\ge 2n+1$. Since the right side of (1) increases with $m$ (the coefficient $n-2$ is positive), \[T\ \ge\ \frac{(n-2)(2n+1)+2(n^2+1)}{n-1}=\frac{4n^2-3n}{n-1}=4n+1+\frac1{n-1}>4n+1 .\] As $T$ is an integer, $T\ge 4n+2$.

In both cases the villages $v,w$ and the species $s,t$ have total productivity at least $4n+2$, as required.

Solution 2 (Any second village works)

Fix a village $v$, and let $w$ be any other village. For each species $j$ ($1\le j\le n$) let $s_j$ be the total productivity of species $j$ in the two villages $v$ and $w$. Each village produces $n^2+1$ pomelos in total, so \[s_1+s_2+\dots+s_n=2(n^2+1).\] Put $x_j=s_j-2n$. These are integers and \[x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n=2n^2+2-n\cdot2n=2 .\] Number the species so that $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_n$ (there are at least two, as $n\ge2$).

  • If $x_2\ge1$, then $x_1\ge x_2\ge1$, so $x_1+x_2\ge2$.

  • If $x_2\le0$, then $x_j\le x_2\le0$ for every $j\ge3$, so $x_1+x_2\ge x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n=2$.

In both cases $s_1+s_2=x_1+x_2+4n\ge4n+2$. So the villages $v$, $w$ and the two species numbered $1$ and $2$ have total productivity at least $4n+2$, as required. (The argument uses only the two village totals: the condition on the species totals is not needed, and every second village $w$ works.)
