Problem 3

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra · Elementary Symmetric Substitution

Let $x$ be a real number with $x\neq 0,1$. Define \[y=1-\frac1x\qquad\text{and}\qquad z=1-\frac1y .\] Suppose $x+y+z=7$. Find $x^2+y^2+z^2$.

Show the answer

$41$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Reciprocals)

From $y=1-\frac1x$ we get $\frac1x=1-y$, and from $z=1-\frac1y$ we get $\frac1y=1-z$. Also $z=1-\frac{x}{x-1}=\frac{1}{1-x}$, so $\frac1z=1-x$. (All of $x,y,z$ are nonzero and different from $1$: $y=0$ would need $x=1$, and $y=1$ is impossible.) Adding, \[\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z=3-(x+y+z)=-4 .\] Moreover $xyz=x\cdot\frac{x-1}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{1-x}=-1$. Hence \[xy+yz+zx=xyz\left(\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z\right)=(-1)(-4)=4,\] and $x^2+y^2+z^2=(x+y+z)^2-2(xy+yz+zx)=49-8=\boxed{41}$.

Solution 2 (Express everything in x)

We have $y=\frac{x-1}{x}$ and $z=1-\frac{x}{x-1}=\frac{1}{1-x}$. Then \[xy=x-1,\qquad yz=\frac{x-1}{x}\cdot\frac1{1-x}=-\frac1x,\qquad zx=\frac{x}{1-x},\] while $x+y+z=x+1-\frac1x+\frac1{1-x}$. Subtracting, \[(xy+yz+zx)-(x+y+z)=-2+\frac{x}{1-x}-\frac{1}{1-x}=-2-1=-3 .\] So $xy+yz+zx=7-3=4$ and $x^2+y^2+z^2=7^2-2\cdot4=\boxed{41}$.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · GCD Logic

What is the largest value of $n$ for which it is possible to arrange $n$ distinct positive integers, each less than or equal to $20$, around a circle so that any two adjacent numbers have greatest common divisor greater than $1$?

Show the answer

$14$

Show the solution

Solution (Exclude, then build)

A circle of one or two numbers cannot beat the construction of $14$ given below, so consider a circle of at least three numbers: every number in it needs two distinct neighbours sharing a prime factor with it.

  • $1$ shares no factor with anything.

  • $11,13,17,19$: their only multiples up to $20$ are themselves, so they have no possible neighbour.

  • $7$: the only other multiple of $7$ up to $20$ is $14$, so $7$ has at most one possible neighbour.

This excludes $1$, $7$, $11$, $13$, $17$, $19$, leaving at most $20-6=14$ numbers: \[2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 8,\ 9,\ 10,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15,\ 16,\ 18,\ 20 .\] All $14$ fit: \[2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 14,\ 20,\ 5,\ 15,\ 3,\ 9,\ 18,\ 12,\ 6,\ 10\ (\text{back to } 2),\] where consecutive numbers share the factor $2,2,2,2,2,5,5,3,3,3,6,6,2,2$ respectively. The answer is $\boxed{14}$.

Problem 7

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Remainder, Factor, and Evaluation Logic

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying $P(1)=1$ and $P(3)=5$. Find the smallest possible value of $|P(7)|$.

Show the answer

$11$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Division by (x-1)(x-3))

Divide $P$ by the monic polynomial $(x-1)(x-3)$: $P(x)=(x-1)(x-3)Q(x)+R(x)$ with $Q$ having integer coefficients and $R$ linear. From $R(1)=1$, $R(3)=5$ we get $R(x)=2x-1$. Hence \[P(7)=6\cdot4\cdot Q(7)+13=13+24Q(7),\] where $Q(7)$ is an integer. Every integer occurs as $Q(7)$ (take $Q$ constant). So the possible values of $P(7)$ are exactly the numbers $\equiv 13\pmod{24}$, and the smallest absolute value is $|13-24|=\boxed{11}$, attained by $P(x)=2x-1-(x-1)(x-3)$.

(The tempting answer $1$, from $P(7)\equiv P(3)\pmod 4$ and $P(7)\equiv P(1)\pmod 6$, is not attainable.)

Solution 2 (The factor theorem twice)

Since $P(x)-P(1)$ vanishes at $x=1$, $P(x)=1+(x-1)U(x)$ with $U$ having integer coefficients. Then $P(3)=1+2U(3)=5$ gives $U(3)=2$. Since $U(7)-U(3)$ is divisible by $7-3=4$, we get $U(7)=2+4t$ for an integer $t$, and \[P(7)=1+6U(7)=13+24t .\] Every $t$ occurs: $U(x)=2+t(x-3)$ gives a valid $P$. The values $13+24t$ closest to $0$ are $13$ and $-11$, so the minimum of $|P(7)|$ is $\boxed{11}$.

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 28: Area Methods and Geometric Measurement · Area Ratios in Triangles and Polygons

Square $ABCD$ has side length $12$. Points $E$ and $F$ lie on sides $BC$ and $CD$, respectively, such that triangle $AEF$ is equilateral. Lines $BF$ and $DE$ intersect at $P$. Find $AP^2$.

Show the answer

$96$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(12,0)$, $C=(12,12)$, $D=(0,12)$. Since $AE=AF$, the right triangles $ABE$ and $ADF$ are congruent, so $BE=DF$; write $E=(12,t)$, $F=(t,12)$. Then $AE^2=EF^2$ reads $144+t^2=2(12-t)^2$, i.e. $t^2-48t+144=0$, and as $0\le t\le12$, $t=24-12\sqrt3$.

The figure is symmetric in the line $AC$ (it swaps $B\leftrightarrow D$, $E\leftrightarrow F$), so $P=BF\cap DE$ lies on $y=x$. Line $DE$ is $y=12-\frac{12-t}{12}x$; setting $y=x$ gives $x\cdot\frac{24-t}{12}=12$, so \[x=\frac{144}{24-t}=\frac{144}{12\sqrt3}=4\sqrt3 .\] Thus $P=(4\sqrt3,4\sqrt3)$ and $AP^2=2\cdot48=\boxed{96}$.

Solution 2 (An area ratio)

As $AE=AF$, the right triangles $ABE$ and $ADF$ are congruent, so the figure is symmetric about the diagonal $AC$ and $P$ lies on $AC$. Let $CE=CF=u$; then $EF=u\sqrt2$ and $AE^2=12^2+(12-u)^2$, so $AE=EF$ gives $2u^2=144+(12-u)^2$, i.e. $u^2+24u-288=0$ and $u=12\sqrt3-12$.

Line $DE$ runs from the vertex $D$ to the interior point $E$ of side $BC$, so it separates $A$ from $C$, and $P$ lies inside segment $AC$. Since $P$ is on line $DE$, the ratio $AP:PC$ equals the ratio of the distances from $A$ and $C$ to line $DE$, which is $[ADE]:[CDE]$. Now $[ADE]=\frac12\cdot12\cdot12=72$ (base $AD$, height $12$) and $[CDE]=\frac12\cdot CE\cdot CD=6u=72(\sqrt3-1)$. Hence \[\frac{AP}{AC}=\frac{72}{72+72(\sqrt3-1)}=\frac1{\sqrt3},\qquad AP^2=\frac{AC^2}{3}=\frac{288}{3}=\boxed{96}.\]

Problem 9

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · Prime-Exponent Bookkeeping

Suppose that $a,b,c$ are positive integers satisfying \[a\gcd(b,c)=1200,\qquad b\gcd(c,a)=432,\qquad c\gcd(a,b)=1344 .\] Find the greatest possible value of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b,c)$.

Show the answer

$302400$

Show the solution

Solution (A bound by divisibility and an example)

Since $a\mid 1200$, $b\mid432$ and $c\mid1344$, the number $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b,c)$ divides \[\operatorname{lcm}(1200,432,1344)=\operatorname{lcm}(2^4\cdot3\cdot5^2,\ 2^4\cdot3^3,\ 2^6\cdot3\cdot7)=2^6\cdot3^3\cdot5^2\cdot7=302400 .\] This bound is attained by $(a,b,c)=(25,432,1344)$: indeed $\gcd(432,1344)=48$, so $a\gcd(b,c)=25\cdot48=1200$; $\gcd(1344,25)=1$, so $b\gcd(c,a)=432$; $\gcd(25,432)=1$, so $c\gcd(a,b)=1344$. And $\operatorname{lcm}(25,432,1344)=2^6\cdot3^3\cdot5^2\cdot7$. The answer is $\boxed{302400}$.

Problem 11

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Equation of Loci and Curves

Let $s$ be a positive integer, and let $ABCD$ be a square of side length $s$. Suppose that there is at least one point $P$ strictly inside the circumcircle of $ABCD$ with $AP=20$ and $CP=40$, and that every such point $P$ lies outside the square. Find the sum of all possible values of $s$.

Show the answer

$170$

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates centred at the square)

Let $d=\frac{s}{\sqrt2}$ be the half-diagonal and put the centre $O$ at the origin with $A=(-d,0)$, $B=(0,d)$, $C=(d,0)$, $D=(0,-d)$. The circumcircle is $x^2+y^2=d^2$ and the square is $|x|+|y|\le d$.

Inside the circle. For any point $P$, $AP^2+CP^2=2\,OP^2+2d^2$ (expand with $A=(-d,0)$, $C=(d,0)$), so $OP^2=1000-d^2$. Hence $P$ is strictly inside the circle iff $1000-d^2<d^2$, i.e. $d^2>500$, i.e. $s^2>1000$.

The points $P$. For $P=(x,y)$: $AP^2-CP^2=4dx=400-1600$, so $x=-\frac{300}{d}$; and $AP^2+CP^2=2(x^2+y^2)+2d^2=2000$. Hence \[y^2=1000-d^2-\frac{90000}{d^2}=400-\left(d-\frac{300}{d}\right)^2 .\] There are two such points $(x,\pm y)$, symmetric about $AC$, when the right side is positive.

Outside the square. With $s^2>1000$ we have $d^2>500$, so $d-\frac{300}d>0$. The point is outside iff $\frac{300}{d}+|y|>d$, i.e. $y^2>\left(d-\frac{300}d\right)^2$, i.e. $\left(d-\frac{300}{d}\right)^2<200$ (this also makes $y^2>0$, so $P$ exists). Since $d-\frac{300}{d}=\frac{s}{\sqrt2}-\frac{300\sqrt2}{s}$, the condition $d-\frac{300}{d}<10\sqrt2$ becomes \[\frac s2-\frac{300}{s}<10\iff s^2-20s-600<0\iff s<10+10\sqrt7\approx36.46 .\] Together with $s^2>1000$ (so $s\ge32$), the possible values are $s=32,33,34,35,36$, with sum $\boxed{170}$.

Problem 12

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · State-Based Construction

Aryaan wants to place the numbers $1,2,\dots,200$ in the cells of a $2\times 100$ grid, one number per cell, so that each pair of consecutive numbers lies in cells that share a side. In how many ways can Aryaan accomplish this task?

Show the answer

$19804$

Show the solution

Solution (The path is forced by its two ends)

A valid placement is a path through all $200$ cells, moving each time to a side-neighbour, starting at the cell of $1$ and ending at the cell of $200$. Write cells as $(r,c)$ with row $r\in\{1,2\}$ and column $c\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, $n=100$; for a row $r$ let $\bar r$ be the other row. Let the path start at $u=(r,i)$ and end at $v$ in column $j$. Every cell other than $u,v$ has exactly two path-neighbours, and $u,v$ have one each.

A useful fact. If a block of consecutive columns, say columns $1,\dots,m$, contains neither endpoint, then the path crosses into it and out of it through the two edges joining column $m$ and column $m+1$; inside the block it goes from $(\rho,m)$ along row $\rho$ to column $1$ and back along row $\bar\rho$ to $(\bar\rho,m)$. Indeed, the corner cells $(1,1),(2,1)$ have only two grid-neighbours each, so the path uses the edges $(1,1)(2,1)$, $(1,1)(1,2)$, $(2,1)(2,2)$; deleting column $1$ and joining $(1,2)$ to $(2,2)$ gives the same situation with one column less, and induction finishes. The same holds for a block of columns at the right end.

Case $i<j$. Columns $1,\dots,i-1$ contain no endpoint, so the path crosses into them through both cells of column $i$; as $u$ has only one path-edge, the path begins $u\to(r,i-1)\to\cdots\to(\bar r,i-1)\to(\bar r,i)$ (if $i=1$: simply $u\to(\bar r,1)$). Then it moves to $(\bar r,i+1)$. Suppose the path has covered exactly the columns before $c$, where $i<c<j$, and enters column $c$ at $(s,c)$. If it then moved right, the cell $(\bar s,c)$ could later be reached only from $(\bar s,c+1)$, so it would be an endpoint, which is impossible. So the path goes $(s,c)\to(\bar s,c)\to(\bar s,c+1)$: it zigzags, and enters column $j$ at a cell $(t,j)$ determined by $u$ and $j$. The end $v$ cannot be $(t,j)$ (the other cell of column $j$ would be left out), so $v=(\bar t,j)$, and by the useful fact the path finishes along row $t$ to column $n$ and back along row $\bar t$ to $v$.

So for each start $u$ and each column $j>i$ there is exactly one valid path ending in column $j$; by left-right symmetry the same holds for $j<i$. This gives $2n\cdot(n-1)$ paths.

Case $i=j$. Then $u,v$ are the two cells of column $i$. If $1<i<n$, both the block to the left and the block to the right of column $i$ need both edges joining them to column $i$, so $u$ would need two path-edges: impossible. If $i=1$ or $i=n$, the useful fact gives exactly one path for each order of the two endpoints: $4$ paths.

Hence the number of placements is $2n(n-1)+4=2\cdot100\cdot99+4=\boxed{19804}$.

Problem 13

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Periodic and Cyclic Behavior Modulo $n$

An integer $x_0$ is chosen uniformly at random from $\{1,2,\dots,2025\}$. For each integer $j\ge 0$, define $x_{j+1}$ to be the least positive residue of $45x_j$ modulo $2026$. Suppose the probability that $x_0<x_1<x_2$ is $\frac mn$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. Find $m+n$.

Show the answer

$2531$

Show the solution

Solution (Multiplication by 45 is a quarter turn)

The key fact is $45^2=2025\equiv-1\pmod{2026}$. So $x_2\equiv-x_0$, i.e. $x_2=2026-x_0$, and the map $T(x)=45x\bmod 2026$ permutes $\{1,\dots,2025\}$ with $T^4=\mathrm{id}$. Write $\bar x=2026-x$.

$T$ fixes $1013$ ($45\cdot1013=22\cdot2026+1013$). For any other $x$, the points $x,T(x),\bar x,\overline{T(x)}$ are distinct: $T(x)=x$ or $T(x)=\bar x$ would mean $2026\mid44x$ or $2026\mid 46x$, i.e. $1013\mid 11x$ or $1013\mid 23x$, so $x=1013$ ($1013$ is prime). So the other $2024$ numbers split into $506$ orbits $\{x,y,\bar x,\bar y\}$ with $T:x\mapsto y\mapsto\bar x\mapsto\bar y\mapsto x$. In each orbit let $p=\min(x,\bar x)<1013$ and $q=\min(y,\bar y)<1013$; then $p\neq q$, and the orbit is $\{p,\bar p,q,\bar q\}$.

The condition $x_0<x_1<x_2=\bar x_0$ forces $x_0<1013$, so $x_0\in\{p,q\}$. If $x_0=p$, then $x_1\in\{q,\bar q\}$ and the condition is $p<x_1<\bar p$, which holds iff $p<q$. If $x_0=q$, it holds iff $q<p$. So exactly one starting value per orbit works, and $1013$ fails. The probability is $\frac{506}{2025}$, already in lowest terms ($506=2\cdot11\cdot23$, $2025=3^45^2$), and $m+n=\boxed{2531}$.

Problem 14

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Complex Factorization and Algebraic Cleanup

For each nonzero complex number $z$, define the bidirectional geometric sequence $S_z=(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb Z}$ by \[a_0=z,\qquad a_{n+1}=(1+i)a_n\ \text{ for all integers } n .\] For example, $S_1$ contains the values \[1,\quad 1+i,\quad 2i,\quad -2+2i,\quad -4\] at indices $0,1,2,3,4$, respectively. Moreover, $S_{1+i}$ contains exactly the same values as $S_1$, but at different indices.

There exists a set of nonzero complex numbers $\{z_1,z_2,\dots,z_k\}$ such that, for all integers $a,b$ satisfying $1\le a^2+b^2\le 36$, the complex number $a+bi$ occurs in at least one of $S_{z_1},S_{z_2},\dots,S_{z_k}$. Compute the smallest positive integer $k$ for which such a set exists.

Show the answer

$52$

Show the solution

Solution (Odd parts of Gaussian integers)

Call $a+bi$ (with $a,b$ integers) a lattice point. Since $\frac{a+bi}{1+i}=\frac{(a+b)+(b-a)i}{2}$, the number $1+i$ divides $a+bi$ (with lattice quotient) iff $a+b$ is even. Call a lattice point odd if $a+b$ is odd. Every nonzero lattice point can be written uniquely as $w=(1+i)^e o$ with $e\ge0$ and $o$ odd: divide by $1+i$ as long as possible (the absolute value drops by $\sqrt2$ each time, so this stops). Call $o$ the odd part of $w$.

Two lattice points lie in a common $S_z$ iff they have the same odd part. If $w_1,w_2\in S_z$, then $w_2=(1+i)^m w_1$ for some integer $m$, say $m\ge0$ (otherwise swap them); then $w_2=(1+i)^{e_1+m}o_1$ with $o_1$ odd, so $o_2=o_1$. Conversely, if both have odd part $o$, both lie in $S_o$.

So each $S_{z_t}$ covers lattice points of at most one odd part, and $S_o$ covers all points with odd part $o$. Hence the minimum $k$ is the number of different odd parts of the lattice points with $1\le a^2+b^2\le36$. The odd part $o$ of such a point satisfies $|o|\le|w|\le6$, so it is an odd point of the same disc; and every odd point of the disc is its own odd part. Therefore $k$ is the number of lattice points with $a+b$ odd and $a^2+b^2\le36$. Counting by $|a|$: \[\begin{array}{c|ccccccc} |a|&0&1&2&3&4&5&6\\\hline \text{allowed } b&\pm1,\pm3,\pm5&0,\pm2,\pm4&\pm1,\pm3,\pm5&0,\pm2,\pm4&\pm1,\pm3&0,\pm2&\text{none}\\ \text{points}&6&2\cdot5&2\cdot6&2\cdot5&2\cdot4&2\cdot3&0 \end{array}\] The total is $6+10+12+10+8+6=\boxed{52}$.

Problem 15

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Closed Forms and Hidden Structure

For a real number $x$, define a sequence $a_0,a_1,\dots$ by \[a_0=x,\qquad a_{n+1}=a_n^2-2\quad(n\ge 0).\] Let $S$ be the set of all real numbers $x$ such that $a_5=a_0$ but $a_1\neq a_0$. Find $\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S}x^2$.

Show the answer

$59$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Cosine substitution and root-of-unity sums)

Only $|x|\le2$ matters. If $a_n>2$ then $a_{n+1}-a_n=(a_n-2)(a_n+1)>0$ and $a_{n+1}>2$. If $|x|>2$, then $a_1=x^2-2>2$ and $a_1>x$ (for $x>2$ since $(x-2)(x+1)>0$; for $x<-2$ trivially), so $a_5\ge a_1>a_0$. So every $x\in S$ has $|x|\le 2$.

Write $x=2\cos\theta$ with $\theta\in[0,\pi]$. Since $(2\cos\phi)^2-2=2\cos2\phi$, we get $a_n=2\cos(2^n\theta)$. The condition $a_5=a_0$ is $\cos32\theta=\cos\theta$, i.e. $32\theta\equiv\pm\theta\pmod{2\pi}$, i.e. $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{31}$ or $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{33}$. In $[0,\pi]$ these are $\frac{2\pi k}{31}$ ($0\le k\le15$) and $\frac{2\pi k}{33}$ ($0\le k\le16$), and they coincide only at $\theta=0$. The condition $a_1\neq a_0$ excludes the solutions of $\cos2\theta=\cos\theta$, namely $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\frac{2\pi}{3}=\frac{2\pi\cdot11}{33}$. As $\cos$ is injective on $[0,\pi]$, $S$ consists of $2\cos\theta$ for $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{31}$, $1\le k\le15$, and $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{33}$, $1\le k\le16$, $k\ne11$: $30$ numbers.

Now $x^2=4\cos^2\theta=2+2\cos2\theta$. For an odd modulus $N$, the residues $\pm2k$ ($1\le k\le\frac{N-1}2$) are all the nonzero residues mod $N$, so \[\sum_{k=1}^{(N-1)/2}\cos\frac{4\pi k}{N}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\cos\frac{2\pi j}{N}=-\frac12 .\] For $N=31$: $\sum(2+2\cos2\theta)=30-1=29$. For $N=33$ over $1\le k\le16$: $32-1=31$, minus the term $k=11$, which is $2+2\cos\frac{4\pi}{3}=1$: this gives $30$. Hence $\sum_{x\in S}x^2=29+30=\boxed{59}$.

Solution 2 (Vieta on the degree-32 polynomial)

By induction, for every $n\ge1$, $a_n$ is a polynomial in $x$ of the form $a_n=x^{2^n}-2^nx^{2^n-2}+(\text{lower terms})$: for $n=1$, $a_1=x^2-2$, and if this holds for $n$, then $a_n^2-2=x^{2^{n+1}}-2\cdot2^nx^{2^{n+1}-2}+\cdots$. So \[Q(x)=a_5-x=x^{32}+0\cdot x^{31}-32x^{30}+\cdots .\]

All roots of $Q$ are real and distinct. For $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ and $x=2\cos\theta$ we have $a_5=2\cos32\theta$ (from $(2\cos\phi)^2-2=2\cos2\phi$), so $Q(2\cos\theta)=0$ whenever $32\theta\equiv\pm\theta\pmod{2\pi}$, i.e. for $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{31}$ ($0\le k\le15$) and $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{33}$ ($0\le k\le16$). These are $16+17-1=32$ different angles in $[0,\pi]$ (only $\theta=0$ is repeated), giving $32$ different real roots, hence all roots of $Q$, each simple.

By Vieta, the sum of the squares of the roots is $0^2-2(-32)=64$. The set $S$ consists of the roots with $a_1\neq a_0$, i.e. all roots except those of $x^2-2=x$, namely $x=2$ and $x=-1$ (both are roots of $Q$, being fixed points). Therefore \[\sum_{x\in S}x^2=64-2^2-(-1)^2=\boxed{59}.\]

Problem 4

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Let $AB$ be a diameter of a unit circle $\omega_1$ centered at $O$. Circles $\omega_2$ and $\omega_3$ lie inside $\omega_1$ and are each tangent to both $\omega_1$ and $AB$. Suppose that both $O$ and the center of $\omega_3$ lie on $\omega_2$. Find the radius of $\omega_3$.

Show the answer

$\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates of the centres)

Put $O=(0,0)$ and $AB$ on the $x$-axis. A circle of radius $r$ inside $\omega_1$, tangent to $AB$ and to $\omega_1$, has centre $(h,\pm r)$ with distance $1-r$ from $O$ (internal tangency), so \[h^2+r^2=(1-r)^2,\qquad h^2=1-2r .\] For $\omega_2$, $O$ lies on it, so its centre is at distance $r_2$ from $O$: $h_2^2+r_2^2=r_2^2$, hence $h_2=0$ and $r_2=\frac12$; $\omega_2$ has centre $\left(0,\frac12\right)$, say.

Let $\omega_3$ have centre $(h,\pm r)$ with $h^2=1-2r$. It lies on $\omega_2$: $h^2+\left(\pm r-\frac12\right)^2=\frac14$. With the sign $+$: $1-2r+r^2-r=0$, i.e. $r^2-3r+1=0$, so $r=\frac{3-\sqrt5}2$ (the other root exceeds $1$). With the sign $-$: $1-2r+r^2+r=0$, i.e. $r^2-r+1=0$, which has no real root. Hence the radius of $\omega_3$ is $\boxed{\dfrac{3-\sqrt5}{2}}$.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{10}$ be positive real numbers satisfying $a_1a_2\cdots a_{10}=1$. Find the minimum possible value of \[a_1+a_2^2+a_3^2+a_4^3+a_5^3+a_6^3+a_7^4+a_8^4+a_9^4+a_{10}^4 .\]

Show the answer

$4\sqrt[4]{24}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Weighted AM-GM)

Let $e_i$ be the exponent of $a_i$ in the sum, so $(e_1,\dots,e_{10})=(1,2,2,3,3,3,4,4,4,4)$ and $\sum_i\frac1{e_i}=1+1+1+1=4$. Apply weighted AM-GM to the numbers $e_ia_i^{e_i}$ with weights $\frac{1}{4e_i}$ (which add up to $1$): \[\sum_i a_i^{e_i}=4\sum_i\frac{1}{4e_i}\left(e_ia_i^{e_i}\right)\ge4\prod_i\left(e_ia_i^{e_i}\right)^{1/(4e_i)}=4\Bigl(\prod_ie_i^{1/e_i}\Bigr)^{1/4}\Bigl(\prod_i a_i\Bigr)^{1/4}.\] Here $\prod_ie_i^{1/e_i}=1\cdot(2^{1/2})^2(3^{1/3})^3(4^{1/4})^4=24$ and $\prod a_i=1$, so the sum is at least $4\sqrt[4]{24}$. Equality holds when all $e_ia_i^{e_i}$ are equal to $\lambda=\sqrt[4]{24}$, i.e. $a_i=(\lambda/e_i)^{1/e_i}$; then $\prod a_i=\lambda^4/24=1$, so this choice is allowed. The minimum is $\boxed{4\sqrt[4]{24}}$.

Solution 2 (Tangent line to the exponential)

Let $e_i$ be the exponent of $a_i$ in the sum and $\lambda=\sqrt[4]{24}$. Choose $c_i>0$ with $c_i^{e_i}=\lambda/e_i$; then $\prod c_i=\lambda^{\sum 1/e_i}\big/\prod e_i^{1/e_i}=\lambda^4/24=1$.

Using $e^t\ge1+t$ with $t=e_i\ln(a_i/c_i)$, \[a_i^{e_i}=c_i^{e_i}\left(\frac{a_i}{c_i}\right)^{e_i}\ge\frac{\lambda}{e_i}\left(1+e_i\ln\frac{a_i}{c_i}\right)=\frac{\lambda}{e_i}+\lambda\ln\frac{a_i}{c_i}.\] Summing over $i$, \[\sum_ia_i^{e_i}\ge\lambda\sum_i\frac1{e_i}+\lambda\ln\frac{\prod a_i}{\prod c_i}=4\lambda+\lambda\ln 1=4\sqrt[4]{24},\] with equality when $a_i=c_i$ for all $i$. The minimum is $\boxed{4\sqrt[4]{24}}$.

Problem 7

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 27: Base Systems and Positional Arithmetic · Digit Patterns Across Bases

Call an integer $n\ge 2$ Ithacan if, for every nonnegative integer $k$ such that $2^{k+2}\le n$, \[\binom{n}{2^k}+\binom{n}{2^{k+1}}+\binom{n}{2^{k+2}}\equiv 1\pmod 2 .\] For example, $4$ is Ithacan, since the only possible value of $k$ is $0$, and \[\binom41+\binom42+\binom44=4+6+1=11\equiv 1\pmod 2 .\] Find the sum of all Ithacan integers less than or equal to $63$.

Show the answer

$188$

Show the solution

Solution (Lucas's theorem in base 2)

Write $n=\sum_k b_k2^k$ in binary, with $L$ digits ($2^{L-1}\le n<2^L$). By Lucas's theorem, $\binom{n}{2^k}\equiv\binom{b_k}{1}=b_k\pmod2$. (Directly: modulo $2$, repeated squaring gives $(1+x)^{2^j}\equiv1+x^{2^j}$, so $(1+x)^n\equiv\prod_{j:\,b_j=1}(1+x^{2^j})$, and by uniqueness of binary expansion the coefficient of $x^{2^k}$ in this product is $b_k$.) The condition $2^{k+2}\le n$ means $k+2\le L-1$. So $n$ is Ithacan iff \[b_k+b_{k+1}+b_{k+2}\ \text{is odd for } 0\le k\le L-3 .\]

  • If $L\le2$ ($n=2,3$) there is no condition: both are Ithacan.

  • If $L\ge3$, subtracting consecutive conditions gives $b_{k+3}=b_k$, so the digits repeat with period $3$, and the block $(b_0,b_1,b_2)$ has odd weight: it is $111$ or has a single $1$.

Block $111$: $n=2^L-1$, giving $7,15,31,63$. A single $1$ in the positions $\equiv\rho\pmod 3$: the top digit $b_{L-1}=1$ forces $\rho\equiv L-1$, so for $L=3,4,5,6$ we get $n=100_2=4$, $1001_2=9$, $10010_2=18$, $100100_2=36$. The Ithacan numbers up to $63$ are $2,3,4,7,9,15,18,31,36,63$, with sum $\boxed{188}$.

Problem 8

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Density and Averaging Arguments

A necklace consists of $2026$ blue beads and some red beads, arranged in a circle. Every block of consecutive beads that contains exactly $8$ blue beads contains at least $5$ red beads. What is the smallest possible number of red beads on the necklace?

Show the answer

$1448$

Show the solution

Solution (Averaging over windows of gaps)

Number the blue beads $1,\dots,2026$ around the circle and let $g_i\ge0$ be the number of red beads between blue bead $i$ and blue bead $i+1$ (indices mod $2026$). A block with exactly $8$ blue beads contains the $7$ gaps between its first and last blue bead, and the block running exactly from blue bead $i$ to blue bead $i+7$ contains nothing else. So the condition is \[g_i+g_{i+1}+\dots+g_{i+6}\ge5\quad\text{for every } i .\]

Lower bound. Adding these $2026$ inequalities, every gap is counted $7$ times: $7R\ge5\cdot2026$, where $R=\sum g_i$. So $R\ge\frac{10130}{7}>1447$, i.e. $R\ge1448$.

Construction. Make $578$ gaps equal to $0$ and the other $1448$ equal to $1$, placing the zero gaps around the circle so that the distances between consecutive zero gaps are $3$ and $4$ alternately, except that three of the $3$s are replaced by $4$s: $286$ distances $3$ and $292$ distances $4$, total $858+1168=2026$. Any two consecutive distances add up to at least $7$, so any $7$ consecutive gaps contain at most $2$ zero gaps, hence at least $5$ ones. The minimum is $\boxed{1448}$.

Problem 9

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

Determine the smallest integer $n\ge 4$ such that, among any $n$ distinct positive integers relatively prime to $13$, one can choose four pairwise distinct numbers $a,b,c,d$ satisfying \[13\mid ad-bc .\]

Show the answer

$8$

Show the solution

Solution (Discrete logarithms and pair sums)

The powers of $2$ modulo $13$ are $2,4,8,3,6,12,11,9,5,10,7,1$, so $2$ is a primitive root and every integer coprime to $13$ is $\equiv2^{e}$ for a unique $e\in\mathbb Z_{12}$, its exponent. Then $13\mid ad-bc$ iff $e_a+e_d\equiv e_b+e_c\pmod{12}$. So we need two disjoint pairs among our numbers with equal exponent sums.

Any $8$ numbers work. (1) If some exponent occurs at least $4$ times, take four such numbers. (2) If two different exponents $x\ne y$ each occur at least twice, take $a,c$ with exponent $x$ and $b,d$ with exponent $y$: $e_a+e_d=x+y=e_b+e_c$. (3) Otherwise at most one exponent repeats, at most $3$ times, so there are at least $6$ different exponents. Pick one number for each of six different exponents. They form $\binom62=15$ pairs, but there are only $12$ possible sums mod $12$, so two pairs have equal sums. They cannot share a number (the other two numbers would then have equal exponents), so they give four distinct numbers.

Seven numbers may fail. Take $1,14,27,2,4,3,11$, with exponents $0,0,0,1,2,4,7$. The sums of two of them are: $0+0=0$; $0+x=1,2,4,7$; and the sums of two of $1,2,4,7$: $3,5,8,6,9,11$. These eleven values are all different mod $12$. Two disjoint pairs with equal sums would therefore be of the same kind with the same values: $\{0,0\}$ twice needs four zeros, $\{0,x\}$ twice needs $x$ twice, $\{x,y\}$ twice needs two copies of $x$; none is available. So no such $a,b,c,d$ exist.

The answer is $n=\boxed{8}$.

Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

A nonempty finite set $A$ of positive integers is called quadratic if the sum of its elements equals the square of the number of its elements. For example, $\{1,2,3,10\}$ is quadratic, since it is a set with $4$ elements and $1+2+3+10=4^2$. Recall that the elements of a set must be distinct.

  • (a) Give an example of a quadratic set with $2026$ elements.

  • (b) Prove that every quadratic set contains at least one odd element.

  • (c) Prove that any two quadratic sets with the same number of elements must have at least one element in common.

Show the answer

proof; for (a) e.g. $\{1,2,\dots,2025,2053351\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Smallest possible sums)

Throughout, the key fact is that $k$ distinct positive integers have sum at least $1+2+\dots+k=\frac{k(k+1)}2$.

(a) Take $A=\{1,2,\dots,2025,N\}$ with $N=2026^2-\frac{2025\cdot2026}{2}=4104676-2051325=2053351$. Since $N>2025$, the $2026$ elements are distinct, and their sum is $2026^2$. So $A$ is quadratic.

(b) Let $A$ have $n$ elements. If all of them were even, they would be $n$ distinct even positive integers, with sum at least $2+4+\dots+2n=n(n+1)>n^2$. So a quadratic set has an odd element.

(c) Suppose $A$ and $B$ are quadratic sets with $n$ elements each and no common element. Then $A\cup B$ consists of $2n$ distinct positive integers with sum $n^2+n^2=2n^2$. But such a sum is at least $\frac{2n(2n+1)}{2}=2n^2+n>2n^2$, a contradiction. Hence $A$ and $B$ share an element.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Let $a$, $b$, and $c$ be positive integers. Determine all triples $(a,b,c)$ for which \[(b^2-c^2)^2=a^2+b+3 .\] Justify your answer.

Show the answer

$(a,b,c)=(2,2,1)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squeezing between consecutive squares)

Let $D=|b^2-c^2|$, a nonnegative integer with $D^2=a^2+b+3$. Then $D^2>a^2$, so $D\ge a+1$ and $a^2+b+3=D^2\ge a^2+2a+1$, which gives $2a\le b+2$. Therefore \[D^2=a^2+b+3\le\frac{(b+2)^2}{4}+b+3=\frac{(b+4)^2}{4},\qquad D\le\frac{b+4}{2}.\] On the other hand $D>0$, so $b\ne c$ and $D=|b-c|(b+c)\ge b+c\ge b+1$. Hence $b+1\le\frac{b+4}2$, i.e. $b\le2$.

  • $b=1$: $c\ge2$ and $D=c^2-1\ge3>\frac52$, impossible.

  • $b=2$: $D\le3$. For $c=1$, $D=3$ and $9=a^2+5$ gives $a=2$. For $c\ge3$, $D\ge5$, impossible.

Check: $(4-1)^2=9=2^2+2+3$. The only solution is $\boxed{(a,b,c)=(2,2,1)}$.

Solution 2 (Factoring a difference of squares)

Let $D=|b^2-c^2|$. The equation reads $(D-a)(D+a)=b+3>0$, so $D>a$ and $D-a\ge1$. Since $D\ne0$ we have $b\ne c$, and $D=|b-c|(b+c)\ge b+c\ge b+1$.

If $D-a\ge2$, then $b+3=(D-a)(D+a)\ge2(D+a)\ge2D\ge 2b+2$, so $b\le1$. Then $b=1$, $c\ge2$, $D=c^2-1\ge3$, and $4=(D-a)(D+a)\ge 2(D+a)\ge 8$, a contradiction. So $D-a=1$, and then $D+a=b+3$, i.e. $2D=b+4$. Combined with $D\ge b+1$ this gives $b+4\ge2b+2$, i.e. $b\le2$, and $b$ must be even, so $b=2$ and $D=3$. Then $|4-c^2|=3$ forces $c=1$, and $a=D-1=2$.

The only solution is $\boxed{(a,b,c)=(2,2,1)}$, which indeed satisfies $(4-1)^2=9=4+2+3$.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

The Cornellia school district is a circular region $15$ miles in diameter. Students may live anywhere in the district, and elementary schools may be built anywhere in it, including on its boundary.

The district wants every student to live within $5$ miles of at least one school. Distances are measured in straight lines.

  • (a) Show how to place $8$ elementary schools so that every student lives within $5$ miles of a school.

  • (b) Show that $6$ elementary schools suffice to provide the same guarantee.

  • (c) What is the smallest number of elementary schools needed to provide this guarantee? Exhibit a placement that works for this number of schools and prove that fewer schools cannot suffice. Depending on your approach, you may find it helpful to know $\cos 36^\circ=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{4}$.

Show the answer

$5$

Show the solution

Solution (Five sectors, and arcs of the boundary)

Let $O$ be the centre, so the district is the disc of radius $\frac{15}{2}$ about $O$, with boundary circle $\Gamma$. A school covers the closed disc of radius $5$ around it.

Five schools suffice. Place schools $S_1,\dots,S_5$ at distance $5$ from $O$, in the directions $90^\circ+72^\circ k$. Cut the district into five sectors of angle $72^\circ$, each centred on the direction of one school. We show that $S_1$ covers its sector; by symmetry the same holds for the others. The disc around $S_1$ contains $O$ (as $OS_1=5$). Let $Q$ be a point of the arc of the sector, at angle $\varphi$ from $OS_1$ with $|\varphi|\le36^\circ$. By the law of cosines, \[S_1Q^2=\tfrac{225}{4}+25-75\cos\varphi\le\tfrac{325}{4}-75\cos36^\circ=\tfrac{325}{4}-\tfrac{75(1+\sqrt5)}{4}=\tfrac{250-75\sqrt5}{4}\le25,\] the last step because $75\sqrt5\ge150$, i.e. $\sqrt5\ge2$. So the disc contains $O$ and every point $Q$ of the arc; being convex, it contains every segment $OQ$, i.e. the whole sector. Hence five schools suffice. This answers (a) and (b) as well: add any $3$ (or $1$) further schools.

Four schools do not suffice. A disc of radius $5$ meets the circle $\Gamma$ in an arc (possibly empty), and any two points of this arc are at most $10$ apart. Such an arc has angle $\alpha<180^\circ$, since otherwise it would contain two diametrically opposite points of $\Gamma$, which are $15$ apart. A chord of $\Gamma$ subtending a central angle $\alpha\le180^\circ$ has length $15\sin\frac\alpha2$; applied to the endpoints of the arc, this gives $\sin\frac\alpha2\le\frac{10}{15}=\frac23<\frac{\sqrt2}2$, i.e. $\alpha<90^\circ$. Four such arcs cover less than $360^\circ$ of $\Gamma$, so some point $Q$ of $\Gamma$ is more than $5$ miles from every school. The uncovered set is open, so points of the district near $Q$, where students live, are uncovered too.

The smallest number of schools is $\boxed{5}$.

Problem 4

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry · Spiral Similarity Centers

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral with $\angle ABC=\angle ADC$. Let $M$ and $N$ lie in the interiors of sides $AB$ and $AD$, respectively, such that $\angle MCD=\angle NCB$.

Suppose that the circumcircles of triangles $AMN$ and $ABD$ intersect at $A$ and at a second point $K\neq A$. Prove that $AK\perp CK$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two similarities that agree on line AB)

Let $X$ and $Y$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $C$ to the lines $AB$ and $AD$, and let $\Omega$ be the circle with diameter $AC$; it passes through $X$ and $Y$ (a foot may coincide with $A$). The points $X,Y,C$ are distinct: $C$ is not on line $AB$ or line $AD$, and $X=Y$ would force $X=Y=A$, with $CA$ perpendicular to both $AB$ and $AD$. So $\Omega$ is the circle through $C$, $X$, $Y$. We use directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: a mirror similarity centred at $C$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in the bisector of $\angle BCD$ followed by the dilation about $C$ with ratio $\frac{CD}{CB}$. Then $\sigma(B)=D$. Since $ABCD$ is convex, $A$ lies on the same side of $BC$ as $D$ and on the same side of $CD$ as $B$, and $\sigma$ exchanges these two sides. As $\angle CBA=\angle CDA$, $\sigma$ maps the ray $BA$ to the ray $DA$, so $\sigma$ maps line $AB$ onto line $AD$, and it maps the foot $X$ to the foot $Y$. Next, the rays $CM$ and $CN$ lie inside $\angle BCD$, and \[\angle BCM=\angle BCD-\angle MCD=\angle BCD-\angle NCB=\angle DCN .\] The image of ray $CM$ under $\sigma$ makes the angle $\angle BCM$ with $CD$ inside $\angle BCD$, so it is the ray $CN$. Hence $\sigma(M)$, which lies on line $AD$ and on ray $CN$, is $N$.

Step 2: the spiral similarity centred at $K$. The point $K$ is not on line $AB$: this line meets the circle $ABD$ only at $A,B$ and the circle $AMN$ only at $A,M$, and $B\ne M$. Likewise $K$ is not on line $AD$. Since $A,B,D,K$ are concyclic and $A,M,N,K$ are concyclic, \begin{align*} \angle(BK,BM)&=\angle(BK,BA)=\angle(DK,DA)=\angle(DK,DN),\\ \angle(MK,MB)&=\angle(MK,MA)=\angle(NK,NA)=\angle(NK,ND). \end{align*} So triangles $KBM$ and $KDN$ are directly similar, and the spiral similarity $\tau$ with centre $K$ taking $B$ to $D$ takes $M$ to $N$; it maps line $AB$ onto line $AD$.

Step 3: $\tau(X)=Y$. Restricted to line $AB$, both $\sigma$ and $\tau$ are maps onto line $AD$ that multiply all distances by a fixed factor; such a map is determined by the images of two distinct points. They agree at $B$ and $M$, so they agree on all of line $AB$; in particular $\tau(X)=\sigma(X)=Y$.

Step 4: $K$ lies on $\Omega$. A spiral similarity turns every line through the same angle $\theta$. Since $K$ is not on lines $AB$, $AD$, we have $K\ne X,Y$, and $\tau$ maps line $KX$ to line $KY$, so $\angle(KX,KY)=\theta$. Also $\tau$ maps line $CX$, the perpendicular to $AB$ at $X$, to the perpendicular to $\tau(AB)=AD$ at $\tau(X)=Y$, which is line $CY$; so $\angle(CX,CY)=\theta$. Hence $\angle(KX,KY)=\angle(CX,CY)$, and $K$ lies on the circle through $C,X,Y$, which is $\Omega$.

Step 5: $K\ne C$, and the conclusion. Suppose $K=C$. Then $C$ lies on the circle $ABD$, so $\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ$ and both angles are $90^\circ$. In the right triangle $MBC$, $\angle BMC<90^\circ$, so $\angle AMC>90^\circ$; likewise $\angle ANC>90^\circ$. But $C$ also lies on the circle $AMN$, and $M,N$ are on opposite sides of $AC$ (as $B,D$ are), so $AMCN$ is a cyclic quadrilateral with $\angle AMC+\angle ANC=180^\circ$, a contradiction. So $K\ne C$, and also $K\ne A$. The angle $\angle AKC$ is inscribed in a semicircle of $\Omega$, so $\angle AKC=90^\circ$, that is, $AK\perp CK$.

Solution 2 (Inversion at A and two projections)

We use position vectors with origin $A$; $P\cdot Q$ is the dot product and $|P|$ the length of $P$.

Step 1: two similar triangles. Since $ABCD$ is convex and $M$, $N$ are interior points of the sides $AB$, $AD$, the rays $CM$ and $CN$ lie inside the angle $BCD$. Hence \[\angle BCM=\angle BCD-\angle MCD=\angle BCD-\angle NCB=\angle DCN .\] As $M$ lies on the segment $AB$, the ray $BM$ is the ray $BA$, so $\angle CBM=\angle CBA=\angle CDA=\angle CDN$. Therefore triangles $BCM$ and $DCN$ are similar ($B\leftrightarrow D$, $C\leftrightarrow C$, $M\leftrightarrow N$), and \[\frac{BM}{BC}=\frac{DN}{DC}=\lambda\qquad\text{for some }\lambda>0 .\]

Step 2: notation and two projections. Let $u$ and $v$ be the unit vectors in the directions of $AB$ and $AD$; they are not parallel, since $A$, $B$, $D$ are not collinear. Write $B=bu$, $D=dv$, $M=mu$, $N=nv$ with $0<m<b$ and $0<n<d$, so $BM=b-m$, $DN=d-n$, and by Step 1, $BC=\frac{b-m}{\lambda}$, $DC=\frac{d-n}{\lambda}$. Let $\gamma=\angle ABC=\angle ADC$. The angle at $B$ between the direction $-u$ (towards $A$) and $C-B$ is $\gamma$, so $(C-B)\cdot(-u)=BC\cos\gamma$, that is, \[C\cdot u=b-BC\cos\gamma=b-\frac{(b-m)\cos\gamma}{\lambda},\quad\text{and likewise}\quad C\cdot v=d-\frac{(d-n)\cos\gamma}{\lambda}.\]

Step 3: inversion. Consider the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $1$, which sends each point $P\ne A$ to $P'=\frac{P}{|P|^2}$. A circle through $A$ with centre $O$ has equation $|P|^2=2\,O\cdot P$; dividing by $|P|^2$ shows that its points $P\ne A$ go to the points of the line $2\,O\cdot P'=1$. So the circle $ABD$ is sent to the line through $B'=\frac ub$ and $D'=\frac vd$, and the circle $AMN$ to the line through $M'=\frac um$ and $N'=\frac vn$. Since $K\ne A$ lies on both circles, $K'$ lies on both lines: \[K'=r\,\frac ub+(1-r)\,\frac vd=s\,\frac um+(1-s)\,\frac vn\qquad\text{for some real }r,s .\] As $u$, $v$ are not parallel, the coefficients agree: $\frac rb=\frac sm$ and $\frac{1-r}d=\frac{1-s}n$. So $s=\frac{rm}b$ and $1-s=\frac{(1-r)n}d$; adding, $\frac{rm}b+\frac{(1-r)n}d=1$. Subtracting this from $r+(1-r)=1$ gives \[\frac{r(b-m)}b+\frac{(1-r)(d-n)}d=0. \tag{1}\]

Step 4: $K'\cdot C=1$. By Step 2 and (1), \begin{align*}K'\cdot C&=\frac rb\,C\cdot u+\frac{1-r}d\,C\cdot v\\&=r+(1-r)-\frac{\cos\gamma}{\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{r(b-m)}b+\frac{(1-r)(d-n)}d\Bigr)=1 .\end{align*} (Nothing is divided by $\cos\gamma$, so the case $\gamma=90^\circ$ is included.) Since $K'=\frac K{|K|^2}$, this says $K\cdot C=|K|^2$, that is, \[(A-K)\cdot(C-K)=(-K)\cdot(C-K)=|K|^2-K\cdot C=0 .\]

Step 5: $K\ne C$, and the conclusion. Suppose $K=C$. Then $C$ lies on the circle $ABD$, so $ABCD$ is cyclic, $\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ$ and $\gamma=90^\circ$. In the right triangle $MBC$ the angle $\angle BMC$ is acute, so $\angle AMC>90^\circ$; likewise $\angle ANC>90^\circ$. But $C$ also lies on the circle $AMN$, and $M$, $N$ lie on opposite sides of $AC$ (as $B$, $D$ do), so $AMCN$ is a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle and $\angle AMC+\angle ANC=180^\circ$, a contradiction. So $K\ne C$, and $K\ne A$ is given. The vectors $A-K$ and $C-K$ are therefore nonzero with dot product $0$, which means $AK\perp CK$.
