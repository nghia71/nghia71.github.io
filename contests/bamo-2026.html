BAMO-8 Problem A

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry · Tilings, Boards, and Lattice Arrangements

Let $\mathcal{T}=ABCD$ be an isosceles trapezoid with side lengths $1$, $1$, $1$, and $2$. (“Isosceles” means that angles $A$ and $B$ in the figure below are equal.)

  • (a) Draw a diagram that illustrates how to dissect $\mathcal{T}$ into four congruent trapezoids, each similar to $\mathcal{T}$. In other words, we divide $\mathcal{T}$ into four congruent trapezoids that do not overlap and have no gaps between them, and each is a scaled-down version of $\mathcal{T}$.

  • (b) Do the same as (a), but now dissect $\mathcal{T}$ into nine congruent trapezoids, each similar to $\mathcal{T}$.

Draw your diagrams carefully, so that it is clear how your dissections work.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the midpoint of the longer base and connect it to both upper vertices. Look for triangles with all three sides the same length.
Hint 2: key idea
Think of the trapezoid as a row of three equilateral triangles with alternating orientations. A row of three smaller triangles arranged in the same way gives a similar trapezoid. (LPS VI, Chapter 50: Combinatorial Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Refine the triangle grid by halving the edges for four pieces and by trisecting them for nine, and count how many small cells each piece must use. Look for groupings in which every piece is a row of three cells with alternating orientations, starting with the corner cells, which fit into only a few such rows. Check coverage by recording which piece owns each small triangle.
Common trap
Each group must form one trapezoid similar to the original, even if two different shapes would have the same area.
Show the answer

construction (see the figure)

Show the solution – Cutting along a triangular grid

Place $\mathcal{T}$ with $A=(0,0)$, $B=(2,0)$. The legs have length $1$ and the top has length $1$, so the top is centred over $AB$: $D=\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bigr)$, $C=\bigl(\tfrac32,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bigr)$, and $\angle A=\angle B=60^\circ$. The midpoint $E=(1,0)$ of $AB$ satisfies $EA=ED=EC=EB=1$, so $\mathcal{T}$ is made of three equilateral triangles $ADE$, $EDC$, $ECB$ of side $1$: it is half of a regular hexagon.

The building block. A trapezoid made of three equilateral triangles of side $s$ placed in a row (up, down, up, or down, up, down) has sides $s,s,s,2s$ and angles $60^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ,120^\circ$, so it is a copy of $\mathcal{T}$ scaled by $s$. Every trapezoid in the dissections below is such a row of three small triangles, so it is similar to $\mathcal{T}$, and all the pieces in one dissection use triangles of the same size, so they are congruent.

(a) Four pieces. Cover $\mathcal{T}$ with the grid of equilateral triangles of side $\tfrac12$; $\mathcal{T}$ consists of $3\cdot4=12$ of them, and each piece must use $3$. Let $M=\bigl(\tfrac12,0\bigr)$ and $N=\bigl(\tfrac32,0\bigr)$ on $AB$, and let $P=\bigl(\tfrac34,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\bigr)$ and $Q=\bigl(\tfrac54,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\bigr)$ on the midline. The four pieces are \[AMPD,\qquad MNQP,\qquad PQCD,\qquad NBCQ.\] Each has three sides of length $\tfrac12$ and one side of length $1$ ($AD$, $MN$, $DC$, $BC$ respectively), and each is a row of three triangles of side $\tfrac12$, so each is $\mathcal{T}$ scaled by $\tfrac12$ (left picture). Together they fill $\mathcal{T}$ without overlap, since they are unions of distinct cells of the grid and contain all $12$ cells.

(b) Nine pieces. Now use the grid of triangles of side $\tfrac13$. Each of the three unit triangles $ADE$, $EDC$, $ECB$ contains $9$ small triangles, and it can be cut into three pieces in a pinwheel: in triangle $ADE$, the first piece lies along $AE$ (from $A$, two thirds of the way to $E$), the second along $ED$ (from $E$, two thirds of the way to $D$) and the third along $DA$ (from $D$, two thirds of the way to $A$); the three pieces meet at the centre of the triangle. Each piece is a row of three small triangles, with sides $\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac23$. Doing the same in all three unit triangles gives nine congruent pieces, each $\mathcal{T}$ scaled by $\tfrac13$ (middle picture).

The dissection in (b) is not unique. For example, $ADCE$ is a rhombus of side $1$; cut it by two lines parallel to $AB$ into three strips, and cut each strip (a parallelogram of three upward and three downward small triangles) into one upright and one upside-down piece; the triangle $ECB$ is cut as a pinwheel (right picture).

BAMO-8 Problem B

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Bounding Integer Solutions

Suppose that

  • $a$ is a positive real number,

  • $b$ is the greatest integer less than or equal to $a$,

  • $c$ is the greatest integer less than or equal to $ab$, and

  • $ac=42$.

Is there any value of $a$ for which this occurs? If so, what are all such value(s), and how do you know there are no others?

(If you are familiar with “floor” notation, this problem is asking you to solve the equation $a\bigl\lfloor a\lfloor a\rfloor\bigr\rfloor=42$.)

Hint 1: understand the problem
Translate the description of $b$ into the interval of possible values of $a$ for a fixed $b$. Check what happens to $c$ if $a$ is less than one.
Hint 2: key idea
Multiply the bounds on $a$ by $b$ to bound $ab$, then use the fact that $c$ is an integer to bound $c$. Combining the bounds on $a$ and $c$ puts $ac$ between expressions involving only $b$. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Compare the lower and upper bounds on $ac$ with the required product to narrow down $b$. List the allowable integers $c$ for each surviving $b$ and use the product equation to get a candidate $a$ from each. Check both floor conditions for those candidates.
Common trap
The upper endpoint of the interval for $a$ is excluded even though its lower endpoint is included.
Show the answer

$a=\frac{42}{11}$

Show solution 1 – Trapping the integer part

If $0<a<1$ then $b=0$, so $ab=0$, $c=0$ and $ac=0\neq42$. Hence $a\ge1$ and $b\ge1$.

From $b\le a<b+1$ we get $b^2\le ab<b(b+1)$. Since $b^2$ is an integer not exceeding $ab$, the greatest such integer $c$ satisfies $c\ge b^2$; also $c\le ab<b(b+1)$, so $b^2\le c\le b^2+b-1$. Therefore \[42=ac\ge b\cdot b^2=b^3\qquad\text{and}\qquad 42=ac<(b+1)\cdot b(b+1)=b(b+1)^2 .\] The first inequality gives $b\le3$. For $b=1$ and $b=2$ the second would say $42<4$ and $42<18$, which is false. So $b=3$, and then $9\le c\le11$ with $a=\dfrac{42}{c}$:

  • $c=9$ gives $a=\tfrac{14}{3}>4$, contradicting $b=3$;

  • $c=10$ gives $a=4.2>4$, again a contradiction;

  • $c=11$ gives $a=\tfrac{42}{11}=3.81\ldots$, so $b=3$, $ab=\tfrac{126}{11}=11.45\ldots$, $c=11$ and $ac=42$. It works.

So there is exactly one value: $\boxed{a=\dfrac{42}{11}}$.

Show solution 2 – The left side is increasing

Let $f(a)=a\bigl\lfloor a\lfloor a\rfloor\bigr\rfloor$ for $a>0$. For $0<a<1$ we have $f(a)=0$. For $1\le a<a'$ we have $\lfloor a\rfloor\le\lfloor a'\rfloor$, hence $a\lfloor a\rfloor<a'\lfloor a'\rfloor$ and $1\le\lfloor a\lfloor a\rfloor\rfloor\le\lfloor a'\lfloor a'\rfloor\rfloor$. Multiplying by $a<a'$ gives $f(a)<f(a')$. So $f$ is strictly increasing on $[1,\infty)$, and the equation $f(a)=42$ has at most one solution.

Now locate it. $f(3)=3\cdot9=27<42$ and $f(4)=4\cdot16=64>42$, so any solution lies in $(3,4)$, where $f(a)=a\lfloor3a\rfloor$. On $\bigl[\tfrac{11}{3},4\bigr)$ we have $\lfloor 3a\rfloor=11$ and $f(a)=11a$, which equals $42$ at $a=\tfrac{42}{11}$; this lies in the interval because $\tfrac{11}{3}=\tfrac{121}{33}<\tfrac{126}{33}=\tfrac{42}{11}<4$.

Hence the only value is $\boxed{a=\dfrac{42}{11}}$.

BAMO-8 Problem C

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry · Scale Effects · also BAMO-12 Problem 1

Let $B$ be a brick (that is, a rectangular prism). This particular brick has the property that a single cut can split $B$ into two congruent bricks that are similar to $B$. In other words, these smaller bricks are scaled-down copies of the original brick. Find possible dimensions for $B$ (length, width, height) and explain your answer.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch a brick sliced into two smaller bricks and label the dimensions of both pieces. Think about what must agree in the two pieces if they are congruent.
Hint 2: key idea
Explain why the cut must be parallel to a pair of faces of the original brick, so it halves one edge length and leaves two unchanged. Use the volume ratio to find the relationship between the linear scale factor and the two volumes. (LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Order the original lengths as $x\le y\le z$ and write the scaled lengths in the same order. Since the smallest scaled length is smaller than every original length, identify which dimension of a half it represents and test which original edge could have been cut. Match the two uncut lengths with the remaining scaled lengths to get the needed proportions.
Common trap
Corresponding dimensions can change direction when one brick is rotated to match the other.
Show the answer

$1\times\sqrt[3]{2}\times\sqrt[3]{4}$ (or any multiple of it)

Show solution 1 – Which edge is halved

The cut is flat. The two pieces are bricks that do not overlap, and two convex solids with no common interior point can always be separated by a plane (the separating plane theorem for convex sets). Since together they fill $B$, each piece is exactly the part of $B$ on one side of that plane, so the cut is a plane cut. Each piece is a brick. Every face of a piece other than the cut face lies in a face of $B$, and the face of the piece opposite the cut face is parallel to it; so the cut is parallel to a pair of faces of $B$. Hence it crosses the edges of one direction, say of length $L$, at distance $t$ from one end, and the pieces have dimensions (the two other edges of $B$, $t$) and (the same two edges, $L-t$). Congruent pieces have the same volume, so $t=L-t$: the cut halves one dimension.

Let the dimensions be $x\le y\le z$ and let each half be $B$ scaled by $k$. Since half the volume is $k^3$ times the volume, $k^3=\tfrac12$, so $k<1$.

The halves have the dimensions $kx\le ky\le kz$. Two of the dimensions of a half are dimensions of $B$ that were not cut, so each of them is at least $x$; but $kx<x$. Hence the smallest dimension $kx$ of a half is the cut one: if the halved edge has length $e$, then $\tfrac e2=kx$, and the two uncut dimensions of $B$ form the multiset $\{ky,kz\}$ (equal dimensions counted twice).

  • If $e=x$ then $\tfrac x2=kx$, so $k=\tfrac12$, contradicting $k^3=\tfrac12$.

  • If $e=y$ then $\{x,z\}=\{ky,kz\}$; but $kz<z$ and $ky<y\le z$, so neither equals $z$: impossible.

  • So $e=z$ and $\{x,y\}=\{ky,kz\}$. Since $ky<y$, we get $ky=x$ and $kz=y$, together with $\tfrac z2=kx$.

Hence $y=kz$, $x=k^2z$ with $k=2^{-1/3}$, and indeed $\tfrac z2=k^3z=kx$. Scaling so that $x=1$: \[\boxed{\text{dimensions } 1,\ \sqrt[3]{2},\ \sqrt[3]{4}}\] (or any common multiple of these). Check: cutting the longest edge $\sqrt[3]4$ in half gives a $1\times\sqrt[3]2\times\tfrac{\sqrt[3]4}{2}$ brick, and $\tfrac{\sqrt[3]4}{2}=2^{-1/3}$, so its dimensions are $2^{-1/3},1,2^{1/3}$, which is $2^{-1/3}$ times $1,\sqrt[3]2,\sqrt[3]4$. These are the only possible shapes.

Show solution 2 – Logarithms on a number line

Two non-overlapping bricks are convex solids with no common interior point, so a plane separates them (the separating plane theorem for convex sets); as together they fill $B$, the cut is a plane cut. Every face of a piece other than the cut face lies in a face of $B$, and the face of a piece opposite the cut face is parallel to it, so the cut is parallel to two faces of $B$; for the two pieces to be congruent it must cut one edge-length $L$ exactly in half (the pieces have dimensions $p,q,t$ and $p,q,L-t$ with the same volume, so $t=L-t$). Each half has half the volume of $B$, so it is $B$ scaled by $k$ with $k^3=\tfrac12$.

Write the three dimensions as powers of $2$: $2^u, 2^v, 2^w$ with $u\le v\le w$, and mark $u,v,w$ on a number line. Halving one dimension moves one of the three marks $1$ unit to the left; scaling by $k=2^{-1/3}$ moves all three marks $\tfrac13$ to the left. The two resulting multisets of marks (coincident marks counted separately) must coincide.

The leftmost new mark is $u-\tfrac13$, which is less than $u,v,w$. In the other description, the only mark that has moved is the halved one, so it is the halved one that sits at $u-\tfrac13$. So the two unmoved marks are $v-\tfrac13$ and $w-\tfrac13$. Both are less than $w$, so the mark at $w$ cannot be unmoved; it is the one that moved: $w-1=u-\tfrac13$, and the unmoved $u$, $v$ equal $v-\tfrac13$, $w-\tfrac13$ (the smaller to the smaller): $u=v-\tfrac13$, $v=w-\tfrac13$. Then $w-1=u-\tfrac13$ holds automatically.

So the marks are equally spaced with gap $\tfrac13$: the dimensions are $2^u,\ 2^{u+1/3},\ 2^{u+2/3}$, that is, proportional to \[\boxed{1,\ \sqrt[3]{2},\ \sqrt[3]{4}}.\] Conversely such a brick works: halving the edge $\sqrt[3]4$ gives $1,\sqrt[3]2,2^{-1/3}$, which is the original brick scaled by $2^{-1/3}$.

BAMO-8 Problem D

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Pythagorean Theorem · also BAMO-12 Problem 2

Two distinct squares overlap to form an octagon and eight right triangles (as shown in the diagram). The squares each have side length $4$ and have the same center. If one of those right triangles has side lengths $a$, $b$, $c$, where $c$ is the hypotenuse, show that the area of that triangle is $a+b-c$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Pick a side of one square and mark where the other square crosses it. Record which pieces of the side belong to the corner triangles and which piece belongs to the central octagon.
Hint 2: key idea
Use quarter-turns about the common centre to compare corner triangles from the same square, and look for a reflection that interchanges the two squares. This lets you relate the pieces of one square side to the sides of your chosen triangle. (LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework)
Hint 3: approach
Follow a side from one corner to the next and account for its middle piece and the legs at its ends. Use Pythagoras to rewrite twice the product of the legs as $(a+b)^2-c^2$. Factor that expression and use the square-side length to simplify one factor.
Common trap
The two end pieces of a square side correspond to different legs of the congruent triangles.
Show solution 1 – Perimeter 4 and a factorisation

All eight triangles are congruent. Let $O$ be the common centre. The second square is the first one turned about $O$ through some angle $\theta$. A quarter turn about $O$ maps each square to itself, so it permutes the four triangles cut off the corners of the first square and also the four cut off the corners of the second. Let $s$ be the reflection in a symmetry line of the first square through $O$ (parallel to a side), and let $r$ be the turn through $\theta$. Then $r\circ s$ is the reflection in the line through $O$ at angle $\tfrac\theta2$ to that symmetry line; it maps the first square to $r(\text{first})=$ second square, and, being a reflection, maps the second square back to the first. So it swaps the two families of triangles. Hence all eight triangles are congruent, with legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$.

The perimeter is $4$. Take a side $VW$ of the second square, where $V$ is the corner of the shaded triangle. Going from $V$ to $W$ we pass along a leg of the triangle at $V$, then along a side of the octagon, which is the hypotenuse of a triangle cut off a corner of the first square, then along a leg of the triangle at $W$. The quarter turn about $O$ that takes $V$ to $W$ takes the triangle at $V$ to the triangle at $W$, and takes the leg of the triangle at $V$ not on $VW$ to the leg of the triangle at $W$ on $VW$. In the figure the leg $a$ at $V$ lies on $VW$ and the leg $b$ at $V$ does not, so the piece of $VW$ at $W$ has length $b$. Therefore \[4=VW=a+c+b.\]

The area. By Pythagoras $a^2+b^2=c^2$, so \[2ab=(a+b)^2-c^2=(a+b-c)(a+b+c)=4(a+b-c).\] Hence the area is $\tfrac12ab=a+b-c$, as required.

Show solution 2 – The inscribed circle

As shown in the first two steps below, the triangle has perimeter $a+b+c=4$.

Congruence. The second square is the image of the first under a turn about the common centre $O$; a quarter turn about $O$ preserves each square, and the reflection in the bisector (through $O$) of the angle between corresponding sides of the two squares swaps the two squares. These symmetries carry any of the eight triangles to any other, so all have legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$.

Perimeter. A side of the second square consists of a leg of the triangle at one end, the hypotenuse of a triangle cut off the first square (a side of the octagon), and a leg of the triangle at the other end. The quarter turn about $O$ taking the first end to the second maps the triangle there to the triangle at the second end, and maps its leg not on this side to the leg of the second triangle that is on this side; so the two legs on the side are one $a$ and one $b$. So $a+b+c=4$.

Area. Let the inscribed circle of the right triangle have radius $r$ and touch the legs and the hypotenuse. The two tangent segments from each vertex are equal; from the right-angle vertex they are sides of a square of side $r$ (the radii to the legs are perpendicular to them), so they have length $r$. The tangent segments from the two acute vertices are then $a-r$ and $b-r$, and together they make the hypotenuse: \[c=(a-r)+(b-r),\qquad r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}.\] Joining the incentre to the three vertices splits the triangle into three triangles of height $r$ on the bases $a,b,c$, so \[\text{area}=\frac{r(a+b+c)}{2}=\frac{a+b-c}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{2}=a+b-c.\]

Show solution 3 – Area of the octagon

Let $O$ be the common centre. A quarter turn about $O$ maps each square to itself, and the reflection in the line through $O$ bisecting the angle between corresponding sides of the two squares exchanges the squares; these symmetries carry each of the eight right triangles onto every other one, so all eight have legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$.

Each side of the second square is made of a leg of the triangle at one of its ends, the hypotenuse of a triangle cut off a corner of the first square, and a leg of the triangle at its other end. The quarter turn about $O$ that moves the first end to the other maps the triangle at the first end to the triangle at the other, and maps its leg not on this side to the leg of the other triangle that lies on this side; so these legs are one $a$ and one $b$. Hence $a+b+c=4$.

The octagon has eight sides, and each is the hypotenuse of one of the triangles, so each has length $c$. Each side lies on a side of one of the squares, at distance $2$ from $O$ (half the side length). Joining $O$ to the eight vertices cuts the octagon into eight triangles with base $c$ and height $2$, so the octagon has area $8\cdot\tfrac12\cdot c\cdot2=8c$.

The first square consists of the octagon and the four triangles cut off its corners, so one triangle has area \[\frac{16-8c}{4}=4-2c=(a+b+c)-2c=a+b-c.\]

Show solution 4 – The centre as an excentre

Let $O$ be the common centre and let the shaded triangle be $PQR$, with the right angle at $R$, legs $RP=a$, $RQ=b$ and hypotenuse $PQ=c$. The triangle is a corner of one square (call it the second square) that sticks out of the other (the first square): $R$ is a corner of the second square, the legs $RP$ and $RQ$ lie on two sides of the second square, and the hypotenuse $PQ$ lies on a side of the first square.

Distances from $O$. Each side of a square of side $4$ lies at distance $2$ from its centre. So $O$ is at distance $2$ from each of the three lines $RP$, $RQ$ and $PQ$.

Where $O$ lies. A square lies entirely on one side of each of its side lines. The point $O$ and the point $Q$ both belong to the second square, so $O$ is on the same side of line $RP$ as $Q$; likewise $O$ is on the same side of line $RQ$ as $P$. Hence $O$ lies inside the angle $PRQ$. On the other hand $O$ is inside the first square, while $R$ is outside it, beyond the side line $PQ$; so $O$ and $R$ are on opposite sides of line $PQ$.

The area. A ray from $R$ inside the angle $PRQ$ meets the segment $PQ$, and $O$ lies beyond $PQ$ on that ray; so the segment $RO$ crosses the segment $PQ$ at a point strictly inside both segments. The diagonals of the quadrilateral $RPOQ$ therefore cross, so it is convex, and we can cut it into two triangles in two ways: along $RO$ and along $PQ$. Comparing areas, \[[PQR]+[OPQ]=[ORP]+[ORQ].\] The triangles $ORP$, $ORQ$, $OPQ$ have bases $a$, $b$, $c$ on lines at distance $2$ from $O$, so their areas are $\tfrac12\cdot2a=a$, $\tfrac12\cdot2b=b$ and $\tfrac12\cdot2c=c$. Hence \[[PQR]=a+b-c,\] as required. (In the language of triangle centres: $O$ is the centre of the excircle of $PQR$ opposite $R$, of radius $2$, and the area is the exradius times $\tfrac{a+b+c}{2}-c$. This argument does not need to know that the eight triangles are congruent or that $a+b+c=4$.)

BAMO-8 Problem E

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants · Parity Arguments · also BAMO-12 Problem 3

Lou is in a logic class with six other students. Their teacher assigned each of them the role of Truth-Teller or Liar. The Truth-Tellers must make only truthful statements, and the Liars must make only false statements. Each student—except Lou, who was daydreaming when the roles were assigned—knows their own role and that of all the other students. The teacher asks the students to sit in a circle, where they will be asked a question, and they must answer “in character.” Lou doesn't know her own role nor anybody else's.

The teacher asks each student in the circle, one after another, “Is the person sitting to your left a Truth-Teller?” to which they must answer “Yes” or “No.” Lou happens to be the last person to be called. She heard “Yes” three times and “No” three times from the other students, in some order. Suddenly Lou realizes that she can act out her role correctly, even though she doesn't know what her role is! When the teacher asks Lou the same question as the others, what should her answer be, and why?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a pair of students with the same role and a pair with different roles. For each, work out what the first student would say about the student on the left.
Hint 2: key idea
Interpret each answer as information about whether the speaker and their left neighbour have the same role. As you travel around the circle, track each time the role changes. (LPS VI, Chapter 18: Parity, Invariants, and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Make one complete circuit, noticing what returning to the starting role implies about the parity of the number of changes. Count how many changes the six reports already account for. Use the missing link to decide what Lou can infer about her relationship to her left neighbour.
Common trap
Each answer reports whether the speaker and the left neighbour have the same role, not whether the neighbour is a Truth-Teller, so do not try to count Liars from the answers.
Show the answer

No

Show solution 1 – Counting changes around the circle

Look at what an answer reveals. A Truth-Teller says “Yes” exactly when the left neighbour is a Truth-Teller. A Liar says “Yes” exactly when the true answer is “No”, that is, when the left neighbour is a Liar. In both cases:

a student answers “Yes” exactly when the left neighbour has the same role, and “No” exactly when the roles differ.

Walk once around the circle, each step going from a student to the student's left neighbour. There are $7$ steps, one for each student's question, and we come back to the role we started from, so the role changes an even number of times. The six other students reported $3$ changes (their three “No” answers). Hence the remaining step, from Lou to her left neighbour, must also be a change: Lou and her left neighbour have different roles.

By the rule above, the in-character answer for Lou is therefore No, whatever her role. Directly: if Lou is a Truth-Teller, her left neighbour is a Liar and the truthful answer is “No”; if Lou is a Liar, her left neighbour is a Truth-Teller, the true answer is “Yes” and she must say No.

Show solution 2 – Signs and a product

Number the students $1,2,\dots,7$ so that the left neighbour of student $i$ is student $i+1$ (and of student $7$ is student $1$), with Lou as student $7$. Give student $i$ the sign $x_i=+1$ for a Truth-Teller and $x_i=-1$ for a Liar.

The question to student $i$ has true answer “Yes” exactly when $x_{i+1}=+1$. A Truth-Teller ($x_i=+1$) reports it as it is and a Liar ($x_i=-1$) reports the opposite, so student $i$ says “Yes” if $x_ix_{i+1}=+1$ and “No” if $x_ix_{i+1}=-1$. Now \[(x_1x_2)(x_2x_3)\cdots(x_6x_7)(x_7x_1)=x_1^2x_2^2\cdots x_7^2=1 .\] The first six factors are the answers of the other students: three equal $+1$ and three equal $-1$, so their product is $-1$. Hence $x_7x_1=-1$, which means that Lou's correct answer is No. Lou can work this out without knowing any role, so she can answer in character.

BAMO-12 Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

A large blackboard initially has the single number $2026$ written on it. Fran writes new numbers on the blackboard by following these rules:

  • If the number $a^2+b^2$ is written on the board, where $a$, $b$ are distinct positive integers, then Fran can write both $a^2$ and $b^2$ on the board.

  • If the two numbers $a^2$ and $b^2$ are both written on the board, where $a$, $b$ are distinct positive integers, then Fran can write $a^2+b^2$ on the board.

Proceeding in this way, will Fran ever be able to write the number $2026^2$ on the board? What about the number $2026^{2026}$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try splitting the starting number into a small square and a large square. Then try writing one of the resulting squares as a sum of two different positive squares.
Hint 2: key idea
List the residues of squares modulo $16$ and look for a collection of residues preserved by both board rules. Separately, two different representations of a sum of squares can let you combine two known squares and split the result to obtain a new one. (LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Test the residue collection under both splitting and combining, then check each target against it. Where the residues give no obstruction, look for an induction producing ever larger admissible squares from an identity of the form $p^2+q^2=r^2+s^2$ with $p,q,r,s$ linear in two parameters. Check the positivity, size and distinctness conditions before applying the rules.
Common trap
A residue invariant can rule out a target, but an allowed residue alone does not show how to write it.
Show the answer

$2026^2$: No; $2026^{2026}$: Yes

Show the solution – An invariant modulo 16 and an inductive construction

An invariant. Call a positive integer good if its remainder modulo $16$ is one of $0,1,2,9,10$. Squares modulo $16$ are $0$ (for multiples of $4$), $1$ or $9$ (for odd numbers) and $4$ (for numbers $\equiv2\pmod4$). So a square $n^2$ is good exactly when $n\not\equiv2\pmod 4$.

We claim that every number on the board is always good. The starting number $2026=16\cdot126+10$ is good.

  • Second rule: if $a^2$ and $b^2$ are good, each is $0,1$ or $9$ modulo $16$, and $a^2+b^2$ is one of $0,1,9,2,10,18\equiv2$ modulo $16$: good.

  • First rule: let $a^2+b^2$ be good. If, say, $a^2\equiv4$, then $a^2+b^2\equiv4+\{0,1,4,9\}=\{4,5,8,13\}\pmod{16}$, none of which is good. So $a^2,b^2\not\equiv4$, and both are good.

Since $2026\equiv2\pmod4$, the number $2026^2\equiv4\pmod{16}$ is not good. So Fran can never write $2026^2$.

Every good square can be written. We prove: for every positive integer $n\not\equiv2\pmod4$, Fran can write $n^2$. Numbers are never erased, so we may use everything written earlier.

Small cases. $2026=1^2+45^2$ gives $1$ and $45^2$. Then \[45^2=27^2+36^2,\quad 1+27^2=730=17^2+21^2,\quad 17^2=8^2+15^2,\quad 1+8^2=65=4^2+7^2,\] \[1+21^2=442=9^2+19^2,\quad 9^2+17^2=370=3^2+19^2,\quad 1+17^2=290=11^2+13^2,\quad 13^2=5^2+12^2 .\] Reading each line as “combine (if needed), then split”, Fran writes $1^2,3^2,4^2,5^2,7^2,8^2,9^2$: all $n\le9$ with $n\not\equiv2\pmod4$.

Induction step. For integers $x,y$ we have the identity \[(2x-y)^2+(x+2y)^2=(2x+y)^2+(x-2y)^2 .\] Let $n\ge10$, $n\not\equiv2\pmod4$, and assume the claim for all smaller numbers. Choose

  • $y=1$, $n=2x+1$ if $n$ is odd and $n\not\equiv1\pmod 8$; then $x=\tfrac{n-1}{2}\not\equiv0\pmod4$, and $a=n-2$, $b=x+2$, $d=x-2$;

  • $y=3$, $n=2x+3$ if $n\equiv1\pmod8$ (so $n\ge17$); then $x=\tfrac{n-3}{2}$ is odd, and $a=n-6$, $b=x+6$, $d=x-6$;

  • $y=2$, $n=2x+2$ if $n\equiv0\pmod4$ (so $n\ge12$); then $x=\tfrac{n-2}{2}$ is odd, and $a=n-4$, $b=x+4$, $d=x-4$.

In each case $a^2+b^2=n^2+d^2$ by the identity. One checks: $a$ and $b$ are odd or multiples of $4$ (in the first case $a$ is odd and $b=x+2\not\equiv2\pmod4$; in the other two cases both are odd, or $a\equiv0\pmod4$ and $b$ odd), $a\ne b$, $a,b<n$, and $1\le d<n$. Indeed, $n\ge10$ gives $x\ge5$, $x\ge7$, $x\ge5$ in the three cases, so $x\ge 2y+1$; hence $d=x-2y\ge1$, $b=x+2y<2x+y=n$ and $a=n-2y<n$. Also $a=b$ would mean $x=3y$, which gives $n=7$, $n=21\not\equiv1\pmod 8$, and $x$ even in the three cases, all excluded. By the induction hypothesis $a^2$ and $b^2$ can be written; Fran combines them into $a^2+b^2=n^2+d^2$ and splits this as $n^2$ and $d^2$. This proves the claim.

Conclusion. $2026^{2026}=m^2$ with $m=2026^{1013}$, which is divisible by $4$. By the claim Fran can write $2026^{2026}$.

BAMO-12 Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Encoding and Representation

Given a deck of cards numbered $1$ through $N$ ($N>1$), not necessarily arranged in order, we define a grand swap as the following $N$-step procedure: swap the part of the deck that's above the $1$ with the part that's below the $1$; then swap the part of the deck that's above the $2$ with the part that's below the $2$; continue in this manner, until finally swapping the part of the deck that's above the $N$ with the part that's below the $N$. (If one of the “parts” that we swap is empty, the swap is equivalent to simply moving the top card to the bottom or vice versa.)

For example, suppose we start with $1,2,4,3$ (with $1$ on the top and $3$ on the bottom). We swap the cards above the $1$ (all zero of them) with the cards below the $1$ (all three of them) to get $2,4,3,1$. Then we swap the cards above the $2$ with the cards below the $2$ to get $4,3,1,2$. Then we swap the card above the $3$ with the cards below the $3$ to get $1,2,3,4$ (notice that the $1$ and the $2$ remain in the order they were in above; though two piles are swapped, each pile stays in its original order). Finally we swap the cards above the $4$ with the cards below the $4$ to get $4,1,2,3$. So, our grand swap sends $1,2,4,3$ to $4,1,2,3$.

Show that performing $N+1$ successive grand swaps always brings the deck back to its original order.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a single swap around card $k$, write the order before and after using one symbol for each block. Notice what changes and what is preserved inside the blocks.
Hint 2: key idea
Put the cards around a circle and mark the gap between the bottom and top with an extra card labelled $0$. Compare the circular orders before and after the step around $k$, starting at whichever label makes the comparison easiest. (LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence)
Hint 3: approach
Show that swapping the two blocks around $k$ exchanges the positions of $0$ and $k$ in the circular model. Follow the label at each original position through the complete sequence of exchanges, then describe one grand swap as a cyclic relabelling. Examine the effect of repeating that relabelling the required number of times.
Common trap
When reading the deck back from the circle, use the current position of the marker.
Show solution 1 – A marker card and relabelling

A marker. Write the deck around a circle and insert an extra card labelled $0$ between the bottom card and the top card. Reading clockwise from the card after $0$ recovers the deck, so the circle with its $N+1$ labels $0,1,\dots,N$ records the deck exactly, and conversely.

One step is a transposition. Suppose the deck is $X\,k\,Y$ (the block $X$ above card $k$, the block $Y$ below). The circle reads $0\,X\,k\,Y$ (and then back to $0$). After the step the deck is $Y\,k\,X$ and the circle reads $0\,Y\,k\,X$. Starting the old circle at $k$ it reads $k\,Y\,0\,X$; exchanging the cards $0$ and $k$ in it gives $0\,Y\,k\,X$. So one step simply exchanges the positions of the cards $0$ and $k$ on the circle. (This also covers empty $X$ or $Y$.)

One grand swap adds $1$ to every label. Let $p_j$ be the place on the circle where card $j$ sits before the grand swap. Exchanging $0$ with $1$ puts $1$ at $p_0$ and $0$ at $p_1$; exchanging $0$ with $2$ puts $2$ at $p_1$ and $0$ at $p_2$; and so on, until exchanging $0$ with $N$ puts $N$ at $p_{N-1}$ and $0$ at $p_N$. So after the grand swap, the place $p_j$ holds the card $j+1$ for $j=0,\dots,N-1$, and $p_N$ holds $0$. In other words, a grand swap replaces every label $j$ on the circle by $j+1$ modulo $N+1$, without moving anything.

Conclusion. After $N+1$ grand swaps every label has been increased by $N+1\equiv0\pmod{N+1}$: the circle is exactly as at the start, and therefore so is the deck.

(Check with the example: $1,2,4,3$ gives the circle $0,1,2,4,3$; adding $1$ modulo $5$ gives $1,2,3,0,4$, which read from $0$ is the deck $4,1,2,3$.)

Show solution 2 – Composing the transpositions

Put an extra card $0$ between the bottom and the top of the deck and think of the $N+1$ cards as sitting at $N+1$ places around a circle; reading on from the card after $0$ gives the deck, and the circle determines the deck. Describe the state by the map $f$ that sends each place to the label of the card there.

One step. If the deck is $X\,k\,Y$, the circle reads $0\,X\,k\,Y$, and after the step the deck $Y\,k\,X$ gives the circle $0\,Y\,k\,X$. Read around, the old circle is $k\,Y\,0\,X$, and exchanging the labels $0$ and $k$ turns it into $0\,Y\,k\,X$. So a step changes $f$ into $\tau_k\circ f$, where $\tau_k$ is the transposition of the labels $0$ and $k$.

A grand swap. It changes $f$ into $\sigma\circ f$ with $\sigma=\tau_N\circ\cdots\circ\tau_2\circ\tau_1$. Compute $\sigma$ on labels: $\tau_1$ sends $0\mapsto1$ and $\tau_2,\dots,\tau_N$ fix $1$, so $\sigma(0)=1$. For $1\le j\le N-1$, $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_{j-1}$ fix $j$, $\tau_j$ sends $j\mapsto0$, $\tau_{j+1}$ sends $0\mapsto j+1$, and the later ones fix $j+1$; so $\sigma(j)=j+1$. Finally $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_{N-1}$ fix $N$ and $\tau_N$ sends $N\mapsto0$, so $\sigma(N)=0$. Thus $\sigma$ is the cycle $0\to1\to2\to\cdots\to N\to0$.

Conclusion. After $m$ grand swaps the state is $\sigma^m\circ f$. The cycle $\sigma$ has length $N+1$, so $\sigma^{N+1}$ is the identity and after $N+1$ grand swaps the state is $f$ again: the deck is back in its original order.
