Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 13: Rates, Motion, and Work · Average Rate and Time Budget

Kaity is moving several boxes from her van to the classroom. She picks up either one or two boxes at the van, carries them to the classroom, drops off the boxes, and then walks back to the van, repeating the process until all the boxes are in the classroom and she is back at the van.

Her walking speed when carrying no boxes is $2.8$ miles per hour, and her walking speed when carrying one box is $2.4$ miles per hour. She finds that it doesn't matter whether she carries one box or two boxes at a time: either way, it will take the same amount of time to move all of the boxes. What is Kaity's walking speed in miles per hour while carrying two boxes? Assume that Kaity picks up and drops off the boxes immediately.

Show the answer

$\frac{21}{25}$

Show the solution – Compare two boxes at a time

Let $d$ be the distance (in miles) from the van to the classroom and let $v$ be her speed (in miles per hour) with two boxes. Let $N$ be the number of boxes; $N\ge 2$, since she is able to carry two boxes at a time. Write $N=2k+r$ with $k\ge 1$ and $r\in\{0,1\}$. Carrying two at a time, she makes $k$ trips with two boxes and, if $r=1$, one last trip with a single box; that single-box trip costs the same as one trip of the other plan. So the two plans differ only in how $k$ pairs of boxes are moved, and since $k\ge1$, they take equal time exactly when one pair of boxes takes equal time in both plans.

One box at a time. Two round trips, each with one loaded walk and one empty walk. Since $\frac{1}{2.4}=\frac{5}{12}$ and $\frac{1}{2.8}=\frac{5}{14}$, this takes \[2\left(\frac{5d}{12}+\frac{5d}{14}\right)\ \text{hours}.\] Two boxes at a time. One round trip: $\dfrac dv+\dfrac{5d}{14}$ hours.

Setting the two times equal and dividing by $d$: \[\frac1v=2\cdot\frac{5}{12}+2\cdot\frac{5}{14}-\frac{5}{14}=\frac56+\frac{5}{14}=\frac{35+15}{42}=\frac{25}{21}.\] Therefore $v=\boxed{\tfrac{21}{25}}$ miles per hour. (It is reasonable that she is much slower with two boxes: the one saved empty walk must pay for the extra slowness.)

Problem 11

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Midpoints and Section Ratios

Define the drift of a point $(x,y)$ in the coordinate plane as $x+y$. Three corners of a square in the coordinate plane have drifts of $8$, $11$, and $25$, in some order. What is the maximum possible drift of the fourth corner of the square?

Show the answer

$28$

Show the solution – Diagonals share a midpoint

Write $\delta(x,y)=x+y$ for the drift. Drift is additive: $\delta$ of a sum of two vectors is the sum of the drifts, and $\delta$ of a midpoint is the average of the drifts.

Let the square be $V_1V_2V_3V_4$ (in order). Its diagonals $V_1V_3$ and $V_2V_4$ have the same midpoint, so \[\delta(V_1)+\delta(V_3)=\delta(V_2)+\delta(V_4).\] Let $w$ be the drift of the fourth corner and $u$ the drift of the given corner opposite to it. Then $w+u$ equals the sum of the other two given drifts, so $w=(\text{sum of the other two})-u$. The three choices of $u$ give \[w=11+25-8=28,\qquad w=8+25-11=22,\qquad w=8+11-25=-6 .\] Hence $w\le 28$.

The value $28$ occurs. Take the square with vertices, in order, \[(0.5,\,7.5),\quad(10.5,\,0.5),\quad(17.5,\,10.5),\quad(7.5,\,17.5).\] Its consecutive side vectors are $(10,-7)$, $(7,10)$, $(-10,7)$, $(-7,-10)$: all of length $\sqrt{149}$, and consecutive ones are perpendicular because $10\cdot 7+(-7)\cdot 10=0$. The drifts of the vertices are $8$, $11$, $28$, $25$. So the maximum possible drift of the fourth corner is $\boxed{28}$.

Problem 12

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Recursive Definitions

Ben and Theo enter a classroom with the number $0$ written on the blackboard. Ben and Theo repeatedly take turns replacing the number on the blackboard, with Ben going first. Ben writes the smallest multiple of $3$ greater than the current number and Theo writes the smallest multiple of $4$ greater than the current number. The first few numbers that appear on the blackboard are \[0,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ \dots .\] What is the $100$th number that appears on the blackboard, including the initial $0$?

Show the answer

$198$

Show the solution – A cycle of six moves

Suppose the board shows a multiple of $12$, say $12k$, and it is Ben's turn. The next six moves are \[\begin{aligned}&12k\ \xrightarrow{\text{Ben}}\ 12k+3\ \xrightarrow{\text{Theo}}\ 12k+4\ \xrightarrow{\text{Ben}}\ 12k+6\\ &\qquad\xrightarrow{\text{Theo}}\ 12k+8\ \xrightarrow{\text{Ben}}\ 12k+9\ \xrightarrow{\text{Theo}}\ 12k+12,\end{aligned}\] and then it is Ben's turn again with the multiple of $12$ given by $12(k+1)$. At the start the board shows $0=12\cdot 0$ and Ben moves first. Hence, by induction, the $(6k+1)$st number on the board is $12k$, and the numbers between follow the pattern above.

Since $97=6\cdot 16+1$, the $97$th number is $12\cdot 16=192$, followed by $195$, $196$, $198$. The $100$th number is $\boxed{198}$.

Problem 13

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 15: GCD, LCM, and Multiplicative Interaction · GCD Logic

For each positive whole number $n$, let $T(n)=1+2+3+\dots+n$ be the $n$th triangular number. It is known that $T(n)=n(n+1)/2$. What is the sum of all positive whole numbers $m$ for which \[\frac{T(m+2)+T(m+4)}{T(m)}\] is a whole number?

Show the answer

$14$

Show solution 1 – Division and a size bound

Using $T(n)=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, \[\frac{T(m+2)+T(m+4)}{T(m)}=\frac{(m+2)(m+3)+(m+4)(m+5)}{m(m+1)}=\frac{2m^2+14m+26}{m^2+m},\] that is, \[\frac{T(m+2)+T(m+4)}{T(m)}=2+\frac{12m+26}{m(m+1)}.\] So we need $m(m+1)\mid 12m+26$. Since $12m+26>0$, this forces $m(m+1)\le 12m+26$, that is $m^2-11m-26\le 0$. For $m\ge 14$ we have $m^2-11m-26=m(m-11)-26\ge 14\cdot 3-26>0$, so $m\le 13$. Checking $m=1,\dots,13$: \[\frac{38}{2},\ \frac{50}{6},\ \frac{62}{12},\ \frac{74}{20},\ \frac{86}{30},\ \frac{98}{42},\ \frac{110}{56},\ \frac{122}{72},\ \frac{134}{90},\ \frac{146}{110},\ \frac{158}{132},\ \frac{170}{156},\ \frac{182}{182};\] only $m=1$ (quotient $19$) and $m=13$ (quotient $1$) give whole numbers. The sum is $1+13=\boxed{14}$.

Show solution 2 – Coprime factors separately

Using $T(n)=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, \[\frac{T(m+2)+T(m+4)}{T(m)}=\frac{(m+2)(m+3)+(m+4)(m+5)}{m(m+1)}=2+\frac{12m+26}{m(m+1)},\] so we need $m(m+1)\mid 12m+26$. Because $m$ and $m+1$ are coprime, this holds exactly when $m\mid 12m+26$ and $m+1\mid 12m+26$.

  • $m\mid 12m+26$ is equivalent to $m\mid 26$, so $m\in\{1,2,13,26\}$.

  • $12m+26=12(m+1)+14$, so $m+1\mid 12m+26$ is equivalent to $m+1\mid 14$, so $m\in\{1,6,13\}$.

Both hold exactly for $m=1$ and $m=13$, and the requested sum is $\boxed{14}$.

Problem 14

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting Principles · Simple Overcount Correction

Jessica has a $2\times 3$ grid of square cells. In how many ways can she color two cells red and the remaining four cells blue if colorings that differ by a rotation are considered the same, but colorings that differ by a reflection are considered different?

Show the answer

$9$

Show the solution – Pair colorings by the half turn

The only rotations that carry a $2\times 3$ grid onto itself are the identity and the half turn ($180^\circ$). Without identification there are $\binom62=15$ colorings. The half turn pairs each coloring with its rotated image; two colorings are the same exactly when they are equal or form such a pair.

A coloring is unchanged by the half turn exactly when its two red cells are swapped by the half turn. The half turn splits the six cells into three pairs (each cell with the cell diametrically opposite), so there are $3$ such colorings. The remaining $15-3=12$ colorings fall into $6$ pairs. The number of distinct colorings is $3+6=\boxed{9}$.

Problem 15

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Constraint Satisfaction

In a $4\times 4$ Sudoku solution, each column, row, and bolded $2\times 2$ region contains exactly one of each digit from $1$ to $4$, and each cell contains exactly one digit. A Sudoku solution is super if each of the two main diagonals also contains exactly one of each digit from $1$ to $4$. How many distinct $4\times 4$ super Sudoku solutions exist if rotations and reflections are considered distinct?

Show the answer

$48$

Show the solution – Fix the first row

Write the cells as $(r,c)$ (row $r$, column $c$). Let the first row be $a,b,c,d$ (any of the $4!=24$ orders of the digits). We show that exactly two super Sudoku solutions have this first row.

The top-left box contains $a,b$ in row $1$, so $(2,1),(2,2)$ hold $c,d$ in some order; similarly $(2,3),(2,4)$ hold $a,b$ in some order. The main diagonal is $(1,1)=a,(2,2),(3,3),(4,4)$.

Case $(2,2)=c$, so $(2,1)=d$. The main diagonal already has $a,c$, so $(3,3),(4,4)\in\{b,d\}$.

  • If row $2$ ends $a,b$: column $3$ has $c,a$ above, so its lower cells hold $\{b,d\}$; column $4$ has $d,b$ above, so its lower cells hold $\{a,c\}$. Then $(4,4)\in\{b,d\}\cap\{a,c\}=\varnothing$: impossible.

  • If row $2$ ends $b,a$: the lower cells of column $3$ hold $\{a,d\}$ and of column $4$ hold $\{b,c\}$. So $(3,3)=d$, $(4,4)=b$, hence $(4,3)=a$, $(3,4)=c$. Rows $3$ and $4$ then need $\{a,b\}$ and $\{c,d\}$ in columns $1,2$; column $1$ already has $a,d$, so $(3,1)=b$, $(4,1)=c$, $(3,2)=a$, $(4,2)=d$.

This gives the grid with rows $abcd,\ dcba,\ badc,\ cdab$. Its columns, boxes and diagonals ($a,c,d,b$ and $d,b,a,c$) are all correct.

Case $(2,2)=d$, so $(2,1)=c$. Now $(3,3),(4,4)\in\{b,c\}$.

  • If row $2$ ends $b,a$: the lower cells of column $3$ hold $\{a,d\}$, so $(3,3)\in\{b,c\}\cap\{a,d\}=\varnothing$: impossible.

  • If row $2$ ends $a,b$: the lower cells of column $3$ hold $\{b,d\}$ and of column $4$ hold $\{a,c\}$, so $(3,3)=b$, $(4,4)=c$, $(4,3)=d$, $(3,4)=a$. Column $1$ has $a,c$ above, and rows $3,4$ need $\{c,d\}$ and $\{a,b\}$ on the left, so $(3,1)=d$, $(4,1)=b$, $(3,2)=c$, $(4,2)=a$.

This gives rows $abcd,\ cdab,\ dcba,\ badc$, again a valid super Sudoku (diagonals $a,d,b,c$ and $d,a,c,b$).

So each first row extends in exactly $2$ ways, and the total is $24\cdot 2=\boxed{48}$.

Problem 16

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Absolute Value as Distance

An ordered triple of numbers $(a,b,c)$ is stable if it is equal to a permutation of $(|a-b|,|b-c|,|c-a|)$ and $a+b+c\le 2026$. For example, $(0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$ are stable. Compute the number of stable ordered triples of nonnegative integers.

Show the answer

$3040$

Show solution 1 – Compare the largest entries

Being stable only depends on the multiset $\{a,b,c\}$, because the multiset $\{|a-b|,|b-c|,|c-a|\}$ does not depend on the order. Let the multiset be $\{x,y,z\}$ with $0\le x\le y\le z$. Its pairwise differences are $y-x$, $z-y$, $z-x$, and the largest of them is $z-x$.

The two multisets are equal, so their largest elements agree: $z=z-x$, hence $x=0$. Now $\{0,y,z\}=\{y,\,z-y,\,z\}$; removing $y$ and $z$ from both sides leaves $0=z-y$, so $y=z$. Thus the multiset is $\{0,t,t\}$ for some integer $t\ge 0$. Conversely, for $(t,t,0)$ the differences are $0,t,t$, so every arrangement of $\{0,t,t\}$ is stable as long as the sum $2t\le 2026$, i.e. $t\le 1013$.

For $t=0$ there is one ordered triple, $(0,0,0)$; for each $t=1,\dots,1013$ there are $3$ ordered triples (choose the position of the $0$). The count is $1+3\cdot 1013=\boxed{3040}$.

Show solution 2 – Equal sums and equal products

Being stable only depends on the multiset $\{a,b,c\}$, because the multiset $\{|a-b|,|b-c|,|c-a|\}$ does not depend on the order. Let the multiset be $\{x,y,z\}$ with $0\le x\le y\le z$; its pairwise differences are $y-x$, $z-y$, $z-x$. If the two multisets are equal, they have the same sum and the same product.

Sum. $x+y+z=(y-x)+(z-y)+(z-x)=2z-2x$, so $z=3x+y$. Then $z-y=3x$ and $z-x=2x+y$.

Product. $xyz=(y-x)(z-y)(z-x)$ becomes $xy(3x+y)=3x(y-x)(2x+y)$. Suppose $x>0$. Dividing by $x$, \[3xy+y^2=3\left(xy+y^2-2x^2\right),\qquad\text{so}\qquad y^2=3x^2 .\] Then $\left(\frac yx\right)^2=3$, which is impossible for integers $x,y$, because $\sqrt3$ is irrational. Hence $x=0$, and the sum condition gives $z=y$.

So a stable triple is an arrangement of $\{0,t,t\}$ with an integer $t\ge 0$. Conversely, for $(t,t,0)$ the differences are $0,t,t$, so every arrangement of $\{0,t,t\}$ is stable as long as $2t\le 2026$, that is $t\le 1013$. For $t=0$ there is one ordered triple, $(0,0,0)$, and for each $t=1,\dots,1013$ there are $3$ (choose the position of the $0$). The count is $1+3\cdot 1013=\boxed{3040}$.

Problem 17

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Tangent and External Angle Relations

Triangle $ABC$ has $AB=BC$ and is inscribed in circle $O$. Circle $P$ passes through $A$ and $B$ and intersects line segment $\overline{AC}$ at $D\neq A$. Line $\ell_1$ passes through $D$ and is parallel to $\overline{AB}$. Line $\ell_2$ is tangent to circle $O$ at $C$ and is perpendicular to $\ell_1$, intersecting it at $E$. Given that $EC=\frac{16}{3}$ and that the longest side length of triangle $ABC$ is $16$, compute the radius of $P$ divided by the radius of $O$.

Show the answer

$\frac{\sqrt3}{3}$

Show solution 1 – Angle chase, then the law of sines

Let $\beta=\angle ABC$ and let $O$ also denote the center of circle $O$.

The shape of the triangle. The tangent $\ell_2$ at $C$ is perpendicular to the radius $OC$, and it is perpendicular to $\ell_1\parallel AB$. Hence $OC\parallel AB$. Since $BA=BC$ and $OA=OC$, both $B$ and $O$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, which bisects $\angle ABC$; moreover $O$ is on the ray from $B$ through the midpoint of $AC$ (this ray also contains the point of the circle opposite to $B$). So $\angle OBC=\frac\beta2$, and since $OB=OC$, also $\angle OCB=\frac\beta2$. For the parallel lines $OC$ and $AB$ cut by $BC$, the angles $\angle OCB$ and $\angle ABC$ are either equal or supplementary. Equal would give $\frac\beta2=\beta$, impossible. So $\frac\beta2+\beta=180^\circ$, giving $\beta=120^\circ$ and $\angle A=\angle C=30^\circ$.

Lengths. The longest side is $AC=16$. By the law of sines, $2R_O=\frac{AC}{\sin 120^\circ}=\frac{32}{\sqrt3}$, so $R_O=\frac{16}{\sqrt3}$; also $AB=BC=2R_O\sin 30^\circ=\frac{16}{\sqrt3}$.

Locating $D$. Since $CE\perp\ell_1$, $CE$ is the distance from $C$ to $\ell_1$. The line $\ell_1$ passes through $D$ on $AC$ and is parallel to $AB$, so it makes an angle of $\angle A=30^\circ$ with $AC$; hence $CE=CD\sin 30^\circ$, and $CD=2\cdot\frac{16}{3}=\frac{32}{3}$, $AD=16-\frac{32}{3}=\frac{16}{3}$.

Circle $P$. In triangle $ABD$, the law of cosines gives \[BD^2=AB^2+AD^2-2\,AB\cdot AD\cos 30^\circ=\frac{256}{3}+\frac{256}{9}-2\cdot\frac{16}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{16}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=\frac{256}{9},\] so $BD=\frac{16}{3}$. Circle $P$ is the circumcircle of $ABD$, so by the law of sines $2R_P=\frac{BD}{\sin\angle BAD}=\frac{16/3}{1/2}$, i.e. $R_P=\frac{16}{3}$. Therefore \[\frac{R_P}{R_O}=\frac{16/3}{16/\sqrt3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}=\boxed{\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}}.\]

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put $A=(-8,0)$ and $C=(8,0)$; since $BA=BC$, the apex is $B=(0,h)$ with $h>0$, and the center of circle $O$ is $O=(0,k)$ on the axis of symmetry, with $OC^2=OB^2$: $64+k^2=(h-k)^2$, so $k=\frac{h^2-64}{2h}$.

The tangent at $C$ is perpendicular to $OC$; it is also perpendicular to $\ell_1$, which is parallel to $AB$. So $OC\parallel AB$, i.e. the vectors $(8,-k)$ and $(8,h)$ are parallel: $-k=h$. Then $-h=\frac{h^2-64}{2h}$ gives $3h^2=64$, $h=\frac{8}{\sqrt3}$, $k=-\frac{8}{\sqrt3}$. The sides are $AB=BC=\sqrt{64+\frac{64}{3}}=\frac{16}{\sqrt3}<16=AC$, so indeed the longest side is $AC=16$, as required, and $R_O=OB=h-k=\frac{16}{\sqrt3}$.

Let $D=(p,0)$. The line $\ell_1$ through $D$ parallel to $AB$ has direction $(8,h)=(8,\frac{8}{\sqrt3})$, i.e. direction $(\sqrt3,1)$, so its equation is $x-\sqrt3\,y=p$. The distance from $C$ to $\ell_1$ is $CE$: \[\frac{|8-p|}{2}=\frac{16}{3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=8-\frac{32}{3}=-\frac83\] (the other value $p=\frac{56}{3}$ is not on segment $AC$). So $D=(-\frac83,0)$.

The center of circle $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $x=-\frac{16}{3}$ of $AD$; write it as $(-\frac{16}{3},s)$. Equal distances to $A$ and $B$: \[\left(\tfrac83\right)^2+s^2=\left(\tfrac{16}{3}\right)^2+\left(s-\tfrac{8}{\sqrt3}\right)^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{16}{\sqrt3}s=\frac{256}{9}-\frac{64}{9}+\frac{64}{3}=\frac{128}{3},\] so $s=\frac{8}{\sqrt3}$ and $R_P^2=\frac{64}{9}+\frac{64}{3}=\frac{256}{9}$, $R_P=\frac{16}{3}$. Hence $\frac{R_P}{R_O}=\frac{16/3}{16/\sqrt3}=\boxed{\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}}$.

Problem 18

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 14: Permutations, Combinations, and Binomial Structure · Permutation Structure

A permutation of the set $\{1,2,\dots,10\}$ is called mountainous if the elements increase to a peak and then decrease. The peak cannot be the first or the last element. What fraction of mountainous permutations have the property that the sum of the first element and the last element is odd?

Show the answer

$\frac23$

Show the solution – The two sides of the peak

In a mountainous permutation the peak is the largest element, $10$. The numbers $1,\dots,9$ are split into a left set $L$ and a right set $R$, both nonempty (the peak is not at an end); the left side is $L$ in increasing order and the right side is $R$ in decreasing order. Conversely, every split with $L,R\ne\varnothing$ gives one mountainous permutation. Hence there are $2^9-2=510$ of them.

The first element is $\min L$ and the last element is $\min R$. The number $1$ is in $L$ or in $R$; by the left-right mirror symmetry it suffices to count the case $1\in L$ and double. Then the first element is $1$ and the sum is odd exactly when $\min R$ is even. Here $R$ is any nonempty subset of $\{2,\dots,9\}$ (then $L$ automatically contains $1$). Counting by the minimum: \[\min R=2:\ 2^7,\quad \min R=4:\ 2^5,\quad \min R=6:\ 2^3,\quad \min R=8:\ 2^1,\] a total of $128+32+8+2=170$. With $1\in R$ there are also $170$, so $340$ permutations qualify, and the fraction is $\frac{340}{510}=\boxed{\tfrac23}$.

Problem 19

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Vieta's Relations

Let $c$ and $d$ be distinct real numbers. The parabolas $y=x^2+cx+d$ and $y=-x^2+dx+c$ are graphed in the coordinate plane. There are two lines that are tangent to both parabolas, and their slopes are $c$ and $d$. Compute $c-d$.

Show the answer

$-4$

Show solution 1 – Use the tangent of slope c

A line $y=kx+m$ is tangent to $y=x^2+cx+d$ exactly when $x^2+(c-k)x+(d-m)=0$ has a double root, i.e. \[(c-k)^2=4(d-m).\tag{1}\] It is tangent to $y=-x^2+dx+c$ exactly when $x^2+(k-d)x+(m-c)=0$ has a double root, i.e. \[(d-k)^2=4(m-c).\tag{2}\] One common tangent has slope $k=c$. Then (1) gives $0=4(d-m)$, so $m=d$, and (2) gives $(d-c)^2=4(d-c)$. Since $c\ne d$, we may divide: $d-c=4$. Hence $c-d=\boxed{-4}$.

(Check with the other slope $k=d$: (2) gives $m=c$, and (1) becomes $(c-d)^2=4(d-c)$, again $d-c=4$; so the data are consistent.)

Show solution 2 – Vieta on the slope equation

A line $y=kx+m$ is tangent to $y=x^2+cx+d$ exactly when $x^2+(c-k)x+(d-m)=0$ has a double root, that is $(c-k)^2=4(d-m)$; and it is tangent to $y=-x^2+dx+c$ exactly when $x^2+(k-d)x+(m-c)=0$ has a double root, that is $(d-k)^2=4(m-c)$. Adding the two conditions eliminates $m$: \[(c-k)^2+(d-k)^2=4(d-c),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad 2k^2-2(c+d)k+\bigl(c^2+d^2+4c-4d\bigr)=0 .\] Conversely, for each root $k$ of this quadratic, $m$ is determined by the first condition and the second then holds. So the slopes of the common tangents are the roots of this quadratic, and these are $c$ and $d$. By Vieta's formulas their product is \[cd=\frac{c^2+d^2+4c-4d}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (c-d)^2+4(c-d)=0 .\] As $c\ne d$, we get $c-d=\boxed{-4}$. (The sum of the roots, $c+d$, matches automatically.)

Problem 20

HGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Cyclic Quadrilaterals

Points $A,B,C,D$, and $E$ lie on a circle in that order such that $\angle ABD=90^\circ$ and $\angle ADC=\angle CBE=60^\circ$. Segments $\overline{BD}$ and $\overline{CE}$ intersect at point $F$. The circle passing through points $A$, $B$, and $F$ intersects $\overline{AE}$ at $G\neq A$. Given $BF=10$ and $DF=8$, compute $AG$.

Show the answer

$9\sqrt3-\sqrt7$

Show solution 1 – Arcs, a similar pair, then a projection

Let $\omega$ be the circle, with radius $R$.

Arcs. $\angle ABD=90^\circ$, so $AD$ is a diameter and the arc $ABCD$ is $180^\circ$. The inscribed angle $\angle ADC=60^\circ$ cuts off arc $ABC$, so arc $AC=120^\circ$ and arc $CD=60^\circ$. The inscribed angle $\angle CBE=60^\circ$ cuts off arc $CDE$ of $120^\circ$, so arc $DE=60^\circ$ and the remaining arc $EA=120^\circ$.

The radius. Arcs $CD$ and $DE$ are equal, so $\angle DEC=\angle DBE=30^\circ$ (each is half of a $60^\circ$ arc). Triangles $DEF$ and $DBE$ share the angle at $D$ (since $F$ lies on $DB$), and $\angle DEF=\angle DEC=\angle DBE$. Hence they are similar, and $\frac{DE}{DB}=\frac{DF}{DE}$: \[DE^2=DF\cdot DB=8\cdot 18=144,\qquad DE=12.\] A chord cutting off a $60^\circ$ arc equals the radius, so $R=12$ and $AD=24$.

Lengths at $A$. In right triangle $ABD$, $AB^2=24^2-18^2=252$, and in right triangle $ABF$, $AF^2=AB^2+BF^2=352$, so $AF=4\sqrt{22}$. In triangle $AFD$, by the law of cosines, \[\cos\angle FAD=\frac{AF^2+AD^2-FD^2}{2\cdot AF\cdot AD}=\frac{352+576-64}{2\cdot 4\sqrt{22}\cdot 24}=\frac{9}{2\sqrt{22}},\] and so $\sin\angle FAD=\sqrt{1-\frac{81}{88}}=\frac{\sqrt7}{2\sqrt{22}}$. Also $\angle DAE=30^\circ$ (half of arc $DE$). The points $B$ (hence $F$) and $E$ lie on opposite sides of the diameter $AD$, so $\angle FAE=\angle FAD+30^\circ$.

A right rectangular prism has side lengths $20$, $30$, and $40$. Point $P$ inside the prism is the apex of six pyramids whose bases are the faces of the prism. The volumes of these six pyramids form an arithmetic sequence of positive numbers in some order. Let $Q$ be the center of the prism, which is where the space diagonals of the prism intersect. Compute the greatest possible integer value of the length $PQ$.

Show the answer

$22$

Show the solution – Opposite pyramids pair up

Put the prism as $0\le x\le 20$, $0\le y\le 30$, $0\le z\le 40$, so $Q=(10,15,20)$, and let $P=(x,y,z)$ inside. A pyramid with a face as base and apex $P$ has height equal to the distance from $P$ to that face. The two faces of area $30\cdot40=1200$ give volumes $400x$ and $400(20-x)$; the faces of area $800$ give $\frac{800}{3}y$ and $\frac{800}{3}(30-y)$; the faces of area $600$ give $200z$ and $200(40-z)$. Each pair of opposite pyramids has total volume $8000$ (one third of the prism's volume $24000$, split between them).

The six volumes form an arithmetic sequence with mean $\frac{24000}{6}=4000$; write them as $4000+ke$ for $k\in\{-5,-3,-1,1,3,5\}$ with $e\ge0$. If $e>0$, two terms sum to $8000$ only when their $k$'s are opposite, so the opposite pairs have deviations $\pm k_1e$, $\pm k_2e$, $\pm k_3e$ with $\{k_1,k_2,k_3\}=\{1,3,5\}$ (if $e=0$, then $P=Q$). Thus \[|x-10|=\frac{k_1e}{400},\qquad |y-15|=\frac{3k_2e}{800},\qquad |z-20|=\frac{k_3e}{200},\] and positivity of all six volumes means $5e<4000$, i.e. $e<800$. Hence \[PQ^2=e^2\left(\frac{k_1^2}{160000}+\frac{9k_2^2}{640000}+\frac{k_3^2}{40000}\right)=\frac{e^2}{640000}\left(4k_1^2+9k_2^2+16k_3^2\right).\] The bracket is largest when the largest $k$ goes with the largest coefficient: $4\cdot1+9\cdot9+16\cdot25=485$. So $PQ^2<\frac{800^2}{640000}\cdot 485=485$, i.e. $PQ<\sqrt{485}<22.03$.

Conversely, with $(k_1,k_2,k_3)=(1,3,5)$ and $e$ running from $0$ to just below $800$, $PQ=\frac{e\sqrt{485}}{800}$ takes every value in $[0,\sqrt{485})$, and the corresponding $P$ is inside the prism with the six volumes $4000\pm e,4000\pm3e,4000\pm5e$ in arithmetic progression. Since $22<\sqrt{485}$, the value $PQ=22$ is attained (with $e=\frac{800\cdot22}{\sqrt{485}}<800$), and no integer larger than $22$ is possible. The answer is $\boxed{22}$.

Problem 20

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Constructive Counting

A ten-digit number $N$ is four-gone if all its digits are distinct and for each $1\le n\le 10$, the $n$-digit number formed by taking the leading $n$ digits of $N$ is not divisible by $4$. For example, $1234567890$ is not four-gone because $12$ and $123456$ are divisible by $4$. How many ten-digit numbers are four-gone?

Show the answer

$144000$

Show the solution – Blocks after the odd digits

A number with at least two digits is divisible by $4$ exactly when its last two digits are, and $\underline{u}\,\underline{v}=10u+v\equiv 2u+v\pmod 4$. So the condition is: the first digit is not $0,4,8$ (the one-digit number must not be divisible by $4$, and the leading digit is not $0$), and for each pair of adjacent digits $u,v$:

  • if $u$ is even, then $v\notin\{0,4,8\}$;

  • if $u$ is odd, then $v\notin\{2,6\}$.

All ten digits are used. Call $0,4,8$ type $Z$ and $2,6$ type $W$. A type-$Z$ digit cannot be first and cannot follow an even digit, so it follows an odd digit. A type-$W$ digit cannot follow an odd digit, so it is first or follows an even digit.

Look at the maximal runs of consecutive even digits. A run that starts the number begins with a $W$; every other run begins right after an odd digit, so it begins with a $Z$; inside a run every digit after the first follows an even digit, so it is a $W$. Hence each of the three $Z$'s starts its own run right after an odd digit, and the two $W$'s are placed either at the very start or right after a $Z$ (or after another $W$ in those places). Conversely every arrangement built this way satisfies all conditions.

Count the constructions: order the five odd digits ($5!=120$); choose which three of the five odd digits are followed by a $Z$-run ($\binom53=10$) and which $Z$ goes where ($3!=6$); finally insert the two distinct $W$'s into the four ordered slots (the start, and after each $Z$): the first $W$ has $4$ choices and the second then has $5$ positions (it may go before or after the first within the same slot), giving $20$. The total is \[120\cdot10\cdot6\cdot20=\boxed{144000}.\]

Problem 13

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Exhaustive Casework

How many ways can five people of distinct heights stand in a line such that no three consecutive people have heights in increasing or decreasing order, and exactly two people stand between the shortest person and the tallest person?

Show the answer

$12$

Show the solution – Peaks and valleys

Label the heights $1<2<3<4<5$. "No three consecutive in increasing or decreasing order" means the line goes up and down alternately, so every inner person is either a peak (taller than both neighbours) or a valley (shorter than both), alternately. The tallest person $5$ is a peak or an end, and the shortest $1$ is a valley or an end. Exactly two people between them means their positions are $\{1,4\}$ or $\{2,5\}$. Reversing the line maps the first case to the second and preserves the conditions, so we count positions $\{1,4\}$ and double.

  • $5$ in position $1$ and $1$ in position $4$: positions $2,4$ are valleys and $3,5$ peaks. Positions $2,3,5$ receive $2,3,4$, and the only remaining condition is $p_2<p_3$ (all others hold automatically because of $5$ and $1$). That is $3$ of the $6$ orders.

  • $1$ in position $1$ and $5$ in position $4$: positions $2,4$ are peaks and $3,5$ valleys. Again positions $2,3,5$ receive $2,3,4$ and the only condition left is $p_2>p_3$: $3$ orders.

So positions $\{1,4\}$ give $6$ lines, and the answer is $2\cdot6=\boxed{12}$.

Problem 15

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations · Composite Angle Chases

Rectangle $ABCD$ has side lengths $AB=20$ and $BC=26$. Points $M$ and $T$ lie on line segments $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AD}$, respectively, such that $AT=6$, $\angle ATM=\angle MTC$, and $\angle BMT=x^\circ$. Find $x$.

Show the answer

$\frac{315}{2}$

Show solution 1 – An isosceles right triangle

Since $AD=BC=26$ and $AT=6$, we have $TD=20=DC$, and $\angle TDC=90^\circ$. So triangle $TDC$ is an isosceles right triangle and $\angle DTC=45^\circ$. As $T$ lies on segment $AD$, $\angle ATC=180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ$. The condition $\angle ATM=\angle MTC$ says $TM$ bisects $\angle ATC$, so $\angle ATM=67.5^\circ$.

In the right triangle $ATM$ (right angle at $A$), $\angle AMT=90^\circ-67.5^\circ=22.5^\circ$. Also $AM=AT\tan 67.5^\circ=6(1+\sqrt2)<20$, so $M$ is indeed on segment $AB$, strictly between $A$ and $B$. Since $A,M,B$ are collinear, \[x=\angle BMT=180^\circ-22.5^\circ=157.5^\circ,\qquad x=\boxed{\tfrac{315}{2}}.\]

Show solution 2 – Coordinates and the bisector direction

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(20,0)$, $C=(20,26)$, $D=(0,26)$, so $T=(0,6)$. The unit vector from $T$ toward $A$ is $(0,-1)$, and from $T$ toward $C=(20,26)$, i.e. along $(20,20)$, it is $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$. Because $\angle ATM=\angle MTC$ and $M$ is inside angle $ATC$, the ray $TM$ has the direction of the sum of these unit vectors: \[\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}-1\right)\ \parallel\ \bigl(1,\ 1-\sqrt2\bigr).\] Meeting the line $y=0$: from $T=(0,6)$ we move $\frac{6}{\sqrt2-1}=6(\sqrt2+1)$ to the right, so $M=(6+6\sqrt2,0)$, which is on segment $AB$ as $6+6\sqrt2<20$.

The ray $MB$ points in direction $(1,0)$ and the ray $MT$ in direction $(-1,\sqrt2-1)$. Since $\tan 22.5^\circ=\sqrt2-1$, the ray $MT$ makes an angle of $22.5^\circ$ with the ray $MA$ (direction $(-1,0)$), and so $\angle BMT=180^\circ-22.5^\circ=157.5^\circ$. Thus $x=\boxed{\tfrac{315}{2}}$.

Problem 17

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 44: Area Ratios and Cevian Geometry · Cevian Partitions

In the figure below, points $D$ and $E$ lie on segments $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, respectively, such that $AC=CD=8$ and $AB=BE=10$. If $AD=4$ and segments $\overline{BE}$ and $\overline{CD}$ intersect at $F$, find the area of non-convex quadrilateral $ACFB$.

Show the answer

$7\sqrt{15}$

Show solution 1 – Two isosceles triangles and Menelaus

The angle at $A$. Triangle $ACD$ is isosceles with $CA=CD=8$ and base $AD=4$; the foot of the altitude from $C$ is the midpoint of $AD$, so $\cos A=\frac{2}{8}=\frac14$ and $\sin A=\frac{\sqrt{15}}{4}$.

The point $E$. Triangle $ABE$ is isosceles with $BA=BE=10$; the foot of the altitude from $B$ is the midpoint of $AE$, so $AE=2\cdot10\cos A=5$, hence $EC=3$. Also $DB=10-4=6$.

Areas. $[ABC]=\frac12\cdot AB\cdot AC\sin A=\frac12\cdot10\cdot8\cdot\frac{\sqrt{15}}{4}=10\sqrt{15}$. Menelaus' theorem in triangle $ABE$ with the line $C,F,D$ gives \[\frac{AD}{DB}\cdot\frac{BF}{FE}\cdot\frac{EC}{CA}=1\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac46\cdot\frac{BF}{FE}\cdot\frac38=1\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{BF}{FE}=4 .\] Triangles with the same apex $C$ and bases on line $BE$ have areas in the ratio of the bases, so $[BFC]=\frac45[BEC]$, and $[BEC]=\frac{EC}{AC}[ABC]=\frac38\cdot10\sqrt{15}$. Hence $[BFC]=\frac45\cdot\frac{15\sqrt{15}}{4}=3\sqrt{15}$. The non-convex quadrilateral $ACFB$ is triangle $ABC$ with triangle $BFC$ removed: \[[ACFB]=10\sqrt{15}-3\sqrt{15}=\boxed{7\sqrt{15}}.\]

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Put $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(10,0)$, so $D=(4,0)$. Since $CA=CD$, the point $C$ is on the perpendicular bisector $x=2$ of $AD$; with $AC=8$, $C=(2,2\sqrt{15})$ (taking $C$ above the line). Since $BA=BE$ and $E$ lies on $AC$, write $E=\lambda C=(2\lambda,2\sqrt{15}\lambda)$; then $BE^2=(10-2\lambda)^2+60\lambda^2=100$ gives $64\lambda^2=40\lambda$, $\lambda=\frac58$, so $E=\left(\frac54,\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{4}\right)$.

$F$ is the intersection of $CD$ and $BE$. Line $CD$: $(4,0)+s(-2,2\sqrt{15})$; line $BE$: $(10,0)+r\left(-\frac{35}{4},\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{4}\right)$. Equal $y$: $2s=\frac{5r}{4}$; equal $x$: $4-2s=10-\frac{35r}{4}$, i.e. $4-\frac{5r}{4}=10-\frac{35r}{4}$, so $r=\frac45$, $s=\frac12$ and $F=(3,\sqrt{15})$.

The shoelace formula for $A(0,0)$, $C(2,2\sqrt{15})$, $F(3,\sqrt{15})$, $B(10,0)$: \[[ACFB]=\frac12\left|\,(2\sqrt{15}-6\sqrt{15})+(0-10\sqrt{15})+0+0\,\right|=\frac12\cdot14\sqrt{15}=\boxed{7\sqrt{15}}.\]
