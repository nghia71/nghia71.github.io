Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms

We call a polynomial $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ good if there exist polynomials $f_1,\ldots,f_n$ in the same $n$ variables such that $f=x_1^2f_1+x_2^2f_2+\cdots+x_n^2f_n$. Let $f$ be a polynomial of degree less than $n/2$. Prove that if the polynomial $(x_1+\cdots+x_n)f$ is good, then $f$ itself is also good.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Squarefree parts and a commutator identity)

We work with real (or rational) coefficients; complex coefficients reduce to this by taking real and imaginary parts.

Reduction. Every monomial of $x_1^2f_1+\dots+x_n^2f_n$ is divisible by some $x_i^2$, and conversely a sum of such monomials is good. So $g$ is good iff its squarefree part $\tilde g$ (the sum of its terms whose monomials are squarefree) is $0$, and $g-\tilde g$ is always good. For a set $S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ write $x_S=\prod_{i\in S}x_i$. Let $s=x_1+\dots+x_n$. Then $sf=s\tilde f+s(f-\tilde f)$, the second part is good, and in $s\cdot x_S=\sum_{i\in S}x_ix_S+\sum_{i\notin S}x_{S\cup\{i\}}$ the first sum is good. Hence the squarefree part of $sf$ is $U(\tilde f)$, where $U$ is the linear map \[U(x_S)=\sum_{i\notin S}x_{S\cup\{i\}} .\] $U$ raises the degree by $1$, so $U(\tilde f)=0$ means $U(g_d)=0$ for each homogeneous component $g_d$ of $\tilde f$. As $\deg f<n/2$, every $d$ here satisfies $2d<n$. So it suffices to show: if $2d<n$ and $g$ is a squarefree form of degree $d$ with $U(g)=0$, then $g=0$.

The down map. Use the inner product in which the monomials $x_S$ are orthonormal, and let $D(x_T)=\sum_{i\in T}x_{T\setminus\{i\}}$. Then $\langle Ug,h\rangle=\langle g,Dh\rangle$ (both sides count the pairs $S\subset T$ with $|T\setminus S|=1$, weighted by the coefficients). For $|S|=d$, \[DU(x_S)=(n-d)\,x_S+\sum_{i\notin S,\ j\in S}x_{S\cup\{i\}\setminus\{j\}},\qquad UD(x_S)=d\,x_S+\sum_{j\in S,\ i\notin S}x_{S\setminus\{j\}\cup\{i\}},\] so $DU-UD=(n-2d)\,\mathrm{id}$ on forms of degree $d$. Hence for such $g$, \[\|Ug\|^2=\langle DUg,g\rangle=\langle UDg,g\rangle+(n-2d)\|g\|^2=\|Dg\|^2+(n-2d)\|g\|^2\ge(n-2d)\|g\|^2 .\] If $2d<n$ and $Ug=0$, then $g=0$. Therefore $\tilde f=0$, i.e. $f$ is good.

Solution 2 (Induction on the number of variables)

Reduction. Every monomial of $x_1^2f_1+\dots+x_n^2f_n$ is divisible by some $x_i^2$, and conversely a sum of such monomials is good; so a polynomial is good iff its squarefree part (the sum of its terms with squarefree monomials) is $0$. Let $A_n$ be the ring of polynomials in $x_1,\dots,x_n$ modulo $x_1^2,\dots,x_n^2$ (we set every $x_i^2=0$ and keep the squarefree terms); then $f$ is good iff $f=0$ in $A_n$. In $A_n$, $f$ equals its squarefree part $\tilde f$, whose homogeneous components $g_d$ have $2d<n$ because $\deg f<n/2$. With $s=x_1+\dots+x_n$, the hypothesis says $s\tilde f=0$ in $A_n$; multiplication by $s$ raises the degree by $1$, so $s\,g_d=0$ for every $d$. It therefore suffices to show that $s\,g=0$ in $A_n$ forces $g=0$ for every squarefree form $g$ of degree $d$ with $2d+1\le n$. We prove more:

Claim. Let $s_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$. If $d,k\ge0$, $2d+k\le n$, $g$ is a squarefree form of degree $d$ and $s_n^k\,g=0$ in $A_n$, then $g=0$.

Induction on $n$. For $k=0$ there is nothing to prove; this covers $n=0$. Let $n\ge1$, $k\ge1$, and write $g=g_0+x_ng_1$ where $g_0$ (degree $d$) and $g_1$ (degree $d-1$, absent if $d=0$) do not involve $x_n$. With $s'=s_{n-1}$ we have $s_n^k=s'^k+k\,x_ns'^{k-1}$ in $A_n$ (all other binomial terms contain $x_n^2$), so \[s_n^kg=s'^kg_0+x_n\bigl(s'^kg_1+k\,s'^{k-1}g_0\bigr).\] Every element of $A_n$ is uniquely $P+x_nQ$ with $P,Q\in A_{n-1}$, so $s_n^kg=0$ means \[s'^kg_0=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad s'^kg_1=-k\,s'^{k-1}g_0\quad\text{in }A_{n-1}.\] If $2d+k\le n-1$: the induction hypothesis gives $g_0=0$; then $s'^kg_1=0$ with $2(d-1)+k\le n-1$, so $g_1=0$.

If $2d+k=n$: multiplying the second equation by $s'$ gives $s'^{k+1}g_1=-k\,s'^kg_0=0$, and $2(d-1)+(k+1)=n-1$, so $g_1=0$. Then $k\,s'^{k-1}g_0=0$, so $s'^{k-1}g_0=0$, and $2d+(k-1)=n-1$, so $g_0=0$.

In both cases $g=0$. Applying the claim with $k=1$ to each component $g_d$ (all have $2d+1\le n$) gives $g_d=0$, so $\tilde f=0$ and $f$ is good.

Solution 3 (Isolating one coefficient by inclusion and exclusion)

We work with real (or rational) coefficients; the argument divides only by positive integers, so it works over any field of characteristic $0$.

Reduction. Every monomial of $x_1^2f_1+\dots+x_n^2f_n$ is divisible by some $x_i^2$, and conversely a sum of terms whose monomials are each divisible by some $x_i^2$ is good. So a polynomial is good if and only if its squarefree part (the sum of its terms whose monomials are squarefree) is $0$. For $S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ write $x_S=\prod_{i\in S}x_i$, and let $\tilde f=\sum_S c_Sx_S$ be the squarefree part of $f$; since $\deg f<n/2$, $c_S=0$ whenever $2|S|\ge n$.

Let $s=x_1+\dots+x_n$. The polynomial $f-\tilde f$ is good, hence so is $s(f-\tilde f)$; and $s\,x_S=\sum_{i\in S}x_ix_S+\sum_{i\notin S}x_{S\cup\{i\}}$, where the first sum is good. Therefore the squarefree part of $sf$ is $\sum_S c_S\sum_{i\notin S}x_{S\cup\{i\}}$, in which the coefficient of $x_T$ is $\sum_{i\in T}c_{T\setminus\{i\}}$. As $sf$ is good, all these coefficients vanish. Fix $d$ with $2d<n$, that is $2d+1\le n$. We obtain \[\sum_{S\subset T,\ |S|=d}c_S=0\qquad\text{for every set }T\text{ with }|T|=d+1. \tag{$*$}\] We show that $c_S=0$ for every $S$ with $|S|=d$; doing this for each $d<n/2$ gives $\tilde f=0$, so $f$ is good.

Step 1: larger sets. For a set $W$ with $|W|\ge d+1$ let $\sigma(W)=\sum_{S\subseteq W,\ |S|=d}c_S$. Add the equations $(*)$ over all $(d+1)$-subsets $T$ of $W$. A $d$-subset $S$ of $W$ lies in exactly $|W|-d$ of them (namely $T=S\cup\{i\}$ with $i\in W\setminus S$), so the sum is $(|W|-d)\,\sigma(W)=0$. As $|W|-d\ge1$, we get $\sigma(W)=0$.

Step 2: one coefficient. Fix $S$ with $|S|=d$. Because $n\ge2d+1$, there is a set $V$ of $d+1$ indices disjoint from $S$. For every $J\subseteq S$ the set $V\cup J$ has at least $d+1$ elements, so $\sigma(V\cup J)=0$ by Step 1. Hence \[0=\sum_{J\subseteq S}(-1)^{d-|J|}\,\sigma(V\cup J)=\sum_{R}c_R\sum_{\substack{J\subseteq S\\ R\subseteq V\cup J}}(-1)^{d-|J|},\] where $R$ runs over the $d$-subsets of $V\cup S$. Since $V$ and $S$ are disjoint, $R\subseteq V\cup J$ means $K\subseteq J$, where $K=R\cap S$. Writing $J=K\cup L$ with $L\subseteq S\setminus K$ and $m=|S\setminus K|=d-|K|$, the inner sum is \[\sum_{L\subseteq S\setminus K}(-1)^{m-|L|}=(1-1)^m,\] which is $0$ if $m\ge1$ and $1$ if $m=0$. Now $m=0$ means $S\subseteq R$, and as $|R|=|S|=d$ this means $R=S$. So the whole sum equals $c_S$, and $c_S=0$.

Thus every squarefree coefficient of $f$ vanishes, $\tilde f=0$, and $f$ is good.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

In a collection of $N=2^k+1$ coins, some are real and the rest are counterfeit. A special scale indicates the parity of the number of real coins in any set of coins placed on it. What is the smallest number of such “weighings” required to determine whether the collection contains more counterfeit or real coins?

Show the answer

$\boxed{2^k}$ weighings (that is, $N-1$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Merging groups; a polynomial of high degree)

Here $k\ge1$, so $N\ge3$ is odd and there is never a tie. Write $x_i=1$ if coin $i$ is real and $x_i=0$ otherwise; a weighing of a set $T$ tells us $\sum_{i\in T}x_i \bmod 2$. Weighings may depend on earlier results.

$2^k$ weighings suffice. We keep a list of groups: sets of coins known to be all of the same kind, each with a representative coin. At the start every coin is a group of size $1$. All sizes are powers of $2$. While two groups have the same size, weigh their two representatives together: the result is $0$ iff they are of the same kind. If they are, merge the two groups (the size doubles). If not, throw both groups away: they contain equally many real and counterfeit coins, so the kind in the majority among the remaining coins is still the majority kind of the whole collection (and the number of remaining coins is still odd). When all sizes are different powers of $2$, the largest group has more coins than all other groups together ($2^j>2^{j-1}+\dots+1$), so its kind is the majority kind; one more weighing (its representative alone) tells us this kind.

Each comparison reduces the number of groups by $1$ (merge) or $2$ (discard). A discard removes an even number of coins, so the number of remaining coins stays odd; hence at least one group remains at the end, say $g\ge1$ groups. Starting from $N$ groups, the number of comparisons is at most $N-g$, and it is at most $N-g-1$ if some discard occurred. If $g\ge2$, the total is at most $N-2+1=N-1$. If $g=1$ and there was no discard, all $N$ coins were merged into one group, whose size is a power of $2$; but $N=2^k+1\ge3$ is odd, so this is impossible. Hence in all cases at most $N-1=2^k$ weighings are used.

Fewer weighings do not suffice. Suppose a procedure always finishes after at most $q\le 2^k-1$ weighings. For each possible run (each leaf of the decision tree) the set of configurations $x\in\{0,1\}^N$ that follow it is described by at most $q$ conditions $\sum_{i\in T}x_i\equiv\epsilon\pmod2$. Working modulo $2$, the indicator of such a set is the product of at most $q$ factors $1+\epsilon+\sum_{i\in T}x_i$, a polynomial of degree at most $q$. The majority function $\mathrm{Maj}(x)$ (equal to $1$ when real coins are in the majority) is the sum of the indicators of the runs that answer “real”. So, as a function $\{0,1\}^N\to\mathbb{F}_2$, $\mathrm{Maj}$ is a polynomial of degree at most $q$; replacing $x_i^2$ by $x_i$ we may take it multilinear, still of degree at most $q$.

A multilinear polynomial $\sum_S c_S x_S$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ is determined by its values, and $c_S=\sum_{T\subseteq S}\mathrm{Maj}(\mathbf 1_T)$ (substituting the indicator vectors and using induction on $|S|$). Take $|S|=2^k$. Then $c_S$ is the parity of the number of subsets $T\subseteq S$ with $|T|\ge2^{k-1}+1$, namely \[\sum_{j>2^{k-1}}\binom{2^k}{j}=\frac{2^{2^k}-\binom{2^k}{2^{k-1}}}{2}.\] Adding $2^{k-1}+2^{k-1}$ in base $2$ produces exactly one carry, so $\binom{2^k}{2^{k-1}}$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$ (Kummer). Hence the count is an even number minus an odd number: it is odd, $c_S=1$, and $\mathrm{Maj}$ has degree at least $2^k>q$. Contradiction. So the minimum is $\boxed{2^k}$.

Solution 2 (The lower bound without polynomials: counting inside a cube)

Here $k\ge1$, so $N\ge3$ is odd and there is never a tie; weighings may depend on earlier results.

Upper bound. Keep groups of coins known to be of one kind, each with a representative, starting with $N$ single coins; all sizes are powers of $2$. While two groups have the same size, weigh their two representatives together (result $0$ iff same kind): merge them if they agree, discard both if not (a discarded pair of groups holds equally many coins of each kind, so the majority among the remaining coins, still an odd number, is unchanged). When all sizes are distinct powers of $2$, the largest group outweighs all others together, and one weighing of its representative tells its kind, which is the majority. Each comparison removes one group (merge) or two (discard). If $g\ge1$ groups remain, there were at most $N-g$ comparisons, and at most $N-g-1$ if a discard happened; $g=1$ without discards is impossible, as $N$ is not a power of $2$. In all cases at most $N-1=2^k$ weighings are used.

Lower bound. Suppose a procedure always finishes after at most $q\le2^k-1$ weighings. Fix $2^k$ of the coins (the set $S$) and look only at the configurations in which the remaining coin is counterfeit: they form a set $V\cong\{0,1\}^{2^k}$, a vector space over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Real coins are in the majority exactly when at least $2^{k-1}+1$ coins of $S$ are real, so the number of such configurations in $V$ is \[\sum_{j>2^{k-1}}\binom{2^k}{j}=\frac{2^{2^k}-\binom{2^k}{2^{k-1}}}{2}.\] Adding $2^{k-1}+2^{k-1}$ in base $2$ produces exactly one carry, so by Kummer's theorem $\binom{2^k}{2^{k-1}}$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$; as $4\mid2^{2^k}$, the number above is odd.

On the other hand, the configurations following one particular run of the procedure are those satisfying at most $q$ conditions $\sum_{i\in T}x_i\equiv\epsilon\pmod2$. Restricted to $V$, these are at most $q$ linear equations in $2^k$ unknowns over $\mathbb{F}_2$, so the set of configurations of $V$ following that run is empty or an affine subspace of dimension at least $2^k-q\ge1$, which has an even number of elements. The procedure is correct, so the majority-real configurations of $V$ are exactly the union of these sets over the runs answering “real”; hence their number is even. This contradiction shows that $2^k-1$ weighings are not enough, and the answer is $\boxed{2^k}$.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry

Let $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n$ be points in three-dimensional space. For a line $l$, let $B_i(l)$ denote the projection of $A_i$ onto $l$. It turns out that for each $k=1,2,\ldots,n$, there exists a line $l_k$ such that the points $B_k(l_k),B_1(l_k),B_2(l_k),\ldots,B_{k-1}(l_k),B_{k+1}(l_k),\ldots,B_n(l_k)$ are located on it in the order shown. What could $n$ be?

Show the answer

every positive integer $n$

Show the solution

Solution (A chain with rapidly growing steps)

We show that every $n$ is possible. Projecting onto a line $l$ with direction vector $\mathbf u$ orders the points by the numbers $\mathbf u\cdot A_i$ (up to reversing the direction of $l$). So we need points $A_1,\dots,A_n$ and, for each $k$, a vector $\mathbf u_k$ with \[\mathbf u_k\cdot A_k<\mathbf u_k\cdot A_1<\mathbf u_k\cdot A_2<\dots<\mathbf u_k\cdot A_n\qquad(\text{the term with index }k\text{ omitted on the right}).\] (The figure shows what is required for $k=3$, $n=5$.)

Construction. Let $A_1$ be the origin and $A_{i+1}=A_i+\mathbf e_i$ for $i=1,\dots,n-1$, where \[\mathbf e_i=10^i\,(i,\ i^2,\ 1).\] For $k=1$ take $\mathbf u_1=(0,0,1)$: then $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf e_i=10^i>0$, so $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_n$ appear in this order.

For $2\le k\le n$ put $j=k-1$ and $\mathbf u_k=\bigl(-2j,\ 1,\ j^2-\frac12\bigr)$. Then \[w_i:=\mathbf u_k\cdot\mathbf e_i=10^i\bigl((i-j)^2-\tfrac12\bigr),\] so $w_j=-\frac12\,10^j<0$, while $w_i\ge\frac12\,10^i>0$ for $i\ne j$, and $w_{j+1}=\frac12\,10^{j+1}$. Since $\mathbf u_k\cdot A_m=w_1+\dots+w_{m-1}$:

  • Consecutive points $A_m,A_{m+1}$ with $m,m+1\ne k$ differ by $w_m$ with $m\ne j$, which is positive.

  • If $k\le n-1$, the step from $A_{k-1}$ to $A_{k+1}$ is $w_j+w_{j+1}=10^j\bigl(-\frac12+5\bigr)>0$.

  • $A_k$ comes first: using $t^2\le3^t$ for $t\ge1$, \[\mathbf u_k\cdot A_k=w_j+\sum_{i<j}w_i\le-\tfrac12\,10^j+\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}10^{j-t}t^2\le10^j\Bigl(-\tfrac12+\sum_{t\ge1}\bigl(\tfrac3{10}\bigr)^t\Bigr)=10^j\Bigl(-\tfrac12+\tfrac37\Bigr)<0=\mathbf u_k\cdot A_1 .\]

So along the line through the origin with direction $\mathbf u_k$ the projections appear in the order $B_k,B_1,\dots,B_{k-1},B_{k+1},\dots,B_n$. Hence $n$ can be any positive integer.

Remark (why space is needed). Taking consecutive points of the orders shows that the vectors $\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_{n-1}$ admit a direction positive on all of them ($k=1$) and, for each $j$, a direction negative on $\mathbf e_j$ and positive on all the others ($k=j+1$). In the plane, of several vectors in an open half-plane only the two angularly extreme ones can be separated in this way, so in the plane only $n\le3$ is possible (we state this without proof; it is not needed for the answer). In space the points $(i,i^2)$ of a parabola play the role of vectors in convex position, and the factors $10^i$ make each step dominate the previous ones.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Prove that for all sufficiently large $N$, the following statement is true. If a connected graph has $N$ vertices, and all their degrees are at most $1000$, then all its vertices can be colored three colors such that the number of edges with identically colored endpoints is divisible by $101$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (One hundred switches worth $+1$ each)

Let $\Delta=1000$. We show that $N\ge100(1+\Delta^2)$ is enough.

Far-apart vertices. A vertex has at most $\Delta+\Delta(\Delta-1)\le\Delta^2$ vertices at distance $1$ or $2$. Pick any vertex, delete it together with all vertices at distance at most $2$ from it, and repeat. Each step deletes at most $1+\Delta^2$ vertices, so we can pick $100$ vertices $v_1,\dots,v_{100}$ any two of which are at distance at least $3$: they are not adjacent and have no common neighbour. Since the graph is connected and $N\ge2$, each $v_i$ has a neighbour.

The colouring. Colour all vertices arbitrarily with colours $1,2,3$, then change the colours near the chosen vertices: $v_i$ gets colour $1$, one chosen neighbour $w_i$ of $v_i$ gets colour $2$, and all other neighbours of $v_i$ get colour $3$. The sets $\{v_i\}\cup N(v_i)$ are pairwise disjoint, so these instructions do not conflict. Let $M$ be the number of monochromatic edges.

Switches. If we recolour $v_i$ from $1$ to $2$, only edges at $v_i$ are affected; before, none of them is monochromatic (no neighbour has colour $1$), afterwards exactly one is (the edge $v_iw_i$). So the count goes up by exactly $1$. Recolouring several $v_i$ has additive effect: the $v_i$ are pairwise non-adjacent, so their edge sets are disjoint, and recolouring $v_j$ does not change the colours of the neighbours of $v_i$ (no $v_j$ is a neighbour of $v_i$).

Choose $t\in\{0,1,\dots,100\}$ with $t\equiv-M\pmod{101}$ and recolour $v_1,\dots,v_t$. The number of monochromatic edges becomes $M+t$, which is divisible by $101$.

Solution 2 (Local repairs and subset sums modulo 101)

Let $N\ge100(1+1000^2)$. A vertex has at most $1000^2$ vertices at distance $1$ or $2$, so by repeatedly picking a vertex and deleting it together with everything at distance at most $2$ from it, we find $100$ vertices $v_1,\dots,v_{100}$ pairwise at distance at least $3$ (not adjacent, no common neighbour); each has a neighbour, since the graph is connected and $N\ge2$. Start from any colouring.

Making every switch useful. For a vertex $v$ let $n_1,n_2,n_3$ be the numbers of its neighbours of each colour. Changing the colour of $v$ from $a$ to $b$ changes the number of monochromatic edges by $n_b-n_a$. If, for some $v_i$, the numbers $n_1,n_2,n_3$ are all congruent modulo $101$, recolour one neighbour of $v_i$ (it has one): this changes one $n$ by $-1$ and another by $+1$, so they are no longer all congruent. These repairs touch only neighbours of the $v_i$, and the neighbourhoods are disjoint and contain no $v_j$, so they do not interfere. Now for every $i$ some colour $b$ gives $\delta_i=n_b-n_{c(v_i)}\not\equiv0\pmod{101}$ (otherwise all $n$ would be congruent to $n_{c(v_i)}$). Recolouring $v_i$ changes only edges at $v_i$; these edge sets are disjoint for different $i$, and no $v_j$ is a neighbour of $v_i$, so the effects of recolouring different $v_i$ add up.

Subset sums. Let $p=101$ and let $\Sigma_j$ be the set of residues of sums $\sum_{i\in I}\delta_i$ over subsets $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,j\}$; $\Sigma_0=\{0\}$. Then $\Sigma_j=\Sigma_{j-1}\cup(\Sigma_{j-1}+\delta_j)$. If $\Sigma_{j-1}$ is not all of $\mathbb{Z}_p$, then $\Sigma_j\ne\Sigma_{j-1}$: otherwise $\Sigma_{j-1}$ would be closed under adding $\delta_j$, and since $\delta_j$ generates $\mathbb{Z}_p$ ($p$ prime), it would be everything. Hence $|\Sigma_j|\ge\min(p,j+1)$, and $\Sigma_{100}=\mathbb{Z}_{101}$.

So some set of switches changes the count by $\equiv-M\pmod{101}$, where $M$ is the current count, and after them the number of monochromatic edges is divisible by $101$.

Solution 3 (Starting from one colour: the degrees as switch values)

Let $\Delta=1000$ and $p=101$. We show that $N\ge10^9$ is enough. We use only the colours $1$ and $2$ (the third colour is allowed but not needed). Since the graph is connected and $N\ge2$, every vertex has degree at least $1$.

Lemma (subset sums). If $r_1,\dots,r_{100}$ are residues modulo $101$, none of them $0$, then every residue is $\sum_{i\in I}r_i$ for some $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,100\}$. Indeed, let $\Sigma_j$ be the set of residues of such sums with $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,j\}$; so $\Sigma_0=\{0\}$ and $\Sigma_j=\Sigma_{j-1}\cup(\Sigma_{j-1}+r_j)$. If $\Sigma_j=\Sigma_{j-1}$, then $\Sigma_{j-1}$ is closed under adding $r_j$, so with any $a$ it contains $a,a+r_j,a+2r_j,\dots$; since $101$ is prime and $r_j\not\equiv0$, these are all residues. Hence $|\Sigma_j|\ge\min(101,j+1)$, and $\Sigma_{100}$ contains all $101$ residues.

Start with every vertex coloured $1$; then all $M$ edges are monochromatic.

Case A: at least $100\cdot1001$ vertices have degree not divisible by $101$. Pick one of them, delete it and its at most $1000$ neighbours from the list of candidates, and repeat. After $99$ picks at most $99\cdot1001$ candidates have been deleted, so a $100$th pick is possible. This gives pairwise non-adjacent vertices $v_1,\dots,v_{100}$ with $\deg v_i\not\equiv0\pmod{101}$. Recolour $v_i$ with colour $2$ for all $i$ in a set $I$. An edge with no endpoint among these vertices stays monochromatic. An edge at a recoloured $v_i$ has its other endpoint outside $\{v_j:j\in I\}$ (the $v_j$ are pairwise non-adjacent), still of colour $1$, so it is no longer monochromatic. Hence the number of monochromatic edges becomes $M-\sum_{i\in I}\deg v_i$. By the lemma we can choose $I$ with $\sum_{i\in I}\deg v_i\equiv M\pmod{101}$, and then this number is divisible by $101$.

Case B: fewer than $100\cdot1001$ vertices have degree not divisible by $101$. Let $B$ be the set of these vertices, so $|B|\le100\,099$; every vertex outside $B$ has degree divisible by $101$. Let $C$ be the set of vertices that are not in $B$ and have no neighbour in $B$. At most $|B|(1+\Delta)\le100\,099\cdot1001<1.01\cdot10^8$ vertices lie outside $C$, so $|C|>8.9\cdot10^8$.

A vertex has at most $1+\Delta+\Delta(\Delta-1)\le1+\Delta^2$ vertices at distance at most $2$ from it (itself included). Pick $u_1\in C$ and a neighbour $w_1$ of $u_1$; delete from $C$ every vertex at distance at most $2$ from $u_1$ or from $w_1$, at most $2(1+10^6)$ vertices; and repeat. After $99$ rounds at most $198(1+10^6)<2.1\cdot10^8$ vertices have been deleted from $C$, so we obtain $100$ edges $u_1w_1,\dots,u_{100}w_{100}$ with these properties:

  • $u_i\notin B$, and $w_i\notin B$ because $u_i$ has no neighbour in $B$; so $\deg u_i$ and $\deg w_i$ are divisible by $101$;

  • for $i<j$, $u_j$ is at distance at least $3$ from $u_i$ and from $w_i$, so its neighbour $w_j$ is at distance at least $2$ from them. Hence the four vertices $u_i,w_i,u_j,w_j$ are distinct and no edge joins $\{u_i,w_i\}$ to $\{u_j,w_j\}$.

For a set $I$ of indices, recolour both $u_i$ and $w_i$ with colour $2$ for all $i\in I$. The edge $u_iw_i$ stays monochromatic. Any other edge at $u_i$ or $w_i$ ($i\in I$) goes to a vertex that is not recoloured (by the second property), still of colour $1$, so it stops being monochromatic; there are $(\deg u_i-1)+(\deg w_i-1)$ such edges. Edges with no recoloured endpoint do not change. So the number of monochromatic edges becomes \[M-\sum_{i\in I}\bigl(\deg u_i+\deg w_i-2\bigr)\equiv M+2|I|\pmod{101}.\] Since $2\cdot51=102\equiv1\pmod{101}$, the number $t\in\{0,1,\dots,100\}$ with $t\equiv-51M\pmod{101}$ satisfies $2t\equiv-M$. Taking $I=\{1,\dots,t\}$ makes the number of monochromatic edges divisible by $101$.

In both cases the required colouring exists, for every connected graph with $N\ge10^9$ vertices and all degrees at most $1000$.

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

I have $100$ coins, $3$ of which are counterfeit (they weigh the same, but are lighter than the real ones). How can I find $35$ real coins in two weighings on a regular two-pan scale?

Show the answer

a scheme: first $47$ against $47$; see the solution

Show the solution

Solution (Forty-seven against forty-seven)

First weighing. Put $47$ coins (pile $A$) against $47$ coins (pile $B$); the $6$ remaining coins form pile $C$. A pan is lighter exactly when it holds more counterfeit coins.

Case 1: balance. Then $A$ and $B$ hold equally many counterfeits: either none (all three are in $C$) or one each (and one in $C$). Split $A=A_1\cup A_2$ with $|A_1|=23$, $|A_2|=24$, and $B=B_1\cup B_2$ with $|B_1|=35$, $|B_2|=12$. Second weighing: $B_1$ (left, $35$ coins) against $A_1\cup B_2$ (right, $35$ coins).

  • If there is no counterfeit in $A\cup B$, the pans balance.

  • Otherwise let $\alpha\in A$, $\beta\in B$ be the counterfeits. If $\beta\in B_2$ the right pan is lighter. If $\beta\in B_1$: the pans balance when $\alpha\in A_1$, and the left pan is lighter when $\alpha\in A_2$.

Left lighter: $\beta\in B_1$, $\alpha\in A_2$, so the $23+12=35$ coins of $A_1\cup B_2$ are real. Right lighter: $\beta\in B_2$, so the $35$ coins of $B_1$ are real. Balance: either $A\cup B$ is all real, or $\alpha\in A_1$ and $\beta\in B_1$; in both cases the $24+12=36$ coins of $A_2\cup B_2$ are real.

Case 2: imbalance, say $A$ is lighter (the other case is symmetric). Then $A$ has more counterfeits than $B$, so $B$ has at most one; the counts $(A,B,C)$ are $(1,0,2)$, $(2,0,1)$, $(3,0,0)$ or $(2,1,0)$. Split $B=B_1\cup B_2\cup B_3$ with $|B_1|=12$, $|B_2|=18$, $|B_3|=17$. Second weighing: $B_1\cup C$ (left, $18$ coins) against $B_2$ (right, $18$ coins).

  • $(1,0,2)$ or $(2,0,1)$: counterfeits only on the left, so the left pan is lighter.

  • $(3,0,0)$: balance.

  • $(2,1,0)$: the counterfeit of $B$ makes the left pan lighter if it is in $B_1$, the right pan lighter if it is in $B_2$, and gives balance if it is in $B_3$.

Left lighter: either $B$ is all real, or $C$ is real and the counterfeit of $B$ is in $B_1$; in both cases the $18+17=35$ coins of $B_2\cup B_3$ are real. Right lighter: the counterfeit of $B$ is in $B_2$ and $C$ is real, so $B_1\cup B_3\cup C$ ($12+17+6=35$ coins) are real. Balance: $(3,0,0)$, or $(2,1,0)$ with the counterfeit in $B_3$; in both cases $B_1\cup B_2\cup C$ ($36$ coins) are real.

In every case we have found at least $35$ coins that are certainly real.
