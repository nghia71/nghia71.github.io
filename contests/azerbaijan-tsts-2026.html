IMO TST Day 1

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 6, Problem 1; 2026-romania-team-selection-tests: Day 2, Problem 4

The sunshine cost of a sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of integers is the largest possible value of \[\left|(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_i)-a_j\right|\] as $i$ and $j$ vary over all integers $1,2,\ldots,100$.

Determine the smallest possible sunshine cost over all sequences $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of pairwise distinct integers.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For one fixed prefix sum, which entries of the sequence could be farthest from it? Try a short sequence with positive and negative entries and see what changes when you reorder it.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the smallest and largest entries to trap all prefix sums in one interval. The entries after the first are differences of two points of that interval, so count how many distinct integers those differences could supply. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Combine the bound on the interval length with the minimum spread of the entries. If a cost just below your candidate forces both bounds to be tight, identify the possible first entry and test its distance to the opposite extreme. To construct a candidate, look for a sequence whose prefix sums stay in a short interval placed so that both extreme entries are about equally far from it.
Common trap
The first entry is itself the first prefix sum, and the interval argument for differences of two prefix sums applies only to later entries.
Show the answer

$75$

Show solution 1 – Prefix sums are trapped in a short interval

Write $P_i=a_1+\cdots+a_i$, let $A=\max a_j$, $B=\min a_j$, $D=A-B$, and let $M$ be the sunshine cost. As the $a_j$ are $100$ distinct integers, $D\ge99$.

Construction with cost $75$. Take $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, followed by the pairs $j,-j$ for $j=1,2,\dots,50$, $j\ne25$ (that is $2+2\cdot49=100$ distinct integers). The prefix sums are $-25$ after $a_1,a_2$ and after every complete pair, and $-25+j$ in the middle of the pair $j,-j$; so they range over $[-25,25]$, while the terms range over $[-50,50]$. Hence $M=\max(25-(-50),\,50-(-25))=75$.

Lower bound. For every $i$ and $j$, $|P_i-a_j|\le M$; with $j$ giving $A$ and $B$ this means \[P_i\in I:=[A-M,\ B+M]\qquad(1\le i\le100),\] an interval of length $L=2M-D$ (so $L\ge0$). For $i\ge2$, $a_i=P_i-P_{i-1}$ is a difference of two points of $I$, so $|a_i|\le L$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-L,L]$, which forces $2L+1\ge99$, i.e. $L\ge49$.

Suppose $M\le74$. Then $D=2M-L\le148-49=99$, so $D=99$, and then $L=2M-99\ge49$ forces $M=74$, $L=49$. So $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ are exactly the integers $-49,\dots,49$, and since all $100$ terms are consecutive integers ($D=99$), $a_1=50$ or $a_1=-50$. But then $|P_1-a_j|=|a_1-a_j|=99>74$ for $a_j=\mp49$. Contradiction. So $M\ge75$.

The smallest sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.

Show solution 2 – Two extreme pairs, and the steps of the partial sums

Write $S_i=a_1+\dots+a_i$ and let \[m=\min_ja_j,\quad \mu=\max_ja_j,\quad s=\min_iS_i,\quad \sigma=\max_iS_i .\] Let $M$ be the sunshine cost. Since $|S_i-a_j|\le M$ for all $i,j$, \[\sigma-m\le M\qquad\text{and}\qquad \mu-s\le M. \tag{1}\] (In fact $M=\max(\sigma-m,\ \mu-s)$.)

Lower bound: $M\ge75$. The $a_j$ are $100$ distinct integers, so $\mu-m\ge99$. Put $L=\sigma-s$. Adding the two inequalities in (1), \[2M\ge L+(\mu-m)\ge L+99. \tag{2}\] For $i\ge2$ we have $a_i=S_i-S_{i-1}$, a difference of two numbers of the interval $[s,\sigma]$, so $|a_i|\le L$.

Case $L\ge50$. Then (2) gives $2M\ge149$, and since $M$ is an integer, $M\ge75$.

Case $L\le49$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-L,L]$, which contains only $2L+1\le99$ integers. Hence $L=49$ and $\{a_2,\dots,a_{100}\}=\{-49,-48,\dots,49\}$, so $m\le-49$ and $\mu\ge49$. Moreover $a_1$ is different from all of these, so $|a_1|\ge50$. If $a_1\ge50$, then $\sigma\ge S_1=a_1\ge50$ and (1) gives $M\ge\sigma-m\ge50+49=99$. If $a_1\le-50$, then $s\le S_1\le-50$ and $M\ge\mu-s\ge99$.

In both cases $M\ge75$.

A sequence with cost $75$. Take \[-25,\ 0,\ \ 1,-1,\ 2,-2,\ \dots,\ 24,-24,\ \ 26,-26,\ 27,-27,\ \dots,\ 50,-50,\] that is, $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, followed by the pairs $(j,-j)$ for $j=1,\dots,24$ and for $j=26,\dots,50$. These are $2+48+50=100$ distinct integers (all integers from $-50$ to $50$ except $25$). After each complete pair the partial sum returns to $-25$, and inside the pair $(j,-j)$ it equals $-25+j$. So all partial sums lie in $[-25,25]$, while all terms lie in $[-50,50]$; hence \[|S_i-a_j|\le25+50=75\quad\text{for all }i,j,\] with equality for $S_i=-25$, $a_j=50$. The cost of this sequence is $75$.

The smallest possible sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.

Remark. For $n=4k$ terms the same argument gives $3k$; for other $n$ the two cases do not balance (e.g. the minimum is $5$ for $n=6$).

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 2, Problem 2; 2026-japan-tst: Problem 11

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with $AB<AC$. Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. The perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$ intersects $\Gamma$ again at $D\neq B$. Let $Q$ be the point on $\Gamma$, different from $B$, such that $AB=AQ$. Lines $BQ$ and $AC$ intersect at $R$. Line $DR$ intersects $\Gamma$ again at $S\neq D$. Segment $BC$ intersects the circumcircle of triangle $QRS$ at $T$. Lines $TQ$ and $RS$ intersect at $X$. Prove that $CX$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Use $AB=AQ$ to compare the arcs cut off by the two chords at $A$. Mark the angles that these arcs determine and check where $R$ lies on the two chords.
Hint 2: key idea
Reflect $Q$ across $AC$ and use directed angles, including angles in $\Gamma$, to test whether the reflected point is on the circle through $Q,R,S$. Compare the powers or positions of $B$ and $C$ relative to that circle to identify the intersection on segment $BC$. (LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing)
Hint 3: approach
Use the internal and external bisectors at $C$ of the angle between $CQ$ and $CB$ to obtain a harmonic division on $BQ$, then project it orthogonally onto $AC$. With $AC$ as coordinate axis and $D$ the reflection of the orthocenter, write the heights of the intersections of $TQ$ with $RD$ and with the altitude from $C$.
Common trap
Finding a point of the circle on the line $BC$ leaves open whether it is the only intersection on the specified segment.
Show solution 1 – $T$ is the reflection of $Q$, then a harmonic division

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of $ABC$; $AB<AC$ gives $\gamma<\beta$. Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (so $B,E,D$ are collinear) and $H$ the orthocenter. We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: where $Q$ and $R$ are. The chords $AQ$ and $AB$ are equal, so the arcs $AQ$ and $AB$ (not containing $C$) both measure $2\gamma$. Since $2\gamma<2\beta$, the point $Q$ lies on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, strictly between $A$ and $C$, and the arc $QC$ measures $2\beta-2\gamma$. Hence chord $BQ$ crosses chord $AC$, so $R$ lies inside both segments, and by inscribed angles \[\angle ABQ=\angle AQB=\angle QCA=\angle ACB=\gamma .\] In particular $\angle BRA=180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma=\beta$.

Step 2: $T$ is the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$. Since $\angle QCA=\angle ACB$, line $CA$ bisects angle $QCB$, so the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CQ^*=CQ$. The chord $CQ$ subtends the arc $2\beta-2\gamma$ and the chord $CB$ the arc $2\alpha$; as $\beta-\gamma<\alpha$ (because $\beta<90^\circ<\alpha+\gamma$), $CQ^*=CQ<CB$ and $Q^*$ lies on segment $BC$.

Now $RQ=RQ^*$ and $QQ^*\perp AC$, so $QQ^*\parallel BD$. Hence \[\measuredangle RQ^*Q=\measuredangle Q^*QR=\measuredangle(BD,BQ)=\measuredangle DBQ=\measuredangle DSQ=\measuredangle RSQ,\] using the isosceles triangle $RQQ^*$, the parallel lines, the circle $\Gamma$ through $B,D,S,Q$, and the collinearity of $D,R,S$. So $Q^*$ lies on the circle $(QRS)$. It remains to see that $Q^*$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on this circle. Since $R$ lies between $B$ and $Q$, the power of $B$ with respect to the circle is $BR\cdot BQ>0$, so $B$ is outside it. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $QQ^*$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $AC$. Because $\angle QCA=\gamma<90^\circ$ and $\angle QRC=\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the point $M$ lies on ray $CA$ and on ray $RC$, hence strictly between $R$ and $C$: the points $R$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the chord $QQ^*$. As $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $QQ^*$, the chord is seen from $R$ under the angle $\angle QRQ^*=2\angle QRM=2\beta$ and from $C$ under $\angle QCQ^*=2\gamma$. A point on the other side of the chord from $R$ lies inside the circle exactly when it sees the chord under an angle greater than $180^\circ-2\beta$; here $2\gamma>180^\circ-2\beta$ because $\alpha<90^\circ$. So $C$ is inside the circle, and segment $BC$, which joins an outside point to an inside point, meets the circle exactly once. Hence $T=Q^*$. Consequently line $TQ$ is the line $QQ^*$, perpendicular to $AC$.

Step 3: a harmonic division on $AC$. Recall that $M$, the midpoint of $QQ^*$, lies on $AC$. The line $CR=CA$ is the internal bisector of angle $QCB$; let the external bisector meet line $BQ$ at $R'$. Then $(B,Q;R,R')=-1$. Project line $BQ$ onto line $AC$ in the direction perpendicular to $AC$: $B\mapsto E$, $Q\mapsto M$, $R\mapsto R$, and $R'\mapsto C$ (since $CR'\perp CA$). Parallel projection preserves cross-ratios, so $(E,M;R,C)=-1$, i.e. \[\frac{\overline{RM}}{\overline{RE}}=-\frac{\overline{CM}}{\overline{CE}}. \tag{1}\]

Step 4: $X$ lies on the altitude from $C$. Use coordinates with $AC$ as $x$-axis; points of $AC$ have abscissae $r,c,m,e$ (for $R,C,M,E$). As the triangle is acute, $D$ is the reflection of $H$ in $AC$: $D=(e,d)$, $H=(e,-d)$. The point $X$ lies on line $QQ^*$, i.e. $X=(m,x)$, and on line $RD$: \[x=d\,\frac{m-r}{e-r}.\] The altitude from $C$ is line $CH$; it meets line $QQ^*$ at $X'=(m,x')$ with $x'=-d\,\dfrac{m-c}{e-c}$. By (1) these are equal, so $X=X'$ lies on line $CH$, and $CX\perp AB$.

Show solution 2 – Three pairs of similar right triangles

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of $ABC$; $AB<AC$ gives $\gamma<\beta$. Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (so $B,E,D$ are collinear). We use $\measuredangle$ for directed angles between lines, modulo $180^\circ$.

Where $Q$ and $R$ are. The chords $AQ$ and $AB$ are equal, so the arcs $AQ$ and $AB$ (not containing $C$) both measure $2\gamma$. Since $2\gamma<2\beta$, the point $Q$ lies on the arc $AC$ not containing $B$, strictly between $A$ and $C$, and the arc $QC$ measures $2\beta-2\gamma$. Hence chord $BQ$ crosses chord $AC$, so $R$ lies inside both segments, and by inscribed angles \[\angle ABQ=\angle AQB=\angle QCA=\angle ACB=\gamma .\] In particular $\angle BRA=180^\circ-\alpha-\gamma=\beta$.

$T$ is the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$. Since $\angle QCA=\angle ACB$, line $CA$ bisects angle $QCB$, so the reflection $Q^*$ of $Q$ in $AC$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CQ^*=CQ$. The chord $CQ$ subtends the arc $2\beta-2\gamma$ and the chord $CB$ the arc $2\alpha$; as $\beta-\gamma<\alpha$ (because $\beta<90^\circ<\alpha+\gamma$), $CQ^*=CQ<CB$ and $Q^*$ lies on segment $BC$.

Now $RQ=RQ^*$ and $QQ^*\perp AC$, so $QQ^*\parallel BD$. Hence \[\measuredangle RQ^*Q=\measuredangle Q^*QR=\measuredangle(BD,BQ)=\measuredangle DBQ=\measuredangle DSQ=\measuredangle RSQ,\] using the isosceles triangle $RQQ^*$, the parallel lines, the circle $\Gamma$ through $B,D,S,Q$, and the collinearity of $D,R,S$. So $Q^*$ lies on the circle $(QRS)$. It remains to see that $Q^*$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on this circle. Since $R$ lies between $B$ and $Q$, the power of $B$ with respect to the circle is $BR\cdot BQ>0$, so $B$ is outside it. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $QQ^*$, i.e. the foot of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $AC$. Because $\angle QCA=\gamma<90^\circ$ and $\angle QRC=\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the point $M$ lies on ray $CA$ and on ray $RC$, hence strictly between $R$ and $C$: the points $R$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the chord $QQ^*$. As $AC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $QQ^*$, the chord is seen from $R$ under the angle $\angle QRQ^*=2\angle QRM=2\beta$ and from $C$ under $\angle QCQ^*=2\gamma$. A point on the other side of the chord from $R$ lies inside the circle exactly when it sees the chord under an angle greater than $180^\circ-2\beta$; here $2\gamma>180^\circ-2\beta$ because $\alpha<90^\circ$. So $C$ is inside the circle, and segment $BC$, which joins an outside point to an inside point, meets the circle exactly once. Hence $T=Q^*$, and line $TQ$ is perpendicular to $AC$ at $M$ and parallel to $BD$; moreover $M$ lies strictly between $R$ and $C$.

Positions on $AC$. Since $\angle BRA=\beta<90^\circ$, the foot $E$ lies on ray $RA$; hence $R$ lies strictly between $E$ and $M$. Since the triangle is acute, $E$ lies inside segment $AC$, and so do $M$ and $R$.

Three similarities. All of the following triangles are right-angled at $M$ or $E$.

  • $RMX\sim RED$: the lines $RX$ and $RD$ coincide (both are line $RS$), so the angles at $R$ are equal. Hence $\dfrac{MX}{ED}=\dfrac{RM}{RE}$.

  • $RMQ\sim REB$: $Q$, $B$ lie on line $BQ$ through $R$. Hence $\dfrac{RM}{RE}=\dfrac{MQ}{EB}$.

  • $CMQ\sim CEB$: $M$ and $E$ lie on ray $CA$ and $\angle MCQ=\gamma=\angle ECB$. Hence $\dfrac{MQ}{EB}=\dfrac{CM}{CE}$.

Together, $\dfrac{MX}{ED}=\dfrac{CM}{CE}$, so the right triangles $CMX$ and $CED$ are similar (legs in proportion), and \[\angle ACX=\angle MCX=\angle ECD=\angle ACD .\]

The side of $X$. As $R$ is between $E$ and $M$, and $MX\parallel ED$ with $X$, $D$ on a line through $R$, the point $X$ is the image of $D$ under a homothety with centre $R$ and negative ratio; so $X$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$, i.e. $X$ is on the same side as $B$.

Conclusion. The points $B$ and $C$ lie on the same arc $AD$ of $\Gamma$ (the order on $\Gamma$ is $A,D,C,B$), so $\angle ACD=\angle ABD=\angle ABE=90^\circ-\alpha$. Let $F$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$; it lies on side $AB$, on the same side of $AC$ as $B$, and $\angle ACF=90^\circ-\alpha$. The rays $CX$ and $CF$ lie on the same side of line $CA$ and make the same angle with ray $CA$, so they coincide. Hence $X$ lies on the altitude $CF$, and $CX\perp AB$.

Show solution 3 – Coordinates: $T$ is the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$

Put $A=(0,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $B=(u,v)$ with $v>0$, and let $r=u^2+v^2=AB^2$ and $n=(1-u)^2+v^2=BC^2=r+1-2u$. Since $AB<AC$, $r<1$. The angles at $A$ and $C$ are acute, so $0<u<1$, and the angle at $B$ is acute: $\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=r-u>0$. Put $w=\frac{r-u}{v}>0$. We shall use the identity \[rn=(r-u)^2+v^2,\tag{$*$}\] which holds because both sides equal $r^2+r-2ur$ (use $u^2+v^2=r$).

$\Gamma$, $D$, $R$, $Q$. The circle $\Gamma$ is $x^2+y^2=x+wy$ (it passes through $A$, $C$, and through $B$ as $u+wv=r$). The perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$ is $x=u$; on it $\Gamma$ gives $y^2-wy+u^2-u=0$, with roots $v$ and $w-v$, so $D=(u,w-v)$. For a point of $\Gamma$ we have $AZ^2=x^2+y^2=x+wy$; so the points of $\Gamma$ at distance $\sqrt r$ from $A$ are its points on the line $x+wy=r$. Thus $BQ$ is the line $x+wy=r$, and $R=(r,0)$, with $0<r<1$.

Let $Q=(q,z)$. Substituting $x=r-wy$ in $x^2+y^2=r$ gives $(1+w^2)y^2-2rwy+r^2-r=0$, with roots $v$ and $z$; so $vz=-\frac{r(1-r)}{1+w^2}$. By $(*)$, $1+w^2=\frac{v^2+(r-u)^2}{v^2}=\frac{rn}{v^2}$, hence \[z=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}<0,\qquad q=r-wz .\]

The point $T$. Let $T'=(q,-z)$ be the reflection of $Q$ in $AC$. The line $BC$ is $vx+(1-u)y=v$, and since $vw+1-u=r-u+1-u=n$, \[vq-(1-u)z=vr-(vw+1-u)z=vr-nz=vr+v(1-r)=v,\] so $T'$ lies on line $BC$. Its height $-z=\frac{v(1-r)}{n}$ lies strictly between $0$ and $v$, because $1-r<n$ is equivalent to $u<r$. So $T'$ lies strictly between $B$ and $C$.

The circle $\omega$ through $Q,R,T'$. As $Q$ and $T'$ are symmetric in $AC$, the centre of $\omega$ is on $AC$, say $(c_0,0)$, and $\omega$ is $f(x,y):=x^2+y^2-2c_0x+2c_0r-r^2=0$ (it passes through $R$). Using $q^2+z^2=AQ^2=r$, the condition $f(Q)=0$ reads $r-r^2=2c_0(q-r)=-2c_0wz$, so \[2c_0=\frac{r(1-r)}{-wz}=\frac{rn}{vw}=\frac{rn}{r-u}.\]

$S$ lies on $\omega$. Let $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-x-wy$, so $\Gamma$ is $g=0$, and $\ell=f-g=(1-2c_0)x+wy+2c_0r-r^2$, an affine function. Since $S\in\Gamma$, we have $S\in\omega$ if and only if $\ell(S)=0$. Write the points of line $DR$ as $R+\mu(D-R)$. Then $g(R+\mu(D-R))$ is a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $|D-R|^2$, constant term $g(R)=r^2-r$, and roots $\mu=1$ (the point $D$) and $\mu_S$ (the point $S$); so $\mu_S=\frac{r^2-r}{|D-R|^2}$. As $\ell$ is affine, $\ell(S)=\ell(R)+\mu_S\bigl(\ell(D)-\ell(R)\bigr)$. Here $\ell(R)=r-r^2$ and, using $2c_0(u-r)=-rn$ and $wv=r-u$, \[\ell(D)-\ell(R)=(1-2c_0)(u-r)+w(w-v)=(u-r)+rn+w^2-wv=rn+w^2-2wv .\] By $(*)$, $|D-R|^2=(u-r)^2+(w-v)^2=rn+w^2-2wv$ as well. Hence $\ell(S)=r-r^2+(r^2-r)=0$, and $S\in\omega$. So $\omega$ is the circle $QRS$.

$T=T'$. The power of $C$ with respect to $\omega$ is $f(1,0)=(1-r)(1+r-2c_0)$, where \[1+r-2c_0=\frac{(1+r)(r-u)-r(r+1-2u)}{r-u}=-\frac{u(1-r)}{r-u}<0;\] so $C$ lies inside $\omega$. Hence $C$ lies between the two points where line $BC$ meets $\omega$; one of them is $T'$, on the side of $B$, so the other one is beyond $C$. Thus $T'$ is the only point of segment $BC$ on $\omega$, and $T=T'=(q,-z)$.

The point $X$. The line $TQ$ is $x=q$. The line $RS$ is the line $RD$, which is not vertical as $u\ne r$; its point with $x=q$ is $R+\mu(D-R)$ with $\mu=\frac{q-r}{u-r}=\frac{-wz}{-vw}=\frac zv$, so $X=\bigl(q,\frac{z(w-v)}{v}\bigr)$.

Conclusion. We have $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(q-1)+z(w-v)$. With $q-1=r-1-wz$ this is $u(r-1)+z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)$. Now $v\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=(r-u)(1-u)-v^2=r-u-ur+u^2-(r-u^2)=-u(1+r-2u)=-un$, so \[z\bigl(w(1-u)-v\bigr)=-\frac{v(1-r)}{n}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{un}{v}\Bigr)=u(1-r),\] and $\overrightarrow{CX}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=u(r-1)+u(1-r)=0$. Hence $CX$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

Problem 3

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Counting Integer Configurations · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 6, Problem 3

For positive integers $n$ and $k$, let $f_k(n)$ denote the remainder when $n$ is divided by $2^k-1$. A positive integer $n$ is grouse if \[f_1(n)\leq f_2(n)\leq f_3(n)\leq f_4(n)\leq\cdots\]

  • (a) Prove that there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • (b) Prove that there exists a positive integer $M$ such that there are at most $\dfrac{M}{2025^{2025}}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compute the first few remainders for several small $n$ near a power of two. What happens to every later remainder once the divisor exceeds $n$?
Hint 2: key idea
Write $d_i=2^i-1$ and replace each remainder by the largest multiple of $d_i$ at most $n$. To build a sequence of candidate multiples, start at $d_k$ and round upward to the nearest multiple of each successive smaller divisor. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the accumulated upward jumps to verify that the first multiple in your chain has the desired remainders. For $2^k\le n<2^{k+1}$, show that the chain is a lower bound for the multiples associated with a grouse number, and test $(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ as the chain value for $i\ge k/2$. Use the index near $k/2$ to bound how many candidates lie in each block, then sum over the blocks.
Common trap
In checking a constructed number, also account for the remainders with indices greater than the starting index $k$.
Show solution 1 – A greedy chain of multiples; grouse numbers hug the powers of two

Write $d_i=2^i-1$. Always $f_1(n)=0$, and $f_i(n)=n$ as soon as $d_i>n$.

The greedy chain. Fix $k\ge1$. Put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,k-2,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$. Then $x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_k$ and $x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. More generally, starting the chain at any $x_i$, summing these steps gives \[0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i. \tag{1}\]

(a) We show that $x_1$ is grouse; since $x_1\ge x_k=2^k-1$ and $k$ is arbitrary, this gives infinitely many grouse numbers.

  • For $i\le k$: $x_i$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and $0\le x_1-x_i<d_i$ by (1), so $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$. As $x_i$ does not increase with $i$, the values $f_1(x_1)\le f_2(x_1)\le\dots\le f_k(x_1)$ are non-decreasing.

  • For $i>k$: by (1), $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge x_1-x_k=f_k(x_1)$.

(b) Claim. If $2^k\le n<2^{k+1}$ and $n$ is grouse, then $n>2^{k+1}-3\cdot2^{k/2}$. (For $k=0$ this is trivial, so let $k\ge1$.)

Step 1: $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$ for $1\le i\le k$. The number $n-f_i(n)$ is the largest multiple of $d_i$ not exceeding $n$. For $i=k$: $d_k\le n$, so $n-f_k(n)\ge d_k=x_k$. If $n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, then, since $n$ is grouse, $n-f_i(n)\ge n-f_{i+1}(n)\ge x_{i+1}$, and as $n-f_i(n)$ is a multiple of $d_i$, the definition of $x_i$ gives $n-f_i(n)\ge x_i$.

Step 2: $x_i=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ for $k/2\le i\le k$. For $i=k$ both sides equal $d_k$. Suppose it holds for $i+1$, where $k/2\le i<k$. The number $y=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)$ is a multiple of $d_i$ and \[y-x_{i+1}=(2^i-1)(2^{k+1-i}-1)-(2^{i+1}-1)(2^{k-i}-1)=2^i-2^{k-i},\] which lies in $[0,d_i)$ because $i\ge k-i$ and $k-i\ge1$. So $y$ is the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is at least $x_{i+1}$, i.e. $y=x_i$.

Step 3. Take $i=\lceil k/2\rceil$. Then $n\ge n-f_i(n)\ge x_i=2^{k+1}-\bigl(2^i+2^{k+1-i}\bigr)+1$. If $k=2t$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=3\cdot2^t=3\cdot2^{k/2}$; if $k=2t-1$, then $2^i+2^{k+1-i}=2^{t+1}=2\sqrt2\cdot2^{k/2}<3\cdot2^{k/2}$. This proves the claim.

Counting. By the claim, the block $[2^k,2^{k+1})$ contains at most $3\cdot2^{k/2}$ grouse numbers. Let $M=2^{b+1}$. The grouse numbers below $M$ lie in the blocks with $k=0,1,\dots,b$, so there are at most \[\sum_{k=0}^{b}3\cdot2^{k/2}<\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\,2^{(b+1)/2}=\frac{3}{\sqrt2-1}\sqrt M\] of them. This is at most $M/2025^{2025}$ as soon as $\sqrt M\ge3\cdot2025^{2025}/(\sqrt2-1)$, which holds for $b$ large enough. Such an $M$ proves (b).

Show solution 2 – Part (b) by forbidden residue classes and the Chinese remainder theorem

(a) Write $d_i=2^i-1$; then $f_i(n)=n$ whenever $d_i>n$. Fix $k\ge1$, put $x_k=d_k$ and, for $i=k-1,\dots,1$, let $x_i$ be the smallest multiple of $d_i$ that is $\ge x_{i+1}$; so $x_{i+1}\le x_i\le x_{i+1}+d_i-1$. Summing these steps, $0\le x_1-x_i\le\sum_{j<i}(d_j-1)=2^i-2i<d_i$. Hence for $i\le k$ we get $f_i(x_1)=x_1-x_i$, which does not decrease as $i$ grows; and for $i>k$ we get $x_1\le d_k+2^k-2k<2^{k+1}-1\le d_i$, so $f_i(x_1)=x_1\ge f_k(x_1)$. Thus $x_1\ge2^k-1$ is grouse, and as $k$ is arbitrary there are infinitely many grouse numbers.

(b) We only use the inequality $f_{2k-1}(n)\le f_{2k+1}(n)$, which every grouse number satisfies.

Forbidden classes. Let $k\ge2$, $a=2^{2k-1}-1$ and $b=2^{2k+1}-1$. Recall $\gcd(2^u-1,2^v-1)=2^{\gcd(u,v)}-1$; since $\gcd(2k-1,2k+1)=1$, the numbers $a,b$ are coprime. For each pair of integers $0\le j<i\le a-1$ (there are $\binom a2$ pairs; note $j<a<b$) the Chinese remainder theorem gives one residue class modulo $ab$ consisting of the $n$ with $n\equiv i\pmod a$ and $n\equiv j\pmod b$, that is, $f_{2k-1}(n)=i>j=f_{2k+1}(n)$. No grouse number lies in these $\binom a2$ classes. So the fraction of classes modulo $ab$ that may contain grouse numbers is at most \[1-\frac{\binom a2}{ab}=1-\frac{a-1}{2b}=1-\frac{2^{2k-2}-1}{2^{2k+1}-1}=1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8(2^{2k+1}-1)}\le1-\frac18+\frac{7}{8\cdot31}=\frac{28}{31},\] using $2^{2k+1}-1\ge31$.

Many independent moduli. Choose $k_1=2$ and $k_j=\prod_{i<j}(2k_i-1)(2k_i+1)$ for $j\ge2$. Each $k_j$ is at least $2$, and every earlier number $2k_i\pm1$ divides $k_j$, so it is coprime to $2k_j\pm1$; also $\gcd(2k_j-1,2k_j+1)=1$. Hence the exponents $2k_j\pm1$ $(j=1,\dots,s)$ are pairwise coprime, and so are the numbers $a_j=2^{2k_j-1}-1$, $b_j=2^{2k_j+1}-1$. Put $M=\prod_{j=1}^{s}a_jb_j$.

Counting. The numbers $0,1,\dots,M-1$ represent each class modulo $M$ once. If such a number is grouse, its class modulo each $a_jb_j$ is not forbidden. By the Chinese remainder theorem the classes modulo $M$ correspond to tuples of classes modulo $a_1b_1,\dots,a_sb_s$, so at most \[\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{28}{31}\,a_jb_j=\Bigl(\frac{28}{31}\Bigr)^{s}M\] numbers below $M$ are grouse. Choosing $s$ with $(28/31)^s\le2025^{-2025}$ gives at most $M/2025^{2025}$ grouse numbers less than $M$.

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Periodic and Cyclic Behavior Modulo $n$ · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: VAIMO 2, Problem 1; 2026-romania-team-selection-tests: Day 1, Problem 2

Let $n$ and $k$ be positive integers such that $n<k<2n$. Suppose $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ are positive integers such that $2^{a_1}+2^{a_2}+\cdots+2^{a_k}$ is divisible by $2^n-1$.

Show that at least three of $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ have the same remainder when divided by $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Replace an exponent by its remainder modulo $n$ when working modulo $2^n-1$. Keep a count of how many terms land at each remainder.
Hint 2: key idea
Assume every remainder occurs at most twice and view the resulting sum as a sum of powers of two with coefficients $0,1,2$. Use the restriction on $k$ to bound this positive multiple of $2^n-1$. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Use the bound to identify the only candidate multiple. Compare the parity of that multiple with the lowest coefficient, then subtract the low term and halve to repeat the argument. Compare the resulting coefficient count with the given restriction on $k$.
Common trap
The coefficients may equal two, so ordinary uniqueness of binary digits cannot be applied without first controlling the low coefficient at each stage.
Show solution 1 – Reading off binary digits

Since $2^n\equiv1\pmod{2^n-1}$, the residue of $2^a$ modulo $2^n-1$ depends only on $a\bmod n$: if $a\equiv r\pmod n$ with $0\le r\le n-1$, then $2^a\equiv2^r$. For $0\le r\le n-1$ let $c_r$ be the number of indices $i$ with $a_i\equiv r\pmod n$. Then $\sum_r c_r=k$ and \[S:=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}c_r2^r\equiv2^{a_1}+\cdots+2^{a_k}\equiv0\pmod{2^n-1}.\] Suppose, for contradiction, that every $c_r\le2$. Then $0<S\le2(2^0+\cdots+2^{n-1})=2(2^n-1)$, with equality only if all $c_r=2$, i.e. $k=2n$, which is excluded. So $S$ is a positive multiple of $2^n-1$ below $2(2^n-1)$, i.e. $S=2^n-1$.

Claim. If $c_0,\dots,c_{m-1}\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $\sum_{r<m}c_r2^r=2^m-1$, then all $c_r=1$. Induction on $m$; $m=1$ is clear. For $m\ge2$, the right side is odd, so $c_0$ is odd, $c_0=1$. Subtracting $1$ and halving gives $\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}c_r2^{r-1}=2^{m-1}-1$, and the induction hypothesis gives $c_1=\cdots=c_{m-1}=1$.

By the claim with $m=n$, every $c_r=1$, so $k=\sum c_r=n$, contradicting $k>n$. Hence some residue class contains at least three of the $a_i$.

Show solution 2 – A number and its complement

As $2^n\equiv1\pmod{2^n-1}$, replace each $a_i$ by its remainder $r_i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ modulo $n$; this does not change the sum modulo $2^n-1$. Assume no remainder occurs three times. Let $U$ be the set of remainders that occur at least once and $W\subseteq U$ the set of those occurring twice, and put \[A=\sum_{r\in U}2^r,\qquad B=\sum_{r\in W}2^r .\] Then $k=|U|+|W|$ and $2^n-1\mid A+B$. Both $A$ and $B$ are at most $2^n-1$, and they are not both equal to $2^n-1$ (that would mean $k=2n$); also $A+B\ge k>0$. Hence $A+B=2^n-1$.

Now $2^n-1$ is the $n$-bit number $11\ldots1$ in binary, and $A$ is an $n$-bit number whose $1$-bits are the positions in $U$. So $B=(2^n-1)-A$ is the number whose $1$-bits are exactly the positions not in $U$ (subtracting from $11\ldots1$ produces no borrows). But the $1$-bits of $B$ are the positions in $W\subseteq U$. A set contained both in $U$ and in its complement is empty, so $W=\varnothing$, $B=0$ and $A=2^n-1$, i.e. $U$ is everything. Then $k=|U|=n$, contradicting $n<k$. So some remainder occurs at least three times.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 1, Problem 2; 2026-japan-tst: Problem 4

There are $2025$ white balls and $2025$ black balls arranged in a row. A balanced segment is a contiguous nonempty segment of balls that contains the same number of white balls as black balls. A balanced removal is the operation of selecting a balanced segment $S$, removing all the balls in $S$, and shifting left the balls that were to the right of $S$ so that the remaining balls form a contiguous row. Determine the smallest positive integer $N$ satisfying the following property: for every configuration of balls and for every integer $k$ satisfying $0\leq k\leq 2025$, there exists a sequence of at most $N$ balanced removals after which there are exactly $2k$ remaining balls.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Record a running count of white minus black balls at the gaps. Describe what must be true at the two ends of a balanced segment.
Hint 2: key idea
Look at the consecutive zero gaps that enclose the desired removal length when measured from the start. Between them the running count stays on one side of zero, which helps locate a balanced segment of a prescribed even length. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Subtract the earlier zero-gap index from the desired length, then slide a window of that remaining length between the two zero gaps and compare the signs of its extreme sums. Use the possible changes of the window sum to locate a balanced window, and check that its removal is compatible with the rest of your plan. For the lower bound, look for configurations in which some value of $k$ cannot be reached with too few removals.
Common trap
The running count for a sliding even-length window changes by at most two, and its parity matters when arguing that it hits zero.
Show the answer

$N=2$

Show the solution – Cut along the zero-level walk

Let $n=2025$. Number the gaps between balls $0,1,\dots,2n$ (gap $t$ is just after the $t$-th ball) and let $P_t$ be the number of white minus the number of black balls among the first $t$ balls. So $P_0=P_{2n}=0$, consecutive values differ by $1$, and the segment of balls between gaps $i<j$ is balanced exactly when $P_i=P_j$.

$N\ge2$. Take the row $BB\,\underbrace{W\cdots W}_{n}\,\underbrace{B\cdots B}_{n-2}$ and $k=1$. One balanced removal leaving $2$ balls would remove all balls except either the first two, or the last two, or the first and the last. These pairs are $BB$, $BB$ and $BB$ (as $n-2\ge2$), never one white and one black, while the removed segment, and hence the remaining pair, would have to be balanced. So one removal is not enough.

$N\le2$. Fix a row and $k$, and put $L=2n-2k$, the number of balls to remove. If $L=0$ do nothing. Otherwise let $z$ be the last gap with $z<L$ and $P_z=0$ (it exists, since $P_0=0$), and let $z'$ be the first gap with $z'\ge L$ and $P_{z'}=0$ (it exists, since $P_{2n}=0$). Between $z$ and $z'$ the walk does not return to $0$, so $P_t$ has constant sign for $z<t<z'$; say $P_t\ge0$ on $[z,z']$ (the other case is symmetric).

Put $\ell=L-z$; it is even and $0<\ell\le z'-z$. For $z\le i\le z'-\ell$ consider $g(i)=P_{i+\ell}-P_i$. Then \[g(z)=P_{z+\ell}\ge0,\qquad g(z'-\ell)=-P_{z'-\ell}\le0,\] all values of $g$ are even (since $\ell$ is even), and $g(i+1)-g(i)\in\{-2,0,2\}$. Hence $g(i)=0$ for some $i$: the balls between gaps $i$ and $i+\ell$ form a balanced segment of length $\ell$ lying between gaps $z$ and $z'$.

Now remove this segment, and then (if $z>0$) the first $z$ balls, which form a balanced segment because $P_z=P_0$ and are still contiguous at the start of the row. Altogether $\ell+z=L$ balls are removed in at most two balanced removals, leaving $2k$ balls.

Hence the smallest $N$ is $\boxed{2}$.

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure · Homogenization and Normalization

For all positive real numbers $a,b,c$ with $a^2b+a^2c+b^2a+b^2c+c^2a+c^2b=1$, show that \[\frac{ab+bc+ca}{1+2abc}\leq\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{4}.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Expand $(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)$ and compare its terms with the six terms in the condition. Mark the extra multiples of $abc$.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the normalization to replace the constants by symmetric expressions and clear the positive denominator. Look for a homogeneous difference that can be grouped by squared differences between pairs of variables. (LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Express the cleared difference in the elementary symmetric sums, then regroup it into cyclic terms using $ab(a+b-c)(a-b)^2$ and its rotations. Common trap
The factors of the form $a+b-c$ need not all be nonnegative.
Show solution 1 – Homogenize, then a sum of squares

Write $p=a+b+c$, $q=ab+bc+ca$, $r=abc$. Then \[a^2b+a^2c+b^2a+b^2c+c^2a+c^2b=pq-3r=1,\qquad (a+b)(b+c)(c+a)=pq-r .\] Hence $1+2abc=(pq-3r)+2r=pq-r=(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)>0$, and \[\frac{ab+bc+ca}{1+2abc}=\frac{q\cdot 1}{pq-r}=\frac{q(pq-3r)}{pq-r}.\] So the inequality is equivalent to the homogeneous inequality $4q(pq-3r)\le p^2(pq-r)$, that is, \[F:=p^3q-4pq^2-p^2r+12qr\ \ge 0 .\] Expanding both sides, one checks the identity \[F=\sum_{\text{cyc}} ab(a+b-c)(a-b)^2=S_c(a-b)^2+S_a(b-c)^2+S_b(c-a)^2,\] where $S_c=ab(a+b-c)$, $S_a=bc(b+c-a)$, $S_b=ca(c+a-b)$. By symmetry we may assume $a\ge b\ge c$. Then $S_c\ge 0$ and $S_b\ge 0$ (because $a+b\ge c$ and $c+a\ge b$). If $S_a\ge 0$ we are done. Otherwise, since $a-c\ge b-c\ge 0$ we have $(c-a)^2\ge (b-c)^2$, so \[S_a(b-c)^2+S_b(c-a)^2\ \ge\ (S_a+S_b)(b-c)^2,\] and \[S_a+S_b=c\bigl[b(b+c-a)+a(c+a-b)\bigr]=c\bigl[(a-b)^2+c(a+b)\bigr]\ge 0 .\] Thus $F\ge 0$, which proves the inequality. Equality holds for $a=b=c=6^{-1/3}$.

Show solution 2 – A generalized Schur inequality

Write $p=a+b+c$, $q=ab+bc+ca$, $r=abc$. Expanding, the constraint reads $pq-3r=1$, and $1+2abc=(pq-3r)+2r=pq-r=(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)$. Multiplying the left side by $1=pq-3r$, the claim becomes $4q(pq-3r)\le p^2(pq-r)$, i.e. $F=p^3q-4pq^2-p^2r+12qr\ge 0$.

Expanding, $F$ is exactly \[F=a^2(b+c)(a-b)(a-c)+b^2(c+a)(b-c)(b-a)+c^2(a+b)(c-a)(c-b).\] Assume $a\ge b\ge c$ and put $x=a^2(b+c)$, $y=b^2(c+a)$, $z=c^2(a+b)$. Then \[x-y=a^2b+a^2c-ab^2-b^2c=ab(a-b)+c(a^2-b^2)\ge 0 .\] Now \[F=x(a-b)(a-c)-y(a-b)(b-c)+z(a-c)(b-c).\] The last term is $\ge 0$. For the first two, $x\ge y$ and $a-c\ge b-c\ge 0$ give $x(a-b)(a-c)\ge y(a-b)(b-c)$. Hence $F\ge 0$, as required, with equality for $a=b=c$.

Show solution 3 – Ordered substitution: every coefficient is positive

Write $p=a+b+c$, $q=ab+bc+ca$, $r=abc$. Expanding, the constraint reads $pq-3r=1$, and $1+2abc=(pq-3r)+2r=pq-r>0$. Multiplying the left side of the claim by $1=pq-3r$, the inequality becomes $4q(pq-3r)\le p^2(pq-r)$, that is, \[F:=p^3q-4pq^2-p^2r+12qr\ \ge\ 0 .\] This is homogeneous of degree $5$, and $F$ is symmetric in $a,b,c$, so we may assume $a\ge b\ge c$. Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

A graph is good if its edges can be colored with $2$ colors so that no cycle has two consecutive edges of the same color. What is the maximum number of edges in a good graph with $1000$ vertices?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try colouring a short cycle under the rule. Record how the colours must change as you travel around it and which cycle lengths this permits.
Hint 2: key idea
Look at two distinct cycles that share an edge. Use a path of one cycle outside the other to find three internally disjoint paths between the same vertices, then inspect their first colours. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Use the resulting restriction on shared cycle edges to bound how many cycles can appear for a given edge count. Delete one edge per cycle and apply the forest bound to the remainder. For a construction, look for a graph in which every step of this bound holds with equality, and check which cycles it contains.
Common trap
The colouring condition applies to every cycle, including ones formed by taking portions of two given cycles.
Show the answer

$1332$

Show solution 1 – No theta subgraph, so the cycles are edge-disjoint

Construction. Take a vertex $v$ and $333$ cycles of length $4$ that pairwise share only $v$; this uses $1+333\cdot 3=1000$ vertices and $333\cdot4=1332$ edges. Colour the edges of each $4$-cycle alternately red and blue. Every cycle of this graph is one of the $4$-cycles (a cycle cannot pass from one $4$-cycle to another, because they meet only at $v$), so the graph is good.

Bound. Fix a good colouring of a good graph $G$ with $n=1000$ vertices.

(i) Every cycle has even length at least $4$. Along a cycle the colours alternate, so its length is even; a simple graph has no cycle of length $2$.

(ii) No two distinct cycles share an edge. Suppose cycles $C_1\ne C_2$ share an edge. A cycle contained in another cycle equals it, so $C_2$ has an edge outside $C_1$. Follow $C_2$ from this edge in both directions until it first meets vertices of $C_1$; we get a path $R$ of $C_2$, with no inner vertex on $C_1$, joining two vertices $x,y$ of $C_1$. Here $x\ne y$: otherwise $C_2$ would meet $C_1$ only at $x$, but $C_2$ contains an edge of $C_1$. Now $x$ and $y$ are joined by three paths with no common inner vertices: the two arcs of $C_1$ and $R$. At $x$ these three paths start with three edges, two of which, say of the paths $R_1,R_2$, have the same colour. Then $R_1\cup R_2$ is a cycle in which these two edges are consecutive (both at $x$) and of the same colour, a contradiction.

(iii) Counting. Let $k$ be the number of cycles of $G$. Delete one edge from each cycle. Every cycle of $G$ has lost an edge, so the remaining graph has no cycle; a forest on $n$ vertices has at most $n-1$ edges. Hence $|E|-k\le n-1$. By (i) and (ii) the $k$ cycles are edge-disjoint and each has at least $4$ edges, so $4k\le |E|$. Therefore \[|E|\le n-1+k\le n-1+\frac{|E|}{4},\qquad\text{so}\qquad |E|\le \frac{4(n-1)}{3}=1332 .\] The maximum number of edges is $\boxed{1332}$.

Show solution 2 – Blocks of the graph

Construction: take $333$ four-cycles sharing one common vertex and no other vertices ($1000$ vertices, $1332$ edges), and colour each four-cycle alternately. Every cycle of this graph is one of the four-cycles, so the graph is good.

Bound. Recall that a block of a graph is a maximal connected subgraph without a cut vertex; every edge lies in exactly one block, every cycle lies inside one block, and a block with at least $3$ vertices is $2$-connected. If the graph has $c$ connected components, then $\sum_B(|V(B)|-1)=n-c$ over all blocks $B$ (this is the usual count in the block–cut-vertex tree of each component).

Let $B$ be a block with at least $3$ vertices and let $vu$, $vw$ be two edges of $B$ at a vertex $v$. Since $B$ is $2$-connected, $B-v$ is connected, so there is a $u$–$w$ path in $B$ avoiding $v$; together with $vu$ and $vw$ it is a cycle in which $vu$ and $vw$ are consecutive. Hence $vu$ and $vw$ have different colours, so $v$ has at most $2$ edges in $B$. A $2$-connected graph in which all degrees are at most $2$ is a cycle, and it has even length because its colours alternate; so $|V(B)|\ge 4$.

So every block is a single edge (contributing $1$ edge and $|V(B)|-1=1$) or an even cycle of length $L\ge4$ (contributing $L$ edges and $|V(B)|-1=L-1\ge 3$). If there are $k$ cycle blocks, \[|E|=\sum_B(|V(B)|-1)+k=n-c+k,\qquad 3k\le \sum_B(|V(B)|-1)=n-c .\] Thus $|E|\le (n-1)+\frac{n-1}{3}=999+333=1332$, so the answer is $\boxed{1332}$.

Hint 2: key idea

Fix $m$ and apply this to $n=mq$ with a prime $q>|f(m)|+m$, separating the possible matching divisors according to whether they contain $q$. Hint 3: approach
Use the large prime to narrow the possible integer values of $f(m)$, then use strong induction and the residues already taken by proper divisors of $m$. Compare two such inputs in the divisor set of $mqr$ for distinct primes $q,r$.
Common trap
The residue from the divisor $n$ itself is $0$, and it also has to be covered.
Show the answer

$f(n)=-n$ for all $n$

Show the solution – Residues as a bijection, and two large primes

$f(n)=-n$ works: for $d\mid n$ take $e=d$; then $d+f(e)=0$.

Now let $f$ satisfy the condition; write $D(n)$ for the set of positive divisors of $n$ and $\tau(n)=|D(n)|$.

Step 1: a bijection. Fix $n$. The numbers $-d$ $(d\in D(n))$ are $\tau(n)$ different residues modulo $n$ (the divisors below $n$ are distinct and nonzero modulo $n$, and $-n\equiv0$). Each of them must be congruent to some $f(e)$ with $e\in D(n)$, and there are only $\tau(n)$ such values. Hence \[\{f(e)\bmod n:\ e\in D(n)\}=\{-d\bmod n:\ d\in D(n)\},\quad\text{and the }f(e)\ (e\in D(n))\text{ are pairwise incongruent mod }n. \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $f(m)=0$ or $f(m)=-d$ for some $d\in D(m)$. Let $a=f(m)$ and pick a prime $q>|a|+m$; then $q\nmid m$. By (1) for $n=mq$, $a\equiv-d\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid mq$, i.e. $d=d'$ or $d=d'q$ with $d'\mid m$. In the first case $mq\mid a+d'$ and $|a+d'|\le|a|+m<mq$, so $a=-d'$. In the second case $q\mid a$, so $a=0$ (as $q>|a|$).

Step 3: $f(m)=-m$ for all $m$, by strong induction. Let $m\ge1$ and assume $f(d)=-d$ for all $d<m$ (nothing is assumed for $m=1$). By (1) for $n=m$, the values $f(d)=-d$ $(d\in D(m),\ d<m)$ already occupy all the required residues except $0$, so $f(m)\equiv0\pmod m$, and by Step 2, $f(m)\in\{0,-m\}$.

Suppose $f(m)=0$. Let $q$ be any prime not dividing $m$ and $n=mq$, so $D(n)=D(m)\cup qD(m)$. The residue $-m$ is required by (1), and $-m\not\equiv0$. It is not $f(d)=-d$ for $d<m$, $d\mid m$, nor $f(m)=0$. So $f(dq)\equiv-m\pmod{mq}$ for some $d\mid m$. By Step 2, $f(dq)$ is $0$ or $-c$ with $c\mid dq$, $0<c\le dq\le mq$; the congruence then forces $f(dq)=-m$ and $m\mid dq$, hence $m\mid d$ and $d=m$. So \[f(mq)=-m\quad\text{for every prime } q\nmid m.\] Take two different such primes $q,r$ and $N=mqr$. Both $mq$ and $mr$ divide $N$ and $f(mq)=f(mr)=-m$, contradicting the incongruence in (1) for $N$. Hence $f(m)=-m$, completing the induction.

The only function is $\boxed{f(n)=-n}$.

Show the solution – Locate $Q$ by an angle chase, then a power-of-a-point computation in coordinates

Let $T$ be the point where the tangent to $\Omega$ at $A$ meets $BC$. From $\angle B>\angle A>\angle C$ we get $AC>BC>AB$ and $\angle A<90^\circ$.

Pascal's theorem for the degenerate hexagon $AABBCC$ on $\Omega$ says that the points $AA\cap BC=T$, $AB\cap CC=F$ and $BB\cap CA=E$ are collinear. As $D$ is the reflection of $A$ in the line $EF$, which passes through $T$, we get $TD=TA$. Also $TA^2=TB\cdot TC$.

Step 2: $Q$ lies on $\Omega$. The tangents give $\theta(TA)=\theta(AB)+\theta(AC)-\theta(BC)$ and $\theta(BP)=\theta(BA)+\theta(BC)-\theta(AC)$. The reflection in $EF$ maps $A$ to $D$ and fixes $E$ and $T$, so $\theta(ED)=2\theta(EF)-\theta(AC)$ and $\theta(TD)=2\theta(EF)-\theta(TA)$. Therefore \[\angle(ED,EB)-\angle(CA,CB)=2\theta(AC)+\theta(BP)-\theta(BC)-2\theta(EF)=\theta(TA)+\theta(BC)-2\theta(EF)=\angle(TD,TB),\] where the middle equality is exactly the two tangent formulas. Since $\angle(XA,XB)=\angle(CA,CB)$, the point $X$ lies on the circle $DEB$ if and only if $\angle(XD,XB)=\angle(ED,EB)$, i.e. if and only if \[\angle(XD,XA)=\angle(TD,TB).\qquad(\ast)\] In the same way (exchange the roles of $B,E$ and $C,F$; the middle expression above is symmetric) a point $X\ne C$ of $\Omega$ lies on the circle $DFC$ if and only if $(\ast)$ holds. If $D$ lies on $BC$, $(\ast)$ says that $X$ lies on the line $AD$; then $Q$ lies on the line $AD$, $Z=D$, and we are done. Otherwise the points $X$ with $(\ast)$ form a circle $\kappa$ through $A$ and $D$ (with $A$ and $D$ removed), which meets $\Omega$ at $A$ and at one more point $Q_0$. Hence the second intersection of the circle $DEB$ with $\Omega$ and the second intersection of the circle $DFC$ with $\Omega$ are both $Q_0$; so $Q_0$ lies on both circles and $Q=Q_0$. In particular $Q\in\Omega$ and $Q\in\kappa$. The centre of $\kappa$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, which is the line $EF$.

Step 3: reduction. The line $AQ$ is the radical axis of $\Omega$ and $\kappa$. The point $Z$ lies on $BC$, so its power with respect to $\Omega$ is $\overline{ZB}\cdot\overline{ZC}$, which is also its power with respect to the circle $DBC$. Hence $A,Z,Q$ are collinear if and only if $Z$ has equal powers with respect to $\kappa$ and to the circle $DBC$, i.e. lies on their radical axis. That radical axis passes through $D$, and $Z$ lies on the line $DP$; so it suffices to prove \[\operatorname{pow}_\kappa(P)=\operatorname{pow}_{(DBC)}(P).\qquad(\ast\ast)\]

Step 4: coordinates. Take $T$ as origin, $TA=1$ (so $\Gamma$ is the unit circle) and $EF$ as the $x$-axis, with $A=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, $0<\alpha<\pi$; then $D=(\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha)$. The point $T$ is outside $\Omega$, so $B$ and $C$ lie on one ray from $T$; let $w=(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)$ be its direction and $w^\perp=(-\sin\psi,\cos\psi)$. As $TB\cdot TC=1$, we have $B=bw$, $C=w/b$ with $b=TB$. For a point $U$ of the unit circle, $|U-bw|^2=1-2b\,U\cdot w+b^2=b^2\,|U-w/b|^2$, so $UB=b\cdot UC$; in particular $AB=b\cdot AC$, and $b<1$ because $AB<AC$. So it passes through $B$ and $C$ if and only if $k=1$ and $V\cdot w=m$, i.e. $V=mw+\nu w^\perp$; it passes through a point $U$ of the unit circle if and only if $V\cdot U=1$. For $\Omega$ (through $A$) and the circle $DBC$ we get centres $V_1=mw+\nu_1w^\perp$, $V_2=mw+\nu_2w^\perp$ with \[\nu_1s=1-m\cos(\alpha-\psi),\qquad \nu_2S=m\cos(\alpha+\psi)-1 .\]

(b) The point $P$. It lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$: $P=mw+\pi w^\perp$. Tangency at $B$ means $(P-B)\cdot(V_1-B)=0$, and $P-B=(m-b)w+\pi w^\perp$, $V_1-B=(m-b)w+\nu_1w^\perp$, $(m-b)^2=h^2$. So $\pi\nu_1=-h^2$. (Here $\nu_1\ne0$: otherwise $BC$ would be a diameter and $\angle A=90^\circ$.)

(c) The circle $\kappa$. Its centre is a point $K=(c,0)$ of $EF$, so its equation is $|X|^2-2cx+2c\cos\alpha-1=0$. By the tangent-chord angle at $D$, $(\ast)$ says that the tangent $t_D$ to $\kappa$ at $D$ satisfies $\angle(t_D,DA)=\angle(TD,TB)=\psi+\alpha$. As $DA$ is vertical and $t_D\perp KD$, this means $\theta(KD)=-(\alpha+\psi)$: the vector $D-K=(\cos\alpha-c,-\sin\alpha)$ is parallel to $(\cos(\alpha+\psi),-\sin(\alpha+\psi))$. Hence $(\cos\alpha-c)S=\sin\alpha\cos(\alpha+\psi)$, that is, \[cS=\sin\psi .\]

(d) The point $E$. The triangles $EAB$ and $EBC$ are similar (common angle at $E$, and $\angle EBA=\angle ECB$ by the tangent-chord angle), so $\frac{EA}{EB}=\frac{EB}{EC}=\frac{AB}{BC}$ and $E$ divides $AC$ externally in the ratio $AB^2:BC^2$: $E=\frac{BC^2\cdot A-AB^2\cdot C}{BC^2-AB^2}$. Since $E$ is on the $x$-axis, $BC^2\sin\alpha=AB^2\,\frac{\sin\psi}{b}=b\,AC^2\sin\psi$. With $BC^2=4h^2$ and $b\,AC^2=b\bigl(1+\tfrac1{b^2}-\tfrac2b\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr)=2\bigl(m-\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr)$ this reads \[2h^2\sin\alpha=\sin\psi\,\bigl(m-\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr).\qquad(\mathrm C)\]

(e) The computation. By (a) and (c), $\operatorname{pow}_\kappa(P)-\operatorname{pow}_{(DBC)}(P)=2G$ with \[G=V_2\cdot P-c\,P_x+c\cos\alpha-1=h^2+\pi(\nu_2+c\sin\psi)+c(\cos\alpha-m\cos\psi),\] using $V_2\cdot P=m^2+\nu_2\pi$, $P_x=m\cos\psi-\pi\sin\psi$ and $m^2-1=h^2$. Indeed, the difference of the two expressions is $h^2U+\sin\psi\,W$ with \[U=\bigl(1-m\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr)S-\bigl(m\cos(\alpha+\psi)-\cos^2\psi\bigr)s-2\sin\alpha\cos\psi+2m\sin\alpha\cos\alpha,\] \[W=\bigl(1-m\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr)(\cos\alpha-m\cos\psi)+\bigl(m-\cos(\alpha-\psi)\bigr)(\cos\psi-m\cos\alpha).\] Since $\cos(\alpha-\psi)S+\cos(\alpha+\psi)s=\sin2\alpha$ and $S-2\sin\alpha\cos\psi=-s$, we get $U=-s+s\cos^2\psi=-s\sin^2\psi$. So $h^2U+\sin\psi\,W=0$, the claim holds, $\nu_1Ss\,G=0$, and since $\nu_1,S,s\ne0$ we get $G=0$. This is $(\ast\ast)$, and by Step 3 the points $A$, $Z$, $Q$ are collinear.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Assume the displayed strict inequality never holds and choose $u,v$ with $f(u)>f(v)$. Can you find a third input so that $v$ is the geometric mean of it and $u$?
Hint 2: key idea
Extend $u,v$ to a geometric progression $(x_k)$ and apply the assumed inequality to the two neighbors of each $x_k$. This compares successive differences of the function values along the progression. (LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the first negative difference to bound all the later differences from above, then sum them to bound $f(x_k)$. Examine what that bound does as $k$ increases.
Common trap
Order $u,v$ by their function values so the progression starts with a negative difference.
Show solution 1 – Walking along a geometric progression

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \[f(a)+f(b)\le 2f\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr)\qquad\text{for all } a,b>0. \tag{1}\] Since $f$ is not constant, there are $u,v>0$ with $f(u)>f(v)$; put $\delta=f(u)-f(v)>0$ and $q=v/u$. Consider the geometric progression $x_k=uq^k$ $(k=0,1,2,\dots)$, so that $x_0=u$, $x_1=v$ and $\sqrt{x_{k-1}x_{k+1}}=x_k$ for all $k\ge1$. Hence $d_k\le-\delta$ for all $k\ge0$, and \[f(x_k)=f(x_0)+d_0+d_1+\dots+d_{k-1}\le f(u)-k\delta .\] For $k>f(u)/\delta$ the right-hand side is negative, which contradicts $f(x_k)>0$. Therefore (1) fails for some $a,b$, that is, there exist $a,b\in\mathbb{R}^+$ with $f(a)+f(b)>2f(\sqrt{ab})$.

Show solution 2 – A value close to the infimum

The values of $f$ are positive, so $m=\inf\{f(x):x>0\}$ exists and $m\ge0$. Since $f$ is not constant, it takes a value larger than $m$: choose $a>0$ with $f(a)>m$ and put $\varepsilon=\tfrac13\bigl(f(a)-m\bigr)>0$. By the definition of the infimum there is $c>0$ with $f(c)<m+\varepsilon$. Let \[b=\frac{c^2}{a}>0,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \sqrt{ab}=c .\] We claim that this pair works. Indeed, if we had $f(a)+f(b)\le2f(c)$, then \[f(b)\le 2f(c)-f(a)<2(m+\varepsilon)-(m+3\varepsilon)=m-\varepsilon<m,\] which is impossible because $m$ is a lower bound for the values of $f$. Hence $f(a)+f(b)>2f(c)=2f\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr)$, as required.

Remark. This argument is direct: it names the pair $(a,b)$, and it only uses that $f$ is bounded below and not constant.

Problem 12

At each step, Bluey chooses a row or column in which the total number of lollies is a multiple of $3$, chooses an empty cell in this row or column, and places a lolly in it. Determine the maximum number of lollies that Bluey can place.

Try a few moves on a small board and write down both line counts that change with each placement. What can happen to the line Bluey did not choose?

Show the solution – Counting visits to multiples of 3

Call rows and columns lines; $n=1000$. A line is open if its number of lollies is a multiple of $3$. Every lolly raises the count of one row and one column; the line Bluey chose was open just before. Call a placement wasteful if both its row and its column were open just before it. Let $T$ be the total number of lollies and $W$ the number of wasteful placements.

When does Bluey stop? An open line has a multiple of $3$ lollies, so at most $999<1000$, hence an empty cell. So Bluey stops exactly when no line is open. In the final position let $n_L$ be the count of line $L$; then $n_L\equiv1$ or $2\pmod 3$ for all $2n$ lines. Let $k$ be the number of lines with $n_L\equiv2$, of which $x$ are rows and $y$ are columns.

The identity $T=4n-3W-k$. For a line $L$, the number of placements made in $L$ while $L$ was open equals the number of multiples of $3$ among $0,1,\dots,n_L-1$, which is $\lceil n_L/3\rceil$. A non-wasteful placement is made while exactly one of its two lines is open, a wasteful one while both are. Hence \[\sum_L\Bigl\lceil\frac{n_L}{3}\Bigr\rceil=T+W.\] If $n_L\equiv\rho_L\in\{1,2\}\pmod3$ then $\lceil n_L/3\rceil=\frac{n_L-\rho_L}3+1$. Using $\sum_Ln_L=2T$ (each lolly is counted in one row and one column) and $\sum_L\rho_L=2n+k$, \[\frac{2T-2n-k}{3}+2n=T+W\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=4n-3W-k.\]

  • Otherwise, in the last placement the chosen line goes to a count $\equiv1$, and the other line was not open, so it had count $\equiv1$ or $\equiv2$. Moreover the sum of the row counts is $T$ and so is the sum of the column counts; modulo $3$ these sums are $n+x$ and $n+y$, so $x\equiv y\pmod3$. With $x+y=k\ge1$ this rules out $k=1$, so $k\ge2$ and $T\le4n-3-2=4n-5$.

Show solution 1 – A double root at $x=1$, then AM-GM

Let $f_n(x)=x^n+2x+1-4x^2$. For every $n$, $f_n(1)=0$ and $f_n'(1)=n+2-8=n-6$.

Show solution 1 – Neighbourhoods must not be prefixes or suffixes; induction from both ends

Yes, such an order always exists.

Reformulation. Let $V$ be the set of users and $N(v)$ the set of friends of $v$ at the start ($v\notin N(v)$). After the users of a set $S$ have swapped, a pair $u,w$ is friends if and only if it was friends at the start and an even number of $u,w$ lie in $S$, or it was not and exactly one of them lies in $S$. So a user $v\notin S$ is lonely exactly when $N(v)=S$, and a user $v\in S$ is lonely exactly when $N(v)=V\setminus S$. For an order $x_1,\dots,x_n$ the sets $S$ are the prefixes $\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$, and $N(v)=V\setminus S$ means that $N(v)$ is a suffix. Hence we need:

an order of $V$ in which no set $N(v)$ is a prefix or a suffix.

(Every $N(v)$ is nonempty and differs from $V$, so the empty and the full prefix are harmless.) Here $n=2025$ is odd.

A lemma (with one-sided conditions). Let $G$ be a graph on an odd number $n$ of vertices and let $U_{\rm pre},U_{\rm suf}$ be disjoint sets of vertices with nonempty neighbourhoods. Unless $G$ is complete, $n\ge3$ and $U_{\rm pre}=V$ or $U_{\rm suf}=V$, there is an order in which $N(v)$ is not a prefix for $v\in U_{\rm pre}$ and not a suffix for $v\in U_{\rm suf}$.

Proof by induction on $n$. For $n=1$ there is nothing to check (no vertex has a neighbour). Let $n\ge3$.

(a) $G$ complete. Then $N(v)=V\setminus\{v\}$ is a prefix exactly when $v$ is last, and a suffix exactly when $v$ is first. We need a first vertex outside $U_{\rm suf}$ and a different last vertex outside $U_{\rm pre}$. Every vertex lies outside $U_{\rm pre}$ or outside $U_{\rm suf}$; neither set is all of $V$; if the only vertex outside $U_{\rm suf}$ were also the only vertex outside $U_{\rm pre}$, all other vertices would be in both sets. So suitable distinct vertices exist.

(b) $G=K_{n-1}$ plus an isolated vertex $z$. Prefixes containing $z$ are never neighbourhoods. If some $w\ne z$ is outside $U_{\rm suf}$, use the order $z,w,\dots$: the only suffix of the form $N(v)=V\setminus\{v,z\}$ is the one with $v=w$. Otherwise $U_{\rm suf}=V\setminus\{z\}$, so $U_{\rm pre}=\varnothing$, and any order ending with $z$ works.

(c) Otherwise. Call $x$ pre-bad if some $v\in U_{\rm pre}$ has $N(v)=\{x\}$, and suf-bad similarly. We first find non-adjacent $p\ne q$ with $p$ not pre-bad and $q$ not suf-bad.

  • If there is an isolated vertex $z$ (it is neither pre-bad nor suf-bad): if some $q\ne z$ is not suf-bad, take $(z,q)$. Otherwise every $x\ne z$ has a neighbour $\ell_x\in U_{\rm suf}$ with $N(\ell_x)=\{x\}$; as $\ell_x$ is suf-bad too, its own such neighbour is $x$, so $N(x)=\{\ell_x\}$ and $x\in U_{\rm suf}$. Then $U_{\rm suf}=V\setminus\{z\}$, $U_{\rm pre}=\varnothing$, nothing is pre-bad, and we take $(p,z)$ with any $p\ne z$.

  • If there is a vertex $\ell$ of degree $1$ whose neighbour $u$ has degree at least $2$: $\ell$ is neither pre-bad nor suf-bad. If some $q\notin\{\ell,u\}$ is not suf-bad, take $(\ell,q)$; if some $p\notin\{\ell,u\}$ is not pre-bad, take $(p,\ell)$. Otherwise every $x\notin\{\ell,u\}$ is pre-bad, via a vertex $y\in U_{\rm pre}$ with $N(y)=\{x\}$; then $y\notin\{\ell,u\}$, so $y$ is also pre-bad and suf-bad, which forces $N(x)=\{y\}$, $x\in U_{\rm pre}$, and (as $x$ is suf-bad) $y\in U_{\rm suf}$: impossible since $y\in U_{\rm pre}$.

  • Otherwise every vertex of degree $1$ lies in a component $\{a,b\}$ with a single edge. If there is no vertex of degree $1$, nothing is bad and any non-adjacent pair works. Else, since $n$ is odd, some vertex $r$ lies outside these two-vertex components; $r$ has no neighbour of degree $1$, so it is neither pre-bad nor suf-bad. For a component $\{a,b\}$: if $b\notin U_{\rm pre}$, then $a$ is not pre-bad and we take $(a,r)$; if $b\in U_{\rm pre}$, then $b\notin U_{\rm suf}$, $a$ is not suf-bad and we take $(r,a)$.

Now put $p$ first and $q$ last, let $G'=G-p-q$, and let $U'_{\rm pre}$ be the set of $v\in U_{\rm pre}$, $v\ne p,q$, adjacent to $p$ but not to $q$, and $U'_{\rm suf}$ the set of $v\in U_{\rm suf}$ adjacent to $q$ but not to $p$. These are disjoint, and their vertices have nonempty neighbourhoods in $G'$ ($N(v)\ne\{p\}$ because $p$ is not pre-bad, and similarly for $q$). If $G'$ were exceptional, say complete with $U'_{\rm pre}=V(G')$ and $|V(G')|\ge 3$, then $q$ would have no neighbour at all and $G-q$ would be complete, i.e. we would be in case (b); similarly for $U'_{\rm suf}$. So by induction $G'$ has a good order, and we use $p,\ (\text{that order}),\ q$.

It is good: for $v\in U_{\rm pre}$, a nonempty prefix contains $p$, and if it contains $q$ it is all of $V$. So if $N(v)$ were a prefix, then $v\ne p$, $v\sim p$, $v\not\sim q$, and $v\ne q$ (as $p\not\sim q$); thus $v\in U'_{\rm pre}$ and $N(v)\setminus\{p\}$ would be a nonempty prefix of the order of $G'$, a contradiction. Suffixes are symmetric. $\square$

The problem. Here both conditions are required for every vertex. Call $x$ bad if $x$ has a neighbour of degree $1$.

Put $p$ first and $q$ last and apply the lemma to $G-p-q$ with $U'_{\rm pre}=\{v: v\sim p,\ v\not\sim q\}$, $U'_{\rm suf}=\{v: v\sim q,\ v\not\sim p\}$ (disjoint, with nonempty neighbourhoods in $G-p-q$ since no $N(v)$ equals $\{p\}$ or $\{q\}$). Show solution 2 – Two safe ends, a random middle and a union bound

Yes, such an order always exists.

Reformulation. Let $V$ be the set of users, $n=2025$, and $N(v)$ the set of friends of $v$ at the start; $v\notin N(v)$, and $N(v)\ne\varnothing$ since nobody is lonely. After the users of a set $S$ have swapped, two users $u,w$ are friends if and only if they were friends at the start and both or neither lie in $S$, or they were not friends and exactly one of them lies in $S$. Hence a user $v\notin S$ is lonely exactly when $N(v)=S$, and a user $v\in S$ is lonely exactly when $N(v)=V\setminus S$. Call the sets $N(v)$ and $V\setminus N(v)$ ($v\in V$) forbidden; there are at most $2n=4050$ of them. For an order $x_1,\dots,x_n$ the sets $S$ that occur are the prefixes $S_k=\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$, so we need an order in which no prefix is forbidden. The prefixes $S_0=\varnothing$ and $S_n=V$ are never forbidden, because $N(v)$ and $V\setminus N(v)$ are nonempty ($v\in V\setminus N(v)$) and differ from $V$.

Prefixes of size $1$ and $n-1$. A leaf is a user with exactly one friend, and a user is universal if they are friends with all others. The set $\{x\}$ is forbidden exactly when $N(v)=\{x\}$ for some $v$ (a leaf whose only friend is $x$), or $V\setminus N(v)=\{x\}$, which forces $v=x$ and $x$ universal. The set $V\setminus\{y\}$ is forbidden exactly when $N(v)=V\setminus\{y\}$ (then $v=y$ is universal) or $N(v)=\{y\}$. Call a user safe if they are not universal and are not the only friend of a leaf. So $S_1$ and $S_{n-1}$ are allowed exactly when $x_1$ and $x_n$ are safe.

Prefixes of size $2$ and $n-2$. Let $\mathcal F$ be the family of $2$-element sets of the form $N(v)$ with $|N(v)|=2$ or $V\setminus N(v)$ with $|N(v)|=n-2$. A $2$-element set is forbidden exactly when it lies in $\mathcal F$, and a set $V\setminus\{w,y\}$ is forbidden exactly when $\{w,y\}\in\mathcal F$ (because $N(v)=V\setminus\{w,y\}$ means $V\setminus N(v)=\{w,y\}$).

Claim: there are $s\ge2$ safe users, and $|\mathcal F|\le s$. Let $\ell$ be the number of leaves and $\mu$ the number of universal users. Every unsafe user is universal or the friend of a leaf, so at most $\mu+\ell$ users are unsafe and $s\ge n-\ell-\mu$. Every member of $\mathcal F$ comes from a user with $2$ or $n-2$ friends, who is neither a leaf nor universal; so $|\mathcal F|\le n-\ell-\mu\le s$. For $s\ge2$:

  • If there is no leaf, the unsafe users are the universal ones. Alice and Bob are not friends, so neither is universal: both are safe.

  • If there is a leaf and a universal user $u$, every leaf is a friend of $u$, so its only friend is $u$; and there is only one universal user (a leaf has one friend). So $u$ is the only unsafe user and $s=n-1$.

  • If there are leaves, no universal user, and at least two non-leaves, then $s\ge n-\ell\ge2$.

  • Not every user is a leaf: then the friendships would split the users into pairs, but $n$ is odd.

  • If there is exactly one non-leaf $w$, then $w$ has at least two friends (it is not lonely and not a leaf), all of them leaves whose only friend is $w$. Such a friend $x$ is not universal ($n>2$), and if $x$ were the only friend of a leaf, that leaf would be a friend of $x$, i.e. it would be $w$, which is not a leaf. So these at least two friends of $w$ are safe.

Choosing the ends. For a user $x$ let $i(x)$ be the number of members of $\mathcal F$ containing $x$. Then $\sum_{x\text{ safe}}i(x)\le2|\mathcal F|\le2s$. Let $p,q$ be two safe users with the smallest values of $i$; the two smallest of $s$ numbers have sum at most $\frac2s$ times their total, so $i(p)+i(q)\le4$.

A random order. Put $p$ first, $q$ last, and the other $n-2=2023$ users in between in a uniformly random order. Then $S_1=\{p\}$ and $S_{n-1}=V\setminus\{q\}$ are allowed. Further:

  • $S_2=\{p,z\}$ with $z$ uniformly random among the $2023$ middle users; it is forbidden only if $\{p,z\}\in\mathcal F$, which happens for at most $i(p)$ values of $z$. Similarly $S_{n-2}=V\setminus\{w,q\}$ with $w=x_{n-1}$ uniformly random among the middle users is forbidden with probability at most $\frac{i(q)}{2023}$. Together: at most $\frac{4}{2023}$.

  • For $3\le k\le n-3$, $S_k$ is $\{p\}$ together with a uniformly random $(k-1)$-element subset of the $2023$ middle users. A fixed forbidden set $F$ of size $k$ can equal $S_k$ only if $p\in F$ and $q\notin F$, and then with probability $1/\binom{2023}{k-1}\le1/\binom{2023}{2}$, since $2\le k-1\le 2021$.

There are at most $4050$ forbidden sets, so the probability that some prefix is forbidden is at most \[\frac{4}{2023}+\frac{4050}{\binom{2023}{2}}=\frac{4}{2023}+\frac{8100}{2023\cdot2022}<\frac{4+5}{2023}<1 .\] Hence some order has no forbidden prefix, and in that order nobody is ever lonely. The answer is Yes.

Show solution 1 – Halving the triple and counting the halvings

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$, $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. For any $g$, \[g(3,5,6)\ \mapsto\ g(4,8,7)\ \mapsto\ g(5,9,11)\ \mapsto\ g(6,10,12)=2g\,(3,5,6),\] so $T_{3m}=2^m(3,5,6)$ for all $m\ge2$, and $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}=3\cdot2^{675}$.

Parity lemma. Call a triple reduced if not all its entries are even. For a reduced triple $r$: if $r$ has exactly one even entry, $\Phi(r)$ has exactly two; if $r$ has exactly two even entries, $\Phi(r)$ is all odd; if $r$ is all odd, $\Phi(r)$ is all even. (A gcd is even only if both arguments are even; e.g. for $a$ even and $b,c$ odd all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ gives odd, even, even.) In particular $\Phi(r)$ is all even if and only if $r$ is all odd.

Reduced sequence. Let $2^{e_t}$ be the largest power of $2$ dividing all of $a_t,b_t,c_t$, $r_t=T_t/2^{e_t}$ (reduced) and $x_t=a_t/2^{e_t}\ge1$, so $a_t=2^{e_t}x_t$. Since $T_{t+1}=2^{e_t}\Phi(r_t)$, the parity lemma gives: if $r_t$ is not all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t$ and $x_{t+1}=x_t+g\ge x_t+1$ ($g$ a gcd); if $r_t$ is all odd then $e_{t+1}=e_t+\delta$ with $\delta\ge1$ and $x_{t+1}=(x_t+g)/2^\delta$.

Let $t_1<\dots<t_K$ be the times $t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}$ with $r_t$ all odd, $\delta_i$ the corresponding increments, $d_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, $s=N-1-t_K$ and $y_i=x_{t_i+1}$. By the parity lemma (one even entry $\to$ two $\to$ none) we have $t_1\le2$, $d_i\le3$ and $s\le2$. Hence $2024=t_1+\sum d_i+s\le2+3(K-1)+2$, so $K\ge675$, and $e_N=e_0+\sum\delta_i\ge K\ge675$. Put \[D=(2-t_1)+\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)=3K-2025+s\ \ (\ge0).\] Also $x_N\ge y_K+s$, $y_1\ge\frac{x_{t_1}+1}{2^{\delta_1}}\ge\frac{t_1+2}{2^{\delta_1}}$ (as $x_{t_1}\ge x_0+t_1$), and \[y_{i+1}\ge\frac{x_{t_{i+1}}+1}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}\ge\frac{y_i+d_i}{2^{\delta_{i+1}}}. \tag{1}\] If $\delta_{j+1}=\dots=\delta_i=1$, the shortfalls $z_i=3-y_i$ satisfy $z_{i+1}\le\frac{z_i+(3-d_i)}{2}$, hence \[z_i\le\frac{z_j}{2^{\,i-j}}+\frac12\sum_{j\le l<i}(3-d_l). \tag{2}\] Since $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N$, it suffices to exclude $e_N=675$ with $x_N\le2$ and $e_N=676$ with $x_N=1$. If $e_N=675$, then $K=675$, $e_0=0$ and all $\delta_i=1$. If $e_N=676$, then either $e_0=1$, $K=675$ and all $\delta_i=1$, or $e_0=0$ and one of the two cases below occurs.

$K=675$, all $\delta_i=1$. Then $D=s$. By (2) with $j=1$ and $z_1\le2-\frac{t_1}2\le2$: $z_K\le2^{-673}+\frac12\sum_{i<K}(3-d_i)\le2^{-673}+\frac s2$. So $x_N\ge y_K+s=3-z_K+s\ge3-2^{-673}+\frac s2>2$, i.e. $x_N\ge3$.

$e_0=0$, $K=676$, all $\delta_i=1$. Now $D=3+s$ and (2) gives $z_K\le2^{-674}+\frac{3+s}2$, so $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge\frac32-2^{-674}+\frac s2>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

$e_0=0$, $K=675$, one $\delta_j=2$. Here $D=s$. If $j<K$: $y_j\ge1$, so $z_j\le2$ and (2) from $j$ gives $z_K\le1+\frac s2$, hence $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge2+\frac s2\ge2$. If $j=K$ and $s\ge1$: $x_N\ge y_K+s\ge1+1=2$. If $j=K$ and $s=0$: then $D=0$, so $t_1=2$ and all $d_i=3$; by (2) (all earlier $\delta=1$) $z_{K-1}<1$, so $y_{K-1}\ge3$, $x_{t_K}\ge y_{K-1}+2\ge5$ and $x_N=y_K\ge\frac{5+1}{4}>1$, i.e. $x_N\ge2$.

In every case $a_N=2^{e_N}x_N\ge3\cdot2^{675}$. Hence the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

Show solution 2 – Doubling every three steps

Write $T_t=(a_t,b_t,c_t)$ and $\Phi(a,b,c)=\bigl(a+\gcd(b,c),\,b+\gcd(c,a),\,c+\gcd(a,b)\bigr)$, so that $T_{t+1}=\Phi(T_t)$. For every positive integer $g$ we have $\Phi(ga,gb,gc)=g\,\Phi(a,b,c)$.

Construction. Start with $T_0=(1,5,14)$. Then $T_1=(2,6,15)$, $T_2=(5,7,17)$, $T_3=(6,8,18)$, $T_4=(8,14,20)$, $T_5=(10,18,22)$ and $T_6=(12,20,24)=4\cdot(3,5,6)$. For every $g$, \[g(3,5,6)\ \mapsto\ g(4,8,7)\ \mapsto\ g(5,9,11)\ \mapsto\ g(6,10,12)=2g\,(3,5,6),\] so $T_{3j}=2^j(3,5,6)$ for all $j\ge2$, and $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}=3\cdot2^{675}$.

Parity. A gcd of two integers is even only if both integers are even. Count the even entries of a triple. If exactly one entry is even, all three gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes that entry odd and the two odd entries even: two even entries. If exactly two entries are even, the gcd of the two even ones is even and the other two gcds are odd, so $\Phi$ makes all three entries odd. If all entries are odd, all gcds are odd and $\Phi$ makes all entries even. If all entries are even, so are all gcds and all new entries. So the number of even entries changes by $1\to2\to0\to3$ and $3\to3$; in particular, whatever triple $R$ of positive integers we start with, all entries of $\Phi(\Phi(\Phi(R)))$ are even.

Powers of two. We show by induction on $j$ that $2^j$ divides every entry of $T_t$ for $3j\le t\le 3j+3$. Suppose $T_{3j}=2^jR$ for a triple $R$ of positive integers (true for $j=0$). Then $T_{3j+i}=2^j\Phi^i(R)$ for $i=0,1,2,3$, and all entries of $\Phi^3(R)$ are even, so $T_{3j+3}=2^{j+1}R'$ for a triple $R'$ of positive integers. This proves the claim.

Lower bound. For $3j\le t\le3j+2$ the numbers $b_t,c_t$ are multiples of $2^j$, so $\gcd(b_t,c_t)\ge2^j$ and $a_{t+1}\ge a_t+2^j$. Hence \[a_{3j+3}\ge a_{3j}+3\cdot2^j\qquad(j\ge0).\] With $j=0$ and $j=1$: $a_3\ge a_0+3\ge4$ and $a_6\ge a_3+6\ge10$. But $a_6$ is a multiple of $2^2=4$, so $a_6\ge12=3\cdot2^2$. If $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for some $j\ge2$, then $a_{3j+3}\ge3\cdot2^j+3\cdot2^j=3\cdot2^{j+1}$. By induction $a_{3j}\ge3\cdot2^j$ for all $j\ge2$; in particular $a_{2025}=a_{3\cdot675}\ge3\cdot2^{675}$.

Together with the construction, the smallest possible value of $a_{2025}$ is $\boxed{3\cdot2^{675}}$.

Consider a table with $m$ rows and $22$ columns. Each cell is filled with a number from the set $A=\{1,2,3,\ldots,2025\}$ (numbers may be repeated), such that for every pair of distinct numbers in $A$, there exists a row that contains exactly one of these two numbers. Find the minimum value of $m$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
For one number, list the rows in which it appears, recording each row only once. What would a separating row show about the lists for two numbers?
Hint 2: key idea
The row sets must be distinct. Compare how many can have size zero or one with the total number of number–row incidences allowed by the row capacity. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Count the incidences using a lower bound on the size of each row set beyond the empty and singleton sets. At the candidate bound every inequality must be tight, so model sets of size two as edges between rows and work out the degree each row must have. Then look for a graph with that regularity on cyclically labelled rows.
Common trap
Repeating a number in a row does not create a new incidence for that row.
Show the answer

$176$

Show the solution – Signatures of the numbers

For a number $x\in A$ let its signature be the set of rows containing $x$. A row contains exactly one of $x,y$ if and only if it lies in exactly one of the two signatures; so the condition says that the $2025$ signatures are pairwise distinct subsets of the set of $m$ rows.

Bound. At most one signature is empty and at most $m$ have one element, so at least $2025-1-m$ signatures have at least two elements. Each row has $22$ cells, so it contains at most $22$ distinct numbers; counting pairs (number, row containing it), \[22m\ \ge\ \sum_x|{\rm sig}(x)|\ \ge\ m+2(2024-m)=4048-m,\] so $23m\ge 4048$ and $m\ge 176$ (since $23\cdot 176=4048$).

Construction for $m=176$. Number the rows $0,1,\dots,175$ and let $H$ be the graph on these rows in which $i$ is joined to $i\pm1,\dots,i\pm10$ and to $i+88$ (indices modulo $176$); $H$ is $21$-regular and has $176\cdot21/2=1848$ edges. Give one number the empty signature (it is never written), $176$ numbers the single rows, and the remaining $2025-1-176=1848$ numbers the edges of $H$ (a number is written in the two rows of its edge). The signatures are distinct, and every row receives $1+21=22$ numbers, one per cell. Hence the minimum is $\boxed{176}$.

Problem 2

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Radical Axis and Radical Center

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AC>AB$, incenter $I$ and $A$-excenter $J$. Assume that its incircle touches $BC$ at point $K$, the $A$-excircle touches $AC$ at point $L$, and $AI$ intersects $BC$ at point $D$. If $P$ is the intersection of the circumcircles of triangles $AIC$ and $KCL$ (other than $C$), prove that $\angle APD=90^\circ$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the internal and external bisectors at $C$ and mark the line through $A,I,D,J$. Look for a circle through $C$ that would connect $P$ to $D$ and $J$.
Hint 2: key idea
Try to place $P$ on $(CDJ)$. To test whether three circles through $C$ share a second point, record their signed intercepts on $CB$ and $CA$ and relate those pairs to projections of their centers. (LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Find the intercept pairs using the arc midpoint and reflection for $(AIC)$, tangent lengths for $(KCL)$, and the angle bisector theorem and power of $A$ for $(CDJ)$. Compare the differences of the intercept pairs to test collinearity of the centers. Set up the angle at $P$ by splitting it at $PC$ and transferring the two parts to the circles through $I$ and $J$.
Common trap
Use signed intercepts since a circle may meet the extension beyond $C$.
Show solution 1 – Three coaxial circles through $C$

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides and $s$ the semiperimeter; $b>c$. Directed angles are taken modulo $180^\circ$. We assume $P\ne C$ (otherwise $P$ is not defined).

Reduction. We show that $P$ also lies on the circle $CDJ$. Then, since $I$, $D$, $J$ lie on the line $AI$, \[\angle(PA,PD)=\angle(PA,PC)+\angle(PC,PD)=\angle(IA,IC)+\angle(JC,JD)=\angle(AI,IC)+\angle(JC,AI)=\angle(JC,IC)=90^\circ,\] because the internal and external bisectors $CI$ and $CJ$ at $C$ are perpendicular. So $\angle APD=90^\circ$.

A criterion. Let $e_1,e_2$ be the unit vectors from $C$ towards $B$ and $A$. A circle through $C$ meets the lines $CB$ and $CA$ again at the points $C+ke_1$ and $C+le_2$ (signed $k,l$; $k=0$ means tangency). Its centre $O$ projects onto these lines at the midpoints of the chords, so $(O-C)\cdot e_1=\frac k2$ and $(O-C)\cdot e_2=\frac l2$. The map $X\mapsto((X-C)\cdot e_1,(X-C)\cdot e_2)$ is a linear bijection, so the centres of several circles through $C$ are collinear if and only if their points $(k,l)$ are collinear. And circles through $C$ with collinear centres have a second common point (the reflection of $C$ in the line of centres).

The three circles.

  • Circle $AIC$: its centre is the midpoint of arc $AC$, on the bisector $BI$, so it is symmetric in $BI$ and meets $BC$ again at the reflection of $A$ in $BI$, at distance $c$ from $B$. So $(k,l)=(a-c,\ b)$.

  • Circle $KCL$: $CK=s-c$ towards $B$, and $L$ lies beyond $C$ on line $AC$ with $CL=AL-b=s-b$. So $(k,l)=(s-c,\ -(s-b))$.

  • Circle $CDJ$: $CD=\frac{ab}{b+c}$ (bisector theorem). Its second point $A_2$ on line $CA$ comes from the power of $A$: $AD\cdot AJ=AC\cdot AA_2$. Since $AI\cdot AJ=bc$ (triangles $ABI$ and $AJC$ are similar) and $AD=AI\cdot\frac{2s}{b+c}$ ($I$ divides $AD$ in the ratio $(b+c):a$), we get $AD\cdot AJ=\frac{2bcs}{b+c}$ and $AA_2=\frac{2cs}{b+c}$, so $l=b-AA_2=\frac{b^2-c^2-ac}{b+c}$. Hence $(k,l)=\bigl(\frac{ab}{b+c},\ \frac{b^2-c^2-ac}{b+c}\bigr)$.

Subtracting the first point from the other two: \[(s-c,-(s-b))-(a-c,b)=(s-a,\,-s),\qquad \Bigl(\tfrac{ab}{b+c}-a+c,\ \tfrac{b^2-c^2-ac}{b+c}-b\Bigr)=\tfrac{c}{b+c}\,(b+c-a,\,-(a+b+c))=\tfrac{2c}{b+c}\,(s-a,\,-s).\] These vectors are parallel, so the three points are collinear and the three circles have a second common point. As the circles $AIC$ and $KCL$ meet again at $P$, the circle $CDJ$ passes through $P$, which completes the proof.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates with $C$ at the origin

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides, $s$ the semiperimeter, and $U=s-a$, $V=s-b$, $W=s-c$ (all positive), so $a=V+W$, $b=U+W$, $c=U+V$, $s=U+V+W$; let $r$ be the inradius. Put $C=(0,0)$, $A=(b,0)$ and $B=a(g,h)$ with $g=\cos C$, $h=\sin C>0$. Since $b>c$, $\angle C<\angle B$, so $\angle C$ is acute and $g>0$.

The data. The incircle touches $CA$ and $CB$ at distance $s-c=W$ from $C$, so $K=W(g,h)$ and $I=(W,r)$. The $A$-excircle touches line $AC$ at the point $L$ with $AL=s$, beyond $C$, so $L=(b-s,0)=(-V,0)$. By the bisector theorem $CD=\frac{ab}{b+c}=:d$, so $D=d(g,h)$. By the cosine rule $1-g=\frac{c^2-(a-b)^2}{2ab}=\frac{2UV}{ab}$, and since $ab=(V+W)(U+W)=UV+Ws$, \[1-g=\frac{2UV}{ab},\qquad 1+g=\frac{2Ws}{ab},\qquad h^2=(1-g)(1+g)=\frac{4UVWs}{a^2b^2}.\] Finally $r=W\tan\frac C2=\frac{W(1-g)}{h}$, so $hr=W(1-g)$ and $\frac hr=\frac{h^2}{W(1-g)}=\frac{1+g}{W}$.

The two circles. The circle $AIC$ passes through $C$ and $A$, so it is $x^2+y^2-bx-\xi y=0$; through $I$: $\xi=\frac{r^2-(b-W)W}{r}=\frac{r^2-UW}{r}$. Hence \[h\xi=hr-UW\frac hr=W(1-g)-U(1+g)=(W-U)-bg=-\frac{2UW}{a}.\] (The last form: $W(1-g)-U(1+g)=\frac{2UW(V-s)}{ab}=-\frac{2UWb}{ab}$.) The circle $KCL$ passes through $C$ and $L$, so it is $x^2+y^2+Vx-\eta y=0$; through $K$: $W+Vg-\eta h=0$, i.e. $h\eta=W+Vg$.

The point $P$. Subtracting the equations, the common chord is $(b+V)x=(\eta-\xi)y$, i.e. $x=\lambda y$ with $\lambda=\frac{\eta-\xi}{s}$ ($b+V=s$), and \[h\lambda=\frac{W+Vg-(W-U)+bg}{s}=\frac{U+sg}{s}=g+\alpha,\qquad \alpha:=\frac Us .\] On this line the circle $AIC$ gives $y^2(1+\lambda^2)=(b\lambda+\xi)y$, so $P=(\lambda y,y)$ with $y=\frac{b\lambda+\xi}{1+\lambda^2}$. Here \[\kappa:=h(b\lambda+\xi)=b(g+\alpha)+(W-U)-bg=W-\frac{UV}{s}=\frac{ab-2UV}{s}=\frac{abg}{s}>0,\] so $y\ne0$ and $P\ne C$.

The circle with diameter $AD$. A point $Z=(x,y)$ lies on it if and only if $(x-b)(x-dg)+y(y-dh)=0$. For $Z$ on the circle $AIC$ we may replace $x^2+y^2$ by $bx+\xi y$, and the condition becomes $dg(x-b)=(\xi-dh)y$. For $P$, multiply by $1+\lambda^2$ and use $y(1+\lambda^2)=b\lambda+\xi$: the condition is $(b\lambda+\xi)(dg\lambda+dh-\xi)=bdg(1+\lambda^2)$, which after expanding and cancelling $bdg\lambda^2$ reads \[d\bigl[h(b\lambda+\xi)+g(\lambda\xi-b)\bigr]=\xi(b\lambda+\xi).\] Multiplying by $h^2$ and using $h(b\lambda+\xi)=\kappa$, $h\lambda=g+\alpha$, this becomes $d\bigl[h^2\kappa+g(g+\alpha)(h\xi)-gbh^2\bigr]=(h\xi)\kappa$, i.e. \[(h\xi)\bigl[\kappa-dg(g+\alpha)\bigr]=dh^2(\kappa-gb).\qquad(\ast)\] Now $s+U=2s-a=b+c$, so $d(1+\alpha)=\frac{ab}{b+c}\cdot\frac{b+c}{s}=\frac{ab}{s}$ and $\kappa=dg(1+\alpha)$. Hence $\kappa-dg(g+\alpha)=dg(1-g)$ and $\kappa-gb=gb\bigl(\frac as-1\bigr)=-\frac{gbU}{s}$. Moreover $P\ne A$ (the circle $KCL$ meets line $CA$ only at $C$ and $L$) and $P\ne D$ (the circle $AIC$ meets line $AI$ only at $A$ and $I$). Therefore $\angle APD=90^\circ$.

Problem 3

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Recursive and Nested Functional Forms

Let $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ be the set of all nonnegative integers. Let $f:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ be an unbounded function such that, if $m$ and $n$ are nonnegative integers satisfying \[f(m+n)=\max\{f(0),f(1),\ldots,f(m+n)\},\] then \[f(m+n)=f(m)+f(n).\] Prove that there exist positive integers $A,B,C$ and $D$ such that for all nonnegative integers $n$, \[f(An+B)=Cn+D.\] We say that $f$ is unbounded if for each nonnegative integer $N$, there exists some nonnegative integer $n$ such that $f(n)\geq N$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark an index whose value equals the maximum up to that index. For two such indices, write the additive identity for each using one summand in common.
Hint 2: key idea
Subtract the identities at two record indices to get an additive shift over an initial range, with step equal to the gap between them. Consider what happens if that same gap occurs at arbitrarily large record indices. (LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Test whether a positive index can have value zero by shifting sufficiently late records backward by that index. For consecutive records, compare the values at intermediate indices with the earlier record and use the later record’s identity to constrain the values at possible gaps. Use positivity and integrality to limit the number of gaps, then look for a gap that recurs.
Common trap
A repeated maximum is a record under the stated hypothesis.
Show solution 1 – Records, gaps between records, and a period

Call $r$ a record if $f(r)=\max\{f(0),\dots,f(r)\}$. Since $f$ is unbounded there are infinitely many records. For a record $r$ the hypothesis (with all splittings $r=m+(r-m)$) gives \[f(m)+f(r-m)=f(r)\qquad (0\le m\le r).\qquad(1)\] With $r=0$ this gives $f(0)=0$.

Step 1. If $r<R$ are records and $t=R-r$, then $f(t+m)=f(m)+f(t)$ for $0\le m\le r$. By (1) for $R$ (split $R=(r-m)+(t+m)$) and for $r$: $f(r-m)+f(t+m)=f(R)$ and $f(r-m)+f(m)=f(r)$. Subtracting, $f(t+m)-f(m)=f(R)-f(r)$, and $m=0$ shows this constant is $f(t)$.

Step 2. If, for a fixed $t\ge1$, infinitely many records $r$ have $r+t$ also a record, then $f(n+t)=f(n)+f(t)$ for all $n\ge0$. Apply Step 1 with such records $r$ arbitrarily large.

Step 3. $f(n)\ge1$ for all $n\ge1$. Suppose $f(z)=0$ for some $z\ge1$. For every record $R\ge z$, (1) gives $f(R-z)=f(R)$, so $R-z$ is also a record (its value is the maximum over $[0,R]$). By Step 2 with $t=z$, $f(n+z)=f(n)$ for all $n$; then $f$ is periodic, hence bounded, a contradiction.

Step 4. Gaps between consecutive records take finitely many values. Let $R<R'$ be consecutive records and $t=R'-R$. For $R<m<R'$ the index $m$ is not a record, and the largest value on $[0,m-1]$ is $f(R)$, so $f(m)<f(R)$. By (1) for $R'$, $f(R'-m)=f(R')-f(m)>f(R')-f(R)=f(t)$ (Step 1). As $m$ runs over $(R,R')$, $R'-m$ runs over $(0,t)$; so $f(j)>f(t)$ for all $0<j<t$. Thus every gap $t$ is a “new strict minimum”: $f(t)<f(j)$ for $1\le j<t$. The values $f(t)$ at successive new strict minima strictly decrease and are positive integers (Step 3), so there are at most $f(1)$ such indices $t$.

Conclusion. There are infinitely many consecutive pairs of records and finitely many possible gaps, so some gap $t$ occurs infinitely often. By Step 2, $f(n+t)=f(n)+f(t)$ for all $n$, and $f(t)\ge1$ by Step 3. Hence for all $n\ge0$ \[f(tn+1)=f(1)+n\,f(t),\] so $A=t$, $B=1$, $C=f(t)$, $D=f(1)$ are positive integers with $f(An+B)=Cn+D$.

Show solution 2 – The first index of the smallest positive value

Call $r$ a record if $f(r)=\max\{f(0),\dots,f(r)\}$, and a strict record if $f(r)>f(i)$ for all $i<r$ (so $0$ is a strict record). For a record $r$ the hypothesis, applied to all splittings $r=i+(r-i)$, gives \[f(i)+f(r-i)=f(r)\qquad(0\le i\le r).\qquad(1)\] With $r=0$ this gives $f(0)=0$. Since $f$ is unbounded, there are arbitrarily large strict records: for each $N$, the least $n$ with $f(n)\ge N$ is one when $N>0$. Between two consecutive strict records $r<s$ the running maximum does not increase, so $\max\{f(0),\dots,f(s-1)\}=f(r)$.

Step 1: $f(i)\ge1$ for $i\ge1$. If $f(i)=0$ with $i\ge1$, take a strict record $s>i$; by (1), $f(s-i)=f(s)$ with $s-i<s$, contradicting strictness. Hence $m:=\min\{f(i):i\ge1\}$ is a positive integer; let $a\ge1$ be the least index with $f(a)=m$.

Step 2. Let $s\ge a$ be a strict record and $r<s$ the previous strict record; put $g=s-r$. For $1\le i\le s$, (1) at $s$ and $s-i\le s-1$ give \[f(i)=f(s)-f(s-i)\ge f(s)-f(r)=f(g),\] the last equality being (1) at $s$ with $i=g$. Taking $i=a$ gives $f(g)\le m$, so $f(g)=m$, and by the choice of $a$, $g\ge a$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments · also 2026-germany-team-selection-test: AIMO 2, Problem 1

In an $n\times n$ board, for each $1\leq i\leq n$ and $1\leq j\leq n$, the cell in the $i$th row from the bottom and $j$th column from the left contains $\gcd(i,j)$ leaves. A koala travels from the bottom left corner to the top right corner. At each step, the koala moves up or to the right by a single cell. The koala eats the leaves in each cell it visits, including the bottom left and top right cells.

Determine the maximum number of leaves that the koala can eat.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw a small board, fill in its gcd values, and trace a path from $(1,1)$ to $(n,n)$. Record the coordinate sum $i+j$ at each step.
Hint 2: key idea
Group the visited cells on the anti-diagonals with sums $2m$ and $2m+1$. Their gcd values both divide the shared coordinate, and any common divisor of those values also divides the two consecutive coordinates. (LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the two-cell contribution first when one gcd is $1$, then use $(a-2)(b-2)\ge0$ when both gcds $a,b$ are at least $2$. Set aside the terminal cell and look for a path that attains the bound on every pair at once.
Common trap
Maximizing the contribution on each anti-diagonal independently can select cells that are not consecutive along any path.
Show the answer

$\dfrac{n^2+3n-2}{2}$

Show the solution – Pairing consecutive anti-diagonals

Write $(i,j)$ for the cell in row $i$ and column $j$ and $g(i,j)=\gcd(i,j)$. Every step increases $i+j$ by $1$, so the koala's path visits exactly one cell on each anti-diagonal $i+j=s$, $s=2,3,\dots,2n$.

Construction. The staircase $(1,1),(1,2),(2,2),(2,3),\dots,(n-1,n),(n,n)$ visits the diagonal cells $(m,m)$, worth $m$, and the cells $(m,m+1)$, worth $\gcd(m,m+1)=1$. It collects $1+2+\cdots+n+(n-1)=\frac{n^2+3n-2}{2}$ leaves.

Key lemma. Let $A$ be the visited cell with $i+j=2m$ $(1\le m\le n-1)$ and $B$ the next visited cell (with $i+j=2m+1$). Then $g(A)+g(B)\le m+1$.

Proof. $A$ and $B$ share a row or a column, so they share a coordinate $c$, and their other coordinates are consecutive integers. Hence $g(A)$ and $g(B)$ both divide $c$, and a common divisor of them divides two consecutive integers, so $\gcd(g(A),g(B))=1$ and $g(A)g(B)\mid c$.

  • If $g(A)=1$: $g(B)$ is at most the smaller coordinate of $B$, which is at most $m$ because the coordinates of $B$ add up to $2m+1$. So the sum is at most $m+1$.

  • If $g(B)=1$: similarly $g(A)\le m$, since the coordinates of $A$ add up to $2m$.

  • If $g(A),g(B)\ge2$: then $(g(A)-2)(g(B)-2)\ge0$ gives $g(A)+g(B)\le\frac{g(A)g(B)}2+2\le\frac c2+2$. The coordinate $c$ belongs to $A$, whose other coordinate is at least $1$, so $c\le2m-1$; and $c=2m-1$ would force the other coordinate of $A$ to be $1$ and $g(A)=1$. So $c\le2m-2$ and $g(A)+g(B)\le m+1$. $\square$

Upper bound. Group the anti-diagonals as $\{2,3\},\{4,5\},\dots,\{2n-2,2n-1\}$ and $\{2n\}$. By the lemma the cells on the pair $\{2m,2m+1\}$ contribute at most $m+1$, and the last cell $(n,n)$ contributes $n$. So the total is at most \[\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(m+1)+n=\frac{(n-1)n}2+(n-1)+n=\frac{n^2+3n-2}{2}.\] Hence the maximum is $\boxed{\dfrac{n^2+3n-2}{2}}$.

Then $x^n+1=-(a_1x+\dots+a_{n-1}x^{n-1})$, and by Cauchy-Schwarz \[(x^n+1)^2\le\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_i^2\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x^{2i}\Bigr).\] With $t=x^2>0$ it remains to show \[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t^i\le\frac{n-1}{4}\,(t^m+1)^2=\frac{n-1}{4}\bigl(1+t^n+2t^m\bigr).\qquad(1)\] Let $u_i=t^i+t^{n-i}$ for $0\le i\le m$. The sequence is convex: $u_{i-1}+u_{i+1}-2u_i=(t-1)^2\bigl(t^{i-1}+t^{n-i-1}\bigr)\ge0$. A convex sequence lies below its chord, so $u_i\le\frac{(m-i)u_0+i\,u_m}{m}$ with $u_0=1+t^n$, $u_m=2t^m$. Summing, \[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t^i=t^m+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}u_i\le t^m+\frac{m-1}{2}(u_0+u_m)=\frac{m-1}2u_0+\frac m2u_m .\] The right side of (1) is $\frac{2m-1}4(u_0+u_m)$, and $\frac{2m-1}4(u_0+u_m)-\frac{m-1}2u_0-\frac m2u_m=\frac{u_0-u_m}{4}=\frac{(t^m-1)^2}{4}\ge0$. Hence (1) holds and $\sum a_i^2\ge\frac{(x^n+1)^2}{\sum x^{2i}}\ge\frac{4}{n-1}$.

Equality. $P(x)=x^n-\frac{2}{n-1}\bigl(x^{n-1}+\dots+x\bigr)+1$ has $P(1)=0$ and $\sum a_i^2=\frac4{n-1}$. The minimum is $\boxed{\dfrac{4}{n-1}}$.

Problem 4

Let $a,b,k$ be positive integers. Suppose that for any integer $n\geq 2025$ there exists a positive integer $x_n>n$ such that \[x_n\mid n^2+a\quad\text{and}\quad x_n+k\mid n^2+b.\] Prove that $k=b-a$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write the two divisibilities as exact products with positive integer cofactors. Compare the products by subtracting them, and note what the hypothesis $x_n>n$ says about the first factor as $n$ grows.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare the two cofactors $u,v$ according to which is larger. For $u>v$, eliminate one cofactor and complete a square to obtain an equation $Y^2-Dn^2=C$, with $D,C$ determined by the bounded difference $u-v$. (LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis)
Hint 3: approach
For large $n$, rule out $v>u$ and bound $u-v$ in the other strict case using $x_n>n$. For square $D$ factor the difference of squares, and for nonsquare $D$ compare two boundedly spaced indices by estimating how close $Y$ is to $\sqrt D\,n$. Use pigeonholing on short progressions of indices, choosing a residue class that excludes $u=v$ if their putative common value exceeds $1$.
Common trap
The cofactor difference can vary with $n$, so compare two nearby indices only after ensuring that they have the same difference.
Show the solution – Compare the cofactors; a Pell-type equation cannot hold on a whole progression

For $n\ge2025$ write $x=x_n$ and \[n^2+a=xu,\qquad n^2+b=(x+k)v\qquad(u,v\ \text{positive integers}).\] Subtracting, $x(v-u)+kv=b-a$.

Case $v>u$. Then $b-a\ge x+kv>n$; impossible for $n>|b-a|$.

Case $v=u$. Then $ku=b-a$, so $u=w:=\frac{b-a}{k}$ is a fixed positive integer and $w\mid n^2+a$.

Case $v<u$. Let $d=u-v\ge1$. Then $xd=kv-(b-a)$, and substituting $v=\frac{xd+b-a}{k}$, $u=v+d$ into $k(n^2+a)=kxu$ gives \[kn^2=dx^2+ex-ka,\qquad e=b-a+kd .\] $d$ is bounded: if $e\ge0$ then $kn^2\ge dx^2-ka>dn^2-ka$, so $d\le k$ for $n^2>ka$; if $e<0$ then $d<\frac{|b-a|}{k}$. So $d\le K:=k+|b-a|$. Multiplying by $4d$: \[Y^2-4dk\,n^2=c_d,\qquad Y=2dx+e,\quad c_d=e^2+4dka>0,\qquad(\ast)\] and $Y>0$ for large $n$ (as $x>n$ and $|e|\le |b-a|+kK$). Claim. Fix a bound $H$. There is $N_0$ such that for every integer $h$ with $1\le h\le H$ and every $d\le K$, the equation $(\ast)$ does not hold for both $n$ and $n+h$ when $n>N_0$; moreover, if $4dk$ is a perfect square, $(\ast)$ has only finitely many solutions $n$.

Indeed, if $4dk=s^2$, then $(Y-sn)(Y+sn)=c_d>0$ forces $sn<Y+sn\le c_d$. If $D=4dk$ is not a square and $(n,Y)$, $(n',Y')$ solve $(\ast)$ with $Y,Y'>0$, then $0<Y-\sqrt Dn=\frac{c_d}{Y+\sqrt Dn}<\frac{c_d}{2\sqrt D\,n}$, and the same for $n'=n+h$. So the integer $Y'-Y$ differs from the irrational number $h\sqrt D$ by less than $\frac{c_d}{2\sqrt D\,n}$. The finitely many numbers $h\sqrt{D}$ ($h\le H$, $d\le K$) have a positive distance $\varepsilon$ to the nearest integers, so this is impossible once $n>\max c_d/(2\sqrt D\,\varepsilon)$.

Conclusion. Take $H=K\,(|b-a|+1)$; note that $w=\frac{b-a}k\le|b-a|$ whenever Case $v=u$ occurs.

  • If Case $v=u$ never occurs, then for all large $n$ we are in Case $v<u$. Among $n,n+1,\dots,n+K$ two indices use the same $d$, which contradicts the Claim. So Case $v=u$ occurs for some $n$, and $w=\frac{b-a}{k}$ is a positive integer.

  • If $w\ge2$: the squares modulo $w$ are not all congruent ($0^2\not\equiv1^2$), so there is a residue $r$ with $r^2\not\equiv-a\pmod w$. For $n\equiv r\pmod w$ Case $v=u$ is impossible (it needs $w\mid n^2+a$), so all large $n\equiv r$ are in Case $v<u$. Among $n,n+w,\dots,n+Kw$ two use the same $d$, again contradicting the Claim.

Hence $w=1$, i.e. $k=b-a$. (Conversely $k=b-a$ works with $x_n=n^2+a$.)

Show solution 1 – Pairs summing to the largest element

Example. $S=\{1003,1004,\dots,2006\}$ has $1004$ elements, and for $x<y<z$ in $S$ we have $x+y\ge2007>z$, so $x+y\nmid z$.

Example. $S=\{1003,1004,\dots,2006\}$ has $1004$ elements, and for $x<y<z$ in $S$ we have $x+y\ge2007>z$, so $x+y\nmid z$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

Find all positive integers $a$ such that for any positive integer $n\geq 5$ we have $2^n-n^2\mid a^n-n^a$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
The divisibility must hold for every allowed $n$. Try to choose $n$ in terms of a prime so that the prime divides $2^n-n^2$.
Hint 2: key idea
For an odd prime $p>a$, choose $n=4p-2$. Its residue modulo $p$ is easy to compute, and its difference from $2$ is a multiple of $p-1$, so Fermat’s little theorem simplifies both exponential terms. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Verify that $p$ divides the divisor in the problem and reduce the other side modulo $p$. Use the arbitrarily large primes to obtain an equality between expressions depending only on $a$. Compare the signs and growth rates of the two expressions to narrow the candidates for direct checking.
Common trap
The test prime must be coprime to $a$ to apply Fermat’s little theorem to $a^n$.
Show the answer

$a\in\{2,4\}$

Show solution 1 – Choose $n=4p-2$

$a=2$ and $a=4$ work. For $n\ge5$ we have $2^n>n^2$. For $a=2$ the two numbers are equal; for $a=4$, $4^n-n^4=(2^n-n^2)(2^n+n^2)$.

No other $a$ works. Let $p$ be a prime with $p>a$, $p\ge3$, and take $n=4p-2\ge10$. By Fermat, $2^n=2^{4(p-1)}\cdot2^2\equiv4$ and $n^2\equiv(-2)^2=4\pmod p$, so $p\mid 2^n-n^2$. If $a$ works, then $p\mid a^n-n^a$; since $p\nmid a$, $a^n=a^{4(p-1)}a^2\equiv a^2$, and $n^a\equiv(-2)^a\pmod p$. Hence $p\mid a^2-(-2)^a$ for every prime $p>a$. A nonzero integer has finitely many prime divisors, so $a^2=(-2)^a$. Then $a$ is even and $a^2=2^a$, which gives $a=2$ or $a=4$ (for $a\ge5$, $2^a>a^2$).

The answer is $\boxed{a\in\{2,4\}}$.

Show solution 2 – An asymptotic expansion killed by a difference operator

$a=2$ and $a=4$ work. For $n\ge5$, $2^n>n^2$. For $a=2$ the two numbers are equal; for $a=4$, $4^n-n^4=(2^n-n^2)(2^n+n^2)$. For $a=1$ and $n=5$: $2^5-5^2=7\nmid 1-5$.

No other $a\ge2$ works. Suppose $a\ge2$ works, and let $s\ge1$ be the integer with $2^s\le a<2^{s+1}$. Then $q_n=\frac{a^n-n^a}{2^n-n^2}$ is an integer for every $n\ge5$. With $\rho=n^2/2^n$ and $\frac1{1-\rho}=1+\rho+\dots+\rho^{s-1}+\frac{\rho^s}{1-\rho}$, \[q_n=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}n^{2j}\lambda_j^{\,n}+R_n,\qquad \lambda_j=\frac{a}{2^{j+1}},\qquad R_n=\frac{n^{2s}(a/2^s)^n-n^a}{2^n-n^2}.\] For $n\ge7$ we have $n^2\le2^{n-1}$, so $2^n-n^2\ge2^{n-1}$ and $|R_n|\le2\bigl(n^{2s}(a/2^{s+1})^n+n^a/2^n\bigr)$; as $a/2^{s+1}<1$, $R_n\to0$.

The operator. Let $E$ be the shift, $(Eu)_n=u_{n+1}$. If $P$ is a polynomial of degree $e$, then $(E-\lambda)\bigl(P(n)\lambda^n\bigr)=\bigl(P(n+1)-P(n)\bigr)\lambda^{n+1}$ is a polynomial of degree at most $e-1$ times $\lambda^n$; so $(E-\lambda)^{e+1}$ annihilates $P(n)\lambda^n$. Hence \[\Phi=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\bigl(2^{j+1}E-a\bigr)^{2j+1}=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}2^{(j+1)(2j+1)}(E-\lambda_j)^{2j+1}\] (these operators commute) annihilates every term $n^{2j}\lambda_j^n$, and $\Phi=\sum_i c_iE^i$ with integers $c_i$. Therefore $(\Phi q)_n=(\Phi R)_n$ for all $n\ge5$. The left side is an integer, the right side tends to $0$ (a fixed combination of shifts of $R$), so $(\Phi R)_n=0$ for all large $n$.

Lemma. If $u_n\to0$ and $\bigl((E-\mu_1)\cdots(E-\mu_N)u\bigr)_n=0$ for all large $n$, where every $\mu_i\ge1$, then $u_n=0$ for all large $n$. Indeed, for $N=0$ there is nothing to prove; for $N\ge1$ let $w=(E-\mu_2)\cdots(E-\mu_N)u$. Then $w_n\to0$ and $w_{n+1}=\mu_1w_n$ for large $n$, so $|w_n|$ is eventually nondecreasing and tends to $0$; hence $w_n=0$ for large $n$, and induction on $N$ finishes.

Every $\lambda_j=a/2^{j+1}\ge a/2^s\ge1$ ($j\le s-1$), so the lemma gives $R_n=0$, i.e. $n^{2s}(a/2^s)^n=n^a$, for all large $n$. If $a>2^s$, then $(a/2^s)^n n^{2s-a}\to\infty$, a contradiction; so $a=2^s$, and then $n^{2s}=n^a$ gives $a=2s$. Thus $2^s=2s$, which holds for $s=1,2$ only (for $s\ge3$, $2^s>2s$, by induction: $2^{s+1}>4s\ge2s+2$). So $a\in\{2,4\}$.

The answer is $\boxed{a\in\{2,4\}}$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try $x=1$ and mark the two arguments of $f$ that depend on $y$. How far apart are those arguments?
Hint 2: key idea
Write $c=f(1)$ and use the shift rule from $x=1$ to simplify the equation with $y$ replaced by $y+c$. Put $z=xy$ and $s=xc$ to express $f(z+s)$ in terms of $f(z)/c$ and a term depending only on $s$. (LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Apply the general shift rule at two positive increments in both orders. If $c\ne1$, use the resulting condition on the shift term to compare the values at two inputs below a common larger argument. Show the answer

$f(x)=x$ for all $x$, and $f(x)=2$ for all $x$

Show the solution – Shifts by multiples of $f(1)$

Both functions work: $x(x+y)=x^2+xy$ and $2\cdot2=2+2$.

Let $f$ be a solution and $c=f(1)$. Case $c\ne1$. Then $\kappa$ is a constant $\kappa_0$, and $f(w)=\frac{f(z)}c+\kappa_0$ for all $0<z<w$. So $f$ takes the same value at any two points below $w$, for every $w$: $f$ is constant, $f\equiv c$, and $c^2=2c$ gives $c=2$.

Case $c=1$. Then $\kappa(s)=f(s)$ (the last term vanishes), and (2) reads $f(z+s)=f(z)+f(s)$. As $f>0$, $f$ is increasing; an additive increasing function on $\mathbb R^+$ is linear (for rational $q$, $f(q)=qf(1)$, and monotonicity squeezes irrational arguments). With $f(1)=1$ we get $f(x)=x$.

So the solutions are $\boxed{f(x)=x}$ and $\boxed{f\equiv2}$.
