Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · GCD and LCM Structure

For any two positive integers $m$ and $n$, we define $l(m,n)$ as their least common multiple and $h(m,n)$ as their highest common factor. Given a prime $p>3$, let $k$ denote the number of ordered pairs of positive integers $(m,n)$ satisfying the equation \[l(m,n)+h(m,n)=p^4.\] Determine the smallest possible value of $k$ across all choices of the prime $p>3$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Divide both integers in a pair by their greatest common divisor. How does the least common multiple depend on the two coprime integers that remain?
Hint 2: key idea
After taking out the common divisor, the equation becomes a product equal to $p^4$. For a fixed exponent of $p$ in the second factor, count the coprime factorizations of the resulting product by assigning its prime-power factors. (LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure)
Hint 3: approach
For each possible exponent $e$, use the factorization of $p^e-1$ to get a uniform lower bound on its number of distinct prime factors. For $e=4$, examine $(p^2+1)/2$ modulo $2$ and $3$ to find a prime beyond those already forced. Test the smallest allowed prime to see whether the separate bounds can all be attained together.
Common trap
Keep the assignments to the first and second entries ordered.
Show the answer

$18$

Show the solution – Factor out the gcd

Write $g=h(m,n)$, $m=gx$, $n=gy$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$; then $l(m,n)=gxy$ and the equation becomes \[g\,(xy+1)=p^4 .\] So $g=p^{\,4-e}$ and $xy=p^e-1$ for some $e\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ ($e\ge1$ because $xy+1\ge2$). Conversely every $e$ and every ordered pair of coprime positive integers $(x,y)$ with $xy=p^e-1$ give a solution $(m,n)=(p^{4-e}x,p^{4-e}y)$, and different data give different pairs (the gcd determines $e$). For a positive integer $M$ with $\omega(M)$ distinct prime factors, the ordered coprime pairs with $xy=M$ correspond to choosing, for each prime power in $M$, whether it goes to $x$ or to $y$: there are $2^{\omega(M)}$ of them. Hence \[k=\sum_{e=1}^{4}2^{\omega(p^e-1)} .\] Lower bound. Let $p>3$ be prime.

  • $p-1$ is even, so $\omega(p-1)\ge1$.

  • $p^2-1$ is divisible by $2$ and by $3$ (as $3\nmid p$), so $\omega(p^2-1)\ge2$.

  • $p^3-1=(p-1)(p^2+p+1)$, where $p-1$ is even and $p^2+p+1$ is odd and greater than $1$; so $\omega(p^3-1)\ge2$.

  • $p^4-1=(p^2-1)(p^2+1)$ is divisible by $2$ and $3$, and $p^2+1\equiv2\pmod 4$ and $p^2+1\equiv2\pmod3$, so $\frac{p^2+1}2$ is an odd number greater than $1$ not divisible by $3$; its prime factors are new. So $\omega(p^4-1)\ge3$.

Therefore $k\ge2+4+4+8=18$.

Equality. For $p=5$: $4=2^2$, $24=2^3\cdot3$, $124=2^2\cdot31$, $624=2^4\cdot3\cdot13$, so $k=2+4+4+8=18$.

The smallest possible value is $\boxed{18}$.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Geometric Reflection and Isometry

The convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ has sides $AD$ and $BC$ not parallel. The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at $X$. The perpendicular bisectors of sides $AD$ and $BC$ meet at $Y$. Suppose that $Y$ lies strictly inside triangle $XCD$.

Prove that $AC=BD$ if and only if $XY$ bisects $\angle DXC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the equal distances from $Y$ given by the two perpendicular bisectors. Also mark which rays of the diagonals contain $A,B,C,D$ relative to $X$.
Hint 2: key idea
For the forward implication, compare triangles $YAC$ and $YDB$ and their heights from $Y$. For the reverse implication, reflect in the assumed bisector $XY$ and track which rays the reflected points lie on. (LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the equal areas and equal bases to compare the distances from $Y$ to the two diagonals. In the reverse direction, intersect line $BD$ with the circles centered at $Y$ through $D$ and through $B$, and identify which intersections are the reflected images of $A,C$. Compare those images with $D,B$ using the foot of the perpendicular from $Y$ to $BD$.
Common trap
The strict interior condition places $Y$ inside $\angle DXC$, so equal distances to the diagonal lines select the internal bisector.
Show solution 1 – Congruent triangles and a reflection

Since $AD\nparallel BC$, the two perpendicular bisectors meet in a single point $Y$, and $YA=YD$, $YB=YC$. As $ABCD$ is convex, $X$ lies between $A$ and $C$ and between $B$ and $D$. Since $Y$ is strictly inside triangle $XCD$, it lies inside the angle $DXC$ and on neither line $AC$ nor line $BD$.

$AC=BD\Rightarrow$ $XY$ bisects $\angle DXC$. The triangles $YAC$ and $YDB$ have $YA=YD$, $YC=YB$ and $AC=DB$, so they are congruent. Congruent triangles have equal areas, and their sides $AC$ and $DB$ are equal, so the heights from $Y$ are equal: $Y$ is equidistant from lines $AC$ and $BD$. A point inside angle $DXC$ that is equidistant from its two sides lies on its bisector. So $XY$ bisects $\angle DXC$.

$XY$ bisects $\angle DXC\Rightarrow AC=BD$. Let $\sigma$ be the reflection in line $XY$. It fixes $X$ and $Y$ and swaps the rays $XC$ and $XD$; hence it also swaps the opposite rays $XA$ and $XB$.

The point $\sigma(A)$ lies on ray $XB$ and satisfies $Y\sigma(A)=YA=YD$. Let $G$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $Y$ to line $BD$. The circle with centre $Y$ through $D$ meets line $BD$ exactly at $D$ and at the reflection $D^*$ of $D$ in $G$. Since $\sigma(A)$ is on ray $XB$, which is opposite to ray $XD$, we have $\sigma(A)\ne D$, so $\sigma(A)=D^*$. In the same way $\sigma(C)$ lies on ray $XD$, at distance $YC=YB$ from $Y$, and $\sigma(C)\ne B$; so $\sigma(C)=B^*$, the reflection of $B$ in $G$.

Reflections preserve lengths, and so does the half-turn about $G$ on line $BD$. Hence \[AC=\sigma(A)\sigma(C)=D^*B^*=DB .\] This proves the equivalence.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates along the bisector

Put $X$ at the origin and let $\theta\in(0^\circ,90^\circ)$ be half of $\angle DXC$. Choose axes so that the bisector of $\angle DXC$ is the positive $x$-axis, with $C=\gamma(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, $D=\delta(\cos\theta,-\sin\theta)$, and hence $A=-\alpha(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, $B=-\beta(\cos\theta,-\sin\theta)$ with $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta>0$; then $AC=\alpha+\gamma$ and $BD=\beta+\delta$. Let $Y=(u,v)$; since $Y$ is inside angle $DXC$, $u>0$.

For points $P,Q$, the condition $YP=YQ$ reads $2\,Y\cdot(Q-P)=|Q|^2-|P|^2$. With $D-A=\bigl((\alpha+\delta)\cos\theta,(\alpha-\delta)\sin\theta\bigr)$ and $C-B=\bigl((\beta+\gamma)\cos\theta,(\gamma-\beta)\sin\theta\bigr)$, the conditions $YA=YD$ and $YB=YC$ become \[2u(\alpha+\delta)\cos\theta+2v(\alpha-\delta)\sin\theta=(\delta-\alpha)(\delta+\alpha),\] \[2u(\beta+\gamma)\cos\theta+2v(\gamma-\beta)\sin\theta=(\gamma-\beta)(\gamma+\beta).\] If $XY$ bisects $\angle DXC$, then $v=0$, and dividing by $\alpha+\delta$ and $\beta+\gamma$ gives $2u\cos\theta=\delta-\alpha=\gamma-\beta$. Hence $\alpha+\gamma=\beta+\delta$, i.e. $AC=BD$.

If $AC=BD$, then $\gamma-\beta=\delta-\alpha=:\Delta$; put $P=\alpha+\delta$ and $P'=\beta+\gamma$. The equations become \[2uP\cos\theta-2v\Delta\sin\theta=\Delta P,\qquad 2uP'\cos\theta+2v\Delta\sin\theta=\Delta P' .\] If $\Delta=0$, the first gives $uP\cos\theta=0$, so $u=0$, impossible. So $\Delta\ne0$; with $w=\frac{2u\cos\theta}{\Delta}$, dividing by $\Delta$ gives \[(w-1)P=2v\sin\theta,\qquad (w-1)P'=-2v\sin\theta .\] Adding, $(w-1)(P+P')=0$, so $w=1$ and then $v\sin\theta=0$, i.e. $v=0$. So $Y$ lies on the $x$-axis, and $XY$ bisects $\angle DXC$.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting

Each cell of a $30\times 30$ grid contains one of the numbers $-1$, $0$ or $1$ with each of these three numbers appearing exactly $300$ times.

Is it possible that the $60$ row and column sums are all different?

Hint 1: understand the problem
List the integer values that a row or column sum can take. If all the line sums differ, use their total to ask which one of the possible values was left out.
Hint 2: key idea
Group the rows and columns according to the signs of their sums. Express the total positive row sum, the total positive column sum, and their negative counterparts using sums of entries in the four resulting blocks. (LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities)
Hint 3: approach
Add the positive totals and subtract the negative totals so that the mixed-sign blocks cancel. Bound the remaining two block contributions by their sizes, writing those sizes in terms of the number of positive rows and the forced number of positive columns. Compare the strongest bound from these sizes with the required sum of positive line sums.
Common trap
The total of all row and column sums counts every cell twice.
Show the answer

No

Show the solution – The sums must be plus or minus 1 to 30

Suppose the $60$ line sums are all different.

The sums are $\pm1,\pm2,\dots,\pm30$. The total of all entries is $300-300=0$; the row sums add up to this total, and so do the column sums, so the $60$ line sums add up to $0$. They are $60$ different integers in $[-30,30]$, a set of $61$ integers with sum $0$; if $v$ is the missing value, the $60$ sums add up to $-v$, so $v=0$. Hence the line sums are exactly $\pm1,\dots,\pm30$: no sum is $0$, $30$ lines have positive sum and $30$ have negative sum.

Four blocks. Let $\mathcal A$, $\mathcal B$ be the sets of rows with positive and negative sum, and $\mathcal C$, $\mathcal D$ the sets of columns with positive and negative sum; let $\alpha=|\mathcal A|$. Then $|\mathcal B|=30-\alpha$, and since there are $30$ positive sums, $|\mathcal C|=30-\alpha$ and $|\mathcal D|=\alpha$. Write $\Sigma(\mathcal U\mathcal V)$ for the sum of the entries in the rows of $\mathcal U$ and the columns of $\mathcal V$. Let $P$ and $Q$ be the sums of the positive row sums and of the positive column sums; then $P+Q=1+2+\dots+30=465$, the negative row sums add up to $-P$ and the negative column sums to $-Q$: \[P=\Sigma(\mathcal{AC})+\Sigma(\mathcal{AD}),\quad Q=\Sigma(\mathcal{AC})+\Sigma(\mathcal{BC}),\quad -P=\Sigma(\mathcal{BC})+\Sigma(\mathcal{BD}),\quad -Q=\Sigma(\mathcal{AD})+\Sigma(\mathcal{BD}).\] Adding the first two and subtracting the last two gives $2(P+Q)=2\Sigma(\mathcal{AC})-2\Sigma(\mathcal{BD})$, so \[465=\Sigma(\mathcal{AC})-\Sigma(\mathcal{BD})\le|\mathcal A||\mathcal C|+|\mathcal B||\mathcal D|=2\alpha(30-\alpha)\le2\cdot15\cdot15=450,\] since every entry is between $-1$ and $1$. This is a contradiction, so the answer is No.

Problem 4

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Monovariants and Directional Change

Let $N$ be a positive integer and let $(k_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a sequence of positive integers with all terms at most $N$. Annabel begins by choosing integers $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N$. She then extends this to an infinite sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge1}$ of integers by defining \[x_n=\sum_{i=n-k_n}^{n-1}x_i\] for each $n>N$.

Show that there are either finitely many strictly positive terms or finitely many strictly negative terms in the infinite sequence $(x_n)$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Write a few possible updates and mark the consecutive terms each one uses. Find a way to write each such consecutive sum using two sums that start from the beginning.
Hint 2: key idea
Use prefix sums so that the next term is the current prefix sum minus a prefix sum from the preceding window. See what happens if the current prefix sum is at least every value in that window, or at most every value. (LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants)
Hint 3: approach
Show that either extreme condition persists as the window shifts. If neither ever occurs, follow the window maximum and minimum and check what happens after the entire old window has left. Use integrality to quantify the change in the width between the extremes.
Common trap
A tie with a window extreme already suffices for the corresponding sign restriction.
Show the solution – Prefix sums and a shrinking window

Let $S_0=0$ and $S_t=x_1+\dots+x_t$. For $n>N$, \[x_n=S_{n-1}-S_{j(n)},\qquad j(n)=n-1-k_n\in[\,n-1-N,\ n-2\,],\] and $j(n)\ge0$. So each new term is the difference between the current prefix sum and one of the $N$ prefix sums just before it.

Claim. If for some $t\ge N$ we have $S_t\ge S_i$ for all $i$ with $t-N\le i\le t-1$, then $x_n\ge0$ for all $n>t$.

Indeed, then $x_{t+1}=S_t-S_{j(t+1)}\ge0$ because $j(t+1)\in[t-N,t-1]$; so $S_{t+1}\ge S_t\ge S_i$ for $t+1-N\le i\le t-1$, and also $S_{t+1}\ge S_t$. Thus the hypothesis holds again at $t+1$, and induction gives the claim. In this case only the terms $x_1,\dots,x_t$ can be strictly negative, so there are finitely many strictly negative terms. In the same way, if $S_t\le S_i$ for all $i\in[t-N,t-1]$ for some $t\ge N$, there are finitely many strictly positive terms.

The remaining case. Suppose that neither happens: for every $t\ge N$ some $S_i$ with $i\in[t-N,t-1]$ is larger than $S_t$, and some is smaller. Let \[M_t=\max\{S_i:\ t-N\le i\le t\},\qquad m_t=\min\{S_i:\ t-N\le i\le t\}\qquad(t\ge N).\] For $t\ge N+1$ we have $S_t<\max_{t-N\le i\le t-1}S_i\le M_{t-1}$, hence $M_t\le M_{t-1}$. Now fix $T\ge N$. For every $i\in[T+1,T+N+1]$ we get $S_i<M_{i-1}\le M_T$, so \[M_{T+N+1}=\max_{T+1\le i\le T+N+1}S_i<M_T .\] The values are integers, so $M$ drops by at least $1$ every $N+1$ steps; in the same way $m$ rises by at least $1$ every $N+1$ steps. But $m_t\le S_t\le M_t$ for all $t$, so after $(N+1)(M_N-m_N+1)$ steps we would get $m_t>M_t$, a contradiction.

Hence one of the two earlier cases occurs, and the sequence has finitely many strictly positive terms or finitely many strictly negative terms.
