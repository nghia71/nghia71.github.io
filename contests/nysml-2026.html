Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 32: Area by Measurement and Decomposition · Cut-and-Reassemble Area

The figure below shows a shaded polygon $P$ inscribed in a square. The polygon $P$ has each of its sides parallel to one of the sides of the square.

Given that $P$ has perimeter $36$ units and area $30$ square units, compute the fraction of the area of the square that $P$ covers.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{10}{27}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sliding the sides outward)

As in the figure, $P$ is a staircase: every horizontal or vertical line meets $P$ in one segment, and $P$ touches all four sides of the square. Let the square have side $s$.

Look at the vertical sides of $P$ that face left (the left boundary of $P$). Sliding each of them horizontally to the left side of the square, they cover that side exactly once, without overlap: every horizontal line strictly between the top and bottom of the square, other than the finitely many lines through vertices of $P$, meets the left boundary of $P$ in exactly one point, which lies inside a left-facing vertical side. So their total length is $s$. The same holds for the right-facing vertical sides and for the upward- and downward-facing horizontal sides. Hence the perimeter of $P$ equals the perimeter $4s$ of the square: $4s=36$, $s=9$, and the square has area $81$.

The fraction covered is $\frac{30}{81}=\boxed{\tfrac{10}{27}}$.

Solution 2 (Projections onto the axes)

The polygon $P$ is a staircase as in the figure: each vertical line $x=t$ crossing the square meets $P$ in a single segment, and so does each horizontal line; $P$ touches all four sides of the square of side $s$.

Every horizontal side of $P$ projects onto the bottom side of the square. A generic vertical line $x=t$ ($0<t<s$) meets the boundary of $P$ in exactly two points, the top and bottom ends of the segment $P\cap\{x=t\}$, and both lie on horizontal sides. So the projections of the horizontal sides cover $[0,s]$ exactly twice, and the horizontal sides have total length $2s$. In the same way the vertical sides have total length $2s$. Therefore the perimeter is $4s=36$, so $s=9$.

The fraction of the square covered by $P$ is $\frac{30}{81}=\boxed{\tfrac{10}{27}}$.

Problem 4

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 35: Factorials and Prime Counting · Prime Exponents in Factorials

Compute the least positive integer that does not divide $26!$ and also is not divisible by any prime greater than $26$.

Show the answer

$289$

Show the solution

Solution (One prime exponent too large)

By Legendre's formula the exponents of the primes in $26!$ are \[v_2=13+6+3+1=23,\ v_3=8+2=10,\ v_5=5+1=6,\ v_7=3,\ v_{11}=2,\ v_{13}=2,\ v_{17}=v_{19}=v_{23}=1 .\] Let $m$ be a positive integer whose prime factors are all at most $23$ (the primes up to $26$). If $v_p(m)\le v_p(26!)$ for every $p$, then $m\mid 26!$. So $m\nmid 26!$ forces $v_p(m)\ge v_p(26!)+1$ for some $p$, hence $m\ge p^{v_p(26!)+1}$. The candidates are \[2^{24},\ 3^{11},\ 5^{7},\ 7^4=2401,\ 11^3=1331,\ 13^3=2197,\ 17^2=289,\ 19^2=361,\ 23^2=529,\] and the smallest is $17^2=289$, which indeed does not divide $26!$. The answer is $\boxed{289}$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Existence via Overcrowding

Compute the number of eight-digit positive integers that have no contiguous substring divisible by $9$.

Show the answer

$40320$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Partial sums form a permutation)

A number is divisible by $9$ iff its digit sum is. For an eight-digit number $d_1d_2\dots d_8$ let $S_0=0$ and $S_k=d_1+\dots+d_k$. The substring $d_{i+1}\dots d_j$ ($0\le i<j\le8$) has digit sum $S_j-S_i$. So the number is good iff $S_0,S_1,\dots,S_8$ are pairwise distinct modulo $9$.

Nine pairwise distinct residues modulo $9$ with $S_0=0$ means that $(S_1,\dots,S_8)$ modulo $9$ is an ordering of the residues $1,2,\dots,8$. Conversely, for every ordering $(r_1,\dots,r_8)$ of $1,\dots,8$ there is exactly one good number with $S_k\equiv r_k$: the digit $d_k$ must satisfy $d_k\equiv r_k-r_{k-1}\not\equiv0\pmod 9$ (with $r_0=0$), and exactly one digit in $\{1,\dots,8\}$ does (the digits $0$ and $9$ are $\equiv0$). The first digit is then nonzero, as needed.

Hence the count is $8!=\boxed{40320}$.

Solution 2 (Choosing the digits one at a time)

A block of consecutive digits is divisible by $9$ iff its digit sum is. Write $S_k$ for the sum of the first $k$ digits, $S_0=0$; the block from position $i+1$ to $j$ has sum $S_j-S_i$.

Build the number from left to right. Suppose the first $k-1$ digits have been chosen and $S_0,\dots,S_{k-1}$ are distinct modulo $9$ (true for $k=1$). The new digit $d_k$ creates the new blocks ending at position $k$, with sums $S_k-S_i$ for $i<k$; they are all non-multiples of $9$ iff $S_k\not\equiv S_i$ for all $i<k$. As $d_k$ runs through $0,1,\dots,9$, the residue $S_k=S_{k-1}+d_k$ takes the value $S_{k-1}$ (for $d_k=0,9$) and each other residue exactly once (for $d_k=1,\dots,8$). The forbidden residues are the $k$ residues $S_0,\dots,S_{k-1}$, which include $S_{k-1}$; so exactly $9-k$ residues are allowed and each comes from exactly one digit. (For $k=1$ this also rules out a leading $0$.)

So the number of good eight-digit numbers is $8\cdot7\cdot6\cdots1=8!=\boxed{40320}$.

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · p-adic Valuation Basics

After expanding $(x+3y+2z)^9$ and collecting like terms, some of the terms are expressible as $n^2x^ay^bz^c$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are nonnegative integers and $n$ is a positive integer. Compute the sum of all possible values of $n$.

Show the answer

$322$

Show the solution

Solution (The prime 7 decides)

By the multinomial theorem the coefficient of $x^ay^bz^c$ ($a+b+c=9$) is \[\binom{9}{a,b,c}3^b2^c=\frac{9!}{a!\,b!\,c!}\,3^b2^c .\] The factor $7$ appears exactly once in $9!$, and in $a!\,b!\,c!$ only if one of $a,b,c$ is at least $7$. So if $a,b,c\le6$, the coefficient is divisible by $7$ but not by $7^2$, and it is not a square. It remains to check the permutations of $(7,1,1)$, $(7,2,0)$, $(8,1,0)$, $(9,0,0)$, whose multinomial coefficients are $72=2^33^2$, $36=2^23^2$, $9=3^2$ and $1$.

  • $(7,1,1)$ in any order: we need $3^b2^{c+3}$ to be a square, so $b$ even; but $b\in\{1,7\}$ is odd. None.

  • $(7,2,0)$ in any order: we need $b$ and $c$ even: $(a,b,c)=(7,2,0)$ gives $36\cdot9=18^2$ and $(7,0,2)$ gives $36\cdot4=12^2$.

  • $(8,1,0)$ in any order: we need $b,c$ even: $(1,8,0)$ gives $9\cdot3^8=243^2$ and $(1,0,8)$ gives $9\cdot2^8=48^2$.

  • $(9,0,0)$ in any order: only $(9,0,0)$ itself, coefficient $1=1^2$ ($3^9$ and $2^9$ are not squares).

The possible values of $n$ are $1,12,18,48,243$, with sum $\boxed{322}$.

Power Question

Problem 4(b)

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

A Heronian triangle is a triangle with positive integer side lengths and positive integer area. (Heron's formula: a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and semiperimeter $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ has area $K=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$.) Show that all Heronian triangles have an even perimeter.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Heron's formula times 16)

Let the sides be $a,b,c$, the perimeter $p=a+b+c$, and the area $K$, a positive integer. Heron's formula, multiplied by $16$, reads \[16K^2=p\,(p-2a)(p-2b)(p-2c).\] If $p$ were odd, then $p-2a$, $p-2b$, $p-2c$ would also be odd, so the right side would be odd. But the left side is even. Hence $p$ is even.

Solution 2 (A symmetric form of Heron's formula)

Expanding Heron's formula gives the well-known form \[16K^2=2(a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2)-(a^4+b^4+c^4),\] where $K$ is the (integer) area and $a,b,c$ are the sides. Suppose the perimeter $a+b+c$ is odd. Then an odd number of the sides (one or three) are odd, so $a^4+b^4+c^4$ is odd (a fourth power has the parity of its base). The right side is then even minus odd, which is odd, while $16K^2$ is even. This contradiction shows the perimeter is even.

Problem 6

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

A Heronian triangle is a triangle with positive integer side lengths and positive integer area. (Heron's formula: a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and semiperimeter $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ has area $K=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$.) Not all positive integers can be the side length of a Heronian triangle.

  • a) Prove that no Heronian triangle has a side length of $1$.

  • b) Prove that no Heronian triangle has a side length of $2$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Heron's formula directly)

We use $16K^2=(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c)$ for a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and area $K$.

a) If one side is $1$ and the others are $x\le y$, the triangle inequality $y<x+1$ forces $y=x$. Then $16K^2=(2x+1)(2x-1)\cdot1\cdot1=4x^2-1$. Since $(2x-1)^2<4x^2-1<(2x)^2$, this is not a perfect square, so $4K$ is irrational and $K$ is not an integer.

b) If one side is $2$ and the others are $x\le y$, then $y<x+2$, so $y=x$ or $y=x+1$.

  • $y=x$: $16K^2=(2x+2)(2x-2)\cdot2\cdot2=16(x^2-1)$, so $K^2=x^2-1$. For $x\ge2$, $(x-1)^2<x^2-1<x^2$, so $K$ is not an integer; for $x=1$ the triangle $(1,1,2)$ is degenerate.

  • $y=x+1$: $16K^2=(2x+3)(2x-1)\cdot3\cdot1$ is odd, so it is not $16$ times an integer square.

In all cases the area is not a positive integer.

Solution 2 (Parity and the isosceles altitude)

Parity lemma. A Heronian triangle has even perimeter: $16K^2=p(p-2a)(p-2b)(p-2c)$ with $p=a+b+c$, and if $p$ were odd the right side would be odd, while $16K^2$ is even.

a) A triangle with sides $1,x,y$ ($x\le y$) must have $y<x+1$, so $y=x$, and its perimeter $2x+1$ is odd. By the lemma it is not Heronian.

b) A triangle with sides $2,x,y$ ($x\le y<x+2$) has $y=x+1$ or $y=x$. If $y=x+1$, the perimeter $2x+3$ is odd, so it is not Heronian. If $y=x$, the triangle is isosceles with base $2$; the altitude to the base bisects it, so its length is $h=\sqrt{x^2-1}$ and the area is $\frac12\cdot2\cdot h=\sqrt{x^2-1}$. For this to be a positive integer $k$ we need $x^2-k^2=1$, i.e. $(x-k)(x+k)=1$, so $x=1$, $k=0$: area $0$, impossible.

So no Heronian triangle has a side of length $1$ or $2$.

Problem 8

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

A Heronian triangle is a triangle with positive integer side lengths and positive integer area. (Heron's formula: a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and semiperimeter $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ has area $K=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$.) Prove that the sine and cosine of each angle of a Heronian triangle are rational numbers.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Law of cosines and the area formula)

Let the triangle be $ABC$ with integer sides $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$ and integer area $K$. By the law of cosines \[\cos A=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc},\] a quotient of integers, hence rational. From $K=\frac12bc\sin A$ we get \[\sin A=\frac{2K}{bc},\] also rational. The same formulas with the letters permuted handle the angles $B$ and $C$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Let the triangle be $ABC$ with integer sides $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$ and integer area $K$. Place $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(c,0)$, and $C=(x,y)$ with $y>0$. Then $y=\frac{2K}{c}$ (the height to $AB$), which is rational. From $x^2+y^2=b^2$ and $(x-c)^2+y^2=a^2$, subtracting, $x=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2c}$, also rational.

Since $C=(b\cos A,\,b\sin A)$, we get $\cos A=\frac xb$ and $\sin A=\frac yb$, both rational. Repeating with $B$ or $C$ placed at the origin gives the same conclusion for the other two angles.

Problem 10

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation · Applications to Integer Equations

A Heronian triangle is a triangle with positive integer side lengths and positive integer area. (Heron's formula: a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and semiperimeter $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$ has area $K=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$.) Prove that there are infinitely many Heronian triangles with side lengths that are consecutive positive integers. Then state, without proof, the smallest five Heronian triangles whose sides have lengths that are consecutive positive integers.

Show the answer

$(3,4,5)$, $(13,14,15)$, $(51,52,53)$, $(193,194,195)$, $(723,724,725)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Heron's formula and a Pell equation)

Let the sides be $n-1,n,n+1$ with $n\ge3$. Then $s=\frac{3n}2$ and Heron's formula gives \[16K^2=3n\cdot(n+2)\cdot n\cdot(n-2)=3n^2(n^2-4).\] If $n$ is odd the right side is odd, which is impossible for an integer $K$. So $n=2m$, and $K^2=3m^2(m^2-1)$. Thus $K$ is an integer iff $3(m^2-1)$ is a perfect square $t^2$; then $3\mid t$, $t=3u$, and \[m^2-3u^2=1 ,\qquad K=3mu .\] Conversely every solution with $m\ge2$, $u\ge1$ gives the Heronian triangle $(2m-1,2m,2m+1)$ with area $3mu$.

Infinitely many. If $m^2-3u^2=1$, then $(2m+3u)^2-3(m+2u)^2=m^2-3u^2=1$, so $(m,u)\mapsto(2m+3u,m+2u)$ produces larger and larger solutions from $(2,1)$: \[(2,1),\ (7,4),\ (26,15),\ (97,56),\ (362,209),\ \dots\]

The smallest five. By the theory of the Pell equation all positive solutions are given by $m+u\sqrt3=(2+\sqrt3)^k$, which is exactly the list above. So the five smallest triangles are $n=4,14,52,194,724$: \[(3,4,5),\ (13,14,15),\ (51,52,53),\ (193,194,195),\ (723,724,725),\] with areas $6$, $84$, $1170$, $16296$, $226974$.

Solution 2 (The altitude to the middle side, and doubling)

Take sides $n-1,n,n+1$ and drop the altitude $h$ to the side of length $n$; measure positions along that side from its endpoint shared with the side $n-1$ (so the other endpoint is at $n$), and let the foot of the altitude be at signed position $x$. From $(n-1)^2-x^2=h^2=(n+1)^2-(n-x)^2$ we get $x=\frac{n-4}{2}$ and \[h^2=(n-1)^2-\Bigl(\frac{n-4}{2}\Bigr)^2=\frac{3(n^2-4)}{4},\qquad K=\frac{nh}2 .\] For $n=2m$ this is $h^2=3(m^2-1)$, so whenever $3(m^2-1)$ is a perfect square, $h$ is an integer and $K=mh$ is an integer.

A doubling step. Suppose $3(m^2-1)=h^2$. Put $m'=2m^2-1$. Then \[3(m'^2-1)=3\bigl((2m^2-1)^2-1\bigr)=3\cdot4m^2(m^2-1)=(2mh)^2,\] again a perfect square. Starting from $m=2$ ($h=3$, triangle $(3,4,5)$) we get $m=7,\,97,\,18817,\dots$, a strictly increasing sequence, hence infinitely many Heronian triangles $(2m-1,2m,2m+1)$.

The smallest five (no proof required): $(3,4,5)$, $(13,14,15)$, $(51,52,53)$, $(193,194,195)$, $(723,724,725)$; the middle sides $4,14,52,194,724$ satisfy $n_{k+1}=4n_k-n_{k-1}$.

Individual Round

Problem 4

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · State-Based Construction

Alice and Bob play a game, taking turns flipping a fair coin, with Alice going first. The first player to flip heads immediately after the other player flipped heads wins. Compute the probability Alice wins.

Show the answer

$\dfrac25$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Four states)

Before each flip the situation is described by whose turn it is and whether the previous flip was heads. Let $x$ and $y$ be Alice's winning probabilities when it is Alice's turn and the previous flip was not heads (or there was none), respectively was heads; let $u$ and $v$ be her winning probabilities when it is Bob's turn and the previous flip was not heads, respectively was heads. Conditioning on the next flip: \[x=\tfrac12v+\tfrac12u,\qquad y=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12u,\qquad u=\tfrac12y+\tfrac12x,\qquad v=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12x .\] Then $v=\frac x2$, $u=\frac{x+y}2$, and the first equation gives $4x=x+x+y$, i.e. $y=2x$. The second gives $2x=\frac12+\frac{3x}{4}$, so $x=\frac25$. Alice wins with probability $\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Solution 2 (Where the first HH occurs)

The game ends at the first occurrence of two consecutive heads in the sequence of flips, and the winner is the player who made the second of these heads. Alice makes flips $1,3,5,\dots$, so she wins iff the first $HH$ ends at an odd position $n$.

The first $HH$ ends at position $n\ge3$ iff flips $n-1,n$ are heads, flip $n-2$ is tails and the first $n-3$ flips contain no $HH$. The number of binary strings of length $L$ without $HH$ is the Fibonacci number $F_{L+2}$ ($F_1=F_2=1$): there are $1$ and $2$ such strings for $L=0,1$, and a valid string of length $L\ge2$ is a valid string of length $L-1$ followed by $T$, or a valid string of length $L-2$ followed by $TH$, so this happens with probability $F_{n-1}/2^n$ (also correct for $n=2$). Hence \[P(\text{Alice})=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{F_{2j}}{2^{2j+1}}=\frac12\sum_{j\ge1}F_{2j}\Bigl(\frac14\Bigr)^j .\] The even-indexed Fibonacci numbers satisfy $F_{2j+2}=3F_{2j}-F_{2j-2}$, so $\sum_{j\ge1}F_{2j}t^j=\frac{t}{1-3t+t^2}$ whenever the series converges (multiply by $1-3t+t^2$ and compare coefficients). It converges at $t=\frac14$, because $F_{2j+2}\le3F_{2j}$ gives $F_{2j}\le3^{j-1}$, so the terms are at most $\left(\frac34\right)^j$. At $t=\frac14$ this equals $\frac{1/4}{5/16}=\frac45$, and $P(\text{Alice})=\frac12\cdot\frac45=\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Solution 3 (Restarting after a tails)

Call a moment fresh if no coin has been flipped yet or the last flip was tails. At a fresh moment the past does not matter: nobody can win with the next flip. Let $p$ be the probability that the player who is about to flip at a fresh moment wins the game; by this observation $p$ does not depend on who that player is or on how many flips came before.

The game ends with probability $1$: flips $1,2$, flips $3,4$, and so on are disjoint pairs, each pair is $HH$ with probability $\frac14$ independently, so the probability that the game lasts beyond $2k$ flips is at most $\left(\frac34\right)^k\to0$. Hence at a fresh moment the player who is not about to flip wins with probability $1-p$.

Start at the beginning, a fresh moment with Alice to flip, and look at the first one or two flips.

  • Alice flips tails (probability $\frac12$). The moment is fresh with Bob to flip, so Alice now wins with probability $1-p$.

  • Alice flips heads and then Bob flips heads (probability $\frac14$). Bob wins at once.

  • Alice flips heads and then Bob flips tails (probability $\frac14$). The moment is fresh with Alice to flip, so she wins with probability $p$.

Therefore \[p=\frac12(1-p)+\frac14\cdot0+\frac14\,p,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac54\,p=\frac12,\] and Alice wins with probability $p=\boxed{\tfrac25}$.

Problem 6

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Suppose that $\triangle ABC$ has a right angle at $C$, and suppose that $AC=20$ and $AB=25$. Vertex $C$ is on a circle with center $O$, and the circle is tangent to $\overline{AB}$ at $G$. Given that chord $\overline{GC}$ has length $13$, compute the area of $\triangle GOC$.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{845}{48}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates with the tangent as an axis)

By the Pythagorean theorem $BC=15$, and the altitude from $C$ to $AB$ is $CH=\frac{15\cdot20}{25}=12$. Since $GC=13$, $GH=\sqrt{13^2-12^2}=5$.

Put $G$ at the origin and the line $AB$ on the $x$-axis, with $C=(\pm5,12)$. The circle is tangent to the $x$-axis at $G$, so its center is $O=(0,r)$ with $r>0$ (on the side of $C$). It passes through $C$: $25+(12-r)^2=r^2$, so $169=24r$ and $r=\frac{169}{24}$.

Triangle $GOC$ has the side $GO$ of length $r$ on the $y$-axis, and $C$ is at distance $5$ from that axis, so its area is \[\frac12\cdot\frac{169}{24}\cdot5=\boxed{\tfrac{845}{48}}.\] (Either position of $G$ on $AB$, $5$ units from $H$, gives the same area.)

Solution 2 (The tangent-chord angle)

Let $H$ be the foot of the altitude from $C$ to $AB$. By the Pythagorean theorem $BC=15$, so $CH=\frac{AC\cdot BC}{AB}=\frac{15\cdot20}{25}=12$, and $GH=\sqrt{13^2-12^2}=5$. So in right triangle $CHG$ the angle $\theta=\angle CGH$ between the chord $GC$ and the tangent line $AB$ satisfies $\sin\theta=\frac{12}{13}$, $\cos\theta=\frac5{13}$.

Since $OG\perp AB$ and $O$, $C$ lie on the same side of $AB$, $\angle OGC=90^\circ-\theta$; the triangle $GOC$ is isosceles ($OG=OC=r$), so $\angle OCG=90^\circ-\theta$ and $\angle GOC=2\theta$. The chord satisfies $GC=2r\sin\theta$, so $r=\frac{13}{2\cdot12/13}=\frac{169}{24}$. Therefore \[[GOC]=\frac12r^2\sin2\theta=r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\Bigl(\frac{169}{24}\Bigr)^2\cdot\frac{60}{169}=\frac{169\cdot60}{576}=\boxed{\tfrac{845}{48}}.\]

Problem 8

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Modular Equations and Solutions

The four-digit base-ten number $\underline{A}\,\underline{B}\,\underline{C}\,\underline{D}$ is a multiple of $13$, where $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are not necessarily distinct digits. The sum of its digits is also a multiple of $13$. The base-eight number $\underline{A}\,\underline{B}\,\underline{C}\,\underline{D}_{\,\text{eight}}$ is also a multiple of thirteen. Compute both possible values of $\underline{A}\,\underline{B}\,\underline{C}\,\underline{D}$.

Show the answer

$4225$ and $6331$

Show the solution

Solution (Three congruences modulo 13)

Since $\underline{ABCD}_{\,\text{eight}}$ is a base-eight numeral, $A,B,C,D\in\{0,\dots,7\}$ and $A\ge1$. Reduce modulo $13$: $1000\equiv-1$, $100\equiv9$, $10\equiv10$, and $512\equiv5$, $64\equiv-1$. The conditions are \[N\equiv-A+9B+10C+D\equiv0,\qquad M\equiv5A-B+8C+D\equiv0,\qquad S=A+B+C+D\equiv0 .\] Subtracting the third from the first two: \[-2A+8B+9C\equiv0,\qquad 4A-2B+7C\equiv0\pmod{13}.\] Multiplying the first by $7$ and the second by $9$ and subtracting eliminates $C$: $-14A+56B-36A+18B\equiv0$, i.e. $50A\equiv74B$, i.e. $11A\equiv9B$; multiplying by $6$ (the inverse of $11$) gives $A\equiv2B\pmod{13}$. With digits $0$–$7$ and $A\ge1$: $(A,B)\in\{(2,1),(4,2),(6,3),(1,7)\}$.

From $9C\equiv2A-8B$ and $9^{-1}\equiv3$: $C\equiv6A-24B\equiv6A+2B\pmod{13}$, giving $C=1,2,3,7$ respectively. Finally $S\equiv0$ with $S\le28$ means $S\in\{13,26\}$, so $D=13-(A+B+C)$ or $26-(A+B+C)$ must be a digit from $0$ to $7$:

  • $(2,1,1)$: $D=9$ or $22$; impossible.

  • $(4,2,2)$: $D=5$, giving $4225$.

  • $(6,3,3)$: $D=1$, giving $6331$.

  • $(1,7,7)$: $D=-2$ or $11$; impossible.

Check: $4225=13\cdot325$, $4+2+2+5=13$, $4225_8=2197=13^3$; $6331=13\cdot487$, $6+3+3+1=13$, $6331_8=3289=13\cdot 253$. The two numbers are $\boxed{4225\text{ and }6331}$.

Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Elementary Bounding and Local Estimates

Compute the value of $x$ that minimizes \[|x-100|+|2x-99|+|3x-98|+\dots+|98x-3|+|99x-2|+|100x-1|.\]

Show the answer

$\dfrac{30}{71}$

Show the solution

Solution (A weighted median)

Write the $k$th term as $|kx-(101-k)|=k\,|x-c_k|$ with $c_k=\frac{101-k}{k}=\frac{101}{k}-1$, so \[g(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{100}k\,|x-c_k|,\qquad c_1>c_2>\dots>c_{100}.\] $g$ is convex and piecewise linear; on an interval containing no $c_k$ its slope is (total weight of the $c_k$ to the left of $x$) minus (total weight to the right). The total weight is $1+2+\dots+100=5050$.

Just to the left of $c_j$, the points to the right are $c_1,\dots,c_j$, with weight $\frac{j(j+1)}2$, so the slope is $5050-j(j+1)$. Just to the right of $c_j$, the points to the right are $c_1,\dots,c_{j-1}$, and the slope is $5050-j(j-1)$. For $j=71$: the slope is $5050-71\cdot72=-62<0$ on the left and $5050-71\cdot70=80>0$ on the right. So $g$ decreases up to $c_{71}$ and increases after it, and the unique minimizer is \[x=c_{71}=\frac{101-71}{71}=\boxed{\tfrac{30}{71}}.\]

Tiebreaker 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Residue Classes and Case Analysis

For each positive integer $n$, let $f(n)$ denote the sum of the first $n$ positive perfect squares. For example, $f(3)=1+4+9=14$. Compute the least positive integer $m$ for which $f(m)$ and $f(2026)$ have the same remainder upon division by $2026$.

Show the answer

$506$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The formula for $f$)

We have $f(n)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$, and $2026=2\cdot1013$ with $1013$ prime. Then \[f(2026)=\frac{2026\cdot2027\cdot4053}{6}=1013\cdot2027\cdot1351,\] an odd multiple of $1013$, so $f(2026)\equiv1013\pmod{2026}$. We need the least $m$ with $f(m)$ an odd multiple of $1013$.

Since $\gcd(1013,6)=1$, $1013\mid f(m)$ iff $1013\mid m(m+1)(2m+1)$, i.e. (as $1013$ is prime) $m\equiv0$, $m\equiv-1$, or $2m+1\equiv0\pmod{1013}$. The least positive solutions of these are $1013$, $1012$, and $506$ (from $2m+1=1013$). Finally $f(506)=\frac{506\cdot507\cdot1013}{6}=253\cdot169\cdot1013$ is odd. So the answer is $\boxed{506}$.

Solution 2 (Symmetry of squares modulo 1013)

Let $p=1013$ (prime), so $2026=2p$, and we compare $f(m)$ and $f(2026)$ modulo $2$ and modulo $p$.

Modulo $2$. $k^2$ has the parity of $k$, so $f(n)\equiv$ (number of odd $k\le n$) $=\lceil n/2\rceil\pmod 2$. Thus $f(2026)\equiv1013\equiv1$.

Modulo $p$. Since $k^2\equiv(p-k)^2$, the sum of $k^2$ over $k=1,\dots,p-1$ is twice the sum over $k=1,\dots,\frac{p-1}{2}$; and it is $\frac{(p-1)p(2p-1)}{6}\equiv0$ because $p>3$. Hence $f(\frac{p-1}2)=f(506)\equiv0$ (as $2$ is invertible), and $f(2026)=f(2p)\equiv2f(p-1)\equiv0$. So we need $f(m)\equiv0\pmod p$ and $f(m)$ odd.

$m=506$ works: $f(506)\equiv0\pmod p$ and $\lceil 506/2\rceil=253$ is odd. No smaller $m$ works: for $m<506$, $6f(m)=m(m+1)(2m+1)$ is a product of three positive integers less than $p$, so $p\nmid 6f(m)$ and $p\nmid f(m)$. The answer is $\boxed{506}$.

Tiebreaker 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Nonlinear Recurrences

Define the sequence $\{a_n\}$ by $a_1=1$, and for all $k\ge1$, \[a_{k+1}=\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{a_k+1}\right\rfloor .\] Compute $a_{2026}$.

Show the answer

$1013$

Show the solution

Solution (Guess and prove by induction)

Computing, $a_1=1$, $a_2=\lfloor\frac22\rfloor=1$, $a_3=\lfloor\frac32\rfloor=1$, $a_4=2$, $a_5=1$, $a_6=3$, $a_7=1$, $a_8=4$. We claim $a_k=1$ for odd $k$ and $a_k=\frac k2$ for even $k$.

This holds for $k\le 2$. Assume it for $k$.

  • If $k$ is odd, $a_k=1$ and $a_{k+1}=\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor=\frac{k+1}2$ since $k+1$ is even.

  • If $k$ is even, $a_k=\frac k2$ and $a_{k+1}=\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{k/2+1}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{2k+2}{k+2}\right\rfloor=1$, because $k+2\le 2k+2<2(k+2)$.

So the claim holds for all $k$, and $a_{2026}=\boxed{1013}$.

Tiebreaker 3

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 40: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Methods · Slope, Parallelism, and Perpendicularity

A square is in the coordinate plane. The product of the slopes of two sides of the square is $2026$. Compute the product of the slopes of the other two sides.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{1}{2026}$

Show the solution

Solution (Parallel and perpendicular sides)

A square has two pairs of parallel sides, and adjacent sides are perpendicular. Since a product of two slopes is given, the two sides have slopes; if they were adjacent, their slopes $m$ and $-\frac1m$ would have product $-1\ne2026$. So they are opposite sides, both with slope $m$, and $m^2=2026$. The other two sides are perpendicular to them, both with slope $-\frac1m$, so their product is $\frac1{m^2}=\boxed{\tfrac{1}{2026}}$.
