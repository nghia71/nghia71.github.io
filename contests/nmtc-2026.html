Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Find all positive integers $m$ and $n$ such that \[(m^2-n^2)^2=23n+8.\]

Show the answer

$(m,n)=(6,7)$

Show the solution

Solution (Bound $n$, then test)

The right side is positive, so $m\ne n$.

If $m>n$, then $m^2-n^2\ge (n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1$, so $(2n+1)^2\le 23n+8$, i.e. $4n^2-19n-7\le0$, which forces $n\le5$. If $m<n$, then $n^2-m^2\ge n^2-(n-1)^2=2n-1$, so $(2n-1)^2\le23n+8$, i.e. $4n^2-27n-7\le 0$, which forces $n\le 7$.

So $n\le7$ and $23n+8\in\{31,54,77,100,123,146,169\}$ must be a perfect square: $n=4$ ($100$) or $n=7$ ($169$). For $n=4$, $m^2=16\pm10\in\{26,6\}$, not a square. For $n=7$, $m^2=49\pm13\in\{62,36\}$, so $m=6$. The only solution is $\boxed{(m,n)=(6,7)}$: indeed $(36-49)^2=169=23\cdot7+8$.

Problem 2

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Direct Double Counting

One hundred boys and one hundred girls go to Navarathri Garba dance. Knowing that each boy dances with a girl at most once and that there are $1050$ couples danced, prove that there are two boys and two girls who danced with both the boys.

Show the solution

Solution (Count pairs of girls)

Let boy $i$ dance with $d_i$ girls, so $\sum_{i=1}^{100}d_i=1050$. Count the triples (boy, two girls who both danced with him). Boy $i$ contributes $\binom{d_i}{2}$, and \[\sum_{i=1}^{100}\binom{d_i}{2}=\frac12\Bigl(\sum d_i^2-\sum d_i\Bigr)\ge\frac12\Bigl(\frac{1050^2}{100}-1050\Bigr)=\frac12(11025-1050)=4987.5\] by the inequality $\sum d_i^2\ge\frac{(\sum d_i)^2}{100}$ (Cauchy-Schwarz, or QM-AM). There are only $\binom{100}{2}=4950<4987.5$ pairs of girls, so by the pigeonhole principle some pair of girls is counted for two different boys. Those two boys and two girls are as required: each of the two boys danced with both girls.

Problem 3

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Positive real number sequences $a_n$ and $b_n$ are defined by \[a_{n+1}=a_n-\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a_i}},\qquad b_{n+1}=b_n+\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{b_i}}.\] If $a_{100}=b_{99}$, show that $a_{100}+b_{100}>a_{101}+b_{101}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two invariants)

Let $S_n=1+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{a_i}$ and $T_n=1+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{b_i}$, so $a_{n+1}=a_n-\frac1{S_n}$ and $b_{n+1}=b_n+\frac1{T_n}$. Then \[a_{n+1}S_{n+1}=a_{n+1}S_n+1=a_nS_n,\qquad b_{n+1}T_{n+1}=b_{n+1}T_n+1=b_nT_n+2.\] Since $a_1S_1=a_1+1$ and $b_1T_1=b_1+1$, we get $a_nS_n=a_1+1$ and $b_nT_n=b_1+2n-1$ for all $n$. Put $u=a_1+1$ and $v=b_1+198$. The claim is \[a_{100}-a_{101}>b_{101}-b_{100}\iff \frac{a_{100}}{u}>\frac{b_{100}}{b_1+199}.\] Estimates. $a_n-a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n}{u}\le\frac{a_1}{u}=1-\frac1u$ (the $a_n$ decrease), so $a_1-a_{100}\le 99-\frac{99}{u}$. Also $b_{n+1}-b_n=\frac1{T_n}<1$ as $T_n>1$, so $b_{99}<b_1+98$. With $c=a_{100}=b_{99}$: \[u=a_1+1\le c+100-\frac{99}{u}<v-\frac{99}{u}.\] In particular $u<v$, and $uv<v^2-\frac{99v}{u}<v^2-99<v^2-1$.

Conclusion. $b_{100}=b_{99}+\frac{b_{99}}{b_1+197}=\frac{cv}{v-1}$, so \[\frac{b_{100}}{b_1+199}=\frac{cv}{(v-1)(v+1)}=\frac{cv}{v^2-1}<\frac{cv}{uv}=\frac cu=\frac{a_{100}}{u},\] which is the required inequality $a_{100}+b_{100}>a_{101}+b_{101}$.

Solution 2 (Compare the steps twice)

Write $A_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac1{a_i}$ and $B_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac1{b_i}$, so that \[a_n-a_{n+1}=\frac1{1+A_n}>0,\qquad b_{n+1}-b_n=\frac1{1+B_n}>0 .\] Thus $(a_n)$ is strictly decreasing and $(b_n)$ is strictly increasing. The claim $a_{100}+b_{100}>a_{101}+b_{101}$ is equivalent to $a_{100}-a_{101}>b_{101}-b_{100}$, that is, to $A_{100}<B_{100}$.

Step 1: $a_{99}>b_{100}$. For $1\le i\le 99$ we have $a_i\ge a_{99}>a_{100}=b_{99}\ge b_i$, so $\frac1{a_i}<\frac1{b_i}$, and adding, $A_{99}<B_{99}$. Hence \[a_{99}-a_{100}=\frac1{1+A_{99}}>\frac1{1+B_{99}}=b_{100}-b_{99},\] and adding $a_{100}=b_{99}$ gives $a_{99}>b_{100}$.

Step 2: $A_{100}<B_{100}$. The term $\frac1{a_{100}}$ of $A_{100}$ equals the term $\frac1{b_{99}}$ of $B_{100}$; remove both. What remains of $A_{100}$ is $\frac1{a_1}+\dots+\frac1{a_{99}}$, and what remains of $B_{100}$ is the sum of the $99$ terms $\frac1{b_j}$ with $j\in\{1,\dots,98,100\}$. For every such $i$ and $j$, \[a_i\ge a_{99}>b_{100}\ge b_j\] by Step 1 and the monotonicity, so each of the $99$ remaining terms of $A_{100}$ is smaller than each of the $99$ remaining terms of $B_{100}$. Therefore $A_{100}<B_{100}$, so \[a_{100}-a_{101}=\frac1{1+A_{100}}>\frac1{1+B_{100}}=b_{101}-b_{100},\] which is the required inequality $a_{100}+b_{100}>a_{101}+b_{101}$.

Problem 4

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Iteration and Self-Composition

Let $\mathbb{N}_+$ be the set of positive integers and the function $f:\mathbb{N}_+\to\mathbb{N}_+$ satisfy the following conditions:

  • (a) $f(n)<f(n+1)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_+$;

  • (b) $f(f(f(f(n))))=5n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_+$.

Find the value of $f(2000)$.

Show the answer

$f(2000)=2625$

Show the solution

Solution (Small values, then scaling by $5$)

Since $f$ is strictly increasing on positive integers, $f(n)\ge n$; and $f(m)=m$ would give $5m=f(f(f(f(m))))=m$, so $f(m)>m$ for all $m$. Hence \[1<f(1)<f(f(1))<f(f(f(1)))<f(f(f(f(1))))=5,\] which forces $f(1)=2$, $f(2)=3$, $f(3)=4$, $f(4)=5$. Next, applying $f$ to (b) at $n$ and using (b) at $f(n)$, \[f(5n)=f\bigl(f^{4}(n)\bigr)=f^{4}\bigl(f(n)\bigr)=5f(n).\] So $f(5^k)=2\cdot5^k$ and $f(4\cdot5^k)=5^{k+1}$. Between $5^k$ and $4\cdot5^k$ there are $3\cdot5^k$ steps, and $f$ rises by exactly $3\cdot5^k$ over them while rising by at least $1$ at each step; so it rises by exactly $1$ at each step: $f(n)=n+5^k$ for $5^k\le n\le4\cdot5^k$. With $k=4$ ($625\le2000\le2500$): \[f(2000)=2000+625=\boxed{2625}.\] (Such an $f$ exists: $f(n)=n+5^k$ for $5^k\le n\le4\cdot5^k$ and $f(n)=5n-15\cdot5^k$ for $4\cdot5^k\le n\le5^{k+1}$.)

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

Two circles $S_1$ and $S_2$ with centers $O_1$ and $O_2$ respectively intersect at $X$ and $Y$. $AB$ is an external common tangent to the circles with $A$ and $B$ lying on $S_1$ and $S_2$ respectively. Tangents to $S_1$ and $S_2$ at $X$ intersect the line of centers $O_1O_2$ at $L$ and $K$ respectively. Show that $XY$, $AL$ and $BK$ are concurrent.

Show the answer

proof (of the corrected statement: $XY$, $AL$, $BK$ are concurrent)

Show the solution

Solution (Coordinates on the line of centres)

Remark on the statement. As printed, the claim is false: for $O_1=(-1,0)$, $O_2=(2,0)$, $X=(0,1)$ and the lower common tangent, $AK$ and $BL$ meet the line $XY$ at heights about $0.77$ and $5.55$. What is true, and proved below, is that $XY$, $AL$ and $BK$ are concurrent (the labels $L$, $K$ are interchanged).

Put the midpoint of $XY$ at the origin, the line $O_1O_2$ as the $x$-axis and $XY$ as the $y$-axis: $X=(0,h)$, $O_1=(-p,0)$, $O_2=(q,0)$ with $p+q>0$ and $p,q\ne0$ (so that $L$ and $K$ exist), and radii $r_1=\sqrt{p^2+h^2}$, $r_2=\sqrt{q^2+h^2}$.

The tangent to $S_1$ at $X$ is $\{(x,y): px+h(y-h)=0\}$ (perpendicular to $O_1X$), which meets the $x$-axis at $L=(h^2/p,0)$; similarly $K=(-h^2/q,0)$.

Let $(u,w)$ be the unit vector from the centres towards the common tangent, so $A=O_1+r_1(u,w)$, $B=O_2+r_2(u,w)$. The tangent line is at distance $r_1$ from $O_1$ and $r_2$ from $O_2$ on the same side, so $(u,w)\cdot O_1+r_1=(u,w)\cdot O_2+r_2$, i.e. \[(p+q)u=r_1-r_2.\] A line through $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,0)$ meets the $y$-axis at height $\frac{y_1x_2}{x_2-x_1}$. For $AL$: \[\frac{r_1w\cdot h^2/p}{h^2/p+p-r_1u}=\frac{r_1wh^2}{r_1^2-pr_1u}=\frac{wh^2}{r_1-pu}.\] For $BK$: \[\frac{r_2w\cdot(-h^2/q)}{-h^2/q-q-r_2u}=\frac{r_2wh^2}{r_2^2+qr_2u}=\frac{wh^2}{r_2+qu}.\] The denominators are equal because $(r_1-pu)-(r_2+qu)=r_1-r_2-(p+q)u=0$. Hence $AL$ and $BK$ meet the line $XY$ at the same point $Z$, i.e. $XY$, $AL$, $BK$ are concurrent at $Z$. (If the common denominator is $0$, then $AL$ and $BK$ are both parallel to $XY$, and the three lines are parallel instead of concurrent.)

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Factorization-Based Solutions

Let $p$ and $q$ be primes such that $p^2+pq+q^2$ is a perfect square. Show that $p^2-pq+q^2$ is a prime.

Show the solution

Solution (Factor a difference of squares)

Let $p^2+pq+q^2=k^2$ with $k>0$. Then $(p+q)^2-k^2=pq$, i.e. \[(p+q-k)(p+q+k)=pq.\] Since $p^2<k^2<(p+q)^2$, the first factor is a positive integer, and the second factor $p+q+k$ exceeds both $p$ and $q$. The positive divisors of $pq$ are $1,p,q,pq$, so $p+q+k=pq$ and $p+q-k=1$. Adding, $2(p+q)=pq+1$, i.e. $(p-2)(q-2)=3$. Hence $\{p,q\}=\{3,5\}$ (check: $9+15+25=49=7^2$), and \[p^2-pq+q^2=9-15+25=19,\] which is a prime.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments · Basic Pigeonhole Principle

Prove that any set of $55$ integers from the set $\{1,2,\dots,100\}$ will contain a pair of numbers that differ by $9$, a pair that differ by $10$, a pair that differ by $12$, and a pair that differ by $13$. Will such a set of $55$ integers also contain a pair of numbers that differ by $11$?

Show the answer

Pairs with differences $9,10,12,13$ always exist; difference $11$: No, not necessarily

Show the solution

Solution (Chains of numbers $d$ apart)

Fix $d$ and split $\{1,\dots,100\}$ into the $d$ chains $r,r+d,r+2d,\dots$ ($1\le r\le d$). Consecutive members of a chain differ by $d$, so a set with no two numbers differing by $d$ contains at most $\lceil L/2\rceil$ numbers from a chain of length $L$. Hence such a set has at most

  • $d=9$ (one chain of $12$, eight of $11$): $6+8\cdot6=54$;

  • $d=10$ (ten chains of $10$): $10\cdot5=50$;

  • $d=12$ (four chains of $9$, eight of $8$): $4\cdot5+8\cdot4=52$;

  • $d=13$ (nine chains of $8$, four of $7$): $9\cdot4+4\cdot4=52$

elements. All are below $55$, so every $55$-element subset has pairs with differences $9,10,12,13$.

For $d=11$ the same count gives $5+10\cdot5=55$, and the bound is attained: \[\{1,\dots,11\}\cup\{23,\dots,33\}\cup\{45,\dots,55\}\cup\{67,\dots,77\}\cup\{89,\dots,99\}\] has $55$ elements; two numbers in the same block differ by at most $10$, and in different blocks by at least $12$. So the answer to the last question is No.

Problem 8

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy · Remainder, Factor, and Evaluation Logic

Find all polynomials $P(x)=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$ where $a,b,c$ are integers such that the following hold:

  • (a) $x-1$ divides $P(x)$;

  • (b) $P(5)=P(-5)$;

  • (c) $P(x)=0$ has a root between $2$ and $3$.

Show the answer

$P(x)=x^3+4x^2-25x+20$ and $P(x)=x^3+5x^2-25x+19$

Show the solution

Solution (Reduce to a quadratic)

From (b), $P(5)-P(-5)=2\cdot125+10b=0$, so $b=-25$. From (a), $P(1)=1+a-25+c=0$, so $c=24-a$, and \[P(x)=(x-1)\,Q(x),\qquad Q(x)=x^2+(a+1)x+(a-24).\] The root in $(2,3)$ is a root of $Q$. Now $Q(2)=3(a-6)$ and $Q(3)=4(a-3)$.

  • If $3<a<6$, then $Q(2)<0<Q(3)$ and $Q$ has a root in $(2,3)$: $a=4,5$.

  • If $a\ge 6$ or $a\le3$, then $Q(2)$ and $Q(3)$ are both $\ge0$ or both $\le0$. For $a\le 3$ both are $\le0$, and an upward parabola that is $\le 0$ at $2$ and $3$ is $<0$ in between. For $a\ge6$ the vertex $-\frac{a+1}{2}<0$, so $Q$ is increasing on $[2,3]$ and $Q>Q(2)\ge0$ there.

So $a\in\{4,5\}$: $\boxed{P(x)=x^3+4x^2-25x+20}$ (root $\frac{-5+\sqrt{105}}{2}\approx2.62$) and $\boxed{P(x)=x^3+5x^2-25x+19}$ (root $-3+2\sqrt7\approx2.29$).

Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework · Pythagorean Triples

$\triangle ABC$ is a right-angled triangle, $\angle ACB=90^\circ$. The sides are of integral length. If the product of the legs is equal to the sum of all sides, then find the sides of the triangle. How many such triangles are there?

Show the answer

The sides are $3,4,5$; there is exactly $1$ such triangle

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Remove $c$)

Let the legs be $a,b$ and the hypotenuse $c$. Then $c=ab-a-b$, and $c^2=a^2+b^2$ gives \[(ab-a-b)^2=a^2+b^2\iff a^2b^2-2a^2b-2ab^2+2ab=0\iff ab\bigl((a-2)(b-2)-2\bigr)=0.\] So $(a-2)(b-2)=2$. Since $a,b\ge1$, both factors are at least $-1$, so a factorisation $2=(-1)(-2)$ is impossible; hence $\{a-2,b-2\}=\{1,2\}$, i.e. $\{a,b\}=\{3,4\}$ and $c=5$. Check: $3\cdot4=12=3+4+5$. There is $\boxed{1}$ such triangle, with sides $3,4,5$.

Solution 2 (The inradius is 1)

Let the legs be $a=BC$, $b=CA$ and the hypotenuse $c=AB$, let $s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}$ be the semiperimeter and $r$ the inradius. The area is $\frac{ab}{2}$, and also $rs$ (split the triangle into three triangles with apex $I$, the incentre, and heights $r$). The condition $ab=a+b+c$ says $\frac{ab}2=s$, so $rs=s$ and $r=1$.

In a right triangle $r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}$: the incircle touches the legs at points at distance $r$ from $C$ (with $I$ these points and $C$ form a square), so the tangent lengths from $B$ and $A$ are $a-r$ and $b-r$, and they add up to $c$. Hence $c=a+b-2$. Substituting into $a^2+b^2=c^2$: \[a^2+b^2=a^2+b^2+4+2ab-4a-4b\iff ab-2a-2b+2=0\iff (a-2)(b-2)=2.\] Both factors are at least $-1$ because $a,b\ge1$, so the factorisation $(-1)(-2)$ is impossible, and $\{a-2,b-2\}=\{1,2\}$. So the legs are $3$ and $4$, and $c=5$; indeed $3\cdot4=12=3+4+5$. There is $\boxed{1}$ such triangle, with sides $3,4,5$.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 31: Linear Diophantine Equations · Existence via GCD

$n$ is a natural number and $n\le 2026$. If $n$ divides $20^n+17k$, for some natural number $k$, then find the number of such $n$.

Show the answer

$1907$

Show the solution

Solution (When is $17$ invertible modulo $n$)

If $17\mid n$, then $n\mid 20^n+17k$ would give $17\mid 20^n$, which is false. If $\gcd(n,17)=1$, then $17$ has an inverse modulo $n$, and $k\equiv-20^n\cdot17^{-1}\pmod n$ has a positive solution $k$; then $n\mid20^n+17k$. So the valid $n$ are exactly those not divisible by $17$: \[2026-\left\lfloor\frac{2026}{17}\right\rfloor=2026-119=\boxed{1907}.\]

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition

In triangle $ABC$, the incircle, with center $I$, touches the side $BC$ at point $D$. Line $DI$ meets $AC$ at $P$. The tangent line from $P$ to the incircle (different from $AC$) intersects $AB$ at $Q$. If line $QI$ and $BC$ intersect at point $R$, prove that quadrilateral $AIRC$ is a cyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (Directions of lines through the tangency points)

Let the incircle touch $AB$, $CA$, $PQ$ at $F$, $E$, $T$ (and $BC$ at $D$). Measure directions of lines as angles modulo $180^\circ$, and positions of points on the incircle as angles $\varphi$ around $I$ (fixed real numbers). We use two facts.

  • The tangent at a point with position $\varphi$ has direction $\varphi+90^\circ$.

  • If two tangents from a point $Z$ touch at positions $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$, then $ZI$ is perpendicular to the chord of contact, and has direction $\frac{\varphi_1+\varphi_2}{2}$ (well defined modulo $180^\circ$).

The two tangents from $P$ are symmetric in the line $PI$, which is the line $DI$ of direction $\varphi_D$; so $T$ is the reflection of $E$ in this line: $\varphi_T=2\varphi_D-\varphi_E$. Therefore \[\text{dir}(AI)=\tfrac{\varphi_F+\varphi_E}{2},\quad \text{dir}(QI)=\tfrac{\varphi_F+\varphi_T}{2}=\tfrac{\varphi_F-\varphi_E}{2}+\varphi_D,\quad \text{dir}(AC)=\varphi_E+90^\circ,\quad\text{dir}(BC)=\varphi_D+90^\circ.\] Writing $\measuredangle(\ell,m)=\text{dir}(m)-\text{dir}(\ell)$ for directed angles, \[\measuredangle(AI,AC)=\tfrac{\varphi_E-\varphi_F}{2}+90^\circ=\measuredangle(QI,BC)=\measuredangle(RI,RC).\] The directed angles subtending $IC$ at $A$ and at $R$ are equal, so $A,I,R,C$ are concyclic.

Problem 4

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Recursive Definitions

Given a sequence: $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$, where $a_n=a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}$, for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $n\ge 3$. Also, $S_n=a_1+a_2+a_3+\dots+a_{n-1}+a_n$. If $S_{2003}=2026$ and $S_{2026}=2003$, then find $S_{2030}$.

Show the answer

$S_{2030}=-2072$

Show the solution

Solution (Period six)

Let $a_1=x$, $a_2=y$. Then the sequence is $x,\ y,\ y-x,\ -x,\ -y,\ x-y,\ x,\ y,\dots$ with period $6$ and sum $0$ over a period. The partial sums $S_1,\dots,S_6$ are \[x,\quad x+y,\quad 2y,\quad 2y-x,\quad y-x,\quad 0,\] and $S_n$ depends only on $n\bmod 6$. Since $2003\equiv5$, $2026\equiv4$, $2030\equiv2\pmod 6$: \[y-x=2026,\qquad 2y-x=2003.\] So $y=-23$, $x=-2049$ and $S_{2030}=x+y=\boxed{-2072}$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 17: Factoring and Structural Decomposition · Factoring to Solve

Find all possible values of natural numbers $m$ and $n$ such that \[12mn-175=105m+140n.\]

Show the answer

$(m,n)=(105,10)$

Show the solution

Solution (Simon's factoring trick)

Multiply by $12$ and factor: \[144mn-1260m-1680n=2100\iff (12m-140)(12n-105)=2100+140\cdot105=16800.\] Let $u=12m-140$, $v=12n-105$, so $uv=16800=2^5\cdot3\cdot5^2\cdot7$, $u\equiv4\pmod{12}$, $v\equiv3\pmod{12}$, $u\ge-128$, $v\ge-93$. Both negative is impossible, since then $uv\le128\cdot93<16800$; so $u,v>0$. As $v$ is odd and $3\nmid u$, we get $u=32t$, $v=3w$ with $tw=175$; then $u\equiv 2t\equiv1\pmod 3$ needs $t\equiv2\pmod3$, so $t\in\{5,35\}$, and $v\equiv3\pmod{12}$ needs $w\equiv1\pmod4$, which rules out $w=35$ and leaves $t=35$, $w=5$. So $u=1120$, $v=15$, giving $\boxed{(m,n)=(105,10)}$. (Check: $12\cdot1050-175=12425=105\cdot105+140\cdot10$.)

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 33: Radicals and Irrational Expressions · Operations with Radicals

Find all natural numbers $x,y,z$ such that \[\sqrt{\frac{2026}{x+y}}+\sqrt{\frac{2026}{y+z}}+\sqrt{\frac{2026}{z+x}}\] is also a natural number.

Show the answer

$(1013,1013,1013)$, $(9117,9117,9117)$, the permutations of $(1013,1013,7091)$ and of $(4052,4052,28364)$

Show the solution

Solution (Each root is rational, then unit fractions)

Each square root is rational. Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0$ be the three square roots (their squares are rational) and $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=N\in\mathbb N$. Then $\alpha+\beta=N-\gamma$; squaring, $2\alpha\beta+2N\gamma=N^2+\gamma^2-\alpha^2-\beta^2=:K$, a rational number, and $K>0$. Squaring $2\alpha\beta=K-2N\gamma$ gives $4\alpha^2\beta^2=K^2-4KN\gamma+4N^2\gamma^2$, so $\gamma=\frac{K^2+4N^2\gamma^2-4\alpha^2\beta^2}{4KN}$ is rational; likewise $\alpha,\beta$.

Form of the sums. If $\sqrt{2026/s}=p/q$ in lowest terms, then $2026q^2=sp^2$, so $p^2\mid2026=2\cdot1013$, which is squarefree; thus $p=1$ and $s=2026q^2$. So \[x+y=2026a^2,\quad y+z=2026b^2,\quad z+x=2026c^2,\qquad \frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c\in\mathbb N.\] Unit fractions. With $a\le b\le c$: the sum $3$ needs $(1,1,1)$; the sum $2$ needs $(1,2,2)$; the sum $1$ needs $(2,3,6)$, $(2,4,4)$ or $(3,3,3)$.

Back to $x,y,z$. $x=1013(a^2+c^2-b^2)$, $y=1013(a^2+b^2-c^2)$, $z=1013(b^2+c^2-a^2)$ must be positive. $(2,3,6)$ fails ($4+9<36$). The others give \[(1013,1013,1013),\ (9117,9117,9117),\ (1013,1013,7091),\ (4052,4052,28364)\] up to order (sums $3,1,2,1$ respectively). So the solutions are the first two triples and all permutations of the last two: $8$ ordered triples in all.

Problem 7

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 14: Permutations, Combinations, and Binomial Structure · Permutation Structure

How many ways can you mark $8$ squares of an $8\times 8$ chessboard so that no two marked squares are in the same row or column, and none of the four corner squares is marked? (Rotations and reflections are considered different.)

Show the answer

$21600$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Place the first and last rows)

A marking is a permutation: row $i$ gets column $\sigma(i)$. The corners are excluded exactly when $\sigma(1)\notin\{1,8\}$ and $\sigma(8)\notin\{1,8\}$. Choose $\sigma(1)$ among the $6$ middle columns, $\sigma(8)$ among the remaining $5$ middle columns, and the other six rows in $6!$ ways: \[6\cdot5\cdot720=\boxed{21600}.\]

Solution 2 (Inclusion-exclusion)

Of the $8!=40320$ permutations, let $X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4$ be the sets using the corners $(1,1),(1,8),(8,1),(8,8)$; each has $7!$ elements. Pairs: only $X_1\cap X_4$ and $X_2\cap X_3$ are nonempty ($6!$ each; the others share a row or column). There are no triple intersections. So the count is \[8!-4\cdot7!+2\cdot6!=40320-20160+1440=\boxed{21600}.\]

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Law of Sines and Law of Cosines

In a triangle $ABC$ we have $\angle ACB=\angle BAC+90^\circ$. The point $D$ is taken on the extended $BC$ such that $AC=AD$. A point $E$ is taken such that points $A$ and $E$ are on the opposite sides of side $BC$ and $\angle EBC=\angle BAC$, $\angle EDC=\frac12\angle BAC$. Prove that $\angle CED=\angle ABC$.

Show the solution

Solution (Ratio lemma in triangle $BED$)

Let $\alpha=\angle BAC$; then $\angle ABC=180^\circ-\alpha-(\alpha+90^\circ)=90^\circ-2\alpha$, so $0<\alpha<45^\circ$. As $\angle ACB$ is obtuse, the foot $H$ of the altitude from $A$ lies on ray $BC$ beyond $C$, and $D$ is the reflection of $C$ in $H$, beyond $C$. Take $BC=1$. By the law of sines $AC=\frac{\sin(90^\circ-2\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{\cos2\alpha}{\sin\alpha}$, and $\angle ACH=90^\circ-\alpha$, so \[CD=2CH=2AC\cos(90^\circ-\alpha)=2\cos2\alpha.\] In triangle $BED$: $\angle B=\alpha$, $\angle D=\frac\alpha2$, $\angle E=180^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}2$, so $\frac{BE}{DE}=\frac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\sin\alpha}$. Let $x=\angle CED$, so $\angle BEC=180^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}{2}-x$. Triangles $BEC$ and $DEC$ have the same height from $E$, so comparing their areas $\tfrac12BE\cdot EC\sin\angle BEC$ and $\tfrac12DE\cdot EC\sin x$ gives \[\frac{BC}{CD}=\frac{BE\sin\angle BEC}{DE\sin x}\iff \frac{1}{2\cos2\alpha}=\frac{\sin(\alpha/2)\,\sin\bigl(180^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}2-x\bigr)}{\sin\alpha\,\sin x}.\] For $x=90^\circ-2\alpha$ the right side is $\frac{\sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)}{\sin\alpha\cos2\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\cos2\alpha}$, so this value works. The right side, as a function of $x\in(0^\circ,180^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}2)$, equals a positive constant times $\frac{\sin(\theta-x)}{\sin x}=\sin\theta\cot x-\cos\theta$ (with $\theta=180^\circ-\frac{3\alpha}{2}$), which is strictly decreasing; so $x$ is unique. Hence $\angle CED=90^\circ-2\alpha=\angle ABC$.
