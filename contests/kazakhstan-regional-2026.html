Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

A problem committee consists of several authors. It is known that among any two authors exactly one trusts the other. Every day, each author invents one new problem and sends it to everyone he trusts, together with all problems received before that day (problems received on the same day are not forwarded). Prove that if on the third day some author does not receive back the problem he sent on the first day, then there exists a problem that does not reach all authors.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Split the authors around $u$)

Draw an arrow $x\to y$ when author $x$ trusts author $y$; then for any two authors exactly one of the arrows $x\to y$, $y\to x$ is drawn. A problem travels only from an author to someone he trusts, so a problem invented by $x$ can reach $y$ only if there is a directed path from $x$ to $y$.

Triangles return problems on day 3. If $u\to v\to w\to u$, then $v$ receives $u$'s first-day problem on day 1; on day 2 he forwards everything received before day 2, so $w$ receives it on day 2; on day 3 $w$ forwards it and $u$ receives it back. Hence the author $u$ of the hypothesis lies on no directed triangle.

Let $O$ be the set of authors whom $u$ trusts and $I$ the set of authors who trust $u$; every author other than $u$ is in exactly one of them. There is no arrow from $O$ to $I$: an arrow $v\to w$ with $v\in O$, $w\in I$ would give the triangle $u\to v\to w\to u$.

  • If $I\neq\varnothing$: every arrow leaving $u$ ends in $O$, and every arrow leaving a member of $O$ ends in $O$ (it cannot end at $u$, since $u\to v$ for $v\in O$, and it cannot end in $I$). So every directed path from $u$ stays in $O$, and the problem $u$ invents on day 1 never reaches any author of $I$.

  • If $I=\varnothing$: nobody trusts $u$, so nobody ever sends anything to $u$. Since there are at least two authors, the problem of any other author never reaches $u$.

In both cases some problem does not reach all authors.

Solution 2 (A shortest cycle through $u$)

Draw an arrow $x\to y$ when $x$ trusts $y$; for any two authors exactly one of the two arrows is present, and nobody trusts himself. Problems move only along arrows, so a problem of $x$ reaches $y$ only if there is a directed path from $x$ to $y$. Also, if $u\to v\to w\to u$, then $u$'s first-day problem reaches $v$ on day 1, $w$ on day 2 (it was received before day 2) and $u$ on day 3.

We prove the contrapositive: assume every problem reaches every author, and let $u$ be any author; we show that $u$ gets his first-day problem back on day 3. Take another author $w$. There are directed paths from $u$ to $w$ and from $w$ to $u$, so there is a closed directed walk through $u$, and hence a directed cycle through $u$ (a shortest closed walk through $u$ repeats no vertex). Let \[u=v_0\to v_1\to\dots\to v_k=u\] be a shortest directed cycle through $u$. Here $k\ge3$, because $k=1$ is impossible and $k=2$ would need both arrows between $u$ and $v_1$.

Suppose $k\ge4$; then $v_2\neq u$. If $v_2\to u$, then $u\to v_1\to v_2\to u$ is a cycle of length $3<k$. If $u\to v_2$, then $u\to v_2\to v_3\to\dots\to v_k=u$ is a cycle of length $k-1$. Both contradict minimality, so $k=3$: author $u$ lies on a directed triangle and receives his problem back on day 3.

Thus, if some author does not receive his problem back on day 3, some problem does not reach all authors.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

In triangle $ABC$ with $AC>BC$, point $K$ is the reflection of point $A$ across the angle bisector $CD$. Let $L$ be such that $BDCL$ is a parallelogram, and let $H$ be the foot of the perpendicular dropped from $C$ to line $BL$. Suppose that lines $AH$ and $CL$ intersect at point $M$. Prove that lines $CD$ and $KM$ are parallel.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates along the bisector)

Put $p=CA$, $q=CB$ (so $p>q$) and $\theta=\tfrac12\angle ACB$; write $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. Take $C$ as the origin and the bisector $CD$ as the positive $x$-axis. Then \[A=(pc,\;ps),\qquad B=(qc,\;-qs).\] The point $K$. Reflection in the $x$-axis maps $A$ to $K=(pc,-ps)$.

The point $H$. In the parallelogram $BDCL$ we have $BL\parallel DC$ and $CL\parallel BD$. So line $BL$ is the horizontal line $y=-qs$, and the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to it is $H=(0,-qs)$. Line $CL$ is the line through $C$ parallel to $AB$.

The point $M$. Let $M_0$ be the point of line $AH$ with $y=-ps$. Writing $M_0=A+t(H-A)$, the $y$-coordinate gives $ps-t(p+q)s=-ps$, so $t=\frac{2p}{p+q}$, and then \[M_0=\Bigl(pc\bigl(1-\tfrac{2p}{p+q}\bigr),\,-ps\Bigr)=\frac{p}{p+q}\bigl(c(q-p),\,-s(p+q)\bigr)=\frac{p}{p+q}\,(B-A).\] So $\overrightarrow{CM_0}$ is a multiple of $\overrightarrow{AB}$, i.e. $M_0$ lies on line $CL$. Since $A$ is not on line $CL$, lines $AH$ and $CL$ have only one common point, so $M=M_0$.

Finally $M$ and $K$ both have $y$-coordinate $-ps$ and are different points (their $x$-coordinates $pc\frac{q-p}{p+q}<0<pc$ differ). Hence line $KM$ is horizontal, that is, $KM\parallel CD$.

Solution 2 (A hidden parallelogram)

Put $p=CA$, $q=CB$, $\theta=\tfrac12\angle ACB$. Let $O$ be the midpoint of $CD$ and let $M'$ be the reflection of $A$ in $O$. Then $ADM'C$ is a parallelogram, so $CM'\parallel AD$; since $CL\parallel BD$ (parallelogram $BDCL$), the point $M'$ lies on line $CL$.

Step 1: $H$ lies on line $AO$. By the angle bisector theorem $AD:DB=p:q$, so $AB/AD=(p+q)/p$. Comparing areas, $[ACD]+[DCB]=[ACB]$ gives $\tfrac12(p+q)\,CD\sin\theta=\tfrac12pq\sin2\theta$, so \[CD=\frac{2pq\cos\theta}{p+q}.\] The homothety with centre $A$ and ratio $AB/AD$ maps $D$ to $B$ and line $CD$ to the line through $B$ parallel to $CD$, which is line $BL$. It maps $O$ to a point $X$ of line $BL$ with $\overrightarrow{BX}=\frac{AB}{AD}\overrightarrow{DO}$; thus $X$ lies on the ray from $B$ in the direction of $\overrightarrow{DC}$, at distance \[BX=\frac{p+q}{p}\cdot\frac{CD}{2}=q\cos\theta .\] On the other hand, the ray from $B$ in the direction of $\overrightarrow{DC}$ makes the angle $\angle DCB=\theta<90^\circ$ with ray $BC$, so the foot $H$ of the perpendicular from $C$ lies on this ray at distance $BC\cos\theta=q\cos\theta$. Hence $X=H$, and $A,O,H$ are collinear. As $O$ is the midpoint of $AM'$, the point $M'$ lies on line $AH$ as well. So $M'$ is on both $AH$ and $CL$; these lines meet only once ($A\notin CL$), hence $M=M'$.

Step 2: $KM\parallel CD$. Since $K$ is the reflection of $A$ in line $CD$, the midpoint $F$ of $AK$ lies on $CD$. Also $O$, the midpoint of $AM$, lies on $CD$. Therefore \[\overrightarrow{KM}=(2O-A)-(2F-A)=2\,\overrightarrow{FO}.\] Here $F\neq O$: otherwise $K=M$, but $K$ lies on line $CB$, $M$ on line $CL$, and these two different lines through $C$ meet only at $C\neq K$. So $KM$ is parallel to $FO$, which is the line $CD$.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Find all quadratic polynomials $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ with real coefficients such that \[P(1)=5,\quad Q(1)=6,\] and \[P(x)-xQ(x)+x+1\ \text{is divisible by}\ x^2-x+1,\] \[xP(-x)+Q(-x)+x-1\ \text{is divisible by}\ x^2+x+1.\] It is understood that for polynomials with real coefficients, $f(x)$ is divisible by $g(x)\not\equiv 0$ if there exists a polynomial $q(x)$ with real coefficients such that $f(x)=g(x)q(x)$.

Show the answer

$P(x)=6x^2-6x+5,\ Q(x)=5x^2-5x+6$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Evaluate at a complex root)

Let $\omega$ be a root of $x^2-x+1$ (so $\omega=\frac{1\pm i\sqrt3}{2}$, $\omega^2=\omega-1$, $\omega\notin\mathbb R$).

Divisibility test. A real polynomial $f$ is divisible by $x^2-x+1$ if and only if $f(\omega)=0$. Indeed, write $f=(x^2-x+1)g+r$ with $r(x)=ux+v$ real; then $f(\omega)=u\omega+v$, which vanishes only if $u=v=0$ because $\omega$ is not real.

Since $(-x)^2-(-x)+1=x^2+x+1$, the roots of $x^2+x+1$ are $-\omega,-\bar\omega$, and the same argument shows that a real $f$ is divisible by $x^2+x+1$ iff $f(-\omega)=0$. So the conditions become \[P(\omega)-\omega Q(\omega)=-(\omega+1),\qquad -\omega P(\omega)+Q(\omega)=\omega+1 .\] From the first, $P(\omega)=\omega Q(\omega)-\omega-1$; substituting in the second, \[(1-\omega^2)Q(\omega)+\omega^2+\omega=\omega+1\iff(1-\omega^2)\bigl(Q(\omega)-1\bigr)=0 .\] As $1-\omega^2=2-\omega\neq0$, we get $Q(\omega)=1$ and then $P(\omega)=-1$. By the divisibility test, $P+1$ and $Q-1$ are divisible by $x^2-x+1$; being of degree $2$, \[P(x)=-1+\alpha(x^2-x+1),\qquad Q(x)=1+\beta(x^2-x+1)\] with real $\alpha,\beta\ne0$. Now $P(1)=-1+\alpha=5$ and $Q(1)=1+\beta=6$ give $\alpha=6$, $\beta=5$.

Conversely, for these polynomials $P(\omega)=-1$, $Q(\omega)=1$, so both displayed conditions hold and, by the test, both divisibilities hold. The answer is \[\boxed{P(x)=6x^2-6x+5,\quad Q(x)=5x^2-5x+6}.\]

Solution 2 (Remainders without complex numbers)

Work modulo $x^2-x+1$, i.e. replace $x^2$ by $x-1$ repeatedly; two real polynomials are congruent when their difference is divisible by $x^2-x+1$, and every polynomial is congruent to exactly one polynomial of degree at most $1$.

Rewriting the second condition. A polynomial $g(x)$ is divisible by $x^2+x+1$ iff $g(-x)$ is divisible by $(-x)^2+(-x)+1=x^2-x+1$. Applying this to $g(x)=xP(-x)+Q(-x)+x-1$, the second condition says that $-xP(x)+Q(x)-x-1$ is divisible by $x^2-x+1$.

Let $P\equiv p_1x+p_0$ and $Q\equiv q_1x+q_0$. Then \[xQ\equiv q_1x^2+q_0x\equiv(q_1+q_0)x-q_1,\qquad xP\equiv(p_1+p_0)x-p_1 .\] First condition: $P-xQ+x+1\equiv(p_1-q_1-q_0+1)x+(p_0+q_1+1)\equiv0$, so \[p_1=q_0+q_1-1,\qquad p_0=-q_1-1 .\] Second condition: $-xP+Q-x-1\equiv(-p_1-p_0+q_1-1)x+(p_1+q_0-1)\equiv0$, so \[q_1=p_1+p_0+1,\qquad q_0=1-p_1 .\] Then $q_1=p_1+p_0+1=p_1-q_1$, so $p_1=2q_1$ and $q_0=1-2q_1$; the relation $p_1=q_0+q_1-1$ becomes $2q_1=-q_1$. Hence $q_1=p_1=0$, $q_0=1$, $p_0=-1$: the two conditions say exactly that \[x^2-x+1\mid P+1\quad\text{and}\quad x^2-x+1\mid Q-1 .\] As $P,Q$ are quadratic, $P=-1+\alpha(x^2-x+1)$ and $Q=1+\beta(x^2-x+1)$, and $P(1)=5$, $Q(1)=6$ give $\alpha=6$, $\beta=5$. The computation is reversible, so the unique answer is \[\boxed{P(x)=6x^2-6x+5,\quad Q(x)=5x^2-5x+6}.\]

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Two non-intersecting circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ have centers $O_1$ and $O_2$, respectively. Let $AB$ be a common external tangent segment to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$. From a point $M$ on segment $AB$, tangents $MP$ and $MQ$ are drawn to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, respectively (here $P\in\omega_1$ and $Q\in\omega_2$, while points $B,Q$ lie on $\omega_2$). Prove that if $\angle AMP=\angle BMQ$, then lines $AQ$, $BP$, and $O_1O_2$ intersect at a single point.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 ($BP$ and $AQ$ are the diagonals of a symmetric trapezoid)

Throughout, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $r_1,r_2$ be the radii; both circles lie on the same side of $AB$.

Step 1: $MA:MB=r_1:r_2$. $MA$ and $MP$ are the two tangents from $M$ to $\omega_1$, so $MO_1$ bisects $\angle AMP$; likewise $MO_2$ bisects $\angle BMQ$. Hence $\angle AMO_1=\angle BMO_2=:\varphi$, and the right triangles $MAO_1$, $MBO_2$ give $MA=r_1\cot\varphi$, $MB=r_2\cot\varphi$.

Step 2: the symmetric picture. Let $C,D$ be the reflections of $A,B$ in line $O_1O_2$. The reflection preserves both circles, so $C\in\omega_1$, $D\in\omega_2$ and $CD$ is the other common external tangent. Let $Z$ be the midpoint of $O_1O_2$. As $O_1A\parallel O_2B$ (both $\perp AB$), $Z$ projects to the midpoint of $AB$, so $ZA=ZB$; and $ZC=ZA$, $ZD=ZB$ by the reflection. So $A,B,C,D$ lie on a circle $\Gamma$ with centre $Z$. Let $E$ be the point of $\Gamma$ diametrically opposite $B$. Then $\angle BAE=90^\circ$, so $E$ lies on line $AO_1$, on the side of the circles, and $AE=2\operatorname{dist}(Z,AB)=r_1+r_2$. By Step 1, $AM:AB=r_1:(r_1+r_2)=AO_1:AE$, so by Thales \[O_1M\parallel EB .\]

Step 3: $P$ lies on line $BC$. Line $BC$ is not tangent to $\omega_1$ (the tangent at $C$ is $CD$, which does not pass through $B$), so it meets $\omega_1$ again at a point $P'\neq C$; also $P'\ne A$ because $A\notin BC$. Using the tangent-chord angle ($AB$ touches $\omega_1$ at $A$), the circle $\Gamma$ and Step 2, \[\measuredangle(AB,AP')=\measuredangle(CA,CP')=\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\measuredangle(EA,EB)=\measuredangle(O_1A,O_1M).\] Since $AB\perp O_1A$, this gives $AP'\perp O_1M$. Now $MA=MP$ and $O_1A=O_1P$, so $MO_1$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AP$: the line through $A$ perpendicular to $MO_1$ meets $\omega_1$ again exactly at $P$. Hence $P'=P$, i.e. $P\in BC$. In the same way (exchange the roles of $\omega_1,A,C$ and $\omega_2,B,D$) $Q\in AD$.

Step 4. So the lines $BP$ and $AQ$ are the lines $BC$ and $AD$. The chords $AC$ and $BD$ of $\Gamma$ are parallel (both $\perp O_1O_2$) and distinct, so $ACDB$ is a trapezoid and its diagonals $AD$, $CB$ meet at a point $W$. The reflection in $O_1O_2$ swaps $A\leftrightarrow C$, $B\leftrightarrow D$, hence swaps lines $AD$ and $CB$ and maps $W$ to a common point of both lines, i.e. to $W$ itself. So $W$ lies on $O_1O_2$, and $AQ$, $BP$, $O_1O_2$ pass through $W$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates centred at $M$)

Since $MA,MP$ are tangents from $M$ to $\omega_1$, line $MO_1$ bisects $\angle AMP$; similarly $MO_2$ bisects $\angle BMQ$. So $\angle AMO_1=\angle BMO_2=:\varphi$, with $0<\varphi<90^\circ$.

Put $M$ at the origin, $AB$ on the $x$-axis and the circles above it; let $a=MA$, $b=MB$, $t=\tan\varphi$, $c=\cos2\varphi$, $s=\sin2\varphi$. Then $A=(-a,0)$, $B=(b,0)$, and from the right triangles $MAO_1$, $MBO_2$, \[O_1=(-a,\,at),\qquad O_2=(b,\,bt).\] The ray $MP$ is the mirror image of ray $MA$ in $MO_1$, so it makes the angle $2\varphi$ with ray $MA$, and $MP=MA=a$; similarly for $Q$: \[P=(-ac,\;as),\qquad Q=(bc,\;bs).\] We will use $s=t(1+c)$, which is the identity $\tan\varphi=\frac{\sin2\varphi}{1+\cos2\varphi}$.

Intersection of $AQ$ and $BP$. Look for $X=A+\lambda(Q-A)=B+\mu(P-B)$. The $y$-coordinates give $\lambda bs=\mu as$, so $\mu=\lambda b/a$. The $x$-coordinates give $-a+\lambda(bc+a)=b-\frac{\lambda b}{a}(b+ac)$, that is \[\lambda\,\frac{a^2+2abc+b^2}{a}=a+b,\qquad \lambda=\frac{a(a+b)}{a^2+2abc+b^2}.\] (The denominator equals $(a+bc)^2+b^2s^2>0$, so the lines do meet.) Thus $X=\bigl(-a+\lambda(a+bc),\ \lambda bs\bigr)$.

$X$ lies on $O_1O_2$. The line $O_1O_2$ is $y=at+\frac{t(b-a)}{a+b}(x+a)$. At $X$ we have $x+a=\lambda(a+bc)$, so we must check \[\lambda bs=at+\frac{t(b-a)}{a+b}\lambda(a+bc).\] Divide by $t$, use $s=t(1+c)$ and multiply by $a+b$: we need $\lambda\bigl[b(1+c)(a+b)-(b-a)(a+bc)\bigr]=a(a+b)$. The bracket is \[ab+b^2+abc+b^2c-ab-b^2c+a^2+abc=a^2+2abc+b^2,\] and $\lambda(a^2+2abc+b^2)=a(a+b)$ by the formula for $\lambda$. Hence $X\in O_1O_2$, and the three lines are concurrent.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

The numbers $1,2,3,\dots,2026$ are written on a board. Eldana and Elaman play the following game. Eldana starts, and then they move alternately. In one move, a player chooses any two numbers $m$ and $n$ on the board, erases them, and writes the number $m^n$ instead. The game continues until only one number remains. If this number ends in the digit $2,3,7$, or $8$, Elaman wins; otherwise, Eldana wins. Who wins under optimal play?

Show the answer

Eldana wins

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Keep an even number for the last move)

All numbers on the board are always positive integers, and $m^n$ has the same parity as $m$ (here $n\ge1$).

Who moves when. There are $2026$ numbers and each move removes one, so the game has $2025$ moves. Eldana makes moves $1,3,\dots,2025$, in particular the last one; before it, each player has made $1012$ moves.

The goal. A square of an integer ends in $0,1,4,5,6$ or $9$, never in $2,3,7,8$. If, at her last move, one of the two remaining numbers is even, Eldana writes $m^n$ with the even number as the exponent $n$; then $m^n=\bigl(m^{n/2}\bigr)^2$ is a perfect square and Eldana wins.

The strategy. On each of her moves before the last one, if some odd number is on the board, Eldana uses an odd number as the exponent (and any other number as the base); otherwise she moves arbitrarily.

Why an even number survives. Let $E$ be the number of even numbers on the board; initially $E=1013$.

  • A move with an odd exponent $n$ never decreases $E$: if $m$ is even, an even number is replaced by the even number $m^n$; if $m$ is odd, no even number is involved.

  • Any move decreases $E$ by at most $1$: it removes at most two even numbers, and if it removes two, the new number $m^n$ is even.

  • Once all numbers are even, they stay even forever, since every new number has an even base.

If at some moment before Eldana's last move all numbers are even, then at the last move both numbers are even. Otherwise, at each of her first $1012$ moves an odd number is available, so her moves do not decrease $E$, while Elaman's $1012$ moves decrease it by at most $1012$; hence $E\ge1013-1012=1$ at the last move. In either case an even number is available, so $\boxed{\text{Eldana wins}}$.

Solution 2 (Remove the bad endings one at a time)

Call a positive integer bad if its last digit is $2,3,7$ or $8$, and good otherwise (last digit $0,1,4,5,6$ or $9$). All numbers on the board are always positive integers, so every exponent is at least $1$.

A power with a good base is good. The last digit of $m^n$ depends only on the last digit of $m$ and on $n$. If $m$ ends in $0,1,5$ or $6$, so does every positive power of $m$, because $0\cdot0$, $1\cdot1$, $5\cdot5=25$ and $6\cdot6=36$ end in $0,1,5,6$ respectively. If $m$ ends in $4$, its powers end alternately in $4$ and $6$ ($4\cdot4=16$, $6\cdot4=24$); if $m$ ends in $9$, they end alternately in $9$ and $1$ ($9\cdot9=81$, $1\cdot9=9$). So if $m$ is good, then $m^n$ is good; equivalently, a newly written number can be bad only if its base was bad.

Counting. Among $1,\dots,2020$ there are $4\cdot202=808$ bad numbers, and among $2021,\dots,2026$ the bad ones are $2022$ and $2023$. So initially there are $810$ bad and $1216$ good numbers. Each move removes one number, so the game has $2025$ moves, and Eldana makes the moves $1,3,\dots,2025$, that is, $1013$ moves.

The number $b$ of bad numbers never increases. A move erases $m,n$ and writes $m^n$. If $m^n$ is bad, then $m$ is bad, so a bad number was erased. Hence no move increases $b$.

The strategy. On each of her turns, if there is a bad number on the board, Eldana erases a good number $m$ and a bad number $n$ and writes $m^n$ (good base, bad exponent); otherwise she moves arbitrarily. Such a move decreases $b$ by $1$ and does not change the number $g$ of good numbers (one good number is erased and one good number is written).

A good base is always available. A move of Elaman decreases $g$ by at most $1$: it erases at most two good numbers, and if it erases two, the new number has a good base and is good. Suppose a bad number is on the board at Eldana's $k$-th turn. Since $b$ never increases, a bad number was present at each of her earlier turns, so each of them decreased $b$ by $1$, and $1\le b\le 810-(k-1)$; hence $k\le810$. Her earlier moves did not change $g$, and Elaman has made $k-1\le809$ moves, so $g\ge1216-809>0$. Thus the move of the strategy is always possible.

Conclusion. By the same count, there is no bad number on the board at Eldana's $811$-th turn, and she has $1013$ turns, so at some moment the board has no bad number. From then on no bad number can appear, because a bad number needs a bad base. Hence the last number is good: it does not end in $2,3,7$ or $8$, and $\boxed{\text{Eldana wins}}$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Solve in natural numbers the equation \[x^4+y^4=n!+2026.\]

Show the answer

no solutions

Show the solution

Solution (Fourth powers modulo $16$)

Fourth powers modulo $16$. If $x$ is even, $16\mid x^4$. If $x$ is odd, $x^2=8k+1$ for an integer $k$ (since $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ is a product of two consecutive even numbers), so $x^4=64k^2+16k+1\equiv1\pmod{16}$. Hence $x^4+y^4\equiv0,1$ or $2\pmod{16}$.

Large $n$. $2026=16\cdot126+10$, so $2026\equiv10\pmod{16}$. For $n\ge6$ we have $16\mid 720=6!\mid n!$, so $n!+2026\equiv10\pmod{16}$, which is not a value of $x^4+y^4$. Hence $n\le5$.

Small $n$. The right-hand side $n!+2026$ equals $2027,2028,2032,2050,2146$ for $n=1,2,3,4,5$, with residues $11,12,0,2,2$ modulo $16$.

  • $n=1,2$: residues $11,12$ are impossible.

  • $n=3$: residue $0$ forces $x,y$ even; with $x=2u$, $y=2v$ we get $u^4+v^4=2032/16=127$. The fourth powers below $127$ are $0,1,16,81$, and $127-81=46$, $127-16=111$, $127-1=126$, $127-0=127$ are not fourth powers.

  • $n=4,5$: residue $2$ forces $x,y$ odd. Odd fourth powers below $2146$ are $1,81,625$ ($7^4=2401$ is too large), and their pairwise sums are at most $1250<2050$.

So the equation has $\boxed{\text{no solutions}}$ in natural numbers. (If $0$ counts as a natural number: $n=0$ gives $0!+2026=2027\equiv11\pmod{16}$, impossible; and $x=0$ or $y=0$ would require $n!+2026$ to be a fourth power for some $n\le5$, which none of the five values is.)

Remark. Modulus $16$ is the efficient choice. Modulo $13$ the fourth powers are $0,1,3,9$, whose pairwise sums miss $2026\equiv11$, so $n\ge13$ is excluded too, but among $n\le12$ only $n=7$ is removed, leaving many cases to bound by hand.

Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

From city $A$ to city $B$, $2025$ trucks depart at equal time intervals, each carrying $2026$ kg of rice. Similarly, from city $B$ to city $A$, $2026$ trucks depart at the same intervals, each carrying $2025$ kg of wheat.

Whenever two trucks meet, they completely exchange their cargos; during the exchange, exactly $1$ kg of each type of grain is spilled onto the ground. After the exchange, both trucks turn around and continue moving. Every truck that leaves a city ($A$ or $B$) eventually arrives at one of the cities $A$, $B$ and finishes its route there.

Assume all trucks have the same speed, exchanges are instantaneous, and at some moment all trucks are simultaneously between cities $A$ and $B$.

After the last truck finishes its journey, how many kilograms of rice and wheat are in each city?

Show the answer

$0$ kg of rice and $0$ kg of wheat in each city

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Follow the cargo, not the trucks)

All trucks move at the same speed. When two trucks meet, they swap cargos and both turn back. So, just before the meeting, a cargo was moving in some direction at that speed, and just after it, the same cargo (on the other truck) moves in the same direction at the same speed. Hence every cargo moves uniformly in one direction: each rice cargo goes straight from $A$ to $B$ and each wheat cargo straight from $B$ to $A$. At every moment the cargos are exactly where the trucks are.

Every rice cargo meets every wheat cargo exactly once. Let the road have length $\ell$ and let both move at speed $v$; put $T=\ell/v$. Suppose a rice cargo leaves $A$ at time $s$ and a wheat cargo leaves $B$ at time $s'$. A rice cargo is on the road during $(s,s+T)$, a wheat cargo during $(s',s'+T)$. At the moment $t_0$ when all trucks (hence all cargos) are on the road, $s<t_0<s'+T$ and $s'<t_0<s+T$, so $|s-s'|<T$. The two cargos are at the same point only at time $\tau=\frac{s+s'+T}{2}$, and $\tau-s=\frac{T+(s'-s)}2\in(0,T)$, $\tau-s'\in(0,T)$, so both are on the road at time $\tau$: they meet exactly once. Two cargos of the same kind move in the same direction and never meet. A meeting of two trucks is exactly a meeting of a rice cargo with a wheat cargo.

Counting. At each meeting the rice cargo involved loses $1$ kg and so does the wheat cargo. A rice cargo meets each of the $2026$ wheat cargos once, so it reaches $B$ with $2026-2026=0$ kg. A wheat cargo meets each of the $2025$ rice cargos once and reaches $A$ with $2025-2025=0$ kg. Grain enters a city only when a truck finishes its route there, so after the last truck finishes all the grain is on the road and \[\boxed{0\text{ kg of rice and }0\text{ kg of wheat in each city}}.\]

Solution 2 (Counting meetings with approaching pairs)

Call a truck right-moving if it moves towards $B$ and left-moving otherwise. At a meeting a right-moving and a left-moving truck meet and both reverse, so a meeting does not change the number of right-moving trucks.

Fix a moment $t_0$ at which all trucks are strictly between the cities and no meeting takes place (there are finitely many meetings, and all trucks stay on the road for a short time around the given moment). No truck has finished before $t_0$ and every truck has started, so at $t_0$ exactly $2025$ trucks are right-moving (one for each departure from $A$) and $2026$ are left-moving. No two trucks are at the same point at $t_0$ (trucks moving the same way keep their distance, and opposite trucks at one point would be a meeting).

Meetings after $t_0$. Let $\Phi$ be the number of pairs (right-moving truck, left-moving truck) on the road with the right-moving one closer to $A$; such a pair is heading for a meeting. Look at a meeting at point $x$: just before it there is a right-moving truck slightly left of $x$ and a left-moving one slightly right of $x$; just after it, a left-moving one slightly left and a right-moving one slightly right. Every other truck sees the same numbers of right- and left-moving trucks on each side, so only the pair itself changes: it was counted before and is not counted after. Thus each meeting lowers $\Phi$ by exactly $1$. After $t_0$ there are no departures, and a truck arriving at $A$ is a left-moving truck with no truck to its left, so it belongs to no counted pair (similarly at $B$). At the end $\Phi=0$, so the number of meetings after $t_0$ equals $\Phi(t_0)$.

Meetings before $t_0$. Run time backwards from $t_0$: the motion is of the same kind with all directions reversed, and departures become arrivals. The same argument shows that the number of meetings before $t_0$ equals the number $\Psi(t_0)$ of pairs (right-moving, left-moving) at $t_0$ with the left-moving one closer to $A$.

Every pair of a right-moving and a left-moving truck is counted in exactly one of $\Phi(t_0)$, $\Psi(t_0)$, so the total number of meetings is $2025\cdot2026$.

Conclusion. Each meeting spills $1$ kg of rice, so $2025\cdot2026$ kg of rice are spilled, which is all the rice there was; likewise all $2026\cdot2025$ kg of wheat are spilled. Since amounts are never negative, the answer is \[\boxed{0\text{ kg of rice and }0\text{ kg of wheat in each city}}.\]

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n\ge 3$ be an integer, and let $\{a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n\}$ be a set of $n$ pairwise distinct positive real numbers. It is known that for every $1\le i\le n$, there exist indices $j\ne k\ne i$ such that \[a_i=1+\frac{a_j}{a_k}.\] (Prove that all $a_i,a_j,a_k$ are distinct.) Show that the set contains two numbers whose sum is greater than $4$.

Comment: As it turned out later, there are no sequences satisfying the condition of the problem. By proving this, you can also get $7$ points.

Show the answer

proof (in fact no such set exists)

Show the solution

Solution (Look at the smallest and the largest number)

Let $m$ and $M$ be the smallest and the largest number of the set; $m<M$ because $n\ge3$ and the numbers are distinct. All numbers exceed $1$, since each equals $1$ plus a positive number.

The parenthetical remark. The indices $i,j,k$ are pairwise distinct, and the numbers of the set are pairwise distinct, so $a_i,a_j,a_k$ are three different numbers.

Part 1: $m+M>4$. We only use $m\le a_j$ and $a_k\le M$ for all indices. Writing the condition for the index of $m$ and for the index of $M$: \[m=1+\frac{a_j}{a_k}\ge1+\frac{m}{M},\qquad M=1+\frac{a_{j'}}{a_{k'}}\le1+\frac{M}{m}.\] Dividing the first by $m$ and the second by $M$: $1\ge\frac1m+\frac1M$ and $1\le\frac1M+\frac1m$. Hence \[\frac1m+\frac1M=1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad m+M=mM .\] By AM-HM (or $(m+M)\bigl(\frac1m+\frac1M\bigr)\ge4$, which is $(m-M)^2\ge0$), $m+M\ge4$ with equality only for $m=M$. As $m<M$, we get $m+M>4$: the set contains two numbers with sum greater than $4$.

Part 2: no such set exists. In Part 1 the first inequality must be an equality, which forces $a_j=m$ (and $a_k=M$). But $j\neq i$, where $a_i=m$, and the numbers are distinct, so $a_j>m$ and the first inequality is strict: $1>\frac1m+\frac1M$. Together with $\frac1m+\frac1M\ge1$ this is a contradiction. So no set satisfies the conditions, which is what the Comment announces; the statement to be proved is then true (vacuously).

Remark. Part 1 uses no condition on the indices at all; Part 2 uses only $j\ne i$. If $j=i$ were allowed, sets do exist, for example $\{\tfrac32,\,3,\,x,\,1+\tfrac{2x}{3}\}$ with $x=\frac{\sqrt{97}-1}{4}$ (here $\frac32=1+\frac{3/2}{3}$, $3=1+\frac{3}{3/2}$, $x=1+\frac{3}{1+2x/3}$, $1+\frac{2x}3=1+\frac{x}{3/2}$), and Part 1 is then the real content of the problem.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

In an acute scalene triangle $ABC$, altitudes $BD$ and $CE$ are drawn. On line $DE$, points $P$ and $Q$ are chosen such that $PC\perp BC$ and $QB\perp BC$. Let $A_1$ be the point diametrically opposite to $A$ on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Prove that point $A_1$ lies on the line containing the common chord of the circumcircles of triangles $BDQ$ and $CEP$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two points of the radical axis: $H$ and the midpoint of $BC$)

Let $H$ be the orthocentre, $M$ the midpoint of $BC$, $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ the circumcircles of $BDQ$ and $CEP$, and $\omega_0$ the circle with diameter $BC$; it passes through $D$ and $E$ because $\angle BDC=\angle BEC=90^\circ$. Powers of points are signed: for a point $Z$ on a line meeting a circle at $U,V$, the power is $\overline{ZU}\cdot\overline{ZV}$. The radical axis of $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ (the line of their common chord) consists of the points with equal powers.

$H$ is on the radical axis. $H$ lies on line $BD$, which meets $\Gamma_1$ at $B,D$, and on line $CE$, which meets $\Gamma_2$ at $C,E$. The power of $H$ with respect to $\omega_0$ gives \[\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(H)=\overline{HB}\cdot\overline{HD}=\overline{HC}\cdot\overline{HE}=\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}(H).\]

$M$ is on the radical axis. Let $X$ and $Y$ be the points where the perpendiculars to $DE$ at $D$ and at $E$ meet $BC$. (They do meet it: $DE$ is not perpendicular to $BC$, since it makes the angle $|\angle B-\angle C|<90^\circ$ with $BC$.) Then $\angle QDX=90^\circ=\angle QBX$, so $B$ and $D$ lie on the circle with diameter $QX$; this circle passes through $B,D,Q$, so it is $\Gamma_1$, and $\Gamma_1$ meets line $BC$ at $B$ and $X$. Likewise $\angle PEY=\angle PCY=90^\circ$, so $\Gamma_2$ is the circle with diameter $PY$ and meets $BC$ at $C$ and $Y$. Thus \[\operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_1}(M)=\overline{MB}\cdot\overline{MX},\qquad \operatorname{pow}_{\Gamma_2}(M)=\overline{MC}\cdot\overline{MY}.\] Now $DX$ and $EY$ are parallel (both perpendicular to $DE$), and the line through $M$ perpendicular to $DE$ is parallel to them and passes through the midpoint of the chord $DE$ of $\omega_0$ (whose centre is $M$). By the intercept theorem this line meets $XY$ at the midpoint of $XY$; but it meets $BC$ at $M$. So $M$ is the midpoint of $XY$: $\overline{MY}=-\overline{MX}$, and also $\overline{MC}=-\overline{MB}$. Hence the two powers of $M$ are equal.

Conclusion. $H\neq M$ ($H$ is inside the acute triangle, $M$ on its side), so the radical axis is the line $HM$. Since $A_1B\perp AB$ and $CH\perp AB$, we have $A_1B\parallel CH$; similarly $A_1C\parallel BH$. So $BHCA_1$ is a parallelogram and its diagonals bisect each other: $M$ is the midpoint of $HA_1$. Therefore $A_1$ lies on line $HM$, the line of the common chord.

Solution 2 (Half-angle coordinates on the circle with diameter $BC$)

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. Since $\angle BDC=\angle BEC=90^\circ$, the points $D,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $BC$. Take $M=(0,0)$, $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$ and $A$ above $BC$; that circle is the unit circle, and as the triangle is acute, $D$ and $E$ lie on its upper half.

A chord formula. For a real $t$ put $U(t)=\bigl(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\bigr)$, the point of the unit circle with polar angle $2\arctan t$. A direct substitution shows that $U(t_1)$ satisfies \[(1-t_1t_2)x+(t_1+t_2)y=1+t_1t_2\qquad(\ast)\] (the left side becomes $\frac{(1-t_1t_2)(1-t_1^2)+2t_1(t_1+t_2)}{1+t_1^2}=1+t_1t_2$), and by symmetry so does $U(t_2)$; so $(\ast)$ is the line $U(t_1)U(t_2)$.

Write $D=U(s)$ and $E=U(1/w)$ with $s,w>0$. The antipodes are $-D=U(-1/s)$ and $-E=U(-w)$.

$H$ and $A_1$. Line $BD$ has slope $\frac{2s/(1+s^2)}{(1-s^2)/(1+s^2)+1}=s$, so it is $y=s(x+1)$; similarly line $CE$ is $y=w(1-x)$. They meet at the orthocentre \[H=\Bigl(\frac{w-s}{w+s},\ \frac{2sw}{w+s}\Bigr).\] As $A_1B\perp AB\perp CH$ and $A_1C\perp AC\perp BH$, the quadrilateral $BHCA_1$ is a parallelogram, so $A_1=B+C-H=-H$.

$P$ and $Q$. By $(\ast)$ with $t_1=s$, $t_2=1/w$ (multiplied by $w$), line $DE$ is $(w-s)x+(sw+1)y=w+s$. At $x=1$ and $x=-1$: \[P=\Bigl(1,\ \frac{2s}{1+sw}\Bigr),\qquad Q=\Bigl(-1,\ \frac{2w}{1+sw}\Bigr).\]

The two circles. Since $EE'$ with $E'=-E$ is a diameter, $\angle EDE'=90^\circ$, i.e. $DE'\perp DE$. By $(\ast)$ with $t_1=s$, $t_2=-w$, line $DE'$ is $(1+sw)x+(s-w)y=1-sw$; it meets the $x$-axis at $X=(\xi,0)$ with $\xi=\frac{1-sw}{1+sw}$. Now $\angle QBX=90^\circ$ (as $QB\perp BC$) and $\angle QDX=90^\circ$, so the circle through $B,D,Q$ is the circle with diameter $QX$: \[\Gamma_1:\ (x+1)(x-\xi)+y\Bigl(y-\frac{2w}{1+sw}\Bigr)=0 .\] In the same way line $ED''$ with $D''=-D$ is, by $(\ast)$ with $t_1=1/w$, $t_2=-1/s$ (multiplied by $sw$), $(sw+1)x+(s-w)y=sw-1$; it meets the $x$-axis at $Y=(-\xi,0)$, and since $\angle PCY=\angle PEY=90^\circ$, \[\Gamma_2:\ (x-1)(x+\xi)+y\Bigl(y-\frac{2s}{1+sw}\Bigr)=0 .\]

The common chord. Subtracting the equations, the line of the common chord is \[2(1-\xi)x+\frac{2(s-w)}{1+sw}\,y=0\iff 2sw\,x+(s-w)\,y=0,\] using $1-\xi=\frac{2sw}{1+sw}$. This line passes through the origin and through $H$, because $2sw(w-s)+(s-w)\cdot2sw=0$. Hence it also passes through $-H=A_1$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Let $a,b,n$ be integers. Suppose that the numbers $b^2-ab$ and $a^2$ are divisible by $n$. Prove that the number \[a^5+ab^4+b^5\] is also divisible by $n$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Congruences)

We may assume $n\neq0$ (if $n=0$, then $a^2=0$ and $b^2=ab=0$, so $a=b=0$ and the claim is trivial). Work modulo $n$. We are given \[a^2\equiv0,\qquad b^2\equiv ab .\] Then \[b^3=b\cdot b^2\equiv b\cdot ab=a\cdot b^2\equiv a\cdot ab=a^2b\equiv0 .\] So $b^3\equiv0$ and $a^2\equiv0$, and every term of $a^5+ab^4+b^5$ is a multiple of $a^2$ or of $b^3$: \[a^5=a^2\cdot a^3,\qquad ab^4=ab\cdot b^3,\qquad b^5=b^2\cdot b^3 .\] Hence $a^5+ab^4+b^5\equiv0\pmod n$.

Solution 2 (Prime by prime)

We may assume $n\ne0$ (for $n=0$ the hypotheses force $a=b=0$). It suffices to show that for every prime $p$ with $p^e\,\|\,n$, $e\ge1$, each of $a^5$, $ab^4$, $b^5$ is divisible by $p^e$. Let $v(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in $x$ (with $v(0)=\infty$), $\alpha=v(a)$, $\beta=v(b)$.

From $p^e\mid a^2$ we get $2\alpha\ge e$. We claim $2\beta\ge e$ as well.

  • If $\beta<\alpha$, then $v(b-a)=\beta$, so $v\bigl(b(b-a)\bigr)=2\beta$; since $p^e\mid b^2-ab=b(b-a)$, we get $2\beta\ge e$.

  • If $\beta\ge\alpha$, then $2\beta\ge2\alpha\ge e$.

Hence $v(a^5)=5\alpha\ge e$, $v(ab^4)=\alpha+4\beta\ge e$ and $v(b^5)=5\beta\ge e$. So $p^e$ divides all three terms and their sum; as this holds for every prime power exactly dividing $n$, $n\mid a^5+ab^4+b^5$.

Remark. The proof shows more: $n\mid b^2$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Two non-intersecting circles $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are given. Segments $AB$ and $CD$ are common external tangents to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ (with $A,C\in\omega_1$ and $B,D\in\omega_2$). Line $CB$ intersects $\omega_1$ again at point $P$, and line $AD$ intersects $\omega_2$ again at point $Q$. Prove that the tangent to $\omega_1$ at $P$ and the tangent to $\omega_2$ at $Q$ intersect on line $AB$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Both tangents pass through the point dividing $AB$ in the ratio $r_1:r_2$)

Throughout, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $r_1,r_2$ be the radii and $O_1,O_2$ the centres; both circles lie on the same side of $AB$. The reflection in line $O_1O_2$ preserves both circles and swaps the two external tangents, so $C$ and $D$ are the reflections of $A$ and $B$. Let $Z$ be the midpoint of $O_1O_2$. Since $O_1A\parallel O_2B$ (both are perpendicular to $AB$), $Z$ projects onto the midpoint of $AB$, so $ZA=ZB$; by the reflection $ZC=ZA$ and $ZD=ZB$. Hence $A,B,C,D$ lie on a circle $\Gamma$ with centre $Z$. Let $E$ be the point of $\Gamma$ diametrically opposite $B$. Then $\angle BAE=90^\circ$, so $E$ lies on line $AO_1$, on the side of the circles, with $AE=2\operatorname{dist}(Z,AB)=r_1+r_2$.

Let $T$ be the point of segment $AB$ with $AT:TB=r_1:r_2$. Then $AT:AB=r_1:(r_1+r_2)=AO_1:AE$, so by Thales $O_1T\parallel EB$.

Claim: $AP\perp O_1T$. Line $CB$ is not tangent to $\omega_1$ (the tangent at $C$ is $CD$, which does not contain $B$), so $P\neq C$; and $P\ne A$ since $A\notin CB$. By the tangent-chord angle ($AB$ touches $\omega_1$ at $A$), the circle $\Gamma$ through $A,B,C,E$, and $EA=O_1A$, $EB\parallel O_1T$: \[\measuredangle(AB,AP)=\measuredangle(CA,CP)=\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\measuredangle(EA,EB)=\measuredangle(O_1A,O_1T).\] Since $AB\perp O_1A$, it follows that $AP\perp O_1T$.

Conclusion for $\omega_1$. The line $O_1T$ passes through the centre of $\omega_1$, so the reflection in it maps $\omega_1$ to itself; it maps $A$ to the second intersection of $\omega_1$ with the line through $A$ perpendicular to $O_1T$, which is $P$ by the claim. It fixes $T$, so it maps the tangent $TA$ to the line $TP$, which is therefore tangent to $\omega_1$ at $P$. So the tangent at $P$ passes through $T$.

The same for $\omega_2$. Exchanging the roles of $(\omega_1,A,C)$ and $(\omega_2,B,D)$ (the configuration is described symmetrically) gives the point $T'$ of $AB$ with $BT':T'A=r_2:r_1$, i.e. $T'=T$, and the tangent to $\omega_2$ at $Q$ passes through $T$. Hence both tangents pass through the point $T$ of line $AB$, so they meet on $AB$. (The two tangents can coincide: this happens when $AB=r_1+r_2$, where both are the perpendicular to $AB$ at $T$; the common tangent still passes through $T$.)

Solution 2 (A dot-product computation)

Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(d,0)$ and the circles above the $x$-axis: $O_1=(0,r_1)$, $O_2=(d,r_2)$. Let $Z=\bigl(\frac d2,\frac{r_1+r_2}2\bigr)$, the midpoint of $O_1O_2$; then $ZA=ZB$. The reflection in line $O_1O_2$ preserves both circles and swaps the two external tangents, so $C$ is the reflection of $A$; as $Z$ lies on the mirror, $ZC=ZA=ZB$. So $C$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with centre $Z$ through $B$, whose point diametrically opposite to $B$ is \[E=2Z-B=(0,\;r_1+r_2).\] By Thales' theorem $\overrightarrow{CB}\cdot\overrightarrow{CE}=0$ (also if $C=E$).

The point $P$. The power of $B$ with respect to $\omega_1$ is $BA^2=d^2$, and $B$ is outside $\omega_1$, so $P$ lies on ray $BC$ with $BP\cdot BC=d^2$: \[P=B+\lambda(C-B),\qquad \lambda=\frac{d^2}{|C-B|^2}.\]

The point $T$. Let $T=\bigl(\frac{dr_1}{r_1+r_2},0\bigr)$. Then \[T-O_1=\Bigl(\frac{dr_1}{r_1+r_2},\,-r_1\Bigr)=\frac{r_1}{r_1+r_2}\,(B-E).\]

$AP\perp O_1T$. Using $B\cdot E=0$ and $(C-B)\cdot(C-E)=0$: \[(P-A)\cdot(B-E)=B\cdot(B-E)+\lambda\,(C-B)\cdot\bigl((B-C)+(C-E)\bigr)=d^2-\lambda|C-B|^2=0 .\] Tangency. The line $O_1T$ contains the centre of $\omega_1$, so the reflection in it preserves $\omega_1$ and fixes $T$. It sends $A$ to the second point of $\omega_1$ on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $O_1T$, which is $P$ ($P\ne A$ because $A$ is not on line $BC$). The tangent $TA$ goes to the line $TP$, which is therefore the tangent to $\omega_1$ at $P$.

The same computation with $(\omega_2,B,D)$ in place of $(\omega_1,A,C)$ (reflect the picture in the perpendicular bisector of $AB$) shows that the tangent to $\omega_2$ at $Q$ passes through the point $T'$ of $AB$ with $BT'=\frac{dr_2}{r_1+r_2}$, and $T'=T$. So both tangents pass through $T\in AB$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

An infinite chessboard is colored in black and white in the standard checkerboard pattern. From it, a connected polyomino consisting of $25$ white and $25$ black cells is cut out, such that any cell of the figure can be reached from any other by moving only through cells of the figure and only via common sides (not diagonally).

What is the maximum number of dominoes (rectangles of size $1\times 2$ or $2\times 1$) that can be guaranteed to be cut from this figure?

Show the answer

$13$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vertex covers)

Form the graph $G$ whose vertices are the $50$ cells of the figure, two cells being joined when they share a side. The figure is connected, so $G$ is connected; every vertex has degree at most $4$; and $G$ is bipartite (adjacent cells have different colours). Cutting out disjoint dominoes means choosing a matching in $G$.

At least $13$ dominoes. By Kőnig's theorem, in a bipartite graph the maximum size of a matching equals the minimum size of a vertex cover (a set of vertices touching every edge). A connected graph on $50$ vertices has at least $49$ edges, and a vertex covers at most $4$ edges, so every vertex cover has at least $49/4$, hence at least $13$, vertices. Therefore $13$ dominoes can always be cut.

A figure with no $14$ disjoint dominoes. Take the row of cells $(x,0)$, $0\le x\le25$, colour $(x,y)$ white when $x+y$ is even, and attach the cells $(x,\pm1)$ for $x\in\{1,3,\dots,11\}$ and for $x\in\{14,16,\dots,24\}$ (see the figure). The row has $13$ white and $13$ black cells, the first group of teeth $12$ white cells, the second group $12$ black cells: $25$ of each colour, and the figure is connected. The $13$ marked cells \[(1,0),(3,0),\dots,(11,0)\quad\text{and}\quad(12,0),(14,0),\dots,(24,0)\] touch every pair of adjacent cells: an edge of the row $(x,0)$–$(x+1,0)$ contains an odd $x\le11$ or an even $x\ge12$, and each tooth is attached to a marked cell (the teeth $(12,\pm1)$ are deliberately omitted). So every domino contains a marked cell, and different dominoes contain different marked cells: at most $13$ dominoes can be cut.

The answer is $\boxed{13}$.

Solution 2 (Peeling a spanning tree)

Form the graph $G$ on the $50$ cells, joining cells with a common side. It is connected, every vertex has degree at most $4$, and disjoint dominoes are exactly matchings of $G$.

Lemma. Every tree with $N\ge1$ vertices and all degrees at most $4$ has a matching with at least $\lceil (N-1)/4\rceil$ edges.

Proof by induction on $N$. For $N\le5$ the bound is at most $1$, and a tree with $N\ge2$ vertices has an edge. Let $N\ge6$, take a longest path $v_0v_1\dots v_k$ in the tree ($k\ge2$, since a tree with at least $3$ vertices is not a single edge). All neighbours of $v_1$ other than $v_2$ are leaves (a neighbour with another neighbour would give a longer path), and there are at most $3$ of them. Match $v_1$ with $v_0$ and delete $v_1$ together with its leaf neighbours: we delete $j\le4$ vertices and what remains is a tree (everything hangs on $v_2$) with $N-j\ge N-4\ge2$ vertices. By induction it has a matching with $\lceil (N-j-1)/4\rceil\ge\lceil (N-5)/4\rceil$ edges, and adding $v_1v_0$ gives $\lceil (N-5)/4\rceil+1=\lceil (N-1)/4\rceil$ edges. $\square$

Applying the lemma to a spanning tree of $G$ ($N=50$) gives a matching with $\lceil 49/4\rceil=13$ edges, so $13$ dominoes can always be cut.

Sharpness. Consider the figure made of the row of cells $(x,0)$, $0\le x\le25$, with the cells $(x,\pm1)$ added for $x\in\{1,3,\dots,11\}$ and $x\in\{14,16,\dots,24\}$, colouring $(x,y)$ white for even $x+y$. It is connected and has $13+12=25$ white and $13+12=25$ black cells. Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that for all real numbers $x,y$ the equality \[f(f(y)+x-y)+f(x-y)=f(xf(y)-y)\] holds. (Here $\mathbb{R}$ denotes the set of real numbers.)

Show the answer

$f(x)=0$ for all $x$

Show the solution

Solution (Put $x=0$)

Clearly $f\equiv0$ satisfies the equation ($0+0=0$). Conversely, let $f$ be a solution.

Putting $x=0$ gives $f(f(y)-y)+f(-y)=f(-y)$, so \[f\bigl(f(y)-y\bigr)=0\quad\text{for all }y .\] In particular $f$ has a zero: $z=f(0)-0=f(0)$ satisfies $f(z)=0$.

Now put $y=z$ in the equation; since $f(z)=0$, \[f(x-z)+f(x-z)=f(x\cdot0-z)=f(-z)\quad\text{for all }x .\] So $f(t)=\tfrac12f(-z)$ for every real $t$: $f$ is a constant $k$. Then the equation reads $k+k=k$, so $k=0$.

The only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=0\text{ for all }x}$.

Remark. A zero of $f$ can also be found from $y=0$ followed by $x=0$, which gives $f(f(0))=0$; the rest is the same.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Call an integer wonderful if it can be represented as the sum of squares of two integers. Consider a set consisting of $n$ consecutive integers. If every element of this set is wonderful, we call such a set a super set. Find the largest natural number $n$ for which there exist infinitely many super sets consisting of $n$ elements.

Show the answer

$3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squares modulo $4$ and an explicit family)

No four consecutive wonderful integers. A square is $\equiv0$ or $1\pmod4$, so a sum of two squares is $\equiv0,1$ or $2\pmod4$, never $3$. Among any four consecutive integers one is $\equiv3\pmod4$. Hence a super set has at most $3$ elements.

Infinitely many super sets with $3$ elements. For every positive integer $k$ let $a=2k^2+1$. Then \[a^2-1=4k^4+4k^2=(2k^2)^2+(2k)^2,\qquad a^2=a^2+0^2,\qquad a^2+1=a^2+1^2,\] so $\{a^2-1,a^2,a^2+1\}$ is a super set. Different $k$ give different sets, so there are infinitely many.

The largest such $n$ is $\boxed{3}$.

Solution 2 (The two-squares identity)

Upper bound. Squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$, so no sum of two squares is $\equiv3\pmod4$; since every four consecutive integers contain one such number, $n\le3$.

Construction by iteration. We use the identity \[(p^2+q^2)(r^2+s^2)=(pr-qs)^2+(ps+qr)^2,\] so a product of two sums of two squares is again a sum of two squares.

Call an integer $a\ge3$ good if $a-1$ and $a+1$ are both sums of two squares. If $a$ is good, then \[a^2-1=(a-1)(a+1),\qquad a^2=a^2+0^2,\qquad a^2+1=a^2+1^2\] are all sums of two squares, so $\{a^2-1,a^2,a^2+1\}$ is a super set; moreover $a^2$ is again good, since $a^2-1$ and $a^2+1$ are sums of two squares. Starting from the good number $3$ ($2=1^2+1^2$, $4=2^2+0^2$) we get the good numbers $3,9,81,6561,\dots$ (each the square of the previous one), strictly increasing, and the super sets \[\{8,9,10\},\ \{80,81,82\},\ \{6560,6561,6562\},\ \dots\] are infinitely many. Hence the answer is $\boxed{3}$.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Through vertex $C$ of triangle $ABC$ a line $\ell$ parallel to $AB$ is drawn. A circle with center $O_1$ is inscribed in angle $BAC$ and tangent to $\ell$, and a circle with center $O_2$ is inscribed in angle $ABC$ and tangent to $\ell$. On segment $AB$ a point $N$ is chosen such that $\angle AO_1N=\angle BO_2N$. The angle bisector of $\angle ACB$ intersects lines $O_1N$ and $O_2N$ at points $M$ and $K$, respectively. Prove that the midpoint of $AB$ is the circumcenter of triangle $MNK$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Lengths, then a vector lemma)

Write $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles at $A,B,C$, $s$ for the semiperimeter and $h$ for the distance from $C$ to $AB$. Let $I$ be the incentre, and let the incircle touch $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ at $T$, $D$, $E$; then $AT=AE=s-a$ and $BT=BD=s-b$. Let $F$ be the midpoint of $AB$.

A circle tangent to the parallel lines $AB$ and $\ell$ has radius $h/2$. The circle with centre $O_1$ is inscribed in angle $BAC$, so $O_1$ is the point of the bisector $AI$ at distance $h/2$ from $AB$. Likewise $O_2$ is the point of the bisector $BI$ at distance $h/2$ from $AB$.

Step 1: $N=T$. Let $U_1$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $O_1$ to $AB$. As $\angle O_1AU_1=\alpha/2$ and $O_1U_1=h/2=\tfrac12 b\sin\alpha$, \[AU_1=\tfrac h2\cot\tfrac\alpha2=b\cos^2\tfrac\alpha2=\tfrac12(b+b\cos\alpha).\] Using $c=a\cos\beta+b\cos\alpha$ (projection of the sides onto $AB$) and $AT=\tfrac12(b+c-a)$, \[TU_1=AU_1-AT=\tfrac12(a-a\cos\beta)>0 .\] So $T$ lies between $A$ and $U_1$, and in the right triangle $O_1U_1T$ (with $O_1U_1=\tfrac12a\sin\beta$) \[\tan\angle TO_1U_1=\frac{1-\cos\beta}{\sin\beta}=\tan\frac\beta2,\qquad \angle TO_1U_1=\frac\beta2 .\] Since $\angle AO_1U_1=90^\circ-\frac\alpha2$ and ray $O_1T$ lies inside this angle, $\angle AO_1T=90^\circ-\frac\alpha2-\frac\beta2=\frac\gamma2$. The same computation at $B$ gives $\angle BO_2T=\frac\gamma2$.

When $N$ moves along segment $AB$ from $A$ to $B$, ray $O_1N$ turns monotonically, so $\angle AO_1N$ strictly increases, while $\angle BO_2N$ strictly decreases. Hence there is at most one point with $\angle AO_1N=\angle BO_2N$, and $N=T$.

Step 2: lines $O_1N$ and $O_2N$. In the right triangle $O_1U_1T$ we found $\angle O_1TU_1=90^\circ-\frac\beta2$, and ray $TU_1$ is ray $TB$. Line $BI$ makes the angle $\frac\beta2$ with ray $BT$, on the same side of $AB$. So in the triangle formed by $T$, $B$ and the intersection of lines $TO_1$, $BI$, two angles add up to $90^\circ$: $TO_1\perp BI$. Also $BT=BD$, so the bisector $BI$ is the perpendicular bisector of $TD$, and $TD\perp BI$. Hence line $O_1N$ is line $TD$. Similarly line $O_2N$ is line $TE$.

Step 3 (lemma): the foot $M^*$ of the perpendicular from $A$ to $CI$ lies on $TD$. Let $A'$ be the reflection of $A$ in line $CI$; as $CI$ bisects angle $C$, $A'$ lies on ray $CB$ with $CA'=b$, and $M^*$ is the midpoint of $AA'$. Use vectors with origin $B$. Then \[A'=C+\tfrac ba(B-C)=\bigl(1-\tfrac ba\bigr)C,\quad T=\tfrac{s-b}{c}\,A,\quad D=\tfrac{s-b}{a}\,C,\quad M^*=\tfrac12A+\tfrac{a-b}{2a}\,C .\] With $\lambda=\frac{a-b}{2(s-b)}$ we have $1-\lambda=\frac{c}{2(s-b)}$, and $(1-\lambda)T+\lambda D=\frac12A+\frac{a-b}{2a}C=M^*$. So $M^*\in TD$. In the same way (exchange $A$ and $B$) the foot $K^*$ of the perpendicular from $B$ to $CI$ lies on $TE$.

Step 4: $M$ and $K$. Lines $TD$ and $CI$ are not parallel, for otherwise $CI\perp BI$, while $\angle BIC=90^\circ+\frac\alpha2$. So $M=CI\cap TD=M^*$, and similarly $K=K^*$.

Step 5: distances from $F$. In triangle $ABA'$, $FM^*$ joins the midpoints of $AB$ and $AA'$, so $FM=\tfrac12BA'=\tfrac12|a-b|$. Symmetrically $FK=\tfrac12|a-b|$. Finally \[FN=FT=\bigl|\tfrac c2-(s-a)\bigr|=\tfrac12|a-b| .\] So $FM=FN=FK$, and $F$ is the circumcentre of $MNK$. (If $a=b$ the three points coincide with $F$ and there is no triangle; the statement presupposes $a\ne b$.)

Solution 2 (Directed angles on the circles with diameters $CI$, $AI$, $BI$)

Throughout, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Write $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles at $A,B,C$, $s$ for the semiperimeter and $h$ for the distance from $C$ to $AB$. Let $I$ be the incentre, and let the incircle touch $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ at $T$, $D$, $E$; then $AT=AE=s-a$ and $BT=BD=s-b$. Let $F$ be the midpoint of $AB$.

A circle tangent to the parallel lines $AB$ and $\ell$ has radius $h/2$. The circle with centre $O_1$ is inscribed in angle $BAC$, so $O_1$ is the point of the bisector $AI$ at distance $h/2$ from $AB$. Likewise $O_2$ is the point of the bisector $BI$ at distance $h/2$ from $AB$. We orient $ABC$ counterclockwise; then $\measuredangle(AB,AC)=\alpha$, $\measuredangle(BC,BA)=\beta$, $\measuredangle(CA,CB)=\gamma$, and for the bisectors $\measuredangle(AB,AI)=\frac\alpha2$, $\measuredangle(BC,BI)=\frac\beta2$, $\measuredangle(CA,CI)=\frac\gamma2$. Consequently \[\measuredangle(AI,BI)=\measuredangle(AI,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,BC)+\measuredangle(BC,BI)=-\tfrac\alpha2-\beta+\tfrac\beta2=-\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2 .\]

$O_1$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $C$ to $AI$. Reflect $C$ in $AI$ to $C'$ on line $AB$. The midpoint of $CC'$ is the foot from $C$ to $AI$; it lies on the bisector inside angle $A$ at distance $\frac h2$ from $AB$, hence it is $O_1$. Likewise $O_2$ is the foot from $C$ to $BI$. So $O_1,O_2,D,E$ lie on the circle with diameter $CI$ (right angles at these points). Note $O_1\ne I$, since $r=\frac{hc}{a+b+c}<\frac h2$.

$O_1$ lies on $TD$ and $O_2$ on $TE$. If $O_1\ne D$, inscribed angles in the circle with diameter $CI$ give \begin{align*}\measuredangle(O_1D,BI)&=\measuredangle(O_1D,O_1I)+\measuredangle(AI,BI)=\measuredangle(CD,CI)-\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2\\&=-\tfrac\gamma2-\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2=90^\circ .\end{align*} Since $BT=BD$, also $TD\perp BI$; so $O_1\in TD$. Similarly ($AT=AE$ gives $TE\perp AI$) $O_2\in TE$.

$N=T$. The triangle $A,T,O_1$ is counterclockwise ($T$ on segment $AB$, $O_1$ on the side of $C$), so its angle at $O_1$ is the counterclockwise turn from ray $O_1A$ to ray $O_1T$, and it is determined by its value modulo $180^\circ$: \[\angle AO_1T\equiv\measuredangle(AI,TD)=\measuredangle(AI,BI)+90^\circ=\tfrac\gamma2 .\] Similarly the counterclockwise triangle $T,B,O_2$ gives $\angle BO_2T\equiv\measuredangle(TE,BI)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(AI,BI)=\frac\gamma2$. As $N$ runs from $A$ to $B$, $\angle AO_1N$ increases and $\angle BO_2N$ decreases strictly, so $N=T$. Thus $M=CI\cap TD$ and $K=CI\cap TE$.

$M$ and $K$ are the feet from $A$ and $B$ to $CI$. Let $M^*$ be the foot from $A$ to $CI$; it lies on the circle with diameter $AI$, together with $T$ and $E$. If $M^*\neq T$ (and $M^*\ne I$, as $\angle AIC\neq90^\circ$), \begin{align*}\measuredangle(M^*T,BI)&=\measuredangle(M^*T,M^*I)+\measuredangle(CI,BI)\\&=\measuredangle(AT,AI)+\measuredangle(CI,CB)+\measuredangle(BC,BI)=\tfrac\alpha2+\tfrac\gamma2+\tfrac\beta2=90^\circ,\end{align*} so $M^*\in TD$, and $M^*=CI\cap TD=M$ (the lines are not parallel since $CI\not\perp BI$). In the same way the foot $K^*$ from $B$ lies on the circle with diameter $BI$ through $T,D$, and $\measuredangle(K^*T,AI)=\measuredangle(BT,BI)+\measuredangle(CI,CA)+\measuredangle(AC,AI)=-\frac\beta2-\frac\gamma2-\frac\alpha2=90^\circ$, so $K^*\in TE$ and $K=K^*$.

Conclusion. Let $A'$, $B'$ be the reflections of $A$, $B$ in $CI$; they lie on rays $CB$, $CA$ with $CA'=b$, $CB'=a$, and $M$, $K$ are the midpoints of $AA'$, $BB'$. By the midline theorem in triangles $ABA'$ and $BAB'$, $FM=\frac12BA'=\frac12|a-b|$ and $FK=\frac12AB'=\frac12|a-b|$. Also $FN=|\frac c2-AT|=|\frac c2-\frac{b+c-a}2|=\frac12|a-b|$. Hence $F$ is equidistant from $M,N,K$ and is the circumcentre of triangle $MNK$ (assuming $a\neq b$; otherwise the three points coincide).

Solution 3 (Coordinates and cotangents)

Let $\alpha,\beta$ be the angles of the triangle at $A,B$. Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(L,0)$ with $L=AB$, and $C$ above the $x$-axis. Each circle touches the parallel lines $AB$ and $\ell$, which are at distance $h$ (the altitude from $C$), so both radii equal $r=h/2$ and both centres are at height $r$. The circle with centre $O_1$ is inscribed in angle $BAC$, so $O_1$ lies on the bisector of that angle; hence $O_1=(u,r)$ with $u=r\cot\frac\alpha2>0$. Likewise $O_2=(L-v,r)$ with $v=r\cot\frac\beta2>0$.

The vertex $C$. We have $C=(X,2r)$. Ray $AC$ makes the angle $\alpha$ with ray $AB$, so $X=2r\cot\alpha$, and \[\cot\alpha=\frac{\cot^2\frac\alpha2-1}{2\cot\frac\alpha2}=\frac{u^2-r^2}{2ur},\qquad\text{so}\qquad X=u-\frac{r^2}{u}.\] In the same way $L-X=2r\cot\beta=v-\frac{r^2}{v}$. Adding, \[L=(u+v)-r^2\Bigl(\frac1u+\frac1v\Bigr)=\frac{(u+v)(uv-r^2)}{uv},\] and $uv>r^2$ because $L>0$. In particular $L<u+v$.

The point $N$. Write $N=(n,0)$ with $0\le n\le L$. If $n=0$, then $\angle AO_1N=0^\circ$ while $\angle BO_2N=\angle BO_2A>0^\circ$; similarly $n=L$ is impossible. So $0<n<L$. For two vectors $p,q$ forming an angle $\theta$ with $0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$, we have $\cot\theta=\dfrac{p\cdot q}{|p_1q_2-p_2q_1|}$. For $\overrightarrow{O_1A}=(-u,-r)$ and $\overrightarrow{O_1N}=(n-u,-r)$ the dot product is $u(u-n)+r^2$ and $|p_1q_2-p_2q_1|=rn$. For $\overrightarrow{O_2B}=(v,-r)$ and $\overrightarrow{O_2N}=(n-L+v,-r)$ they are $v(v-(L-n))+r^2$ and $r(L-n)$. The cotangent is strictly decreasing on $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$, so $\angle AO_1N=\angle BO_2N$ holds exactly when \[\frac{u(u-n)+r^2}{rn}=\frac{v(v-(L-n))+r^2}{r(L-n)},\] that is, when $f(n)=0$, where \[\begin{aligned}f(n)&=\bigl(u(u-n)+r^2\bigr)(L-n)-\bigl(v(v-L+n)+r^2\bigr)n\\&=(u-v)n^2-\bigl(u^2+v^2+2r^2+(u-v)L\bigr)n+(u^2+r^2)L.\end{aligned}\] Using $uvL=(u+v)(uv-r^2)$ one checks that \[uv\,f(n)=-\bigl(vn+r^2-uv\bigr)\bigl(u(v-u)n+(u^2+r^2)(u+v)\bigr):\] on both sides the coefficient of $n^2$ is $uv(u-v)$, the constant term is $uv(u^2+r^2)L$, and the coefficient of $n$ is $-(2u^3v+2uvr^2+v^2r^2-u^2r^2)$. The second factor is positive: if $v\ge u$ both of its terms are nonnegative and the second is positive; if $u>v$, then $n<L<u+v$ gives \[\begin{aligned}u(v-u)n+(u^2+r^2)(u+v)&>-u(u-v)(u+v)+(u^2+r^2)(u+v)\\&=(u+v)(uv+r^2)>0.\end{aligned}\] Hence $vn+r^2-uv=0$, so \[n=u-\frac{r^2}{v},\qquad L-n=v-\frac{r^2}{u},\qquad L-2n=\frac{(u-v)(r^2-uv)}{uv}.\]

The lines. Line $O_1N$ passes through $(n,0)$ and $(u,r)$, so its points are $(n+zy,\,y)$ with $z=\frac{u-n}{r}=\frac rv$. Line $O_2N$ passes through $(n,0)$ and $(L-v,r)$, so its points are $(n+zy,\,y)$ with $z=\frac{L-v-n}{r}=-\frac ru$.

The unit vectors from $C$ towards $A$ and $B$ are $(-\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha)$ and $(\cos\beta,-\sin\beta)$. Their sum points along the bisector of angle $ACB$, and its vertical component $-(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)$ is negative. So the bisector is the line $x=X+m(y-2r)$, where, by the sum-to-product formulas, \[m=\frac{\cos\alpha-\cos\beta}{\sin\alpha+\sin\beta}=\tan\frac{\beta-\alpha}2=\frac{\tan\frac\beta2-\tan\frac\alpha2}{1+\tan\frac\alpha2\tan\frac\beta2}=\frac{r(u-v)}{uv+r^2},\] since $\tan\frac\alpha2=\frac ru$ and $\tan\frac\beta2=\frac rv$. A point $(n+zy,y)$ lies on the bisector exactly when $(z-m)y=X-2rm-n$. Here \[X-n=\frac{r^2}{v}-\frac{r^2}{u}=\frac{r^2(u-v)}{uv},\]so \[X-2rm-n=r^2(u-v)\Bigl(\frac1{uv}-\frac{2}{uv+r^2}\Bigr)=\frac{r^2(u-v)(r^2-uv)}{uv(uv+r^2)},\] and \[\frac rv-m=\frac{r(r^2+v^2)}{v(uv+r^2)},\qquad -\frac ru-m=-\frac{r(r^2+u^2)}{u(uv+r^2)}.\] Both are nonzero, so each line meets the bisector in exactly one point: $M$ (for $z=\frac rv$) and $K$ (for $z=-\frac ru$), with heights \[y_M=\frac{r(u-v)(r^2-uv)}{u(r^2+v^2)},\qquad y_K=-\frac{r(u-v)(r^2-uv)}{v(r^2+u^2)}.\] In both cases $y=\dfrac{z(L-2n)}{1+z^2}$: for $z=\frac rv$ we have $\frac{z}{1+z^2}=\frac{rv}{r^2+v^2}$, and for $z=-\frac ru$ we have $\frac{z}{1+z^2}=-\frac{ru}{r^2+u^2}$; multiply by $L-2n=\frac{(u-v)(r^2-uv)}{uv}$.

The circle. Let $F=(\frac L2,0)$ be the midpoint of $AB$ and $N'=(L-n,0)$ the reflection of $N$ in $F$. The circle with diameter $NN'$ has centre $F$ and equation $(x-n)(x-(L-n))+y^2=0$. For a point $(n+zy,y)$ the left side equals \[zy\bigl(zy-(L-2n)\bigr)+y^2=y\bigl((1+z^2)y-z(L-2n)\bigr),\] which vanishes for $M$ and for $K$ by the formula for their heights. So $M$, $N$, $K$ lie on this circle, and $FM=FN=FK$.

Nondegeneracy. If $CA\ne CB$, then $\alpha\ne\beta$, so $u\ne v$; also $uv\ne r^2$. Hence $y_M\ne0$ and $y_K\ne0$, so $M\ne N$ and $K\ne N$. The lines $O_1N$ and $O_2N$ are different ($z$ has opposite signs) and meet only at $N$, so $M\ne K$. Three distinct points of a circle are not collinear, so $MNK$ is a triangle and its circumcircle is the circle with centre $F$. Therefore the midpoint of $AB$ is the circumcenter of triangle $MNK$. (If $CA=CB$, then $u=v$, $n=\frac L2$ and $M=N=K$.)

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Is it possible to place the $64$ consecutive integers into the cells of an $8\times 8$ board (one number per cell) so that in every T-tetromino the sum of the four numbers is divisible by $7$? (A T-tetromino is a T-shaped figure consisting of $4$ cells.)

Show the answer

No

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Four T-tetrominoes around one cell)

Suppose such a placement exists, and work modulo $7$.

Neighbours of an inner cell are congruent. Let $c$ be a cell not on the border of the board, with neighbours $n_1,n_2,n_3,n_4$ (see the figure; we use the same letters for the numbers in them). For each $i$, the cell $c$ with the three neighbours other than $n_i$ is a T-tetromino, with sum $S-n_i$ where $S=c+n_1+n_2+n_3+n_4$. All four sums are divisible by $7$, so \[n_1\equiv n_2\equiv n_3\equiv n_4\pmod 7 .\]

Spreading the congruence. Number rows and columns $1,\dots,8$ and colour the board like a chessboard; the inner cells are those $(r,k)$ with $2\le r,k\le7$. Call two cells linked if they have a common inner neighbour; linked cells have congruent numbers.

  • Two diagonally adjacent cells $(r,k)$, $(r+1,k+1)$ (or $(r,k+1)$, $(r+1,k)$) inside the $6\times6$ inner block are linked, because their common neighbour $(r,k+1)$ (resp. $(r,k)$) is inner. The cells of one colour in a $6\times6$ block are connected by such diagonal steps, so the $18$ inner cells of each colour carry congruent numbers.

  • A border cell that is not a corner, say $(1,k)$ with $2\le k\le7$, has the inner neighbour $(2,k)$, which is also adjacent to $(2,k-1)$ and $(2,k+1)$; at least one of these is inner, it has the colour of $(1,k)$, and it is linked to $(1,k)$. The other sides are similar.

Hence, for each colour, the $30$ cells of that colour other than its two corners carry numbers that are all congruent modulo $7$.

Contradiction. Among $64$ consecutive integers ($64=9\cdot7+1$), one residue class modulo $7$ contains $10$ of them and each other class contains $9$. No class contains $30$ numbers. Hence the answer is $\boxed{\text{No}}$.

Solution 2 (Two T-tetrominoes on the same bar)

Suppose such a placement exists and work modulo $7$. Number rows and columns $1,\dots,8$; write $(r,k)$ for the cell in row $r$, column $k$, and also for the number in it.

Local relations. Let $2\le r\le7$ and $2\le k\le7$. The cells $(r,k-1),(r,k),(r,k+1)$ form a horizontal bar of three cells, and adding either $(r-1,k)$ or $(r+1,k)$ gives a T-tetromino. Both sums are divisible by $7$ and they differ by $(r-1,k)-(r+1,k)$, so \[(r-1,k)\equiv(r+1,k).\] Using the vertical bar $(r-1,k),(r,k),(r+1,k)$ with the extra cell $(r,k-1)$ or $(r,k+1)$ in the same way, \[(r,k-1)\equiv(r,k+1).\] In words: in each of the columns $2,\dots,7$, cells two apart are congruent, and in each of the rows $2,\dots,7$, cells two apart are congruent.

Four classes of $15$ cells. Fix parities $(\rho,\kappa)$ and consider the $16$ cells $(r,k)$ with $r\equiv\rho$, $k\equiv\kappa\pmod2$. Exactly one of them is a corner of the board. Let $r_0\in\{2,3\}$ and $k_0\in\{2,3\}$ have the parities $\rho,\kappa$. Row $r_0$ is one of the rows $2,\dots,7$, so all cells $(r_0,k)$ of the class (with $k\equiv\kappa$) are congruent to $(r_0,k_0)$. Now take any non-corner cell $(r,k)$ of the class.

  • If $2\le k\le7$, column $k$ gives $(r,k)\equiv(r_0,k)\equiv(r_0,k_0)$.

  • If $k\in\{1,8\}$, then $2\le r\le7$ (the cell is not a corner); row $r$ gives $(r,k)\equiv(r,k_0)$, and column $k_0$ gives $(r,k_0)\equiv(r_0,k_0)$.

So the $15$ non-corner cells of each parity class carry congruent numbers.

Contradiction. Among $64$ consecutive integers ($64=9\cdot7+1$) each residue modulo $7$ occurs $9$ or $10$ times, never $15$. Hence the answer is $\boxed{\text{No}}$.

Solution 3 (The sums force multiples of $7$ in a $4 imes4$ block)

Suppose such a placement exists, and work modulo $7$; we use the same letter for a cell and for the number in it.

Neighbours of a cell. Let $x$ be a cell whose four side neighbours $n_1,n_2,n_3,n_4$ are on the board. For each $i$, the cell $x$ together with the three neighbours other than $n_i$ is a T-tetromino, with sum $S-n_i$, where $S=x+n_1+n_2+n_3+n_4$. All four sums are divisible by $7$, so $n_1\equiv n_2\equiv n_3\equiv n_4\pmod7$, and every such T-tetromino gives $x+3n_1\equiv0$.

A $4\times4$ block. Take any $4\times4$ block of the board and call its four central cells \[\begin{matrix} a&b\\ c&d\end{matrix}\] ($a$ top left, $b$ top right, $c$ bottom left, $d$ bottom right). Each of them has all four side neighbours inside the block. The neighbours of $a$ include $b$ and $c$, so all four neighbours of $a$ are congruent to $b$, and $c\equiv b$. The neighbours of $b$ include $a$ and $d$, so all four neighbours of $b$ are congruent to $a$, and $d\equiv a$. A T-tetromino with centre $a$ and one with centre $b$ give \[a+3b\equiv0,\qquad b+3a\equiv0\pmod7.\] Subtracting, $2(b-a)\equiv0$, so $a\equiv b$ (as $2$ is invertible modulo $7$), and then $4a\equiv0$, so $a\equiv0$. Hence $a\equiv b\equiv c\equiv d\equiv0\pmod7$: the four central cells of every $4\times4$ block contain multiples of $7$.

Contradiction. Split the $8\times8$ board into four disjoint $4\times4$ blocks. Their central cells give $16$ cells containing multiples of $7$. But among $64$ consecutive integers ($64=9\cdot7+1$) there are at most $10$ multiples of $7$. Hence the answer is $\boxed{\text{No}}$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry

An isosceles triangle $ABC$ with $AC=BC$ is inscribed in a circle $\omega$. On segment $AB$ a point $D$ is chosen ($D$ is closer to $A$ than to $B$). On the smaller arc $CB$ of $\omega$ a point $K$ is chosen. The line through $D$ perpendicular to $CK$ intersects lines $AK$ and $BK$ at points $P$ and $Q$, respectively. Prove that all circumcircles of triangles $PQK$ pass through a fixed point independent of the choice of $K$.

Show the answer

proof (the fixed point is the second intersection of line $C^{\prime}D$ with $\omega$, where $C^{\prime}$ is the antipode of $C$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Directed angle chase)

Throughout, $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ denotes the directed angle from line $\ell$ to line $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $C'$ be the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite $C$, and let $X$ be the second intersection of line $C'D$ with $\omega$. Since $D$ is an interior point of segment $AB$ (so $D\ne A$, $D\ne B$), it lies inside $\omega$, hence $X\neq C'$ and $X\neq D$; also $X\ne A$ (line $C'A$ meets $AB$ only at $A\ne D$) and similarly $X\ne B$. The point $X$ does not depend on $K$. Denote by $\ell$ the line through $D$ perpendicular to $CK$. We show that $X$ lies on the circle $PQK$.

Two facts. (i) $\measuredangle(KC,\ell)=90^\circ$ by definition, and (ii) $\measuredangle(KC,KC')=90^\circ$ because $CC'$ is a diameter (here $K\neq C,C'$).

Non-degeneracy. Since $CA=CB$, line $CC'$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AB$. The point $K$ lies on the minor arc $CB$, i.e. on the same side of $CC'$ as $B$, so line $C'K$ meets $AB$ on the $B$-side of the midpoint; $D$ is on the $A$-side, so $D\notin C'K$. As $\ell\parallel C'K$ by (i),(ii), $\ell$ does not pass through $K$; so $P\ne K$, $Q\neq K$, and $P,Q,K$ are not collinear. Also $P\ne A$ (else $\ell=AB$ and $AB\perp CK$, forcing $K=C'$) and $P\neq D$ (as $D\notin AK$); similarly $Q\ne B$, $Q\ne D$.

$X$ lies on circle $ADP$. Using $P\in AK$, (i), (ii), the inscribed angle theorem in $\omega$ and $X\in C'D$: \begin{align*}\measuredangle(PA,PD)&=\measuredangle(KA,\ell)=\measuredangle(KA,KC)+90^\circ\\&=\measuredangle(KA,KC')=\measuredangle(XA,XC')=\measuredangle(XA,XD).\end{align*} So $A,D,P,X$ are concyclic. In the same way \[\measuredangle(QB,QD)=\measuredangle(KB,KC)+90^\circ=\measuredangle(KB,KC')=\measuredangle(XB,XD),\] so $B,D,Q,X$ are concyclic.

$X$ lies on circle $PQK$. If $X$ equals $P$ or $Q$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, using the two circles just found, \begin{align*}\measuredangle(XP,XQ)&=\measuredangle(XP,XD)+\measuredangle(XD,XQ)=\measuredangle(AP,AD)+\measuredangle(BD,BQ)\\&=\measuredangle(AK,AB)+\measuredangle(AB,BK)=\measuredangle(KP,KQ).\end{align*} Hence $X,P,Q,K$ are concyclic: every circle $PQK$ passes through the fixed point $X$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates centred at $K$)

Let $C'$ be the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite $C$, and $X$ the second intersection of line $C'D$ with $\omega$; $X$ does not depend on $K$, and we show that $X$ lies on the circle $PQK$.

Two observations. Since $CA=CB$, $C$ and $C'$ are the midpoints of the two arcs $AB$, and $K$ lies on the arc containing $C$; so $KC'$ bisects $\angle AKB$ and $KC\perp KC'$ is the external bisector. Thus the lines $KA$ and $KB$ are symmetric with respect to line $KC$. Moreover $\ell$, the line through $D$ perpendicular to $KC$, is parallel to $KC'$, and $\ell\ne KC'$ because $D\notin KC'$ ($KC'$ meets $AB$ on $B$'s side of its midpoint, $D$ is on $A$'s side).

Coordinates. Put $K=(0,0)$, line $KC'$ as the $x$-axis and $KC$ as the $y$-axis: $C'=(\kappa,0)$, $C=(0,\gamma)$ with $\kappa,\gamma\ne0$. As $\angle CKC'=90^\circ$, the circle $\omega$ has diameter $CC'$: \[\omega:\ x^2+y^2=\kappa x+\gamma y .\] Lines $KA$, $KB$ are $y=mx$ and $y=-mx$ for some $m\ne0$. Intersecting with $\omega$: \[A=\Bigl(\tfrac{\kappa+\gamma m}{1+m^2},\ m\tfrac{\kappa+\gamma m}{1+m^2}\Bigr),\qquad B=\Bigl(\tfrac{\kappa-\gamma m}{1+m^2},\ -m\tfrac{\kappa-\gamma m}{1+m^2}\Bigr).\] The line $\ell$ is horizontal, $y=e$ with $e\ne0$, so $P=(e/m,\,e)$ and $Q=(-e/m,\,e)$.

The point $D$. Write $A=(a_1,ma_1)$, $B=(b_1,-mb_1)$; then $b_1-a_1=-\frac{2\gamma m}{1+m^2}$ and $a_1+b_1=\frac{2\kappa}{1+m^2}$. The point of line $AB$ with $y=e$ has \[x_D=a_1+(b_1-a_1)\frac{e-ma_1}{-m(a_1+b_1)}=a_1+\frac{\gamma(e-ma_1)}{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa^2-\gamma^2m^2+\gamma e(1+m^2)}{\kappa(1+m^2)} .\]

The circle $PQK$. It passes through $K=(0,0)$ and is symmetric in the $y$-axis, so it is $x^2+y^2=uy$; substituting $P$ gives $u=e\,\frac{1+m^2}{m^2}$.

Its second common point with $\omega$. Subtracting the equations of the two circles, their common points satisfy $\kappa x=(u-\gamma)y$. Solving with $x^2+y^2=uy$, the common point $Y\ne K$ is \[Y=\frac{u\kappa}{(u-\gamma)^2+\kappa^2}\,\bigl(u-\gamma,\ \kappa\bigr),\qquad Y-C'=\frac{\kappa}{(u-\gamma)^2+\kappa^2}\,\bigl(\gamma(u-\gamma)-\kappa^2,\ u\kappa\bigr).\] The line $C'Y$ reaches height $y=e$ at the parameter where the second coordinate equals $e$, i.e. at \[x=\kappa+\frac{e\,(\gamma(u-\gamma)-\kappa^2)}{u\kappa}=\kappa+\frac{m^2(\gamma(u-\gamma)-\kappa^2)}{\kappa(1+m^2)}=\frac{\kappa^2+\gamma m^2(u-\gamma)}{\kappa(1+m^2)},\] using $e/u=m^2/(1+m^2)$. Since $\gamma m^2u=\gamma e(1+m^2)$, this equals $x_D$. Hence $D$ lies on line $C'Y$, i.e. $Y$ is the second intersection of line $C'D$ with $\omega$ ($Y\ne C'$, as $C'$ is not on $x^2+y^2=uy$). So $Y=X$, and the circle $PQK$ passes through the fixed point $X$.

Problem 6

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Find all natural numbers $n$ such that \[2^{n!}-1\] is divisible by \[(2^1-1)(2^2-1)\cdots(2^{n-1}-1)(2^n-1).\] (Here $n!$ denotes the product of all natural numbers from $1$ to $n$.)

Show the answer

$n=1,2,3,4,5$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The prime $3$ (and $7$ once))

Write $P_n=(2^1-1)(2^2-1)\cdots(2^n-1)$ and let $v_p$ denote the exponent of the prime $p$. All factors are odd, so only odd primes matter. For an odd prime $p$ let $d$ be the order of $2$ modulo $p$ (the least $d\ge1$ with $p\mid2^d-1$; then $2\le d\le p-1$) and $e=v_p(2^d-1)\ge1$.

Lemma. $p\mid 2^k-1$ iff $d\mid k$, and then $v_p(2^k-1)=e+v_p(k/d)$. (The first part is the definition of the order; the second is the lifting-the-exponent lemma applied to $(2^d)^{k/d}-1^{k/d}$, since $p\mid2^d-1$.)

Consequently, with $m=\lfloor n/d\rfloor$, the multiples of $d$ up to $n$ are $d,2d,\dots,md$, and \[v_p(P_n)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(e+v_p(j)\bigr)=em+v_p(m!).\] If $d\le n$, then $d\mid n!$ and $p\nmid d$, so $v_p(2^{n!}-1)=e+v_p(n!/d)=e+v_p(n!)$. Thus $P_n\mid2^{n!}-1$ iff for every odd prime $p$ with $d\le n$ \[em+v_p(m!)\le e+v_p(n!).\qquad(\ast)\]

$n\le5$ works. $P_1=1$, $P_2=3\mid2^2-1$, $P_3=21\mid 2^6-1=63$. $P_4=315=9\cdot5\cdot7$ and $4!=24$: $63=2^6-1$ and $15=2^4-1$ divide $2^{24}-1$, so the coprime factors $9,5,7$ do. $P_5=9765=9\cdot5\cdot7\cdot31$ and $5!=120$: $63$, $15$, $31=2^5-1$ divide $2^{120}-1$, so the coprime factors $9,5,7,31$ do.

$n\ge6$ fails. Take $p=3$: $d=2$, $e=1$, $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\ge3$, and $(\ast)$ reads $m+v_3(m!)\le1+v_3(n!)$. By Legendre's formula $v_3(n!)=\frac{n-S(n)}2$, where $S(n)$ is the sum of the base-$3$ digits of $n$; note $S(n)\equiv n\pmod 2$.

  • $n$ even: $m=\frac n2$ and $v_3(m!)\ge1$, so $m+v_3(m!)\ge\frac n2+1>1+\frac{n-1}2\ge1+v_3(n!)$.

  • $n$ odd: $m=\frac{n-1}2$ and \[m+v_3(m!)-1-v_3(n!)=v_3(m!)+\frac{S(n)-3}2 .\] $S(n)$ is odd. If $S(n)\ge3$, this is $\ge v_3(m!)\ge1>0$. If $S(n)=1$, then $n=3^k$ with $k\ge2$; for $k\ge3$, $m\ge13$ and $v_3(m!)\ge2$, so the difference is positive.

  • The only remaining case is $n=9$. Use $p=7$: $d=3$, $e=1$, $m=3$, and $(\ast)$ would say $3+0\le1+v_7(9!)=2$, which is false.

So $(\ast)$ fails for some prime whenever $n\ge6$. The answer is $\boxed{n=1,2,3,4,5}$.

Solution 2 (Mainly the prime $7$)

Write $P_n=(2^1-1)(2^2-1)\cdots(2^n-1)$ and let $v_p$ denote the exponent of the prime $p$. All factors are odd, so only odd primes matter. For an odd prime $p$ let $d$ be the order of $2$ modulo $p$ (the least $d\ge1$ with $p\mid2^d-1$; then $2\le d\le p-1$) and $e=v_p(2^d-1)\ge1$.

Lemma. $p\mid 2^k-1$ iff $d\mid k$, and then $v_p(2^k-1)=e+v_p(k/d)$. (The first part is the definition of the order; the second is the lifting-the-exponent lemma applied to $(2^d)^{k/d}-1^{k/d}$, since $p\mid2^d-1$.)

Consequently, with $m=\lfloor n/d\rfloor$, the multiples of $d$ up to $n$ are $d,2d,\dots,md$, and \[v_p(P_n)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(e+v_p(j)\bigr)=em+v_p(m!).\] If $d\le n$, then $d\mid n!$ and $p\nmid d$, so $v_p(2^{n!}-1)=e+v_p(n!/d)=e+v_p(n!)$. Thus $P_n\mid2^{n!}-1$ iff for every odd prime $p$ with $d\le n$ \[em+v_p(m!)\le e+v_p(n!).\qquad(\ast)\]

$n\le5$ works. $P_1=1$, $P_2=3\mid2^2-1$, $P_3=21\mid 2^6-1=63$. $P_4=315=9\cdot5\cdot7$ and $4!=24$: $63=2^6-1$ and $15=2^4-1$ divide $2^{24}-1$, so the coprime factors $9,5,7$ do. $P_5=9765=9\cdot5\cdot7\cdot31$ and $5!=120$: $63$, $15$, $31=2^5-1$ divide $2^{120}-1$, so the coprime factors $9,5,7,31$ do.

$n\ge6$ fails. Take $p=7$: $2^3=8\equiv1\pmod7$, so $d=3$, $e=v_7(7)=1$, and $(\ast)$ requires $\lfloor n/3\rfloor+v_7(\lfloor n/3\rfloor!)\le1+v_7(n!)$; in particular $\lfloor n/3\rfloor\le1+v_7(n!)$. By Legendre's formula $v_7(n!)=\frac{n-S_7(n)}6\le\frac{n-1}6$ (here $S_7(n)\ge1$ is the base-$7$ digit sum), and $\lfloor n/3\rfloor\ge\frac{n-2}3$. For $n\ge10$, \[\frac{n-2}3>1+\frac{n-1}6\iff 2n-4>n+5\iff n>9,\] so $(\ast)$ fails. For $n=6$: $2>1+0$; for $n=9$: $3>1+1$; again it fails.

For $n=7,8$ use $p=3$ ($d=2$, $e=1$, $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$): $(\ast)$ would require $m+v_3(m!)\le1+v_3(n!)$, but for $n=7$ it reads $3+1\le1+2$ and for $n=8$ it reads $4+1\le1+2$, both false.

Hence exactly $\boxed{n=1,2,3,4,5}$ work.
