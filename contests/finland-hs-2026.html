Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Discrete Summation Identities

Find the value of \[1^2-2^2-3^2+4^2+5^2-6^2-7^2+8^2+\dots+2025^2-2026^2.\]

Show the answer

$-2027$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Blocks of four)

The signs repeat with period $4$: the term $k^2$ has sign $+$ when $k\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and $-$ when $k\equiv2,3\pmod4$. For every $a$, \[a^2-(a+1)^2-(a+2)^2+(a+3)^2=\bigl(2a^2+6a+9\bigr)-\bigl(2a^2+6a+5\bigr)=4 .\] Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$, the terms $1^2,\dots,2024^2$ form $506$ such blocks (with $a=1,5,\dots,2021$), contributing $4\cdot506=2024$. The last two terms are $+2025^2-2026^2=-(2025+2026)=-4051$. The value is $2024-4051=\boxed{-2027}$.

Solution 2 (Pairing the ends)

Let $s_k=\pm1$ be the sign of $k^2$, so $s_k=+1$ for $k\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and $s_k=-1$ for $k\equiv2,3\pmod4$. Since $2027-k\equiv3-k\pmod4$, the residues $0,1,2,3$ of $k$ go to $3,2,1,0$, so $s_{2027-k}=-s_k$. Pair the term $k^2$ with $(2027-k)^2$ for $k=1,\dots,1013$; these pairs use every index $1,\dots,2026$ once, and the pair $k$ contributes \[s_k\bigl(k^2-(2027-k)^2\bigr)=2027\,s_k\,(2k-2027).\] For four consecutive indices $k=4j+1,\dots,4j+4$ the signs are $+,-,-,+$, and \[(2k-2027)-(2k+2-2027)-(2k+4-2027)+(2k+6-2027)=0\qquad(k=4j+1).\] Since $1013=4\cdot253+1$, the indices $k=1,\dots,1012$ form $253$ such groups and contribute $0$. The last index $k=1013$ has $s_{1013}=+1$ and contributes $2027\,(2026-2027)=-2027$. The value is $\boxed{-2027}$.

Problem 2

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Solve in positive integers: \[a+b+c+d+e=abcde.\]

Show the answer

the permutations of $(1,1,1,2,5)$, $(1,1,1,3,3)$ and $(1,1,2,2,2)$ ($40$ ordered solutions)

Show the solution

Solution (Order the variables and bound the product)

The equation is symmetric, so first find the solutions with $a\le b\le c\le d\le e$. Then \[abcde=a+b+c+d+e\le 5e,\qquad\text{so}\qquad abcd\le5 .\] With $a\le b\le c\le d$ and $abcd\le5$, the quadruple $(a,b,c,d)$ is $(1,1,1,d)$ with $1\le d\le5$, or $(1,1,2,2)$. The equation becomes:

  • $(1,1,1,d)$: $3+d+e=de$, i.e. $(d-1)(e-1)=4$. With $1\le d\le e$ this gives $d-1=1,e-1=4$ or $d-1=e-1=2$: the solutions $(1,1,1,2,5)$ and $(1,1,1,3,3)$.

  • $(1,1,2,2)$: $6+e=4e$, so $e=2$: the solution $(1,1,2,2,2)$.

All three check: $10=10$, $9=9$, $8=8$. Hence the solutions in positive integers are exactly the permutations of \[\boxed{(1,1,1,2,5),\ (1,1,1,3,3),\ (1,1,2,2,2)},\] that is, $20+10+10=40$ ordered quintuples.

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

On a chessboard, a move of the camel is performed as follows:

  • first, the camel moves $3$ squares in one direction;

  • then it moves $1$ square in a direction that is orthogonal to the first movement

(the length of the move is always $\sqrt{10}$).

A $4\times 8$ board is colored like a chessboard. Can the camel visit all black squares exactly once and return to its starting position?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (A square that would need three tour neighbours)

Label the columns $a,\dots,h$ (numbers $1,\dots,8$) and the rows $1,\dots,4$, with $a1$ black as on a chessboard; a square is black when its column number plus row number is even. A camel move changes the column by $\pm3$ and the row by $\pm1$ or the other way round, so it changes column$+$row by an even number: the camel always stays on squares of one colour. (If the other colour is called black, reflect the board in its vertical axis; with $8$ columns this swaps the two colours and maps camel moves to camel moves, so the answer is the same.)

List the camel moves from three black squares:

  • from $b2$: only to $e1$ and $e3$ (every other move leaves the board);

  • from $h2$: only to $e1$ and $e3$;

  • from $h4$: only to $e3$ and $g1$.

In a closed tour every square is entered once and left once, so it has exactly two neighbours along the tour, which must be camel moves. A square with only two possible moves must use both of them. Hence $b2$, $h2$ and $h4$ all have $e3$ as a neighbour along the tour, so $e3$ would have three neighbours along the tour, which is impossible.

So the answer is No.

Problem 4

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Centroid, Incenter, Circumcenter, and Orthocenter

Let $A$, $B$, $C$, $P$, $D$ and $Q$ be points on a circle in this order such that $PD$ and $BP$ are perpendicular. Let $R$ be the intersection of chords $AC$ and $BD$. Let $E$ and $F$ be the intersections of line $PR$ with lines $AB$ and $BC$, respectively. If $|DP|=|DQ|=|DR|$, prove that $B$, $E$, $F$ and $Q$ are concyclic.

Show the solution

Solution (R is the incenter of BPQ)

Let $\Omega$ be the circle and $O$ its centre. We write $\measuredangle(\ell,m)$ for the directed angle between lines $\ell$ and $m$, taken modulo $180^\circ$.

Step 1: $BP=BQ$. Since $\angle BPD=90^\circ$, $BD$ is a diameter. The point $D$ of $\Omega$ satisfies $DP=DQ$, so $D$ is the midpoint of the arc $PQ$ on which it lies (this arc does not contain $B$, by the order of the points). Hence $OD\perp PQ$, and the line $BD$ through $O$ is the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$. So $BP=BQ$ and $\angle BPQ=\angle BQP$.

Step 2: $R$ is the incenter of triangle $BPQ$. Let $I$ be that incenter. The bisector of $\angle PBQ$ passes through $D$, the midpoint of arc $PQ$ not containing $B$, and $DI=DP$ (the incenter–excenter lemma). Indeed, put $\theta=\angle IPB=\frac12\angle BPQ$ and $\varphi=\angle PBI=\frac12\angle PBQ$; the angle sum of the isosceles triangle $BPQ$ gives $4\theta+2\varphi=180^\circ$, i.e. $2\theta+\varphi=90^\circ$. As $I$ lies on segment $BD$ and $\angle BPD=90^\circ$, $\angle DPI=90^\circ-\theta=\theta+\varphi$, while $\angle DIP=\theta+\varphi$ as an exterior angle of triangle $BIP$. So triangle $DPI$ is isosceles with $DI=DP$. Both $I$ and $R$ lie on the segment $BD$ (the chords $AC$ and $BD$ meet inside $\Omega$), and since $DB>DP$ there is only one point of that segment at distance $DP$ from $D$. So $R=I$: the lines $PR$ and $QR$ bisect $\angle BPQ$ and $\angle BQP$.

Step 3: $PR\parallel BM$. Let $M$ be the second intersection of line $QR$ with $\Omega$; it is the midpoint of the arc $BP$ not containing $Q$. By inscribed angles and Step 2, \[\angle MBP=\angle MQP=\tfrac12\angle BQP=\tfrac12\angle BPQ=\angle BPR .\] The points $M$ and $R$ lie on opposite sides of line $BP$ ($R$ is inside triangle $BPQ$, $M$ on the arc cut off by $BP$ away from $Q$), so these are alternate angles and $BM\parallel PR$.

No degenerate positions. We check that every line and every angle used below is well defined.

  • $M\ne A$ and $M\ne C$. The point $M$ lies on the arc $BP$ that contains $C$, while $A$ lies on the other arc $QB$; so $M\ne A$. Line $PR$ is parallel to $BM$, so line $BC$ meets line $PR$ (as the statement requires, at $F$) only if $BC\not\parallel BM$, that is, $C\ne M$.

  • $E\ne A$ and $F\ne C$. If $E=A$, then $A$ lies on line $PR$; line $AR$ is line $AC$, which meets $\Omega$ only at $A$ and $C$, so $P\in\{A,C\}$, which is false. In the same way $F=C$ would force $P\in\{A,C\}$.

  • $E,F\ne R$ and $E,F\ne B$. The point $R$ lies on line $AC$, which meets line $AB$ only at $A$ and line $BC$ only at $C$; as $R$ is strictly inside $\Omega$, it is on neither line $AB$ nor line $BC$. And $B\notin PR$, since $R$ lies strictly inside triangle $BPQ$, not on line $BP$.

  • $Q$ is different from $A,C,R,E,F,B,M$: a line meets $\Omega$ in at most two points, so $Q\notin AB\cup BC$, and $R$ is inside $\Omega$.

Consequently the triples $A,E,R$ and $C,F,R$ and $B,E,F$ are not collinear (for instance $A,E$ lie on line $AB$, which does not contain $R$), so each angle equality below is a genuine concyclicity criterion for four distinct points.

Step 4: two circles through $Q$ and $R$. Since line $ER$ is line $PR\parallel BM$, and $Q,R,M$ are collinear, \[\measuredangle(EA,ER)=\measuredangle(BA,BM)=\measuredangle(QA,QM)=\measuredangle(QA,QR),\] the middle step by inscribed angles in $\Omega$. Hence $A,E,R,Q$ are concyclic. In the same way \[\measuredangle(FC,FR)=\measuredangle(BC,BM)=\measuredangle(QC,QM)=\measuredangle(QC,QR),\] so $C,F,R,Q$ are concyclic.

Step 5: conclusion. Using these two circles and the collinearities $A,E,B$; $A,R,C$; $B,C,F$: \[\measuredangle(QE,QF)=\measuredangle(QE,QR)+\measuredangle(QR,QF)=\measuredangle(AE,AR)+\measuredangle(CR,CF)=\measuredangle(AB,AC)+\measuredangle(AC,CB)=\measuredangle(AB,CB),\] and $\measuredangle(AB,CB)=\measuredangle(BE,BF)$. Therefore $\measuredangle(QE,QF)=\measuredangle(BE,BF)$, and $B,E,F,Q$ are concyclic.

(Steps 4 and 5 say that $Q$ is the Miquel point of the four lines $AB$, $BC$, $CA$, $PR$.)

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control · Higher-Order Inclusion-Exclusion

We are given $4052$ cards such that each of the numbers $1,2,3,\dots,2026$ appears exactly twice among them. If the cards are shuffled randomly, determine whether or not the probability that there are two consecutive cards with the same number is at least $0.5$.

Show the answer

Yes (the probability is about $0.632$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two terms of inclusion-exclusion)

Let $N=2026$ and make the two cards with the same number distinguishable; then all $(2N)!$ orders are equally likely. For $1\le i\le N$ let $A_i$ be the event that the two cards $i$ are adjacent.

Gluing the two cards $i$ into one block gives $(2N-1)!$ orders of the blocks and $2$ orders inside the block, so \[\Pr(A_i)=\frac{2\,(2N-1)!}{(2N)!}=\frac1N .\] In the same way, for $i\ne j$, \[\Pr(A_i\cap A_j)=\frac{4\,(2N-2)!}{(2N)!}=\frac{2}{N(2N-1)} .\] Bonferroni's inequality. If an outcome lies in exactly $m$ of the events, then $m-\binom m2=\frac{m(3-m)}2\le1$, and it is $\le0$ when $m=0$. So $\mathbf 1[m\ge1]\ge m-\binom m2$ for every outcome, and taking expectations, \[\Pr\Bigl(\bigcup A_i\Bigr)\ \ge\ \sum_i\Pr(A_i)-\sum_{i<j}\Pr(A_i\cap A_j)=N\cdot\frac1N-\binom N2\frac{2}{N(2N-1)}=1-\frac{N-1}{2N-1}=\frac{N}{2N-1}.\] Since $\frac{N}{2N-1}>\frac12$, the probability that two consecutive cards carry the same number is greater than $0.5$: the answer is Yes. (Its exact value is the full inclusion–exclusion sum $\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-1)^{j+1}\binom Nj\frac{2^j(2N-j)!}{(2N)!}$, obtained by gluing $j$ prescribed pairs; for $N=2026$ it is about $0.632$, close to $1-1/e$.)

Solution 2 (Second moment)

Let $N=2026$, make equal cards distinguishable (all $(2N)!$ orders equally likely), and let $X$ be the number of values $i$ whose two cards are adjacent. The event in question is $X>0$.

Gluing the two cards $i$ into a block shows that they are adjacent with probability $\frac{2(2N-1)!}{(2N)!}=\frac1N$; gluing two pairs shows that two given pairs are both adjacent with probability $\frac{4(2N-2)!}{(2N)!}=\frac2{N(2N-1)}$. Hence \[\mathbb E[X]=N\cdot\frac1N=1,\qquad \mathbb E[X(X-1)]=N(N-1)\cdot\frac{2}{N(2N-1)}=\frac{2(N-1)}{2N-1},\] since $X(X-1)$ counts ordered pairs of distinct values that are both adjacent. So $\mathbb E[X^2]=1+\frac{2(N-1)}{2N-1}=\frac{4N-3}{2N-1}$.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $X=X\cdot\mathbf 1[X>0]$, \[1=\bigl(\mathbb E[X]\bigr)^2=\bigl(\mathbb E[X\,\mathbf 1[X>0]]\bigr)^2\le \mathbb E[X^2]\cdot\Pr(X>0),\] so \[\Pr(X>0)\ \ge\ \frac{2N-1}{4N-3}>\frac12 .\] The answer is Yes.
