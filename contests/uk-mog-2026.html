Problem 1

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 9: Ratios, Proportions, and Percent Change · Proportion and Scaling

This question requires answers only.

A rectangular apartment is divided into four rectangular rooms, by two straight interior walls which are parallel to the exterior walls. The rooms have areas $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ metres squared.

  • (a) Find the value of $D$ when $A=8$, $B=4$ and $C=12$.

  • (b) Find the value of $C$ when $A=7.5$, $B=6.5$ and $D=26$.

  • (c) Find an expression for $C$ in terms of $A$, $B$ and $D$.

  • (d) In this part of the question, $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ have the values found in part (a). Additionally, the length and the width of each room are whole numbers. Let $P$ be the perimeter of the apartment. Find all possible values of $P$.

Show the answer

(a) $D=6$; (b) $C=30$; (c) $C=\dfrac{AD}{B}$; (d) $P=22$ or $P=26$

Show the solution

Solution (Products of the side lengths)

Let the left rooms have width $p$ and the right rooms width $q$, and let the top rooms have height $h$ and the bottom rooms height $k$ (see the figure). Then \[A=ph,\qquad B=qh,\qquad C=pk,\qquad D=qk,\] so $AD=pqhk=BC$.

(c) $C=\dfrac{AD}{B}$ (and in the same way $D=\dfrac{BC}{A}$).

(a) $D=\dfrac{BC}{A}=\dfrac{4\cdot12}{8}=\boxed{6}$.

(b) $C=\dfrac{AD}{B}=\dfrac{7.5\cdot26}{6.5}=\dfrac{195}{6.5}=\boxed{30}$.

(d) Now $ph=8$, $qh=4$, $pk=12$, $qk=6$ with whole numbers $p,q,h,k$. From the first two, $p=2q$. Then $h$ divides $qh=4$, so $h\in\{1,2,4\}$, and $k=\dfrac{12}{p}=\dfrac{12h}{8}=\dfrac{3h}{2}$ must be a whole number, so $h=2$ or $h=4$.

  • $h=2$: $p=4$, $q=2$, $k=3$, and $P=2(p+q+h+k)=2\cdot11=22$.

  • $h=4$: $p=2$, $q=1$, $k=6$, and $P=2\cdot13=26$.

Both satisfy all four areas, so the possible perimeters are $\boxed{22\text{ and }26}$ metres.

Problem 2

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 12: Circle Angles and Cyclic Geometry · Diameter and Semicircle Geometry

  • (a) Triangle $PQR$ has a right angle at $Q$ and $W$ is the midpoint of the side $PR$. Use the diagram below to show that $QW=RW$.

  • (b) Triangle $ABC$ has a right angle at $B$ and $AB<BC$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AC$. Let $D$ be the reflection of $C$ in the line $BM$ (extended beyond $M$).

    Given that angle $DAC$ is $x^\circ$ find, in terms of $x$, the size of angle $BAC$.

    You must show all your calculations and say what geometrical result(s) you used at each step.

Show the answer

(a) proof; (b) $\angle BAC=\left(90-\dfrac x2\right)^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Isosceles triangles around the midpoint)

(a) Complete the triangle to the rectangle $PQRS$ as in the diagram (draw $PS$ parallel to $QR$ and $RS$ parallel to $QP$; the angle at $Q$ is a right angle, so all four angles are right angles). The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so the midpoint $W$ of $PR$ is also the midpoint of $QS$. The diagonals of a rectangle are equal (triangles $PQR$ and $SRQ$ are congruent by SAS), so $QS=PR$ and \[QW=\tfrac12QS=\tfrac12PR=RW .\]

(b) Let $\angle BAC=\alpha^\circ$; since $AB<BC$, the angle opposite the shorter side is the smaller acute angle, so $\alpha>45$.

Step 1. By part (a) applied to triangle $ABC$ (right angle at $B$), $MB=MA=MC$.

Step 2. Triangle $MAB$ is isosceles with $MA=MB$, so $\angle MBA=\angle MAB=\alpha^\circ$, and by the angle sum $\angle AMB=(180-2\alpha)^\circ$. The angle $\angle BMC$ is the supplement (angles on the straight line $AC$): $\angle BMC=2\alpha^\circ$.

Step 3. Reflection in the line $BM$ keeps $M$ fixed and preserves lengths and angles, so $MD=MC=MA$ and $\angle BMD=\angle BMC=2\alpha^\circ$. The points $A$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of the line $BM$ (it passes through the midpoint $M$ of $AC$), and the reflection puts $D$ on the opposite side from $C$, so $D$ and $A$ are on the same side of $BM$. Since $\angle BMA=180-2\alpha<2\alpha=\angle BMD$ (as $\alpha>45$), the ray $MA$ lies inside the angle $BMD$ and \[\angle AMD=\angle BMD-\angle BMA=2\alpha-(180-2\alpha)=(4\alpha-180)^\circ .\]

Step 4. Triangle $MAD$ is isosceles with $MA=MD$, so its base angles are equal: \[\angle MAD=\frac{180-(4\alpha-180)}{2}=(180-2\alpha)^\circ .\] As $M$ lies on $AC$, $\angle DAC=\angle MAD$, so $x=180-2\alpha$ and \[\angle BAC=\alpha^\circ=\boxed{\left(90-\frac x2\right)^\circ}.\]

Solution 2 (The circle on AC and the perpendicular to the mirror)

(a) Complete the triangle to the rectangle $PQRS$ as in the diagram ($PS\parallel QR$ and $RS\parallel QP$; since the angle at $Q$ is a right angle, all four angles are right angles). The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so the midpoint $W$ of $PR$ is also the midpoint of $QS$; the diagonals of a rectangle are equal, so $QS=PR$. Hence $QW=\tfrac12QS=\tfrac12PR=RW$.

(b) Let $\theta=\angle ACB$. The angles of the right triangle $ABC$ give $\theta+\angle BAC=90^\circ$, and since $AB<BC$ the smaller side is opposite the smaller angle: $\theta<\angle BAC$. So $\theta<45^\circ$.

Step 1: a circle. By part (a), $MA=MB=MC$. Reflection in the line $BM$ keeps $M$ fixed and preserves lengths, so $MD=MC$. Hence $D$ lies on the circle with centre $M$ and diameter $AC$.

Step 2: the right angle at $D$. $D\ne C$, since $C$ is not on the line $BM$ (this line meets $AC$ only at $M$). $D\ne A$: otherwise $BM$ would be the perpendicular bisector of $AC$ and $BA=BC$. By the angle in a semicircle (Thales' theorem), $\angle ADC=90^\circ$, and the angle sum in triangle $ADC$ gives \[\angle ACD=90^\circ-x^\circ .\]

Step 3: the angle $BCD$. Let $F$ be the midpoint of $CD$. Since $D$ is the reflection of $C$ in $BM$, the line $BM$ is the perpendicular bisector of $CD$: $F$ lies on $BM$ and $CF\perp BM$. Triangle $MBC$ is isosceles ($MB=MC$), so its base angles are equal: $\angle MBC=\angle MCB=\theta$. This angle is acute, so the foot $F$ of the perpendicular from $C$ lies on the ray $BM$ (with $BF=BC\cos\theta>0$), and in the right triangle $BFC$ the angle sum gives $\angle BCF=90^\circ-\theta$. As $D$ lies on the ray $CF$ beyond $F$, $\angle BCD=90^\circ-\theta$.

Step 4: the angle $ACD$. The points $A$ and $D$ lie on the same side of the line $BC$ as $M$ (for $D$: it is on the ray from $C$ through $F$, and $F$ is on the ray $BM$). Since $\angle BCA=\theta<90^\circ-\theta=\angle BCD$, the ray $CA$ lies inside the angle $BCD$, and \[\angle ACD=\angle BCD-\angle BCA=90^\circ-2\theta .\]

Step 5. Comparing Steps 2 and 4, $x^\circ=2\theta$, so \[\angle BAC=90^\circ-\theta=\Bigl(90-\frac x2\Bigr)^\circ .\]

Problem 3

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 34: Distributions and Stars-and-Bars · Identical Objects into Distinct Boxes

This question requires answers only.

  • (a) You have three red and four blue cubes. The cubes of the same colour are identical.

    This part of the question is about the number of ways to distribute all seven cubes among some boxes, following these rules:

    • A box may be empty (so it is possible to put all seven cubes in one box).

    • The boxes are considered different (so putting all seven cubes in the first box counts as a different solution from putting all seven cubes in the second box).

    • (i) How many ways are there to distribute all seven cubes among two boxes?

    • (ii) How many ways are there to distribute all seven cubes among three boxes, so that no colour appears in all three boxes?

  • (b) The factorial of a positive integer $n$ is defined as $n!=1\times2\times3\times\cdots\times n$. For example, $3!=1\times2\times3=6$.

    In how many ways can we write $10!=abc$, where $a$, $b$, $c$ are positive integers, such that there is no factor greater than one which is common to all three of them?

    Reorderings of $a$, $b$, $c$ count as different solutions. For example, $120\times54\times560$ is not allowed because all three numbers share a common factor of $2$, while $640\times162\times35$ is allowed and counts as a different solution from $162\times640\times35$.

Show the answer

(a)(i) $20$; (a)(ii) $108$; (b) $5184$

Show the solution

Solution (Colours and primes are independent)

The red cubes and the blue cubes can be distributed independently, so in each case we multiply.

(a)(i) The number of red cubes in the first box can be $0,1,2,3$ ($4$ ways; the rest go to the second box), and the number of blue cubes $0,\dots,4$ ($5$ ways). So there are $4\cdot5=\boxed{20}$ ways.

(a)(ii) Distributing $n$ identical cubes among $3$ boxes is choosing nonnegative $n_1+n_2+n_3=n$, which can be done in $\binom{n+2}{2}$ ways (stars and bars); with every box nonempty it is $\binom{n-1}{2}$ ways. A colour must not appear in all three boxes, so \[\text{red: }\binom52-\binom22=10-1=9,\qquad \text{blue: }\binom62-\binom32=15-3=12,\] and the total is $9\cdot12=\boxed{108}$.

(b) $10!=2^8\cdot3^4\cdot5^2\cdot7$. Writing $10!=abc$ means sharing out the exponent of each prime among $a$, $b$, $c$, independently for the four primes; the three numbers have no common factor greater than $1$ exactly when no prime appears in all three. This is the situation of (a)(ii), with the primes as colours: a prime with exponent $e$ can be shared in $\binom{e+2}{2}-\binom{e-1}{2}$ allowed ways, where $\binom{e-1}{2}=0$ for $e\le2$ (three positive parts need $e\ge3$). Hence the count is \[\Bigl(\tbinom{10}{2}-\tbinom72\Bigr)\Bigl(\tbinom62-\tbinom32\Bigr)\Bigl(\tbinom42-0\Bigr)\Bigl(\tbinom32-0\Bigr)=24\cdot12\cdot6\cdot3=\boxed{5184}.\]

Problem 4

ECombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Take-Away and Resource Games

This question is about finding winning strategies in two-player games. We say that a player has a winning strategy if they can win the game no matter what moves the other player makes.

  • (a) In the first game, Players A and B start with a pile of $100$ counters and take turns to remove either $2$ or $3$ counters from the pile. The winner is the player who removes the final counter. If only one counter remains, the result is a draw.

    Player A goes first. Describe the winning strategy for Player B, and explain why this strategy guarantees a win.

  • (b) Players A and B are now playing a different game. They start with a pile of $700$ counters, and take turns to remove $1$, $3$ or $4$ counters from the pile. The winner is the player who removes the final counter. (If there are only two counters left, the current player must remove one counter.)

    Player A goes first.

    • (i) Show that Player B can ensure that, at the end of their first turn, there are either $693$ or $695$ counters remaining.

    • (ii) Show that Player B can win if, at the end of one of their turns, there are $7$ or $9$ counters remaining.

    • (iii) Determine which player has a winning strategy. You should

      • Describe how the winning player should select their moves, which may depend on the other player's moves.

      • Explain why this strategy guarantees that this player will take the final counter.

Show the answer

(a) B keeps a multiple of $5$; (b)(iii) Player B wins

Show the solution

Solution (Target positions)

(a) Player B always removes $5$ minus the number Player A has just removed (so $3$ after a $2$, and $2$ after a $3$). Each round then removes exactly $5$ counters, so after each of B's turns the pile is a multiple of $5$: $95, 90, \dots, 5, 0$. When A is to move the pile is a positive multiple of $5$, so at least $5$; A cannot take the last counter (at most $3$ are taken) and cannot leave exactly $1$ (A leaves $5m-2$ or $5m-3\ge2$). B can always reply, and the round in which A faces $5$ ends with B taking the final counter.

(b) Call a pile size safe if its remainder on division by $7$ is $0$ or $2$. We show that B can always move to a safe pile after A has moved from a safe pile.

(i) From $700$, A leaves $699$, $697$ or $696$. B then removes $4$ (leaving $695$), $4$ (leaving $693$), or $3$ (leaving $693$), respectively.

(ii) If A faces $7$, A leaves $6$, $4$ or $3$: B removes $4$ (leaving $2$), or takes all $4$, or all $3$, and wins. From $2$, A must remove one counter and B takes the last one. If A faces $9$, A leaves $8$, $6$ or $5$: B removes $1$ (leaving $7$, which B wins as just shown), $4$ (leaving $2$) or $3$ (leaving $2$), and wins.

(iii) Player B has a winning strategy: after each of B's turns, leave a safe number of counters. This is possible:

  • If A faces $7m$ with $m\ge1$ and leaves $7m-1$, $7m-3$ or $7m-4$, B removes $4$, $4$ or $3$ respectively, leaving $7(m-1)+2$, $7(m-1)$ or $7(m-1)$. (These moves are legal: the piles are at least $6$, $4$, $3$.)

  • If A faces $7m+2$ with $m\ge1$ and leaves $7m+1$, $7m-1$ or $7m-2$, B removes $1$, $4$ or $3$ respectively, leaving $7m$, $7(m-1)+2$ or $7(m-1)+2$.

  • If A faces $2$, A must remove $1$ and B takes the last counter.

The game starts at $700=7\cdot100$, which is safe, with A to move. A always faces a safe pile, which is never $0$ before B wins; a safe pile of at least $7$ cannot be emptied in one move of at most $4$, and from $2$ A can only remove one counter. So A never takes the last counter. The pile strictly decreases, so the game ends, and it must end with B taking the final counter.

Problem 5

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 51: Counterexamples, Contradiction, and Infinite Descent · Infinite Descent

A cuboid has side lengths $a-x$, $a$ and $a+x$, where $a$, $x$ are positive integers with $a>x$. The main body diagonal of the cuboid (connecting one vertex to the opposite vertex, passing through the centre of the cuboid) has length $d$.

This question asks you to prove that it is impossible for $d$ to be an integer.

  • (a) Write down and simplify an equation relating $a$, $x$ and $d$.

  • (b) Find all possible remainders that a square number can give when divided by $8$. Hence prove that it is not possible for $d$ to be an odd integer.

  • (c) Now suppose that $d$ is an even integer.

    • (i) Prove that $a$ and $x$ must also be even.

    • (ii) Let $a=2a_1$, $x=2x_1$ and $d=2d_1$. Find and simplify an equation relating $a_1$, $x_1$ and $d_1$.

  • (d) Complete the proof that it is impossible for $d$ to be an integer.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Descent modulo 2)

(a) By Pythagoras in three dimensions, \[d^2=(a-x)^2+a^2+(a+x)^2=3a^2+2x^2 .\]

(b) Every integer is $4m$, $4m\pm1$ or $4m+2$, with squares $16m^2$, $16m^2\pm8m+1$, $16m^2+16m+4$; so a square leaves remainder $0$, $1$ or $4$ when divided by $8$. Hence $3a^2$ leaves remainder $0$, $3$ or $12\equiv4$, and $2x^2$ leaves remainder $0$, $2$ or $8\equiv0$. The possible remainders of $3a^2+2x^2$ are therefore $0,2,3,5,4,6$, never $1$. An odd square leaves remainder $1$, so $d$ cannot be odd.

(c)(i) If $d$ is even, $d^2$ is a multiple of $4$. If $a$ were odd, $3a^2$ would leave remainder $3$ on division by $4$ and $2x^2$ remainder $0$ or $2$, giving $3$ or $1$, not $0$. So $a$ is even; then $2x^2=d^2-3a^2$ is a multiple of $4$, so $x^2$ is even and $x$ is even.

(c)(ii) Substituting $a=2a_1$, $x=2x_1$, $d=2d_1$ and dividing by $4$: \[d_1^2=3a_1^2+2x_1^2 .\]

(d) Suppose some cuboid of this kind has an integer diagonal, and choose one with the smallest possible $a$. By (b), $d$ is even, and by (c), $a=2a_1$, $x=2x_1$, $d=2d_1$ with positive integers satisfying $d_1^2=3a_1^2+2x_1^2$ and $a_1>x_1$. So the cuboid with sides $a_1-x_1$, $a_1$, $a_1+x_1$ has integer diagonal $d_1$, and $a_1=\tfrac a2<a$. This contradicts the choice of $a$. Hence $d$ is never an integer.

Solution 2 (Descent modulo 3)

(a) By Pythagoras in three dimensions, $d^2=(a-x)^2+a^2+(a+x)^2=3a^2+2x^2$.

(b) An integer is $4m$, $4m\pm1$ or $4m+2$, so a square is $16m^2$, $8(2m^2\pm m)+1$ or $16m^2+16m+4$: the remainders modulo $8$ are $0,1,4$. Then $3a^2\equiv0,3,4$ and $2x^2\equiv0,2$ (as $2\cdot4=8\equiv0$), so $3a^2+2x^2\equiv0,2,3,4,5,6\pmod 8$, never $1$. An odd square is $\equiv1\pmod8$, so $d$ is not odd.

(c) Let $d$ be even, so $4\mid d^2$. (i) If $a$ were odd, then modulo $4$ we would get $3a^2+2x^2\equiv3+0$ or $3+2\equiv1$, not $0$; so $a$ is even, then $4\mid 2x^2$, so $x$ is even. (ii) With $a=2a_1$, $x=2x_1$, $d=2d_1$, dividing by $4$ gives $d_1^2=3a_1^2+2x_1^2$.

(d) Part (c) already gives a descent by $2$; here is an independent descent using remainders modulo $3$. Suppose $d^2=3a^2+2x^2$ holds with positive integers $a>x$ and an integer $d$, and choose such a solution with $a$ as small as possible.

Squares leave remainder $0$ or $1$ on division by $3$ (as $(3m)^2$, $(3m\pm1)^2=9m^2\pm6m+1$). Reducing modulo $3$, $d^2\equiv2x^2$. If $x$ were not a multiple of $3$, then $2x^2\equiv2$, which is not a square remainder. So $3\mid x$, hence $d^2\equiv0$ and $3\mid d$. Then $3a^2=d^2-2x^2$ is a multiple of $9$, so $3\mid a^2$ and $3\mid a$.

Write $a=3a_1$, $x=3x_1$, $d=3d_1$; dividing by $9$ gives $d_1^2=3a_1^2+2x_1^2$ with positive integers $a_1>x_1$ and $a_1<a$. This contradicts the minimality of $a$, so $d$ cannot be an integer.
