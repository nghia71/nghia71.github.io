Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Some cells ($1\times 1$ squares) in a rectangular grid are colored in such a way that no row nor column contains more than $n$ colored cells, where $n\geq 2$ is a given number. We call a set $M$ of colored cells good if, for any two cells positioned in different rows and columns, there exists a colored cell in the same row as one of the two cells and the same column as the other one. Determine, as a function of $n$, the largest possible cardinality of a good set $M$.

Show the answer

$n^2$

Show the solution

Solution (A line with the most cells of $M$)

Throughout, the two cells in the definition of a good set are cells of $M$.

Example. The size of the grid is not prescribed, so take a grid with at least $n$ rows and $n$ columns. Colour an $n\times n$ block of cells and let $M$ be the whole block. Each row and column contains at most $n$ coloured cells, and for two cells $(r_1,c_1),(r_2,c_2)$ of the block, the cell $(r_1,c_2)$ is in the block, hence coloured. So $|M|=n^2$ is possible.

Bound. Let $M$ be good and non-empty. Call rows and columns lines, and let $d$ be the largest number of cells of $M$ in one line; so $d\le n$. Transposing the grid if necessary, we may assume that this maximum is attained by a row $\rho$; let its cells of $M$ lie in the columns $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_d$. Let \[R^*=\{\text{rows } r\ne\rho:\ \text{the cells }(r,\gamma_1),\dots,(r,\gamma_d)\text{ are all coloured}\}.\] Column $\gamma_1$ has at most $n$ coloured cells and one of them is $(\rho,\gamma_1)$, so $|R^*|\le n-1$. Split $M$ into three parts.

  • $M_1$: cells of $M$ in the columns $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_d$. Each column has at most $d$ cells of $M$, so $|M_1|\le d^2$.

  • $M_2$: cells $(r,\gamma)\in M$ with $\gamma\notin\{\gamma_i\}$ and $r\in R^*$. Row $r$ has coloured cells in the $d$ columns $\gamma_i$, so it has at most $n-d$ further coloured cells; thus $|M_2|\le(n-1)(n-d)$.

  • $M_3$: the other cells $(r,\gamma)\in M$: $\gamma\notin\{\gamma_i\}$ and $r\notin R^*$. Then $r\ne\rho$ (all cells of $M$ in row $\rho$ are in the columns $\gamma_i$), and some cell $(r,\gamma_i)$ is not coloured. The cells $(\rho,\gamma_i)$ and $(r,\gamma)$ of $M$ are in different rows and columns, so $(\rho,\gamma)$ or $(r,\gamma_i)$ is coloured; hence $(\rho,\gamma)$ is coloured. So $\gamma$ is one of the at most $n-d$ columns, other than the $\gamma_i$, in which row $\rho$ has a coloured cell. Each such column contains at most $d$ cells of $M$, so $|M_3|\le d(n-d)$.

Adding, \[|M|\le d^2+(n-1)(n-d)+d(n-d)=d^2+(n-d)(n-1+d)=n^2-n+d\le n^2 .\] Hence the largest possible size of a good set is $\boxed{n^2}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $a,b,c$ be positive real numbers. Prove that \[\frac{a(a-b)}{\sqrt{b^2+1}}+\frac{b(b-c)}{\sqrt{c^2+1}}+\frac{c(c-a)}{\sqrt{a^2+1}}\geq 0.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Removing the products, then AM-GM)

Write $f(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+1}}>0$ and sum cyclically over $(a,b,c)$.

Step 1. $a(a-b)-\frac{a^2-b^2}{2}=\frac{(a-b)^2}{2}\ge0$, so \[\sum a(a-b)f(b)\ \ge\ \frac12\sum\,(a^2-b^2)f(b).\] Step 2. Put $A=a^2+1$, $B=b^2+1$, $C=c^2+1$; then $a^2-b^2=A-B$ and $f(b)=\frac1{\sqrt B}$, so we must show \[\frac{A-B}{\sqrt B}+\frac{B-C}{\sqrt C}+\frac{C-A}{\sqrt A}\ge0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{A}{\sqrt B}+\frac{B}{\sqrt C}+\frac{C}{\sqrt A}\ge\sqrt A+\sqrt B+\sqrt C .\] By AM-GM, $\frac{A}{\sqrt B}+\sqrt B\ge2\sqrt A$, and similarly $\frac{B}{\sqrt C}+\sqrt C\ge2\sqrt B$, $\frac{C}{\sqrt A}+\sqrt A\ge2\sqrt C$. Adding these three inequalities and cancelling $\sqrt A+\sqrt B+\sqrt C$ gives exactly the claim. Hence the original sum is $\ge0$; equality holds for $a=b=c$.

Solution 2 (Cauchy-Schwarz and the rearrangement inequality)

Write $f(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+1}}>0$ and sum cyclically over $(a,b,c)$. We must show $\sum ab\,f(b)\le\sum a^2f(b)$.

Cauchy-Schwarz: \[\sum ab\,f(b)=\sum\bigl(a\sqrt{f(b)}\bigr)\bigl(b\sqrt{f(b)}\bigr)\le\sqrt{\sum a^2f(b)}\ \sqrt{\sum b^2f(b)} .\] Rearrangement: the numbers $a^2,b^2,c^2$ and $f(a),f(b),f(c)$ are oppositely ordered, since $f$ is decreasing on $(0,\infty)$. By the rearrangement inequality, pairing each $x^2$ with its own $f(x)$ gives the smallest sum among all pairings, so \[\sum b^2f(b)=a^2f(a)+b^2f(b)+c^2f(c)\le a^2f(b)+b^2f(c)+c^2f(a)=\sum a^2f(b).\] Combining, $\sum ab\,f(b)\le\sqrt{\sum a^2f(b)}\sqrt{\sum a^2f(b)}=\sum a^2f(b)$, which is the inequality. Equality holds for $a=b=c$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure

Determine all positive integers $z$ such that $z$, $z+7$, and $z+11$ are prime powers (a prime power is a number of the form $p^n$, where $p$ is a prime number, and $n$ is a positive integer).

Show the answer

$z\in\{2,\,16\}$

Show the solution

Solution (Parity, then powers of $3$)

Recall that $1$ is not a prime power, so $z\ge2$.

$z$ is a power of $2$. If $z$ were odd, then $z+7$ and $z+11$ would be even prime powers, i.e. powers of $2$, say $2^u$ and $2^v$, with $2^u=z+7\ge10$, so $u\ge4$. But $2^v-2^u=4$ gives $2^u\mid4$, a contradiction. So $z$ is even, i.e. $z=2^a$ with $a\ge1$.

One of the numbers is a power of $3$. Modulo $3$ the numbers $z,\ z+7,\ z+11$ are $z,\ z+1,\ z+2$, so one of them is divisible by $3$; it is not $z=2^a$. So $z+7$ or $z+11$ is a power of $3$.

Case $2^a+7=3^b$. If $a\ge3$, then $3^b\equiv7\pmod8$, impossible because powers of $3$ are $\equiv1$ or $3\pmod 8$. For $a=2$, $11$ is not a power of $3$. For $a=1$: $z=2$, and $2,9,13$ are prime powers.

Case $2^a+11=3^c$. For $a=1,2,3$ we get $13,15,19$, not powers of $3$; for $a=4$: $z=16$, and $16,23,27$ are prime powers. Let $a\ge5$. Then $3^c\equiv11\pmod{32}$. The powers $3^1,3^2,\dots$ modulo $32$ are $3,9,27,17,19,25,11,1$ and then repeat, so $c\equiv7\pmod8$. Modulo $41$ we have $3^4=81\equiv-1$, hence $3^8\equiv1$ and $3^c\equiv3^7=3^4\cdot27\equiv-27\equiv14$, so \[2^a\equiv14-11=3\pmod{41}.\] But $2^{10}=1024=25\cdot41-1\equiv-1$, so the powers of $2$ modulo $41$ repeat with period $20$ and are $1,2,4,8,16,32,23,5,10,20,40,39,37,33,25,9,18,36,31,21$; the residue $3$ does not occur. Contradiction.

Therefore $z\in\boxed{\{2,16\}}$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 53: Barycentric and Projective-Flavored Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with orthocenter $H$, and let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $AH$. Let $P,Q\in\omega$, $M\in AB$, $N\in AC$ be such that $PQ\parallel MN\parallel BC$. Let $K$ be the midpoint of the segment $BC$, $T=AK\cap\omega$, and $S=PM\cap QN$. Prove that the lines $AS$, $BH$, and $QT$ are concurrent, i.e. they either have a common point or are all parallel.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Replacing $S$. Let $A'$ be the intersection of the line through $P$ parallel to $AB$ with the line through $Q$ parallel to $AC$. The triangles $A'PQ$ and $AMN$ have pairwise parallel sides ($A'P\parallel AM$, $A'Q\parallel AN$, $PQ\parallel MN$), so they are homothetic, and the centre of the homothety lies on $PM$, $QN$ and $A'A$; that centre is $S$. Hence the line $AS$ is the line $AA'$. (We assume, as the problem does, that $S$ exists and $S\neq A$.)

Set-up. Take the foot of the altitude from $A$ as origin, $BC$ as the $x$-axis and the altitude as unit: $A=(0,1)$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$ with $b\ne c$ and $bc\ne-1$ (the angle at $A$ is not right, otherwise $H=A$ and $\omega$ does not exist), and $b\ne0$ (otherwise $H=B$ and the line $BH$ does not exist). Then $H=(0,-bc)$, since $(H-B)\cdot(C-A)=(-b,-bc)\cdot(c,-1)=0$. The circle with diameter $AH$ is \[\omega:\ x^2+(y-1)(y+bc)=0 ,\] and the line $BH$, the perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$, is $cx-y-bc=0$. As $PQ\parallel BC$ and $\omega$ is symmetric in the $y$-axis, $Q=(p,k)$ and $P=(-p,k)$ with $p\ne0$ and \[p^2+(k-1)(k+bc)=0. \tag{$\dagger$}\] The point $A'$. Solving $P+s(B-A)=Q+s'(C-A)$ gives $s=s'=\frac{2p}{b-c}$ and $A'=\bigl(\frac{p(b+c)}{b-c},\ k-\frac{2p}{b-c}\bigr)$.

Slopes through $A$. Write $u=y-1$ and $w=1+bc\ne0$. Then $A$ is the origin of the $(x,u)$-coordinates and \[\omega:\ x^2+u^2+wu=0,\qquad BH:\ u=cx-w .\] The line $u=mx$ through $A$ meets $\omega$ again at the point $Z_m$ with $x(1+m^2)=-wm$. For two such points $Z_m,Z_{m'}$ ($m\ne m'$) the line \[(m+m')\,x+(mm'-1)\,u+w\,mm'=0 \tag{$\ast$}\] passes through both: substituting $u=mx$, $x=-\frac{wm}{1+m^2}$ gives $-\frac{wm}{1+m^2}\bigl(m+m'+m^2m'-m\bigr)+wmm'=0$, and the equation is symmetric in $m,m'$.

Let $m$ be the slope of $AQ$ (it exists, as $p\ne0$), so $Q=Z_m$ and $k-1=mp$. If $b+c=0$, then $K$ is the foot of the altitude, $T=H$, and $A'$ has $x$-coordinate $0$; so $AA'$, $BH$ and $QT=QH$ all pass through $H$. Let $b+c\ne0$. The median $AK$ has slope $\tau=-\frac{2}{b+c}$, so $T=Z_\tau$, and (for $Q\ne T$, i.e. $m\ne\tau$, so that the line $QT$ is defined) by $(\ast)$ \[QT:\ (m+\tau)\,x+(m\tau-1)\,u+w\,m\tau=0 .\] From the formula for $A'$, \[A'-A=\Bigl(\frac{p(b+c)}{b-c},\ mp-\frac{2p}{b-c}\Bigr)=\frac{p}{b-c}\,\bigl(b+c,\ m(b-c)-2\bigr),\] so \[AA':\ u=\sigma x,\qquad \sigma=\frac{m(b-c)-2}{b+c}=m\Bigl(1+\tau c\Bigr)+\tau=m+\tau+m\tau c ,\] because $1+\tau c=\frac{b-c}{b+c}$. Now \[(\sigma x-u)-m\tau\,(cx-u-w)=(\sigma-m\tau c)\,x+(m\tau-1)\,u+w\,m\tau=(m+\tau)\,x+(m\tau-1)\,u+w\,m\tau .\] So the equation of $QT$ is a combination of the equations of $AA'$ and $BH$. These two lines are different ($A\notin BH$ because $w\ne0$); hence $QT$ passes through their common point, or, if they are parallel, is parallel to both. Therefore $AS=AA'$, $BH$ and $QT$ are concurrent or all parallel.

Solution 2 (A projective map from $\omega$ to $BH$)

Replacing $S$. Let $A'$ be the point where the line through $P$ parallel to $AB$ meets the line through $Q$ parallel to $AC$. The triangles $A'PQ$ and $AMN$ have pairwise parallel sides, so they are homothetic with centre $PM\cap QN=S$, and $A,A',S$ are collinear: the line $AS$ is $AA'$. (We assume $S$ exists and $S\ne A$.)

$A'$ depends affinely on $Q$. The circle $\omega$ has its diameter $AH$ on the altitude from $A$, and $PQ\perp AH$, so $P$ is the reflection of $Q$ in the line $AH$. Let $e=\frac{C-B}{BC}$ (a unit vector along $BC$) and $\ell(Z)=(Z-A)\cdot e$; the reflection in $AH$ is $Z\mapsto Z-2\ell(Z)e$. Writing $A'=P+s(B-A)=Q+t(C-A)$ and comparing components along $AH$ (where $B-A$ and $C-A$ agree) gives $s=t$, and then $P-Q=t(C-B)$, i.e. $t=-2\ell(Q)/BC$. So $A'=\Phi(Q)$ for the affine map \[\Phi(Z)=Z-\frac{2\ell(Z)}{BC}\,(C-A).\] Note $\Phi(A)=A$ and $\Phi(H)=H$. The linear part of $\Phi$ fixes the vectors along $AH$ (for them $\ell=0$) and sends $e$ to $e-\frac{2}{BC}(C-A)=\frac{2A-B-C}{BC}=\frac{2(A-K)}{BC}$, where $K$ is the midpoint of $BC$.

If $AB=AC$, then $K$ is the foot of the altitude, $T=H$, and $A'=\Phi(Q)$ lies on $AH$, so $AA'$, $BH$ and $QT=QH$ all pass through $H$. From now on $AB\ne AC$; then $A-K\nparallel AH$, so $\Phi$ is invertible. So $A'$ runs over the conic $\omega'=\Phi(\omega)$, which passes through $A$. The tangent to $\omega$ at $A$ has direction $e$ (as $AH$ is a diameter); its image, the tangent to $\omega'$ at $A$, has direction $A-K$, i.e. it is the median $AK$.

Two projectivities. For $Q\in\omega$ define two points of the (projectively extended) line $BH$: \[f(Q)=QT\cap BH,\qquad g(Q)=A\Phi(Q)\cap BH,\] where for $Q=T$ the line $QT$ means the tangent at $T$, and for $Q=A$ the line $A\Phi(Q)$ means the tangent $AK$ to $\omega'$ at $A$. The map $f$ is the projection of the conic $\omega$ from its point $T$ onto the line $BH$, hence a projectivity. The map $g$ is the affine (so projective) map $\Phi:\omega\to\omega'$ followed by the projection of $\omega'$ from its point $A$ onto $BH$, hence also a projectivity. Two projectivities from a conic to a line that agree at three points are equal. We check three points of $\omega$:

  • $Q=H$: $f(H)=H$ as $H\in BH$ (the projection from $T$ is defined because $T\notin BH$: the only points of $\omega$ on $BH$ are $H$ and $E$; $T\ne H$ because $AB\ne AC$, and $T\ne E$ because $E\in AC$, $T\in AK$ and $T\ne A$); $\Phi(H)=H$, so $g(H)=AH\cap BH=H$.

  • $Q=E$, the foot of the altitude from $B$ (it lies on $\omega$ since $\angle AEH=90^\circ$, and on $AC$ and $BH$): $f(E)=E$; and $\Phi(E)=E+t(C-A)$ lies on $AC$, so $g(E)=AC\cap BH=E$.

  • $Q=A$: $f(A)=AT\cap BH=AK\cap BH$ since $T\in AK$; and $g(A)=AK\cap BH$ by the choice of the tangent.

If $\angle C\ne90^\circ$, the three points $H,E,A$ are distinct: $E\ne A$ and $H\ne A$ as $\angle A\ne90^\circ$, and $H=E$ would put $H$ on $AC$, so the altitude from $A$ would be the line $AC$, i.e. $\angle C=90^\circ$. Then $f=g$. For our $Q$ this says that $QT$ and $AA'=AS$ meet $BH$ at the same point (possibly at infinity): the lines $AS$, $BH$, $QT$ are concurrent or all parallel.

If $\angle C=90^\circ$, then $H=E=C$ and only two test points remain. This case follows from Solution 1, or by continuity: for fixed $Q$ (moving with the triangle) both $f(Q)$ and $g(Q)$ depend continuously on the triangle, and they agree for all nearby triangles with $\angle C\ne90^\circ$.

Problem 5

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n\geq 3$ be an integer. Each cell of an $n\times n$ board is colored so that adjacent cells (sharing a side) are of different colors. A coloring is called nice if, for every cell, no color appears exactly twice or exactly four times among its adjacent cells. Determine, as a function of $n$, the minimum number of colors required for a nice coloring.

Show the answer

$5$

Show the solution

Solution (Diagonal pairs and parity)

The answer is $5$.

Five colours suffice. Number rows and columns from $0$ and colour the cell $(i,j)$ with $(i+2j)\bmod 5$. If a cell has colour $x$, its neighbours have colours $x\pm1$ (vertical neighbours) and $x\pm2$ (horizontal neighbours) modulo $5$. These four values are distinct and differ from $x$. So adjacent cells have different colours, and every colour appears at most once around each cell: the colouring is nice for every $n$ (left part of the figure).

What a nice colouring with at most four colours must look like. Suppose a nice colouring uses at most $4$ colours.

  • An inner cell has $4$ neighbours. They cannot have $4$ different colours (together with the cell's own colour that would be $5$ colours), and no colour may appear exactly $2$ or $4$ times; so one colour appears $3$ times and another once (pattern $3+1$). In particular exactly one of the two opposite pairs (left/right, up/down) is monochromatic.

  • A cell on a side (not a corner) has $3$ neighbours: they all have the same colour, or all have different colours.

  • At a corner the two neighbours have different colours.

Diagonal pairs. Number the rows and columns $0,1,\dots,n-1$ and let $c(i,j)$ be the colour of the cell $(i,j)$. For $1\le x,y\le n-1$ look at the $2\times2$ block formed by the rows $x-1,x$ and the columns $y-1,y$, and put \[\alpha(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if } c(x-1,y)=c(x,y-1),\\ 0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad \beta(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if } c(x-1,y-1)=c(x,y),\\ 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

  • (A) Inner cells. Let $1\le i,j\le n-2$ and let $N,E,S,W$ be the colours of the cells above, to the right of, below and to the left of $(i,j)$. Three of them are equal and the fourth is different. If the different one is $N$ or $W$, then $N\ne W$ and $S=E$; if it is $S$ or $E$, then $S\ne E$ and $N=W$. So exactly one of $N=W$, $S=E$ holds. Now $N=W$ means $c(i-1,j)=c(i,j-1)$, i.e. $\alpha(i,j)=1$, and $S=E$ means $c(i+1,j)=c(i,j+1)$, i.e. $\alpha(i+1,j+1)=1$. Hence \[\alpha(i,j)+\alpha(i+1,j+1)=1 .\]

  • (B) Two side cells. The neighbours of $(0,1)$ are $(0,0),(0,2),(1,1)$; they are all equal or all different, so $c(0,0)=c(1,1)$ holds exactly when $c(0,2)=c(1,1)$: $\ \beta(1,1)=\alpha(1,2)$. In the same way the neighbours $(n-3,n-1),(n-1,n-1),(n-2,n-2)$ of the cell $(n-2,n-1)$ give $\alpha(n-2,n-1)=\beta(n-1,n-1)$.

  • (C) Corners. The two neighbours of a corner are different: $\alpha(1,1)=0$ and $\alpha(n-1,n-1)=0$.

Odd $n$. By (A) with $i=j=k$, $\alpha(k+1,k+1)=1-\alpha(k,k)$ for $k=1,\dots,n-2$. Starting from $\alpha(1,1)=0$ and making $n-2$ steps (an odd number) we get $\alpha(n-1,n-1)=1$, against (C).

Even $n$ (so $n\ge4$; the cells used are shaded in the right part of the figure). We first show that a corner cell has the colour of its diagonal neighbour: \[c(0,0)=c(1,1),\quad\text{i.e. }\beta(1,1)=1,\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad\beta(n-1,n-1)=1. \tag{D}\] The neighbours of the inner cell $(1,1)$ are $(0,1),(1,0),(1,2),(2,1)$, and $c(0,1)\ne c(1,0)$ by (C). Three of the four are equal, so $c(1,2)=c(2,1)=:e$, and $e$ equals $c(0,1)$ or $c(1,0)$; by the symmetry in the diagonal we may assume $e=c(0,1)$. Consider the side cell $(1,0)$ with neighbours $(0,0),(1,1),(2,0)$, and suppose $c(0,0)\ne c(1,1)$. Then these three neighbours have three different colours, all different from $c(1,0)$; with at most four colours, they are all the colours except $c(1,0)$. As $e=c(0,1)\ne c(1,0)$, one of the three has colour $e$. But $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are adjacent to $(0,1)$, and $(2,0)$ is adjacent to $(2,1)$, which have colour $e$: a contradiction. Hence $c(0,0)=c(1,1)$. Turning the board by $180^\circ$ gives the second equality in (D).

Now $\beta(1,1)=1$ gives $\alpha(1,2)=1$ by (B). By (A) with $(i,j)=(k,k+1)$, $\alpha(k+1,k+2)=1-\alpha(k,k+1)$ for $k=1,\dots,n-3$; after $n-3$ steps (an odd number) $\alpha(n-2,n-1)=0$. By (B), $\beta(n-1,n-1)=0$, against (D).

So no nice colouring uses at most four colours, and the minimum is $\boxed{5}$ for every $n\ge3$.
