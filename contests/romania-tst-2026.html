Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ be a non-constant function. Prove that there exist $a,b\in\mathbb{R}^+$ such that \[f(a)+f(b)>2f\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr).\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Walking along a geometric progression)

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \[f(a)+f(b)\le 2f\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr)\qquad\text{for all } a,b>0. \tag{1}\] Since $f$ is not constant, there are $u,v>0$ with $f(u)>f(v)$; put $\delta=f(u)-f(v)>0$ and $q=v/u$. Consider the geometric progression $x_k=uq^k$ $(k=0,1,2,\dots)$, so that $x_0=u$, $x_1=v$ and $\sqrt{x_{k-1}x_{k+1}}=x_k$ for all $k\ge1$. Applying (1) to $a=x_{k-1}$, $b=x_{k+1}$ gives \[f(x_{k+1})-f(x_k)\le f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})\qquad(k\ge1).\] So the differences $d_k=f(x_{k+1})-f(x_k)$ form a non-increasing sequence, and $d_0=f(v)-f(u)=-\delta$. Hence $d_k\le-\delta$ for all $k\ge0$, and \[f(x_k)=f(x_0)+d_0+d_1+\dots+d_{k-1}\le f(u)-k\delta .\] For $k>f(u)/\delta$ the right-hand side is negative, which contradicts $f(x_k)>0$. Therefore (1) fails for some $a,b$, that is, there exist $a,b\in\mathbb{R}^+$ with $f(a)+f(b)>2f(\sqrt{ab})$.

Solution 2 (A value close to the infimum)

The values of $f$ are positive, so $m=\inf\{f(x):x>0\}$ exists and $m\ge0$. Since $f$ is not constant, it takes a value larger than $m$: choose $a>0$ with $f(a)>m$ and put $\varepsilon=\tfrac13\bigl(f(a)-m\bigr)>0$. By the definition of the infimum there is $c>0$ with $f(c)<m+\varepsilon$. Let \[b=\frac{c^2}{a}>0,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \sqrt{ab}=c .\] We claim that this pair works. Indeed, if we had $f(a)+f(b)\le2f(c)$, then \[f(b)\le 2f(c)-f(a)<2(m+\varepsilon)-(m+3\varepsilon)=m-\varepsilon<m,\] which is impossible because $m$ is a lower bound for the values of $f$. Hence $f(a)+f(b)>2f(c)=2f\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr)$, as required.

Remark. This argument is direct: it names the pair $(a,b)$, and it only uses that $f$ is bounded below and not constant.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $n$ and $k$ be positive integers such that $n<k<2n$. Suppose $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ are positive integers such that $2^{a_1}+2^{a_2}+\cdots+2^{a_k}$ is divisible by $2^n-1$.

Show that at least three of $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k$ have the same remainder when divided by $n$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Balanced digits)

Let $M=2^n-1$. Since $2^n\equiv1\pmod M$, we have $2^{a_i}\equiv2^{r_i}\pmod M$, where $r_i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$ is the remainder of $a_i$ modulo $n$. For $j=0,1,\dots,n-1$ let $c_j$ be the number of indices $i$ with $r_i=j$. Then \[c_0+c_1+\dots+c_{n-1}=k\qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j2^j\equiv\sum_{i=1}^k2^{a_i}\equiv0\pmod M .\] Suppose, for a contradiction, that no three of the $a_i$ have the same remainder, i.e. $c_j\in\{0,1,2\}$ for all $j$. Put $e_j=c_j-1\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and \[D=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e_j2^j=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j2^j-M\equiv0\pmod M .\] Now $|D|\le\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}2^j=M$, with equality only if all $e_j$ are equal to $1$ or all are equal to $-1$, that is, only if $k=2n$ or $k=0$. Both are excluded ($n<k<2n$), so $|D|<M$, and a multiple of $M$ of absolute value less than $M$ is $0$: $D=0$.

If some $e_j$ were non-zero, let $t$ be the least such index. Then $D=2^t\bigl(e_t+2e_{t+1}+4e_{t+2}+\cdots\bigr)$, and the bracket is odd because $e_t=\pm1$; so $D\ne0$, a contradiction. Hence all $e_j=0$, i.e. $c_j=1$ for every $j$ and $k=n$. This contradicts $k>n$. Therefore some $c_j\ge3$: at least three of the numbers $a_1,\dots,a_k$ have the same remainder modulo $n$.

Solution 2 (Averaging over the cyclic shifts)

Let $M=2^n-1$, and for $j=0,\dots,n-1$ let $c_j$ be the number of $a_i$ with remainder $j$ modulo $n$; so $\sum_jc_j=k$. For an integer $t\ge0$ write $\langle t\rangle\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ for its remainder modulo $n$; note $2^t\equiv2^{\langle t\rangle}\pmod M$, because $2^n\equiv1$.

For each shift $s=0,1,\dots,n-1$ consider the integer \[N_s=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j\,2^{\langle j+s\rangle}\equiv2^s\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j2^j\equiv2^s\sum_{i=1}^k2^{a_i}\equiv0\pmod M .\] Suppose that every $c_j\le2$. As $j$ runs through $0,\dots,n-1$, so does $\langle j+s\rangle$; hence \[0<N_s\le2\,(1+2+\dots+2^{n-1})=2M,\] and $N_s=2M$ would force all $c_j=2$, i.e. $k=2n$, which is excluded. So $N_s$ is a positive multiple of $M$ smaller than $2M$: \[N_s=M\qquad\text{for every } s=0,1,\dots,n-1 .\] Now add these $n$ equalities. For a fixed $j$, the exponents $\langle j+s\rangle$ $(s=0,\dots,n-1)$ are $0,1,\dots,n-1$ in some order, so $c_j$ is multiplied in total by $1+2+\dots+2^{n-1}=M$: \[nM=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}N_s=M\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j=kM .\] Thus $k=n$, contradicting $k>n$. Hence some $c_j\ge3$, which is the claim.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

Let $ABCDEF$ be a convex hexagon with $AB=CD$, $AF=DE$ and $BC=EF$. The perpendicular bisectors of $AC$ and $BD$ intersect in $X$, while the perpendicular bisectors of $DF$ and $AE$ intersect in $Y$. The rays $AB$ and $DC$ cut in $U$, while the rays $AF$ and $DE$ cut in $V$. Let $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ be the circumcircles of triangles $BCU$ and $EFV$ respectively. Suppose that $X\ne Y$ and that $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are not tangent, nor do they share common interior points. Show that the intersection point of the common interior tangents of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ lies on the line $XY$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Projections on the two lines through U)

Let $O_1,O_2$ and $r_1,r_2$ be the centres and radii of $\omega_1,\omega_2$. The circles lie outside each other, so their common interior tangents meet at the internal centre of similitude \[T=\frac{r_2O_1+r_1O_2}{r_1+r_2},\] the point of segment $O_1O_2$ with $O_1T:TO_2=r_1:r_2$ (the homothety with centre $T$ and ratio $-r_2/r_1$ maps $\omega_1$ onto $\omega_2$). We shall prove the following.

Lemma. $XO_1\perp AD$ and $XO_1=\dfrac{AD}{BC}\,r_1$; moreover the vector $\overrightarrow{O_1X}$ points from the side of line $AD$ containing $U$ to the other side.

Proof. Because the hexagon is convex, $C,D$ lie strictly on one side of line $AB$, so the point $U$ of ray $DC$ lies beyond $C$; similarly it lies beyond $B$ on ray $AB$. Take $U$ as the origin and let $e_1,e_2$ be the unit vectors from $U$ towards $A$ and towards $D$. Put $\alpha=UA$, $\beta=UB$, $\gamma=UC$, $\delta=UD$, so that \[A=\alpha e_1,\quad B=\beta e_1,\quad C=\gamma e_2,\quad D=\delta e_2,\qquad \alpha-\beta=AB=CD=\delta-\gamma>0 .\] In particular $\alpha+\gamma=\beta+\delta$. Since $e_1,e_2$ are linearly independent, a vector $w$ is determined by the two numbers $w\cdot e_1$, $w\cdot e_2$.

The point $X$. $XA=XC$ means $2X\cdot(A-C)=|A|^2-|C|^2$, and $XB=XD$ means $2X\cdot(B-D)=|B|^2-|D|^2$. With $p=X\cdot e_1$, $q=X\cdot e_2$ these read \[2(\alpha p-\gamma q)=\alpha^2-\gamma^2,\qquad 2(\beta p-\delta q)=\beta^2-\delta^2 .\] The pair $p=q=\frac12(\alpha+\gamma)=\frac12(\beta+\delta)$ is a solution, and the solution is unique because the determinant $-\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma$ is not $0$ ($\beta<\alpha$, $\gamma<\delta$). So $X\cdot e_1=X\cdot e_2=\frac12(\alpha+\gamma)$.

The point $O_1$. From $|O_1|=|O_1-B|=|O_1-C|$ we get $O_1\cdot e_1=\frac12\beta$ and $O_1\cdot e_2=\frac12\gamma$.

Hence $w=\overrightarrow{O_1X}=X-O_1$ satisfies \[w\cdot e_1=\tfrac12(\alpha+\gamma-\beta)=\tfrac12\delta,\qquad w\cdot e_2=\tfrac12\alpha .\] Therefore $w\cdot(A-D)=\alpha\,(w\cdot e_1)-\delta\,(w\cdot e_2)=0$, i.e. $XO_1\perp AD$.

For the length, let $O'$ be the circumcentre of triangle $UAD$; as above $O'\cdot e_1=\frac12\alpha$, $O'\cdot e_2=\frac12\delta$. The reflection in the bisector of angle $AUD$ interchanges $e_1$ and $e_2$ and preserves dot products, so the image $w'$ of the vector $\overrightarrow{UO'}$ has $w'\cdot e_1=\frac12\delta$, $w'\cdot e_2=\frac12\alpha$; thus $w'=w$ and \[XO_1=|w|=UO'=\frac{AD}{2\sin\angle AUD}=\frac{AD}{BC}\cdot\frac{BC}{2\sin\angle BUC}=\frac{AD}{BC}\,r_1 ,\] by the law of sines in triangles $UAD$ and $UBC$ (which have the same angle at $U$).

Finally, let $n$ be the unit vector perpendicular to $AD$ pointing from the side of $U$ to the other side; then $n\cdot e_1>0$, because $\alpha e_1=\overrightarrow{UA}$ leads from $U$ to the line $AD$. Since $w\parallel n$ and $w\cdot e_1=\frac12\delta>0$, the vector $w$ is a positive multiple of $n$. $\blacksquare$

Conclusion. Put $\kappa=AD/BC=AD/EF$ (here $BC=EF$ is used). The Lemma gives $X=O_1+\kappa r_1n$. The hypotheses are symmetric under $B\leftrightarrow F$, $C\leftrightarrow E$, $U\leftrightarrow V$, $X\leftrightarrow Y$, $\omega_1\leftrightarrow\omega_2$ (indeed $AF=DE$, the rays $AF,DE$ meet at $V$, and $Y$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AE$ and $FD$). So the Lemma also gives $Y=O_2+\kappa r_2n'$, where $n'$ is the unit normal of $AD$ pointing away from the side of $V$. The diagonal $AD$ of the convex hexagon separates $B,C$ from $E,F$; $U$ lies on the side of $B$ and $V$ on the side of $F$, hence $n'=-n$ and \[X=O_1+\kappa r_1n,\qquad Y=O_2-\kappa r_2n .\] Consequently $r_2X+r_1Y=r_2O_1+r_1O_2$, that is \[T=\frac{r_2O_1+r_1O_2}{r_1+r_2}=\frac{r_2X+r_1Y}{r_1+r_2}.\] So $T$ lies on the line $XY$ (it divides the segment $XY$ in the ratio $XT:TY=r_1:r_2$), which is what we had to prove.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers and the centre of rotation)

Use complex coordinates, a point and its coordinate being denoted by the same letter in upper and lower case, and let the hexagon $ABCDEF$ be labelled counterclockwise. Put $m=BC=EF$.

The point $X$. Let $\omega=\dfrac{d-c}{b-a}$. Then $|\omega|=1$ since $AB=CD$, and $\omega\ne\pm1$ since the lines $AB$, $CD$ are not parallel. Define $x=\dfrac{c-\omega a}{1-\omega}$. A direct check gives \[c-x=\omega(a-x),\qquad d-x=(c-x)+(d-c)=\omega(a-x)+\omega(b-a)=\omega(b-x).\tag{1}\] So $|x-c|=|x-a|$ and $|x-d|=|x-b|$: the point $x$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AC$ and $BD$, hence it is $X$. (So $X$ is the centre of the rotation $z\mapsto x+\omega(z-x)$ taking $A,B$ to $C,D$.)

The centre $O_1$. Write $\omega=e^{i\varphi}$. By (1) the directed angle from line $AB$ to line $CD$ is $\varphi$ (mod $\pi$), i.e. $\angle(UB,UC)=\varphi$. By the inscribed angle theorem (central angle $=2\times$ inscribed angle, as directed angles), $c-o_1=\omega^2(b-o_1)$, so \[o_1=\frac{c-\omega^2b}{1-\omega^2},\qquad r_1=|b-o_1|=\frac{|c-b|}{|1-\omega^2|}=\frac{m}{2|\sin\varphi|}.\] By (1), $c=x+\omega(a-x)$ and $b=x+\overline{\omega}(d-x)$; hence $c-\omega^2b=(1-\omega^2)x+\omega(a-d)$ and \[o_1=x+\frac{\omega(a-d)}{1-\omega^2}=x+\frac{a-d}{\overline{\omega}-\omega}=x+\frac{i\,(a-d)}{2\sin\varphi}.\tag{2}\]

The other side. The same computation with $(B,C,U,X,\omega_1)$ replaced by $(F,E,V,Y,\omega_2)$ (now $AF=DE$, and $Y$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AE$, $FD$) gives, with $\omega'=\dfrac{d-e}{f-a}=e^{i\varphi'}$, \[o_2=y+\frac{i\,(a-d)}{2\sin\varphi'},\qquad r_2=\frac{m}{2|\sin\varphi'|}.\tag{3}\]

Signs. Going counterclockwise round the convex hexagon, the direction of the sides turns to the left; from $\overrightarrow{AB}$ to $\overrightarrow{CD}$ it turns by the sum of the exterior angles at $B$ and $C$, which are the angles of triangle $UBC$ at $B$ and $C$. So $\varphi=\pi-\angle BUC\in(0,\pi)$ and $\sin\varphi>0$. Similarly the direction turns from $\overrightarrow{DE}$ to $\overrightarrow{FA}$ by $\pi-\angle EVF\in(0,\pi)$; as $\omega'=\dfrac{e-d}{a-f}$ is the inverse of the corresponding quotient, $\varphi'=-(\pi-\angle EVF)$ and $\sin\varphi'<0$. Put $\sigma=\sin\varphi>0$, $\sigma'=-\sin\varphi'>0$; then $r_1=\dfrac m{2\sigma}$, $r_2=\dfrac m{2\sigma'}$, and (2), (3) become \[o_1=x+\frac{i(a-d)}{2\sigma},\qquad o_2=y-\frac{i(a-d)}{2\sigma'} .\]

Conclusion. The circles are exterior to each other, so their common interior tangents meet at the internal centre of similitude $t=\dfrac{r_2o_1+r_1o_2}{r_1+r_2}$. Multiplying numerator and denominator by $2\sigma\sigma'/m$, \[t=\frac{\sigma o_1+\sigma'o_2}{\sigma+\sigma'}=\frac{\sigma x+\tfrac i2(a-d)+\sigma'y-\tfrac i2(a-d)}{\sigma+\sigma'}=\frac{\sigma x+\sigma'y}{\sigma+\sigma'} .\] The coefficients are real and add up to $1$, so $T$ lies on the line $XY$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

Consider a $2026\times 2026$ grid. A domino is a pair of unit cells with a common side. A domino covering is a set $\Gamma$ of dominoes, such that each unit cell is covered by exactly one domino in $\Gamma$. Let $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ be two disjoint domino coverings. Show that there exists a domino covering $\Gamma$ such that \[|\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma|+|\Gamma_2\cap\Gamma|\le 2026.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The unused edges form paths; tile the top and bottom rows horizontally)

Let $N=2026$. Consider the graph whose vertices are the unit cells, two cells being joined when they share a side; a domino is an edge, and a domino covering is a perfect matching. Call an edge used if it belongs to $F=\Gamma_1\cup\Gamma_2$ and free otherwise. We look for a perfect matching $\Gamma$ containing at most $N$ used edges.

Step 1: the free edges. Since $\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma_2=\emptyset$, every cell lies on exactly two used edges. An interior cell has $4$ neighbours, a non-corner boundary cell has $3$, a corner has $2$. So in the graph $G'$ of free edges

  • every interior cell has degree $2$,

  • every boundary cell that is not a corner has degree $1$,

  • every corner has degree $0$.

Hence $G'$ consists of the four isolated corners, of cycles through interior cells, and of paths whose two endpoints are non-corner boundary cells and whose other cells are interior. Each cycle has even length, because the cells can be coloured as a chessboard and adjacent cells have different colours.

Step 2: parity of the paths. Call the top and bottom rows the horizontal sides and the first and last columns the vertical sides; a non-corner boundary cell belongs to exactly one side.

Claim. A path of $G'$ has an odd number of cells if and only if one of its endpoints lies on a horizontal side and the other on a vertical side.

Proof. The $4N-4$ boundary cells form a closed ring in which consecutive cells are adjacent, so their chessboard colours alternate along the ring. Let a path $\pi$ have endpoints $u,w$, and let $\rho$ be one of the two arcs of the ring strictly between $u$ and $w$. Draw every path of $G'$ as a polygonal line through the centres of its cells. Extend the polygonal line of $\pi$ at both ends up to the boundary of the board; it then cuts the board into two parts, one containing the centres of the cells of $\rho$ and the other the centres of the cells of the complementary arc. Two different paths have no common cell, so their polygonal lines are disjoint; therefore another path cannot have one endpoint on $\rho$ and the other on the complementary arc (it would have to cross $\pi$). So the non-corner cells of $\rho$ are matched in pairs by the paths of $G'$, and \[\#\{\text{cells of }\rho\}\equiv\#\{\text{corners on }\rho\}\pmod 2 .\] Along a path the colours alternate, so $\pi$ has an odd number of cells iff $u,w$ have the same colour, iff the number of cells of $\rho$ is odd, iff $\rho$ contains an odd number of corners. Finally, $\rho$ contains an odd number ($1$ or $3$) of the four corners exactly when $u$ and $w$ lie on two adjacent sides, i.e. one horizontal and one vertical; if they lie on the same side or on opposite sides, $\rho$ contains $0$, $2$ or $4$ corners. $\blacksquare$

Step 3: the covering $\Gamma$. Since $N$ is even, the top row and the bottom row can each be covered by $N/2$ horizontal dominoes; put these $N$ dominoes into $\Gamma$. They cover the four corners and all the path endpoints lying on the horizontal sides. Now look at the remaining cells:

  • A cycle of $G'$ contains only interior cells and has even length; cover it by every second edge of the cycle.

  • Let $\pi$ be a path of $G'$ with $p$ cells, and delete those of its endpoints that lie on a horizontal side. What is left is again a path (possibly empty), since only endpoints were removed, and by the Claim it has an even number of cells: if no endpoint or both endpoints were removed then $p$ is even, and if exactly one endpoint was removed then $p$ is odd. Cover it by every second edge.

Every cell is now covered exactly once, so $\Gamma$ is a domino covering. All its dominoes outside the top and bottom rows are free edges, i.e. they belong neither to $\Gamma_1$ nor to $\Gamma_2$. Hence \[|\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma|+|\Gamma_2\cap\Gamma|=|F\cap\Gamma|\le N=2026 .\]

Remark. The bound cannot be improved in general: if $\Gamma_1$ consists of horizontal and $\Gamma_2$ of vertical dominoes, both formed inside the $2\times2$ blocks of the grid, then a computer search for $N\le12$ shows that every covering $\Gamma$ shares at least $N$ dominoes with $\Gamma_1\cup\Gamma_2$ (we do not prove this here).

Solution 2 (Doubling alternate boundary edges and splitting a 2-regular graph)

Let $N=2026$. Let $G$ be the graph whose vertices are the $N^2$ unit cells, two cells being joined by an edge when they share a side. A domino is an edge of $G$, and a domino covering is a perfect matching of $G$ (a set of edges such that every cell lies on exactly one of them). Colour the cells like a chessboard; every edge joins a black cell and a white cell, so every closed walk in $G$ has even length. In $G$ an interior cell has degree $4$, a boundary cell that is not a corner has degree $3$, and a corner has degree $2$.

Step 1: a $4$-regular multigraph. Each side of the board is a row of $N$ cells $c_1,c_2,\dots,c_N$, and consecutive cells $c_i,c_{i+1}$ are joined by an edge $e_i$ ($1\le i\le N-1$). Since $N$ is even, the edges $e_1,e_3,e_5,\dots,e_{N-1}$ are pairwise disjoint and together contain every cell of that side exactly once. For each of these $N/2$ edges we add a second, parallel copy, called a new edge (the edges of $G$ are old). We do this on all four sides (see the figure for a $6\times6$ board), adding $4\cdot\frac N2=2N$ new edges. A boundary cell that is not a corner lies on one side and gains one new edge; a corner lies on two sides and gains two; an interior cell gains none. So in the resulting multigraph $H$ every cell has degree exactly $4$. A new edge joins the same two cells as an old edge, so closed walks in $H$ still have even length.

Step 2: delete the two coverings. Delete from $H$ the old edges that belong to $\Gamma_1$ or to $\Gamma_2$; all new edges stay. Since $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ are disjoint perfect matchings, every cell loses exactly two edges. In the remaining multigraph $R$ every cell has degree exactly $2$, and $R$ contains all $2N$ new edges.

Step 3: two perfect matchings. In a multigraph in which every vertex has degree $2$, the edges split into disjoint cycles: start with any edge and keep leaving the current vertex along its other edge; since every vertex has only two edges, the walk can return only to its starting vertex, and it closes up there. (A cycle may have length $2$: an old edge and its new copy, both still present.) Every cycle of $R$ has even length, so we may colour its edges alternately red and blue. Every cell lies on exactly one cycle and meets exactly one red and one blue edge of it. Hence the red edges form a perfect matching, so do the blue edges, and every edge of $R$ is in exactly one of the two.

Step 4: the covering $\Gamma$. The $2N$ new edges are split between the red and the blue matching, so one of them, call it $M$, contains at most $N$ new edges. Replace each new edge of $M$ by the domino formed by its two cells. Since every cell lies on exactly one edge of $M$, the resulting set $\Gamma$ of dominoes is a domino covering.

Step 5: the overlap. Let $\delta$ be a domino of $\Gamma$ that belongs to $\Gamma_1$ or to $\Gamma_2$. The old edge $\delta$ was deleted in Step 2, so $\delta$ came from a new edge of $M$. Different dominoes of $\Gamma$ come from different edges of $M$, hence \[|\Gamma\cap(\Gamma_1\cup\Gamma_2)|\le(\text{number of new edges in }M)\le N .\] Since $\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma_2=\emptyset$, the left side equals $|\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma|+|\Gamma_2\cap\Gamma|$. Therefore $|\Gamma_1\cap\Gamma|+|\Gamma_2\cap\Gamma|\le N=2026$.

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute-angled triangle. Let $I_B$ and $I_C$ be the excenters of triangle $ABC$ opposite $B$ and $C$, respectively. Let $E$ be a point on line $BA$ such that $\angle EI_BB=90^\circ$. Let $F$ be a point on line $CA$ such that $\angle CI_CF=90^\circ$. Let the circle with center $I_B$ and radius $I_BE$ intersect the segment $I_BB$ at $X$. Let the circle with center $I_C$ and radius $I_CF$ intersect the segment $I_CC$ at $Y$. Prove that $BY$ is perpendicular to $CX$.

The excenter of a triangle $ABC$ opposite vertex $B$ is the center of the circle that is tangent to line segment $AC$, to ray $BA$ beyond $A$, and to ray $BC$ beyond $C$. The excenter opposite $C$ is similarly defined.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Tangent lengths and two similar right triangles)

Let $\beta=\frac12\angle B$, $\gamma=\frac12\angle C$ and $t_b=\tan\beta$, $t_c=\tan\gamma$. The triangle is acute, so $\beta,\gamma<45^\circ$ and $0<t_b,t_c<1$. Let $a=BC$ and let $s$ be the semiperimeter.

Two classical lengths. Let the excircle with centre $I_B$ (radius $r_b$) touch line $BC$ at $T_B$. Then $BT_B=s$ and $CT_B=s-a$. Since $BI_B$ bisects angle $B$, the right triangle $BT_BI_B$ gives $r_b=s\,t_b$. Since $CI_B$ bisects the exterior angle at $C$, $\angle T_BCI_B=90^\circ-\gamma$, so the right triangle $CT_BI_B$ gives $s-a=r_b\tan\gamma=s\,t_bt_c$. Thus \[r_b=s\,t_b,\qquad a=s(1-t_bt_c),\qquad\text{and symmetrically}\quad r_c=s\,t_c .\]

The point $X$. In the right triangle $BI_BE$ (right angle at $I_B$, $\angle I_BBE=\beta$) we have $I_BE=t_b\cdot BI_B<BI_B$. So $X$ is the point of segment $BI_B$ with $I_BX=t_b\,BI_B$, i.e. $BX=(1-t_b)\,BI_B$. Let $X'$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $X$ to $BC$. As $XX'\parallel I_BT_B$, the triangles $BXX'$ and $BI_BT_B$ are homothetic with ratio $1-t_b$: \[BX'=(1-t_b)s,\qquad XX'=(1-t_b)r_b=s\,t_b(1-t_b),\qquad X'C=a-BX'=s\,t_b(1-t_c)>0 .\] In particular $X'$ lies between $B$ and $C$.

The point $Y$. In the same way, if $Y'$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $Y$ to $BC$, then $Y'$ lies between $B$ and $C$ and \[CY'=(1-t_c)s,\qquad YY'=s\,t_c(1-t_c),\qquad BY'=s\,t_c(1-t_b) .\]

Conclusion. The right triangles $XX'C$ and $BY'Y$ satisfy \[\frac{XX'}{X'C}=\frac{1-t_b}{1-t_c}=\frac{BY'}{Y'Y},\] so they are similar, with $\angle XCX'=\angle BYY'=90^\circ-\angle YBY'$. Let $t_b=\tan\frac B2$, $t_c=\tan\frac C2$; since the triangle is acute, $0<t_b,t_c<1$. Take $B=(0,0)$, the line $BC$ as the $x$-axis and $A$ in the upper half-plane; choose the unit of length so that $C=(1-t_bt_c,\,0)$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

The sunshine cost of a sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of integers is the largest possible value of \[\left|(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_i)-a_j\right|\] as $i$ and $j$ vary over all integers $1,2,\ldots,100$.

Determine the smallest possible sunshine cost over all sequences $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{100}$ of pairwise distinct integers.

Show the answer

$75$

Show the solution

Solution (Two extreme pairs, and the steps of the partial sums)

Write $S_i=a_1+\dots+a_i$ and let \[m=\min_ja_j,\quad \mu=\max_ja_j,\quad s=\min_iS_i,\quad \sigma=\max_iS_i .\] Let $M$ be the sunshine cost. Since $|S_i-a_j|\le M$ for all $i,j$, \[\sigma-m\le M\qquad\text{and}\qquad \mu-s\le M. \tag{1}\] (In fact $M=\max(\sigma-m,\ \mu-s)$.)

Lower bound: $M\ge75$. The $a_j$ are $100$ distinct integers, so $\mu-m\ge99$. Put $L=\sigma-s$. Adding the two inequalities in (1), \[2M\ge L+(\mu-m)\ge L+99. \tag{2}\] For $i\ge2$ we have $a_i=S_i-S_{i-1}$, a difference of two numbers of the interval $[s,\sigma]$, so $|a_i|\le L$.

Case $L\ge50$. Then (2) gives $2M\ge149$, and since $M$ is an integer, $M\ge75$.

Case $L\le49$. The $99$ distinct integers $a_2,\dots,a_{100}$ lie in $[-L,L]$, which contains only $2L+1\le99$ integers. Hence $L=49$ and $\{a_2,\dots,a_{100}\}=\{-49,-48,\dots,49\}$, so $m\le-49$ and $\mu\ge49$. Moreover $a_1$ is different from all of these, so $|a_1|\ge50$. If $a_1\ge50$, then $\sigma\ge S_1=a_1\ge50$ and (1) gives $M\ge\sigma-m\ge50+49=99$. If $a_1\le-50$, then $s\le S_1\le-50$ and $M\ge\mu-s\ge99$.

In both cases $M\ge75$.

A sequence with cost $75$. Take \[-25,\ 0,\ \ 1,-1,\ 2,-2,\ \dots,\ 24,-24,\ \ 26,-26,\ 27,-27,\ \dots,\ 50,-50,\] that is, $a_1=-25$, $a_2=0$, followed by the pairs $(j,-j)$ for $j=1,\dots,24$ and for $j=26,\dots,50$. These are $2+48+50=100$ distinct integers (all integers from $-50$ to $50$ except $25$). After each complete pair the partial sum returns to $-25$, and inside the pair $(j,-j)$ it equals $-25+j$. So all partial sums lie in $[-25,25]$, while all terms lie in $[-50,50]$; hence \[|S_i-a_j|\le25+50=75\quad\text{for all }i,j,\] with equality for $S_i=-25$, $a_j=50$. The cost of this sequence is $75$.

The smallest possible sunshine cost is $\boxed{75}$.

Remark. For $n=4k$ terms the same argument gives $3k$; for other $n$ the two cases do not balance (e.g. the minimum is $5$ for $n=6$).

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

A circle $\omega$ touches a half-plane $H$ if $H$ does not contain $\omega$ and $\omega$ is tangent to the boundary line of $H$. Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a finite non-empty set of half-planes in a plane satisfying the following three conditions simultaneously:

  • 1. The boundary lines of the half-planes in $\mathcal{H}$ are in general position (no two are parallel and no three are concurrent);

  • 2. For any three half-planes in $\mathcal{H}$ there exists a circle touching them all;

  • 3. No circle touches all half-planes in $\mathcal{H}$.

Prove that there exists a circle touching exactly three half-planes in $\mathcal{H}$.

Show the solution

Solution (Lifting to a cylinder and an ordinary plane)

We take half-planes to be closed. (Any reading under which the clause “$H$ does not contain $\omega$” is not vacuous gives the same meaning.) A circle tangent to the boundary line $\ell$ of $H$ lies in one of the two closed half-planes bounded by $\ell$, so $\omega$ touches $H$ exactly when it is tangent to $\ell$ and lies on the side of $\ell$ opposite to $H$ (meeting $H$ only at the point of tangency).

1. A dictionary. Write each $H\in\mathcal H$ as $H=\{x\in\mathbb R^2:\ n\cdot x\le p\}$ with a unit vector $n=(n_1,n_2)$ (the outer normal) and a real number $p$, and assign to $H$ the point \[\Phi(H)=(n_1,n_2,p)\in\mathbb R^3,\] which lies on the cylinder $Z:\ X^2+Y^2=1$. To a circle $\omega$ with centre $c=(c_1,c_2)$ and radius $r>0$ assign the plane \[\Pi(\omega):\quad Z=c_1X+c_2Y-r .\] The signed distance from $c$ to the boundary line, counted positively outside $H$, is $n\cdot c-p$. Hence \[\omega\text{ touches }H\iff n\cdot c-p=r\iff \Phi(H)\in\Pi(\omega). \tag{1}\]

2. The point set. Let $P=\{\Phi(H):H\in\mathcal H\}$.

(a) No three points of $P$ are collinear, and no three lie in a vertical plane. Two half-planes of $\mathcal H$ have non-parallel boundary lines, so their normals are different: the points of $P$ lie on different vertical lines (generators) of $Z$. A non-vertical line meets the cylinder in at most two points, and a vertical plane meets it in at most two generators; the claim follows.

(b) Every plane through three points of $P$ is $\Pi(\omega)$ for a circle $\omega$. By (a) three points $\Phi(H_1),\Phi(H_2),\Phi(H_3)$ span a unique plane, and it is not vertical, so it has an equation $Z=c_1X+c_2Y-r$ with uniquely determined $c_1,c_2,r$. By (1), a circle touching $H_1,H_2,H_3$ must have this centre $c$ and this radius $r$; by condition 2 such a circle exists, so $r>0$ and the plane is $\Pi(\omega)$ for that circle.

(c) $|\mathcal H|\ge4$ and $P$ is not contained in a plane. A circle touching one given half-plane exists: take any circle tangent to its boundary line on the side opposite to it. For two half-planes, the solutions $(c,r)$ of $n_1\cdot c-r=p_1$, $n_2\cdot c-r=p_2$ form a line in $\mathbb R^3$ with direction $(n_1+n_2,\ 1+n_1\cdot n_2)$, and $1+n_1\cdot n_2>0$ because $n_2\ne-n_1$; so $r$ takes positive values on it and a circle touching both exists. With condition 2 this shows that $|\mathcal H|\le3$ would contradict condition 3. So $|\mathcal H|\ge4$. If all points of $P$ were in one plane, this plane would pass through three of them, hence by (b) it would be $\Pi(\omega)$ for a circle $\omega$, and by (1) $\omega$ would touch all half-planes, contradicting condition 3.

3. An ordinary plane. We use the Sylvester–Gallai theorem: if finitely many points of a plane are not all on one line, then some line contains exactly two of them. (Proof, due to Kelly: among all pairs (point $Q$ of the set, line $\ell$ through at least two points of the set with $Q\notin\ell$) choose one with the least distance $d(Q,\ell)$. If $\ell$ contained three points of the set, two of them, $R_1,R_2$, would lie on the same side of the foot $F$ of the perpendicular from $Q$ to $\ell$, say in the order $F,R_1,R_2$ with possibly $R_1=F$. Then $d(R_1,QR_2)\le d(F,QR_2)<QF=d(Q,\ell)$, because in the right triangle $QFR_2$ the altitude from $F$ is shorter than the leg $QF$. This contradicts the minimality. So $\ell$ contains exactly two points.)

Lemma. Let $P\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a finite set, not contained in a plane, with no three points collinear. Then some plane contains exactly three points of $P$.

Proof. Fix $P_0\in P$ and choose a plane $\Sigma$ not passing through $P_0$ and not parallel to any of the finitely many lines $P_0Q$, $Q\in P\setminus\{P_0\}$. Project from $P_0$: let $Q'$ be the point where the line $P_0Q$ meets $\Sigma$. Since no three points of $P$ are collinear, the points $Q'$ are distinct; they are not all on one line $g$, otherwise $P$ would lie in the plane through $P_0$ and $g$. By Sylvester–Gallai there is a line $g\subset\Sigma$ containing exactly two of them, $Q_1'$ and $Q_2'$. The plane through $P_0$ and $g$ contains $P_0,Q_1,Q_2$; any further point $Q\in P$ in this plane would have $Q'\in g$, which is impossible. So this plane contains exactly three points of $P$. $\blacksquare$

4. Conclusion. By 2(a) and 2(c) the Lemma applies to $P$: there is a plane containing exactly three points $\Phi(H_1),\Phi(H_2),\Phi(H_3)$ of $P$. By 2(b) this plane is $\Pi(\omega)$ for a circle $\omega$, and by (1) the circle $\omega$ touches $H_1,H_2,H_3$ and no other half-plane of $\mathcal H$. So $\omega$ touches exactly three half-planes of $\mathcal H$.

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle such that $\angle A$ is obtuse. Let $D$ and $E$ be points on sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively, such that $BDEC$ is cyclic. Suppose the circumcircle of triangle $ADE$ intersects side $BC$ at two points $X$ and $Y$, where $B,X,Y$ and $C$ lie in that order. Let the circumcircles of triangles $BDX$ and $CEY$ be $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$, respectively. Let $P$ be a point such that $PD$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $PE$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Let $Q$ be a point such that $QB$ is tangent to $\Omega$ and $QC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$. Prove that points $A$, $P$ and $Q$ are collinear.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (All three points lie on the radical axis)

Angles $\angle(\ell,\ell')$ between lines are directed and taken modulo $180^\circ$. Let $\omega$ be the circle through $A,D,E,X,Y$. We show that $A$, $P$, $Q$ have equal powers with respect to $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$.

Step 1: $AX=AY$. Let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $A$. By the tangent–chord theorem and since $B,D,E,C$ are concyclic, \[\angle(t,AD)=\angle(EA,ED)=\angle(EC,ED)=\angle(BC,BD)=\angle(BC,AD).\] So $t\parallel BC$. The diameter of $\omega$ through $A$ is perpendicular to $t$, hence to the chord $XY$, hence it bisects $XY$; thus $AX=AY$, i.e. \[\angle(XY,XA)=\angle(YA,YX). \tag{1}\]

Step 2: $QB=QC$. $QB$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $B$ and $QC$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $C$. Using the tangent–chord theorem in $\Omega$ and in $\Gamma$, and then the circle $\omega$, \[\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(BQ,BX)=\angle(DB,DX)=\angle(DA,DX)=\angle(YA,YX),\] \[\angle(CB,CQ)=\angle(CY,CQ)=\angle(EY,EC)=\angle(EY,EA)=\angle(XY,XA).\] By (1), $\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(CB,CQ)$, so triangle $QBC$ is isosceles: $QB=QC$.

Step 3: $PD=PE$. $PD$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $D$ and $PE$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $E$. Hence \[\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(DP,DB)+\angle(DB,DE)=\angle(XD,XB)+\angle(CB,CE)=\angle(AD,AY)+\angle(BC,AC),\] where we used the tangent–chord theorem in $\Omega$, then $\angle(XD,XB)=\angle(XD,XY)=\angle(AD,AY)$ in $\omega$, and the circle $BDEC$. Similarly \[\angle(ED,EP)=\angle(ED,EC)+\angle(EC,EP)=\angle(BD,BC)+\angle(YC,YE)=\angle(AB,BC)+\angle(AX,AE).\] Subtracting (recall that $AD=AB$ and $AE=AC$ as lines), \[\angle(DP,DE)-\angle(ED,EP)=\bigl[\angle(AB,AY)+\angle(BC,AB)\bigr]+\bigl[\angle(BC,AC)+\angle(AC,AX)\bigr]=\angle(BC,AY)+\angle(BC,AX),\] and this is $\angle(YX,YA)+\angle(XY,XA)=0$ by (1). So $\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(ED,EP)$, triangle $PDE$ is isosceles, and $PD=PE$.

Step 4. The power of $A$ with respect to $\Omega$ is $AD\cdot AB$ and with respect to $\Gamma$ it is $AE\cdot AC$; these are equal because $B,D,E,C$ are concyclic. The power of $P$ is $PD^2$ with respect to $\Omega$ and $PE^2$ with respect to $\Gamma$; the power of $Q$ is $QB^2$, respectively $QC^2$. By Steps 2 and 3, each of $A,P,Q$ has equal powers with respect to $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$. The centres of $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$ lie on the perpendicular bisectors of $BX$ and $CY$, two different parallel lines (the midpoints differ since $B,X,Y,C$ lie in this order), so the circles are not concentric and the points of equal power form a line, the radical axis. Hence $A$, $P$, $Q$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Inversion about A)

Let $\rho^2=AD\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC$ (equal since $B,D,E,C$ are concyclic), and let $\mathcal I$ be the inversion with centre $A$ and radius $\rho$. It interchanges $B\leftrightarrow D$ and $C\leftrightarrow E$.

1. $X$ and $Y$ are fixed, and $\Omega,\Gamma$ are invariant. The circle $\omega=(ADE)$ passes through $A$, so $\mathcal I(\omega)$ is the line through $\mathcal I(D)=B$ and $\mathcal I(E)=C$, i.e. the line $BC$. The point $X$ lies on both $\omega$ and $BC$, so $\mathcal I(X)$ also lies on both, i.e. $\mathcal I(X)\in\{X,Y\}$; but $\mathcal I(X)$ is on the ray $AX$, which does not contain $Y$ (a line through $A$ meets $\omega$ in only one further point). So $\mathcal I(X)=X$ and likewise $\mathcal I(Y)=Y$: \[AX=AY=\rho .\] $\mathcal I(\Omega)$ is the circle through $\mathcal I(B)=D$, $\mathcal I(D)=B$, $\mathcal I(X)=X$, that is $\Omega$ itself; similarly $\mathcal I(\Gamma)=\Gamma$. Moreover $AX^2=\rho^2=AD\cdot AB$ is the power of $A$ with respect to $\Omega$, so $AX$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $X$; similarly $AY$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ at $Y$.

2. Reduction to $QB=QC$. The line $PD$ is tangent to $\Omega$ at $D$ and does not pass through $A$ (the line $AD$ meets $\Omega$ again at $B$). So $\mathcal I(PD)$ is a circle $\delta$ through $A$ which is tangent to $\mathcal I(\Omega)=\Omega$ at $\mathcal I(D)=B$; hence $\delta$ is tangent at $B$ to the tangent line $QB$ of $\Omega$. Likewise $\mathcal I(PE)$ is a circle $\varepsilon$ through $A$ tangent to the line $QC$ at $C$. The point $P'=\mathcal I(P)$ lies on $\delta$ and on $\varepsilon$, and $P'\ne A$; so the common chord $AP'$ is the radical axis of $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$, and it is the line $AP$ (because $A,P,P'$ are collinear). The power of $Q$ with respect to $\delta$ is $QB^2$ and with respect to $\varepsilon$ it is $QC^2$. Therefore \[Q\in AP\iff QB=QC .\]

3. $QB=QC$. The tangents to $\Omega$ at the ends $B$, $X$ of the chord $BX$ are mirror images in the perpendicular bisector of $BX$; these tangents are $BQ$ and $XA$. A reflection reverses directed angles, so \[\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(BQ,BX)=-\angle(XA,XB)=\angle(BC,AX).\] In the same way, in $\Gamma$ (chord $CY$, tangents $CQ$ and $YA$): $\angle(CQ,BC)=\angle(BC,AY)$. Since $AX=AY$, the lines $AX$ and $AY$ are symmetric with respect to the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$, so $\angle(BC,AX)=-\angle(BC,AY)$. Altogether \[\angle(BQ,BC)=\angle(BC,AX)=-\angle(BC,AY)=-\angle(CQ,BC)=\angle(CB,CQ),\] so triangle $QBC$ is isosceles with $QB=QC$. By Step 2, $Q$ lies on the line $AP$: the points $A,P,Q$ are collinear.

Solution 3 (Oblique coordinates and two pairs of tangent lines)

Scale the figure so that $BC=1$. Put $u=CX$ and $v=BY$. The point $X$ is not $B$: the line $AB$ meets the circle $(ADE)$ only at $A$ and $D$. Likewise $Y\ne C$. As $B,X,Y,C$ lie in this order, $BX+YC<BC$, that is $(1-u)+(1-v)<1$; so \[0<u<1,\qquad 0<v<1,\qquad u+v>1 .\]

Coordinates. Take $A$ as origin and write every point $Z$ as $Z=r\,\overrightarrow{AB}+s\,\overrightarrow{AC}$, with coordinates $Z=(r,s)$. Put $U=AB^2$, $V=AC^2$ and $W=\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}$; then $W<0$ because $\angle A$ is obtuse. The dot product of $(r,s)$ and $(r',s')$ is $Urr'+W(rs'+r's)+Vss'$, and $|Z|^2=Z\cdot Z$. From $BC^2=1$ we get $U+V-2W=1$. Then \[B=(1,0),\quad C=(0,1),\quad X=(u,1-u),\quad Y=(1-v,v).\] Let $h=AD\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC$, the power of $A$ with respect to the circle $BDEC$ (the two products are equal by that circle). Then $D=(h/U,0)$ and $E=(0,h/V)$. The power of $B$ with respect to the circle $(ADEXY)$ gives $BD\cdot BA=BX\cdot BY$, that is $U-h=(1-u)v$; similarly $CE\cdot CA=CY\cdot CX$. So \[U=h+(1-u)v,\qquad V=h+(1-v)u,\qquad 2W=U+V-1=2h+u+v-2uv-1 .\]

The two circles. For a circle with centre $O$ and radius $\rho$, the power of a point $Z$ is $f(Z)=|Z-O|^2-\rho^2=|Z|^2-L(Z)+f(A)$, where $L(Z)=2\,O\cdot Z$ is linear in $(r,s)$. For $\Omega$: the power of $A$ is $AD\cdot AB=h$; $B$ lies on $\Omega$; and the power of $C$ is $CX\cdot CB=u$. Writing $L(Z)=\alpha r+\beta s$, these give $U-\alpha+h=0$ and $V-\beta+h=u$, so $\alpha=U+h$ and $\beta=V+h-u=2h-uv$: \[f_\Omega(Z)=|Z|^2-(U+h)r-(2h-uv)s+h .\] In the same way (power $AE\cdot AC=h$ at $A$, $C$ on $\Gamma$, power $BY\cdot BC=v$ at $B$), \[f_\Gamma(Z)=|Z|^2-(2h-uv)r-(V+h)s+h .\] Notice that the whole set-up is symmetric under exchanging $r\leftrightarrow s$, $u\leftrightarrow v$, $U\leftrightarrow V$, $B\leftrightarrow C$, $D\leftrightarrow E$, $X\leftrightarrow Y$ and $\Omega\leftrightarrow\Gamma$.

Tangent lines. Let $T$ be a point of a circle whose power is $|Z|^2-L(Z)+h$, with centre $O$. The tangent at $T$ is $(T-O)\cdot(Z-T)=0$, i.e. $2T\cdot Z-L(Z)=2|T|^2-L(T)$. Since $L(T)=|T|^2+h$, the tangent at $T$ is \[2\,T\cdot Z-L(Z)=|T|^2-h .\]

The point $Q$. Here $B\cdot Z=Ur+Ws$ and $|B|^2=U$, so the tangent to $\Omega$ at $B$ is \[(U-h)\,r+(2W-2h+uv)\,s=U-h .\] Now $U-h=(1-u)v$ and $2W-2h+uv=u+v-uv-1=-(1-u)(1-v)$; dividing by $1-u>0$, \[v\,r-(1-v)\,s=v .\] By the symmetry, the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $C$ is $-(1-u)\,r+u\,s=u$. The determinant of this system is $uv-(1-u)(1-v)=u+v-1>0$, and its solution is \[Q=\Bigl(\frac{u}{u+v-1},\ \frac{v}{u+v-1}\Bigr)\] (indeed $vu-(1-v)v=v(u+v-1)$). Hence $Q$ lies on the line $\ell:\ v\,r=u\,s$, which passes through $A=(0,0)$.

The point $P$. Here $D\cdot Z=hr+\frac{hW}{U}s$ and $|D|^2=h^2/U$, so the tangent to $\Omega$ at $D$ is \[(h-U)\,r+\Bigl(\frac{2hW}{U}-2h+uv\Bigr)s=\frac{h^2}{U}-h .\] Multiply by $\dfrac{U}{U-h}=\dfrac{U}{(1-u)v}$. The coefficient of $r$ becomes $-U=uv-h-v$, the right side becomes $-h$, and the coefficient of $s$ becomes \[\frac{2hW-U(2h-uv)}{(1-u)v}=\frac{h(V-U-1)+uvU}{(1-u)v}=\frac{h(u-v-1)+uv(h+v-uv)}{(1-u)v},\] and since $h(u-v-1)+uv(h+v-uv)=h(uv+u-v-1)+uv^2(1-u)=(1-u)\bigl(uv^2-h(1+v)\bigr)$, this equals $uv-h-\dfrac hv$, where we used $2W=U+V-1$, $V-U=u-v$ and $U=h+v-uv$. So the tangent at $D$ is \[(uv-h-v)\,r+\Bigl(uv-h-\frac hv\Bigr)s=-h, \tag{1}\] and by the symmetry the tangent to $\Gamma$ at $E$ is \[\Bigl(uv-h-\frac hu\Bigr)r+(uv-h-u)\,s=-h. \tag{2}\] The point $P$ satisfies both equations, hence also their difference (1)$-$(2): \[\Bigl(\frac hu-v\Bigr)r+\Bigl(u-\frac hv\Bigr)s=0,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad (uv-h)\Bigl(\frac sv-\frac ru\Bigr)=0 .\] Finally $uv\ne h$: otherwise $U=v$, $V=u$ and $2W=U+V-1=u+v-1>0$, contradicting $W<0$. Therefore $s/v=r/u$ at $P$, i.e. $P$ lies on $\ell$.

So $A$, $P$ and $Q$ all lie on the line $\ell$: they are collinear.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Fix an integer $n\ge 2$. The sets $B$, $C$ and $C_b$ in the statement are all finite, non-empty and consist of positive real numbers. A set $C$ is good for a set $B$ if, for any $b\in B$, there exists a subset $C_b$ of $C$ such that $|\overline{c}_b-b|<|\overline{c}_b-b'|$ for all $b'\in B\setminus\{b\}$, where $\overline{c}_b$ is the arithmetic mean of the numbers in $C_b$. In terms of $n$, determine the smallest positive integer $m$ satisfying the following condition: For every $n$-element set $B$, there exist an $m$-element set $C$ that is good for $B$.

Show the answer

$m=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+1$

Show the solution

Solution (Every other element is enough; a geometric progression needs that many)

The answer is $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$.

Let $B=\{b_1<b_2<\dots<b_n\}$ and $t_i=\frac12(b_i+b_{i+1})$ for $1\le i\le n-1$. A real number $\mu$ is strictly closer to $b_i$ than to every other element of $B$ exactly when $\mu$ lies in the open interval $I_i=(t_{i-1},t_i)$, where $t_0=-\infty$, $t_n=+\infty$. So $C$ is good for $B$ iff every interval $I_1,\dots,I_n$ contains the mean of some non-empty subset of $C$.

Upper bound. Let \[C=\{b_1,b_3,b_5,\dots\}\cup\{b_n\},\] i.e. all $b_i$ with odd $i$, together with $b_n$. This set has $\frac{n+1}2$ elements if $n$ is odd and $\frac n2+1$ elements if $n$ is even, that is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ in both cases. It is good for $B$:

  • if $b_i\in C$, take $C_{b_i}=\{b_i\}$, with mean $b_i\in I_i$;

  • if $b_i\notin C$, then $i$ is even and $i<n$, so $b_{i-1},b_{i+1}\in C$; take $C_{b_i}=\{b_{i-1},b_{i+1}\}$ with mean $\mu=\frac12(b_{i-1}+b_{i+1})$. Since $b_i$ lies strictly inside the interval $(b_{i-1},b_{i+1})$ whose midpoint is $\mu$, \[|\mu-b_i|<\tfrac12(b_{i+1}-b_{i-1})=|\mu-b_{i-1}|=|\mu-b_{i+1}|,\] and every other element of $B$ is even farther from $\mu$. So $\mu\in I_i$.

If a larger number of elements is prescribed, add arbitrary further positive numbers; the set stays good. So for every $B$ there is a good set with exactly $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ elements.

Lower bound. Let $m$ be a positive integer for which every $n$-element set has an $m$-element good set. Choose a real number $K>m$ and let \[B=\{K,K^2,\dots,K^n\},\qquad t_i=\tfrac12(K^i+K^{i+1})=\tfrac12K^i(1+K),\qquad \frac{t_{i+1}}{t_i}=K .\] Let $C=\{c_1<c_2<\dots<c_m\}$ be good for $B$. Consider a non-empty subset $S\subseteq C$ whose largest element is $c_j$. Its mean $\mu$ satisfies \[\frac{c_j}{m}\le\frac{c_j}{|S|}<\mu\le c_j\quad(\text{for }|S|\ge2),\qquad \mu=c_j\quad(\text{for }S=\{c_j\}),\] because all elements are positive and at most $c_j$. Thus all means of subsets with largest element $c_j$ lie in $J_j=(c_j/m,\ c_j]$. The ratio of the endpoints of $J_j$ is $m<K$, so $J_j$ contains at most one of the numbers $t_1,\dots,t_{n-1}$, and therefore $J_j$ meets at most two of the intervals $I_1,\dots,I_n$. For $j=1$ the only subset is $\{c_1\}$, which gives a single mean. Altogether the means of the non-empty subsets of $C$ lie in at most \[1+2(m-1)=2m-1\] of the intervals $I_i$. As $C$ is good, all $n$ intervals are needed: $2m-1\ge n$, i.e. $m\ge\frac{n+1}2$, hence $m\ge\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$.

Therefore the smallest such $m$ is $\boxed{\left\lfloor \tfrac n2\right\rfloor+1}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let $n$ be a positive integer and let $A$ be a finite non-empty set of positive odd integers such that no two distinct elements of $A$ divide one another. Suppose that for any subset $S\subset A$ of size $n+1$, there exist distinct elements $x,y,z\in S$ such that $x\mid y+z$. Prove that $|A|<2^{n^3}$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Halving from below, then a greedy choice from above)

Call a triple of distinct elements $x,y,z$ with $x\mid y+z$ a bad triple with divisor $x$. We must show: if $|A|\ge2^{n^3}$, then some $(n+1)$-element subset of $A$ contains no bad triple.

For $n=1$ this is clear: a $2$-element set contains no three distinct elements, so the hypothesis forces $|A|\le1<2$. Let $n\ge2$ and put \[t=\frac{n^2+n+2}{2}\ \le n^3 .\] (Indeed $n^2+n+2\le2n^3$ for $n\ge2$.) We prove the stronger statement $|A|\le2^t-2$. Suppose, on the contrary, that $|A|\ge2^t-1$.

Three observations. Let $x,y,z\in A$ be distinct.

  • (O1) If $x$ is the largest of the three, then $x\nmid y+z$. Indeed $0<y+z<2x$, so $x\mid y+z$ would give $y+z=x$; but $y+z$ is even and $x$ is odd.

  • (O2) The remainders of $y$ and $z$ modulo $x$ are non-zero (no element of $A$ divides another) and different from $x/2$ ($x$ is odd). If both remainders are smaller than $x/2$, or both are larger than $x/2$, then their sum lies strictly between $0$ and $x$, or strictly between $x$ and $2x$; in both cases $x\nmid y+z$.

  • (O3) If $y<x<z$ and $x\mid y+z$, then $y=x-(z\bmod x)$; so $y$ is determined by $x$ and $z$.

Phase 1 (no bad triple whose divisor is its smallest element). Define sets $A=A_0\supset A_1\supset\cdots$ and elements $u_1<u_2<\cdots$ as follows. If $A_{i-1}\neq\emptyset$, let $u_i=\min A_{i-1}$; split $A_{i-1}\setminus\{u_i\}$ into the elements whose remainder modulo $u_i$ is smaller than $u_i/2$ and those whose remainder is larger, and let $A_i$ be the larger of the two parts. Then $|A_i|\ge\frac12(|A_{i-1}|-1)$, and from $|A_0|\ge2^t-1$ we get by induction $|A_i|\ge2^{t-i}-1$. In particular $A_{t-1}\ne\emptyset$, so $u_1<u_2<\dots<u_t$ are defined. Let $U=\{u_1,\dots,u_t\}$. If $i<j<k$, then $u_j,u_k\in A_i$ have their remainders modulo $u_i$ on the same side of $u_i/2$, so by (O2) \[u_i\nmid u_j+u_k\qquad(i<j<k). \tag{1}\]

Phase 2 (no bad triple whose divisor is its middle element). Now choose $s_1>s_2>\dots>s_{n+1}$ from $U$ greedily. Let $U_0=U$. For $j=1,2,\dots$, let $s_j=\max U_{j-1}$ and \[U_j=U_{j-1}\setminus\bigl(\{s_j\}\cup\{\,s_j-(s_i\bmod s_j):\ 1\le i<j\,\}\bigr).\] At step $j$ at most $1+(j-1)=j$ elements are removed, so $|U_j|\ge t-(1+2+\dots+j)$, and \[|U_n|\ge t-\frac{n(n+1)}2=1 .\] Hence $s_1,\dots,s_{n+1}$ can indeed be chosen. Let $S=\{s_1,\dots,s_{n+1}\}\subseteq U$.

$S$ contains no bad triple. Suppose $x,y,z\in S$ are distinct with $x\mid y+z$, and $y<z$.

  • $x$ is not the largest of the three, by (O1).

  • $x$ is not the smallest, by (1), since $S\subseteq U$.

  • So $y<x<z$. Write $x=s_j$, $z=s_i$, $y=s_l$; then $i<j<l$. Since $y$ was chosen after step $j$, we have $y\in U_j$, hence $y\ne s_j-(s_i\bmod s_j)=x-(z\bmod x)$. This contradicts (O3).

So the $(n+1)$-element subset $S$ of $A$ contains no $x,y,z$ with $x\mid y+z$, contrary to the hypothesis. Therefore $|A|\le2^t-2<2^t\le2^{n^3}$.

Solution 2 (Counting middle divisors, then halving from below)

Call a triple $a<b<c$ of elements of $A$ a middle triple if $b\mid a+c$.

Two observations.

  • (O1) If $a<b<c$ are in $A$, then $c\nmid a+b$: we have $0<a+b<2c$, so $c\mid a+b$ would force $a+b=c$, but $a+b$ is even and $c$ is odd.

  • (O2) For given $b<c$ in $A$ there is at most one $a<b$ in $A$ with $b\mid a+c$: such an $a$ satisfies $a\equiv-c\pmod b$ and $1\le a\le b-1$, and only one number in this range has a prescribed remainder. Hence a subset of $A$ with $M$ elements contains at most $\binom M2$ middle triples (at most one for each pair $b<c$).

If $n=1$, the hypothesis applied to a $2$-element subset $S$ would require three distinct elements of $S$, which is impossible; so $A$ has no $2$-element subset and $|A|=1<2$. Let $n\ge2$, and suppose for a contradiction that $M=|A|\ge2^{n^3}$. Put $t=2^n$; then $t\le M$ and $M\ge 2^8$. We shall find an $(n+1)$-element subset of $A$ containing no distinct $x,y,z$ with $x\mid y+z$.

Step 1: a $t$-element subset without middle triples. Count the pairs $(T,\tau)$ where $T$ is a $t$-element subset of $A$ and $\tau$ is a middle triple contained in $T$. Each of the at most $\binom M2$ middle triples lies in $\binom{M-3}{t-3}$ sets $T$, so the number of pairs is at most $\binom M2\binom{M-3}{t-3}$. Using $\binom{M-3}{t-3}=\binom Mt\binom t3\big/\binom M3$, this equals \[\binom Mt\cdot\frac{\binom M2\binom t3}{\binom M3}=\binom Mt\cdot\frac{3\binom t3}{M-2}.\] Now $3\binom t3=\frac{t(t-1)(t-2)}2<\frac{t^3}2=\frac{2^{3n}}2\le\frac{2^{n^3}}2\le\frac M2\le M-2$ (we used $3n\le n^3$ for $n\ge2$, and $M\ge4$). So the number of pairs is less than $\binom Mt$, the number of sets $T$. Hence some $t$-element subset $T$ of $A$ contains no middle triple.

Step 2: halving from below. Put $T_0=T$. For $j=1,2,\dots$, as long as $T_{j-1}\ne\emptyset$, let $x_j=\min T_{j-1}$ and construct $T_j\subseteq T_{j-1}\setminus\{x_j\}$ as follows. No element $z$ of $T_{j-1}\setminus\{x_j\}$ is divisible by $x_j$ (no element of $A$ divides another), so its remainder modulo $x_j$ is non-zero. As $x_j$ is odd, the non-zero remainders split into the pairs $\{q,\,x_j-q\}$ with $q\ne x_j-q$. For each such pair, keep the elements of $T_{j-1}\setminus\{x_j\}$ in whichever of the two remainder classes has more of them (either one in case of a tie), and discard those in the other class. Let $T_j$ be the set of kept elements. Then:

  • $|T_j|\ge\frac12\bigl(|T_{j-1}|-1\bigr)$, since from each pair of classes we keep at least half;

  • if $y,z\in T_j$ are distinct, then $x_j\nmid y+z$. Indeed $y+z\equiv0\pmod{x_j}$ would need remainders $q$ and $x_j-q$, which lie in different classes of the same pair and are never both kept, or two equal remainders $q$ with $2q\equiv0$, i.e. $q\equiv0$ since $x_j$ is odd, which is excluded.

By induction $|T_j|\ge2^{n-j}$ for $j=0,1,\dots,n$: this holds for $j=0$, and if $|T_{j-1}|\ge2^{n-j+1}$ then $|T_j|\ge2^{n-j}-\frac12$, so $|T_j|\ge2^{n-j}$ as it is an integer. In particular $T_n\ne\emptyset$; let $x_{n+1}=\min T_n$ and $S=\{x_1,\dots,x_{n+1}\}$. Each $x_{j+1}$ belongs to $T_j\subseteq T_{j-1}\setminus\{x_j\}$, whose elements exceed $x_j=\min T_{j-1}$; so $x_1<x_2<\dots<x_{n+1}$, $S$ has $n+1$ elements, and $x_k\in T_j$ whenever $k>j$.

Step 3: $S$ contains no bad triple. Suppose $x,y,z\in S$ are distinct and $x\mid y+z$.

  • $x$ is not the largest of the three, by (O1).

  • $x$ is not the smallest: if $x=x_j$, then $y,z$ are later elements $x_k$ ($k>j$), so $y,z\in T_j$ and $x_j\nmid y+z$ by Step 2.

  • $x$ is not the middle one: then the three would form a middle triple contained in $S\subseteq T$, and $T$ has none.

This contradicts the hypothesis applied to the $(n+1)$-element subset $S$. Therefore $|A|<2^{n^3}$.

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Quadratic solitaire is a single-player game. To start the game, the player chooses two distinct nonzero integers $a$ and $b$ and writes the equation $x^2+ax+b=0$ on a blackboard. On each turn, if the equation currently written on the blackboard has two distinct nonzero integer solutions $x=u$ and $x=v$, then the player erases the equation and replaces it by one of the equations $x^2+ux+v=0$ and $x^2+vx+u=0$ of their choosing. Otherwise, the game ends. Determine all initial choices of $a$ and $b$ such that quadratic solitaire can be played forever.

Show the answer

$(a,b)=(1,-2)$ only

Show the solution

Solution (The constant term can only shrink)

Answer: only $a=1$, $b=-2$.

Suppose the game goes on forever, and let $x^2+a_kx+b_k=0$ $(k=0,1,2,\dots)$ be the equations on the blackboard, $(a_0,b_0)=(a,b)$. The next equation has as its coefficients the two roots of the present one, so by Vieta \[a_{k+1}+b_{k+1}=-a_k,\qquad a_{k+1}b_{k+1}=b_k\qquad(k\ge0), \tag{1}\] and all $a_k,b_k$ are non-zero integers (the roots are required to be non-zero).

Step 1. From (1), $|b_k|=|a_{k+1}|\,|b_{k+1}|\ge|b_{k+1}|$. So $|b_0|\ge|b_1|\ge\cdots$ is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers, hence constant from some index $K$ on. For $k\ge K$ the equality $|b_k|=|b_{k+1}|$ forces $|a_{k+1}|=1$. Thus $a_k=\pm1$ for all $k\ge K+1$.

Step 2. Let $k\ge K+1$, $a_k=\varepsilon$, $a_{k+1}=\varepsilon'$ with $\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\in\{1,-1\}$. By (1), $b_{k+1}=-\varepsilon-\varepsilon'\in\{-2,0,2\}$; as $b_{k+1}\ne0$, we get $\varepsilon'=\varepsilon$ and $b_{k+1}=-2\varepsilon$. Hence $a_k$ has a constant value $\varepsilon$ for $k\ge K+1$, and for $k\ge K+2$ the equation on the blackboard is $x^2+\varepsilon x-2\varepsilon=0$. It must have integer roots, so its discriminant $1+8\varepsilon$ is a perfect square; this excludes $\varepsilon=-1$. So for all $k\ge K+2$ the equation is \[x^2+x-2=0 .\]

Step 3. If the $(k+1)$-st equation is $x^2+x-2=0$, then the roots of the $k$-th equation are its coefficients $1$ and $-2$, so the $k$-th equation is $(x-1)(x+2)=x^2+x-2=0$ as well. Going backwards from $k=K+2$ we find that the initial equation is $x^2+x-2=0$, i.e. $(a,b)=(1,-2)$.

Conversely, $a=1$, $b=-2$ are distinct non-zero integers, and $x^2+x-2=0$ has the distinct non-zero integer roots $u=1$, $v=-2$; the player may replace it by $x^2+ux+v=x^2+x-2=0$, the same equation, and repeat this forever.

The game can be played forever exactly for $\boxed{(a,b)=(1,-2)}$.

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Do there exist a positive integer $N$ and an infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ of positive integers such that \[a_{n+1}=\frac{a_1a_2\dots a_n}{a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n}\quad\text{for all } n\ge N\,?\]

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (The valuations of the partial sums stabilise)

No, such $N$ and such a sequence do not exist. Suppose they did. Put \[S_n=a_1+\dots+a_n,\qquad P_n=a_1a_2\cdots a_n ,\] so that $a_{n+1}S_n=P_n$ for all $n\ge N$.

Step 1: a recurrence. For $n\ge N+1$ we have $P_n=a_nP_{n-1}=a_n\cdot a_nS_{n-1}$, hence \[a_{n+1}S_n=a_n^2S_{n-1}\qquad(n\ge N+1). \tag{1}\] Since $S_{n-1}=S_n-a_n$, this can be written $a_{n+1}=a_n^2-\dfrac{a_n^3}{S_n}$; as $a_{n+1}$ is an integer, \[S_n\mid a_n^3\qquad(n\ge N+1). \tag{2}\]

Step 2: only finitely many primes. Let $n\ge N+1$ and let $p$ be a prime dividing $S_n$. By (2), $p\mid a_n$, hence $p\mid S_n-a_n=S_{n-1}$. By induction, every prime dividing some $S_n$ $(n\ge N)$ divides $S_N$.

Step 3: each valuation is eventually constant. Fix a prime $p$ and write $\sigma_n=v_p(S_n)$, $\alpha_n=v_p(a_n)$. From (1), \[\alpha_{n+1}+\sigma_n=2\alpha_n+\sigma_{n-1}\qquad(n\ge N+1), \tag{3}\] and from $S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n$: $\ \sigma_n\ge\min(\sigma_{n-1},\alpha_n)$, with equality whenever $\sigma_{n-1}\ne\alpha_n$. We claim that $\sigma_n$ is constant for large $n$.

  • (i) Suppose $\alpha_n>\sigma_{n-1}$ for some $n\ge N+1$. Then $\sigma_n=\sigma_{n-1}$, and (3) gives $\alpha_{n+1}=2\alpha_n>\alpha_n>\sigma_n$ (note $\alpha_n\ge1$). So the same inequality holds at $n+1$, and by induction $\sigma_{n-1}=\sigma_n=\sigma_{n+1}=\cdots$.

  • (ii) Suppose $\alpha_n<\sigma_{n-1}$ for some $n\ge N+1$. Then $\sigma_n=\alpha_n$, and (3) gives $\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha_n+\sigma_{n-1}>\alpha_n=\sigma_n$. So case (i) occurs at $n+1$, and $\sigma$ is constant from the index $n$ on.

  • (iii) Otherwise $\alpha_n=\sigma_{n-1}$ for all $n\ge N+1$. Applying the hypothesis at $n$ and at $n+1$ ($\sigma_{n-1}=\alpha_n$, $\sigma_n=\alpha_{n+1}$), (3) reads $\alpha_{n+1}+\alpha_{n+1}=2\alpha_n+\alpha_n$, i.e. $2\alpha_{n+1}=3\alpha_n$ for all $n\ge N+1$. Hence $\alpha_{N+1+j}=(3/2)^j\alpha_{N+1}$ is an integer for every $j\ge0$, so $2^j\mid\alpha_{N+1}$ for all $j$, and $\alpha_{N+1}=0$. Then all $\alpha_n=0$ and $\sigma_n=\alpha_{n+1}=0$ for $n\ge N$.

Step 4: contradiction. By Step 2 the integers $S_n$ $(n\ge N)$ have all their prime factors in the finite set of primes dividing $S_N$, and by Step 3 the exponent of each of these primes in $S_n$ is eventually constant. So $S_n$ is constant for all large $n$. But $S_{n+1}=S_n+a_{n+1}>S_n$. This contradiction shows that the answer is No.

Prove that line $RO$ bisects angle $\angle DRB$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A homothety centred at D and three powers of one point)

Let $m$ be the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, and let $H$ be the circumcentre of triangle $ACD$. Since $\angle ADC>90^\circ$, $H$ lies on $m$ on the side of $AC$ opposite to $D$, which is the side of $B$. We measure heights as signed distances from the line $AC$, positive on the side of $B$; thus $D$ has height $-q$ with $q>0$ and $H$ has height $h>0$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $H$ parallel to $AC$.

Let $\Gamma_1$ be the circle $BXC$ with centre $O_1$, and $\Gamma_2$ the circle $BYA$ with centre $O_2$.

Step 1: $O_1\in DX$, $O_2\in DY$, and $\overrightarrow{O_1H}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{XA}$, $\overrightarrow{O_2H}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{YC}$. By the tangent–chord theorem, the tangent to $\Gamma_1$ at $X$ makes with the chord $XC$ the angle $\angle CBX$, on the side of $AC$ opposite to $B$, i.e. on the side of $D$. Since $\angle CBX=\angle CAD$ and the rays $XC$ and $AC$ have the same direction, this tangent is parallel to $AD$. As $DX\perp AD$, the line $DX$ is perpendicular to the tangent at $X$, so it passes through $O_1$.

The perpendicular bisector of $AD$ passes through $H$ and is parallel to $DX$ (both are perpendicular to $AD$); it passes through the midpoint of $AD$, so in the right triangle $ADX$ it is a midline and passes through the midpoint $N$ of $AX$. Now consider the translation by the vector $\overrightarrow{XN}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{XA}$. It maps the line $XD$ to the parallel line through $N$, which contains $H$; and it maps the perpendicular bisector of $XC$ (the perpendicular to $AC$ at the midpoint of $XC$) to the perpendicular to $AC$ at the point $\tfrac12(X+C)+\tfrac12(A-X)=\tfrac12(A+C)$, i.e. to $m$. The line $XD$ is not perpendicular to $AC$ (otherwise $AD\parallel AC$), so $O_1$ is the unique common point of $XD$ and the perpendicular bisector of $XC$, and its image is the common point $H$ of the two image lines. Hence $\overrightarrow{O_1H}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{XA}$. The statements about $O_2$ follow in the same way (interchange $A\leftrightarrow C$, $X\leftrightarrow Y$). In particular $O_1,O_2\in\ell$.

Step 2: the homothety. Let $G$ be the point where the line $DH$ meets $AC$, and let $\chi$ be the homothety with centre $D$ and ratio $\lambda=DH/DG>1$. It maps the line $AC$ onto $\ell$, and, since $O_1\in DX$, $O_2\in DY$: \[\chi(X)=O_1,\qquad \chi(Y)=O_2,\qquad \chi(G)=H .\] Therefore $\overrightarrow{O_1H}=\lambda\overrightarrow{XG}$ and $\overrightarrow{O_2H}=\lambda\overrightarrow{YG}$. Introduce a coordinate on the line $AC$ with origin $G$, and let $x,y,a,c$ be the coordinates of $X,Y,A,C$. Comparing with Step 1: $-\lambda x=\tfrac12(a-x)$ and $-\lambda y=\tfrac12(c-y)$, that is \[a=-(2\lambda-1)\,x,\qquad c=-(2\lambda-1)\,y. \tag{1}\]

Step 3: $G$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $B$. The power of $G$ with respect to $\Gamma_1$ (which passes through $X,C$) is $xc$, and with respect to $\Gamma_2$ (through $Y,A$) it is $ya$. By (1) both equal $-(2\lambda-1)xy$. So $G$ lies on the radical axis of $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$. These circles have different centres ($\overrightarrow{O_2O_1}=\lambda\overrightarrow{YX}\neq0$) and both pass through $B$; hence their radical axis is the line through $B$ perpendicular to $O_1O_2=\ell$, i.e. perpendicular to $AC$. Thus $BG\perp AC$.

Let $B'$ be the second common point of $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ ($B'=B$ if they are tangent); it is the mirror image of $B$ in $\ell$. If $\beta>0$ is the height of $B$, then $B'$ has height $2h-\beta$, and the power of $G$ with respect to $\Gamma_1$ is \[\beta\,(2h-\beta)=xc=-(2\lambda-1)\,xy. \tag{2}\]

Step 4: the height of $O$. Let $\kappa$ be the circle $BXY$, with centre $O$, and let the line $BG$ meet $\kappa$ again at $B''$ (with $B''=B$ in case of tangency), of height $\beta''$. The power of $G$ with respect to $\kappa$ gives \[\beta\beta''=xy. \tag{3}\] From (2) and (3), $\beta(2h-\beta)=-(2\lambda-1)\beta\beta''$, hence $2h-\beta=-(2\lambda-1)\beta''$, i.e. \[\frac{\beta+\beta''}{2}=h+\lambda\beta''. \tag{4}\] The centre $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of the chord $BB''$, which is parallel to $AC$; so $O$, and therefore also $R$ (as $OR\parallel AC$), has height $\frac12(\beta+\beta'')$.

Step 5: $D,B'',R$ are collinear. The homothety $\chi$ maps the line $BG$ (perpendicular to $AC$ at $G$) onto the perpendicular to $AC$ at $\chi(G)=H$, i.e. onto $m$. It maps a point of height $z$ to a point of height $-q+\lambda(z+q)$; since $G$ (height $0$) goes to $H$ (height $h$), we have $h=(\lambda-1)q$, so the image has height $h+\lambda z$. Hence $\chi(B'')$ is the point of $m$ of height $h+\lambda\beta''$, which by (4) is the height of $R$. As $R\in m$, we get \[\chi(B'')=R .\] So $D$, $B''$, $R$ are collinear and $\overrightarrow{DR}=\lambda\overrightarrow{DB''}$ with $\lambda>1$. Moreover $B''\ne D$: otherwise $R=D$ would lie on $m$, so $DA=DC$ and the whole configuration would be symmetric in $m$ ($X\leftrightarrow Y$, $G$ the midpoint of $AC$, $B\in m$), giving $O\in m$, i.e. $R=O$, which is excluded. Consequently $B''$ lies strictly between $D$ and $R$, and the rays $RD$ and $RB''$ coincide.

Step 6: conclusion. The line $RO$ passes through the centre $O$ of $\kappa$ and is perpendicular to the chord $BB''$. The reflection in $RO$ therefore maps $\kappa$ to itself and the line $BB''$ to itself, so it interchanges $B$ and $B''$, and it fixes $R$. Thus it maps the ray $RB$ onto the ray $RB''=RD$. Hence the line $RO$ bisects the angle $\angle DRB$.

Let the midpoint of $AC$ be the origin, $A=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $D=(p,-q)$ with $q>0$, and $B=(b_1,b_2)$ with $b_2>0$ ($B$ and $D$ are on opposite sides of $AC$). Since $\angle ADC>90^\circ$, $D$ is inside the circle with diameter $AC$: $p^2+q^2<1$. Put \[h=\frac{1-p^2-q^2}{2q}>0,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad 1-p^2=q^2+2qh .\]

The points $X=(x,0)$, $Y=(y,0)$. From $\overrightarrow{DX}\cdot\overrightarrow{DA}=0$ and $\overrightarrow{DY}\cdot\overrightarrow{DC}=0$, \[x=p+\frac{q^2}{1+p},\qquad y=p-\frac{q^2}{1-p},\qquad 1-x=\frac{2qh}{1+p},\qquad 1+y=\frac{2qh}{1-p}. \]

The two circles through $B$. Let $\alpha=\angle CAD$; it is acute and $\cot\alpha=\frac{1+p}{q}$. The point $B$ sees the segment $XC$ under the acute angle $\alpha$, so the centre of the circle $BXC$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $XC$, on the side of $B$, at height $\frac12XC\cot\alpha=\frac{1-x}2\cdot\frac{1+p}q=h$. Thus the circle $BXC$ has the equation \[(X-x)(X-1)+Y^2-2hY=0, \tag{1}\] and in the same way ($\cot\angle DCA=\frac{1-p}q$, $\ \frac{1+y}{2}\cdot\frac{1-p}{q}=h$) the circle $BYA$ has the equation $(X+1)(X-y)+Y^2-2hY=0$. Subtracting, $(b_1-x)(b_1-1)=(b_1+1)(b_1-y)$, i.e. $b_1(2+x-y)=x+y$. Here \[x+y=2p-\frac{2pq^2}{1-p^2}=\frac{4pqh}{1-p^2},\qquad 2+x-y=\frac{2(1-p^2+q^2)}{1-p^2}=\frac{4q(q+h)}{1-p^2},\] so \[b_1=\frac{ph}{q+h}. \tag{2}\] Also, by (1), \[b_2^2=2hb_2-(b_1-x)(b_1-1). \tag{3}\]

The points $O$ and $R$. $O=\bigl(\frac{x+y}2,k\bigr)$, and $OB=OX$ gives $2kb_2=(b_1-x)(b_1-y)+b_2^2$; with (3), \[k=h+\frac{(b_1-x)(1-y)}{2b_2}. \tag{4}\] $R=(0,k)$, and $R\ne O$ means $x+y\neq0$, i.e. $p\ne0$.

Key identity: \[k\,(b_1+p)=p\,b_2-q\,b_1. \tag{5}\] By (2), $b_1+p=\dfrac{p(2h+q)}{q+h}$ and $pb_2-qb_1=\dfrac{p\,(b_2(q+h)-qh)}{q+h}$, so (5) is equivalent to $k(2h+q)=b_2(q+h)-qh$. Insert (4) and multiply by $2b_2$: \[2hb_2(2h+q)+(b_1-x)(1-y)(2h+q)=2b_2^2(q+h)-2qhb_2 .\] By (3) the right-hand side equals $2hb_2(2h+q)+2(b_1-x)(1-b_1)(q+h)$. So it suffices to show $(1-y)(2h+q)=2(1-b_1)(q+h)$. Indeed $2h+q=\frac{1-p^2}{q}$ and $1-y=\frac{(1-p)^2+q^2}{1-p}$, so the left side is $\frac{(1+p)\,((1-p)^2+q^2)}{q}$; and by (2) \[2(1-b_1)(q+h)=2q+2h(1-p)=\frac{2q^2+(1-p)(1-p^2-q^2)}{q}=\frac{(1+p)\bigl(q^2+(1-p)^2\bigr)}{q}.\] This proves (5).

Conclusion. The line $RO$ is the horizontal line through $R$. We have $\overrightarrow{RD}=(p,\,-(q+k))$, and the mirror image of $\overrightarrow{RB}=(b_1,\,b_2-k)$ in the horizontal direction is $(b_1,\,-(b_2-k))$. Their cross product is \[-p(b_2-k)+b_1(q+k)=k(b_1+p)-(pb_2-qb_1)=0\] by (5), so they are parallel; and their first coordinates $p$ and $b_1=\frac{ph}{q+h}$ are non-zero and have the same sign, so they are positive multiples of each other. Hence the ray $RD$ is the mirror image of the ray $RB$ in the line $RO$, i.e. $RO$ bisects $\angle DRB$.
