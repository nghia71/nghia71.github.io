Problem 1

MAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 49: Systems of Equations and Multiple Constraints · Partial Totals and Cyclic Sums

Alice has a set of $25$ weights, with masses $a_1,\dots,a_{25}$; Bob also has a set of $25$ weights, with masses $a_1+a_2,\ a_2+a_3,\ \dots,\ a_{25}+a_1$. Bob was able to put all his weights on the two pans of a balance scale so that the pans are balanced. Prove that Alice can add some of her weights (at least one) to each pan so that the pans are balanced again.

Show the solution – Count how often each of Alice's weights is used

Put $a_{26}=a_1$ and $a_0=a_{25}$, and let $b_i=a_i+a_{i+1}$ $(i=1,\dots,25)$ be Bob's weights. Write $\varepsilon_i=+1$ if $b_i$ lies on the left pan and $\varepsilon_i=-1$ if it lies on the right pan; put $\varepsilon_0=\varepsilon_{25}$. The pans are balanced, so \[\sum_{i=1}^{25}\varepsilon_i\,(a_i+a_{i+1})=0 .\] The mass $a_i$ occurs in exactly two of Bob's weights, namely $b_{i-1}$ and $b_i$. Collecting the terms with $a_i$ we get \[\sum_{i=1}^{25}c_i\,a_i=0,\qquad c_i=\varepsilon_{i-1}+\varepsilon_i\in\{-2,0,2\}.\] Here $c_i=2$ exactly when $b_{i-1}$ and $b_i$ are both on the left pan, and $c_i=-2$ exactly when both are on the right pan. Let \[X=\{i: c_i=2\},\qquad Y=\{i: c_i=-2\}.\] Then $2\sum_{i\in X}a_i-2\sum_{i\in Y}a_i=0$, that is, \[\sum_{i\in X}a_i=\sum_{i\in Y}a_i .\tag{1}\]

$X$ and $Y$ are not empty. Go once around the cycle $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\dots,\varepsilon_{25},\varepsilon_1$. If neighbouring signs were always different, the signs would alternate around a cycle of odd length $25$, which is impossible. Hence $\varepsilon_{i-1}=\varepsilon_i$ for some $i$, so $c_i=\pm2$ and $X\cup Y\neq\varnothing$. All masses are positive, so if one side of (1) is a nonempty sum, it is positive, and the other side must be a nonempty sum too. Thus both $X$ and $Y$ are nonempty.

Alice's move. The sets $X$ and $Y$ are disjoint. Alice puts the weights $a_i$ with $i\in X$ on one pan and the weights $a_i$ with $i\in Y$ on the other pan. Each pan receives at least one weight, the added masses are equal by (1), and Bob's weights were balanced, so the pans are balanced again.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Midsegments and Internal Frameworks

The diagonals of parallelogram $ABCD$ intersect at point $O$. A point $M$ is chosen on the side $BC$ so that $AM=DM$. Prove that the length of segment $OM$ is equal to half of one of the sides of the parallelogram.

Show the answer

proof ($OM=\tfrac12 AB$)

Show solution 1 – A reflection and a midline

We prove that $OM=\tfrac12AB$.

Since $AM=DM$, the point $M$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $\ell$ of $AD$; let $N$ be the midpoint of $AD$, so $\ell$ is the line $MN$ and $\ell\perp AD$. Because $BC\parallel AD$, also $\ell\perp BC$.

Let $s$ be the reflection in $\ell$. It swaps $A$ and $D$, and it maps the line $BC$ (which is perpendicular to $\ell$) onto itself. Let $B'=s(B)$. Then $B'$ lies on line $BC$, the point $M=\ell\cap BC$ is the midpoint of $BB'$, and the reflection maps segment $AB$ onto segment $DB'$, so $DB'=AB$.

If $B'\neq B$, consider triangle $BB'D$. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so $O$ is the midpoint of $BD$; and $M$ is the midpoint of $BB'$. Hence $OM$ is a midline of triangle $BB'D$ and \[OM=\tfrac12\,DB'=\tfrac12\,AB .\] If $B'=B$, then $M=B$ and $AB=DB$ (because $AM=DM$); then $OM=OB=\tfrac12BD=\tfrac12AB$. In all cases $OM$ is half of the side $AB$.

Show solution 2 – Coordinates

Choose coordinates with $A=(0,0)$, $D=(2d,0)$ and $B=(p,q)$, where $d>0$ and $q\neq0$. Then $C=B+D-A=(p+2d,\,q)$, and $O$, the common midpoint of the diagonals, is \[O=\Bigl(\tfrac{p+2d}{2},\,\tfrac q2\Bigr).\] The points equidistant from $A$ and $D$ form the line $x=d$, and side $BC$ lies on the line $y=q$; so $M=(d,q)$. Therefore \[\overrightarrow{OM}=\Bigl(d-\tfrac{p+2d}{2},\ q-\tfrac q2\Bigr)=\Bigl(-\tfrac p2,\ \tfrac q2\Bigr),\qquad OM=\tfrac12\sqrt{p^2+q^2}=\tfrac12\,AB .\] So $OM$ equals half of the side $AB$ of the parallelogram.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 53: Sequences, Series, and Recursive Patterns · Series and Summation Patterns

Alice computed the sum of some $n$ consecutive positive integers, and Bob computed the sum of the next $n$ consecutive integers (immediately following Alice's). Can Bob's sum be divisible by Alice's sum if

  • (a) $n=2025$;

  • (b) $n=2026$?

Show the answer

(a) Yes; (b) No

Show solution 1 – The quotient is less than 3

Let Alice's numbers be $a,a+1,\dots,a+n-1$ with $a\ge1$, and let \[S_A=na+\frac{n(n-1)}2\] be her sum. Each of Bob's numbers is exactly $n$ larger than the corresponding number of Alice ($a+n,\dots,a+2n-1$), so Bob's sum is $S_B=S_A+n^2$. Hence $S_A\mid S_B$ if and only if $S_A\mid n^2$.

Since $a\ge1$, we have $S_A\ge 1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2>\frac{n^2}2$, so $0<\frac{n^2}{S_A}<2$. If $S_A\mid n^2$, the quotient is the integer $1$, that is, $S_A=n^2$: \[na+\frac{n(n-1)}2=n^2\iff a=\frac{n+1}2 .\] So Bob's sum is divisible by Alice's sum exactly when $n$ is odd and Alice starts at $a=\frac{n+1}2$.

(a) For $n=2025$ take $a=1013$: Alice adds $1013,\dots,3037$ and gets $S_A=2025\cdot2025$, Bob gets $S_B=2\cdot 2025^2$, which is divisible by $S_A$. The answer is Yes.

(b) For $n=2026$ the number $\frac{n+1}2$ is not an integer, so this is impossible: No.

Show solution 2 – Divisors of $2n$

Let Alice's numbers be $a,\dots,a+n-1$ with $a\ge1$. Her sum is $S_A=\frac{n(2a+n-1)}2$, and Bob's numbers $a+n,\dots,a+2n-1$ give $S_B=S_A+n^2$. Thus $S_A\mid S_B$ if and only if $S_A\mid n^2$, that is, \[\frac{n(2a+n-1)}2\ \Bigm|\ n^2\iff n(2a+n-1)\mid 2n^2\iff (2a+n-1)\mid 2n .\] Put $m=2a+n-1$; since $a\ge1$, $m\ge n+1$.

(a) $n=2025$: $m$ must be a divisor of $4050$ with $m\ge 2026>\frac{4050}2$. The only such divisor is $4050$ itself, which gives $2a+2024=4050$, $a=1013$. Indeed then $S_A=2025\cdot2025$ and $S_B=2\cdot2025^2$. Yes.

(b) $n=2026$: $m=2a+2025$ is odd and divides $4052=4\cdot1013$, where $1013$ is prime. The odd divisors of $4052$ are $1$ and $1013$, both smaller than $n+1=2027\le m$. No.

Problem 4

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 46: Combinatorial Games and Strategy · Symmetry Strategies

Two players take turns placing dominoes ($2\times1$ rectangles) on a $5\times10$ grid ($5$ rows, $10$ columns). The first player always places dominoes vertically, and the second player always places them horizontally. The dominoes must follow the grid lines and cannot overlap. The player who cannot make a move loses. Which player can guarantee a win, no matter how the opponent plays?

Show the answer

the second player (the one placing horizontal dominoes)

Show solution 1 – Answer inside $5 imes2$ blocks

The second player wins. Number the rows $1,\dots,5$ from the top. Cut the board into five blocks of size $5\times2$ (columns $1$–$2$, $3$–$4$, …, $9$–$10$). A vertical domino always lies in one column, hence inside one block. The second player answers every move of the first player by a horizontal domino in the same block, covering a whole row of that block. We show that such an answer always exists; then the second player always has a move, the game is finite, so the first player is the one who eventually cannot move.

Since the second player only plays in a block after the first player has played there, it suffices to look at one block, with columns $c$ and $c'$. Let the first vertical domino in the block cover rows $r,r+1$ of column $c$.

  • $r=1$. The second player covers row $4$. Now column $c$ has free cells only in rows $3$ and $5$, which are not adjacent; column $c'$ has free cells in rows $1,2,3,5$. So the next vertical domino in this block (if any) lies in column $c'$ inside rows $1$–$3$. Row $5$ is then still free, and the second player covers it. Afterwards column $c$ has only row $3$ free and column $c'$ has only one free cell among rows $1$–$3$, so no vertical domino fits in the block any more.

  • $r=4$ is the same with the rows read upwards: answer row $2$, and after the next vertical domino (inside rows $3$–$5$ of column $c'$) answer row $1$.

  • $r=2$. The second player covers row $5$. Column $c$ has free cells only in rows $1$ and $4$ (not adjacent); column $c'$ has rows $1$–$4$ free. The next vertical domino lies in column $c'$ within rows $1$–$4$; it meets at most one of rows $1$ and $4$, so one of these rows is still free in both columns, and the second player covers it. Now column $c'$ has exactly one free cell among rows $1$–$4$ ($4$ cells, $2$ taken by the domino and $1$ by the answer) and column $c$ has at most the non-adjacent rows $1$, $4$, so no vertical domino fits any more.

  • $r=3$ is the same with the rows read upwards: answer row $1$, then row $2$ or row $5$.

So in every block the first player can make at most two moves, and each of them can be answered. Hence the second player can always move, and the first player loses.

Show solution 2 – Copy the move in the transposed square

The second player wins. Cut the board into two $5\times5$ squares $S_1$ (columns $1$–$5$) and $S_2$ (columns $6$–$10$), and write a cell as (row, column) with rows $1,\dots,5$. Define a pairing of the cells: \[\sigma(r,c)=(c,\ r+5)\ \text{ for } (r,c)\in S_1,\qquad \sigma(r,c+5)=(c,\ r)\ \text{ for } (r,c+5)\in S_2\quad(1\le r,c\le5).\] Then $\sigma(\sigma(x))=x$ for every cell $x$, and $\sigma$ maps each square onto the other one (it is the transposition of the square, followed by moving it to the other square). A vertical domino $\{(r,c),(r+1,c)\}$ in $S_1$ goes to $\{(c,r+5),(c,r+6)\}$, a horizontal domino in $S_2$; similarly a vertical domino in $S_2$ goes to a horizontal domino in $S_1$.

Strategy of the second player: whenever the first player places a vertical domino $V$, answer with the horizontal domino $\sigma(V)$.

Why the answer is always legal. We claim that after each answer the set of covered cells is mapped onto itself by $\sigma$. This is true at the start (no cells covered). Suppose it is true and the first player covers the free cells of $V$. The cells of $\sigma(V)$ are free: if a cell $\sigma(x)$ with $x\in V$ were covered, then by the symmetry of the covered set the cell $x=\sigma(\sigma(x))$ would be covered too, which is false. Also $\sigma(V)$ does not overlap $V$, because they lie in different squares. So the answer can be made, and after it the covered set (old set, plus $V$, plus $\sigma(V)$) is again symmetric.

Thus the second player can always answer; since the game is finite, the first player is eventually unable to move and loses.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 22: Pigeonhole Principle and Guaranteed Existence · Worst-Case Thresholds

  • (a) Several boxes, each of weight at most $1$ ton, were delivered by two boats, each with a capacity of $12$ tons. Prove that these boxes can be transferred to $7$ trucks, each with a capacity of $4$ tons.

  • (b) Several boxes, each of weight at most $1$ ton, were delivered by $7$ trucks, each with a capacity of $4$ tons. We need to load these boxes onto two boats, each with a capacity of $W$ tons. What is the smallest $W$ for which this is always possible? ($W$ need not be an integer.)

Show the answer

(a) proof; (b) $W=14.4$ tons

Show the solution – Cutting a line of boxes; splitting one truck

(a) Take the first boat and put its boxes in a row along the segment $[0,w]$ of the number line, where $w\le12$ is their total weight: the boxes occupy consecutive intervals whose lengths are their weights. Load into truck $1$ the boxes whose intervals lie inside $[0,4]$, into truck $2$ those inside $[4,8]$ and into truck $3$ those inside $[8,12]$. Each of these trucks carries at most $4$ tons, because its boxes occupy disjoint intervals inside a segment of length $4$. A box that is not loaded contains the point $4$ or the point $8$ in the interior of its interval; the intervals do not overlap, so there are at most two such boxes. Do the same with the second boat and trucks $4,5,6$. At most $4$ boxes are left, each of weight at most $1$ ton, so truck $7$ can take all of them.

(b) The answer is $W=14.4$.

$W$ cannot be smaller. Let there be $35$ boxes of $0.8$ ton, five in each truck ($5\cdot0.8=4$). One of the two boats receives at least $18$ boxes, that is, at least $18\cdot0.8=14.4$ tons.

$W=14.4$ always suffices. First we show:

Lemma. The boxes of one truck can be split into two groups of at most $2.4$ tons each.

Let the boxes of the truck weigh $w_1\ge w_2\ge\dots\ge w_m$, with total $T\le4$. If $T\le2.4$ there is nothing to do. Otherwise let $s_j=w_1+\dots+w_j$ be the largest prefix sum with $s_j\le2.4$; then $j<m$ and $s_{j+1}>2.4$. Since $w_1+w_2\le2\le2.4$, we have $j\ge2$. We claim $s_j>1.6$:

  • if $w_{j+1}\le0.8$, then $s_j=s_{j+1}-w_{j+1}>2.4-0.8=1.6$;

  • if $w_{j+1}>0.8$, then $w_1\ge w_2\ge w_{j+1}>0.8$, so $s_j\ge w_1+w_2>1.6$.

Hence the first group $w_1,\dots,w_j$ weighs at most $2.4$ and the second group, the rest, weighs $T-s_j<4-1.6=2.4$.

Now load the boxes of trucks $1,2,3$ and the first group of truck $7$ into the first boat, and the boxes of trucks $4,5,6$ and the second group of truck $7$ into the second boat. Each boat gets at most $3\cdot4+2.4=14.4$ tons.

So the smallest $W$ is $\boxed{14.4}$.

Problem 1

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Forcing Structure via Extremes

A set of sticks of lengths $100$ cm, $101$ cm, $\dots$, $200$ cm (one stick of each length) is placed on the table. On each move, Alice takes a stick, breaks it into two integer length pieces, and places them back on the table; however, it is required that at all times, any three sticks on the table can form a non-degenerate triangle. Alice continues breaking the sticks as long as it is possible. How many sticks can there be on the table at the end? (Find all possible answers.)

Show the answer

$201$ (the only possible number)

Show the solution – Only the unique longest stick can be broken

For three sticks $x\le y\le z$ the triangle condition is $x+y>z$; so “any three sticks form a non-degenerate triangle” means: the two shortest sticks together are longer than the longest stick.

Key observation. Suppose Alice breaks a stick of length $\ell$ into pieces $x$ and $y$ ($x+y=\ell$). The two shortest sticks after the move have total length at most $x+y=\ell$. So if after the move some stick of length at least $\ell$ is still on the table, the condition fails. Hence a move is possible only on a stick that is strictly longer than every other stick.

The game is forced. We show by induction that the moves are exactly: break $200$ into $100+100$, then $199$ into $99+100$, then $198$ into $99+99$, and so on, each stick $\ell=200,199,\dots,101$ being broken into $\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor$ and $\lceil\ell/2\rceil$.

Suppose the sticks $200,\dots,\ell+1$ have been broken into halves (none yet if $\ell=200$), where $101\le\ell\le200$. The sticks now are $100,101,\dots,\ell$ and the halves $\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil$ for $\ell<k\le200$; all halves are at most $100<\ell$. So $\ell$ is the unique longest stick, and by the key observation only $\ell$ can be broken. The shortest stick on the table is $s=\lceil \ell/2\rceil$ (it is $\lfloor(\ell+1)/2\rfloor$ if $\ell<200$, and $100$ if $\ell=200$). Let $\ell$ be broken into $x\le y$. After the move the longest stick is $\ell-1$ (because $y\le\ell-1$ and $\ell-1\ge100$), the shortest is $x\le\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor\le s$, and the second shortest is at most $\min(y,s)$. We need $x+\min(y,s)\ge\ell$. If $y>s$, this says $x\ge \ell-s=\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor$, so $x=\lfloor \ell/2\rfloor$ and $y=\lceil\ell/2\rceil=s$, contradicting $y>s$. Hence $y\le s=\lceil\ell/2\rceil$, which together with $x\le y$, $x+y=\ell$ forces $x=\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor$, $y=\lceil\ell/2\rceil$. Conversely this move is legal: the two shortest sticks are then $x$ and $y$, with $x+y=\ell>\ell-1$, and $\ell-1$ is the longest stick.

The end. After the stick $101$ is broken into $50+51$, all sticks are at most $100$, and the length $100$ occurs at least twice (the original stick $100$ and the halves of $200$). By the key observation no stick can be broken: a longest stick is not unique, and every other stick is shorter than a stick that stays. So the process stops here. The sticks on the table are the original stick $100$ and two halves of each of $101,\dots,200$: \[1+2\cdot100=\boxed{201}\] sticks. This is the only possible answer.

Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

Point $M$ is chosen inside the parallelogram $ABCD$ so that $\angle AMB=90^\circ$. The circumcircles of triangles $BMC$ and $AMD$ are tangent to each other. Prove that these circles are equal.

Show solution 1 – Tangent–chord angles and the law of sines

Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $BMC$ (radius $r_1$) and $\omega_2$ the circle $AMD$ (radius $r_2$). Two different tangent circles have exactly one common point; here $M$ is common, so they touch at $M$. Let $t$ be their common tangent at $M$.

The circles touch externally. Otherwise both circles lie in one closed half-plane bounded by $t$, and then $A,B,C,D$ and the whole parallelogram lie in that half-plane. But $M$ is an interior point of the parallelogram lying on $t$, and points of the parallelogram near $M$ lie on both sides of $t$; contradiction. So $B,C$ lie strictly on one side of $t$ and $A,D$ strictly on the other side.

Since $M$ is inside the convex quadrilateral, the rays $MA,MB,MC,MD$ go around $M$ in this order. The line $t$ separates $\{B,C\}$ from $\{A,D\}$, so one of its rays, say $MT$, lies inside the angle $AMB$ and the opposite ray lies inside the angle $CMD$.

By the tangent–chord theorem in $\omega_1$ (the point $C$ and the ray $MT$ lie on different sides of line $MB$), $\angle TMB=\angle MCB$; in $\omega_2$, similarly, $\angle TMA=\angle MDA$. Adding, \[\angle MCB+\angle MDA=\angle TMB+\angle TMA=\angle AMB=90^\circ .\tag{1}\] Next, $\angle ABC+\angle BAD=180^\circ$ in the parallelogram, and $M$ lies inside both angles, so \[(\angle ABM+\angle MBC)+(\angle BAM+\angle MAD)=180^\circ .\] In the right triangle $AMB$ we have $\angle ABM+\angle BAM=90^\circ$, hence \[\angle MBC+\angle MAD=90^\circ .\tag{2}\] From the angle sums of triangles $BMC$ and $AMD$, and (1), (2), \[\angle BMC+\angle AMD=360^\circ-(\angle MBC+\angle MAD)-(\angle MCB+\angle MDA)=180^\circ,\] so $\sin\angle BMC=\sin\angle AMD$. By the law of sines and $BC=AD$, \[r_1=\frac{BC}{2\sin\angle BMC}=\frac{AD}{2\sin\angle AMD}=r_2 .\] The circles are equal.

Show solution 2 – A homothety and a rectangle in the circle

Let $\omega_1$ be the circle $BMC$ with centre $O_1$ and radius $r_1$, and $\omega_2$ the circle $AMD$ with radius $r_2$. The circles have the common point $M$ and are tangent, so they touch at $M$, and their centres lie on a line through $M$. Hence there is a homothety $h$ with centre $M$ and ratio $k=\pm\frac{r_1}{r_2}$ (negative for external, positive for internal tangency) that maps $\omega_2$ onto $\omega_1$.

Let $A_1=h(A)$ and $D_1=h(D)$. They lie on $\omega_1$; $A_1$ lies on line $AM$, $A_1\neq M$, and the segment $A_1D_1$ is parallel to $AD$ with \[A_1D_1=|k|\cdot AD .\tag{1}\]

$BA_1$ is a diameter of $\omega_1$. The points $A,M,A_1$ are collinear and $\angle AMB=90^\circ$, so $\angle BMA_1=90^\circ$ (whichever side of $M$ the point $A_1$ is on). The inscribed angle $BMA_1$ is right, so $BA_1$ is a diameter.

$A_1D_1=BC$. The central symmetry of $\omega_1$ about $O_1$ maps $B$ to $A_1$, and maps the line $BC$ onto the parallel line through $A_1$. Since $A_1D_1\parallel AD\parallel BC$, that line is $A_1D_1$. The symmetry maps $\omega_1$ to itself, so it maps the chord cut on line $BC$ (the chord $BC$) onto the chord cut on line $A_1D_1$ (the chord $A_1D_1$). Hence $A_1D_1=BC$.

Now (1) and $BC=AD$ give $|k|\cdot AD=AD$, so $|k|=1$, that is, $r_1=r_2$: the circles are equal.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

A circle is divided into $100$ sectors, numbered $1$ to $100$ clockwise; some are black and the rest are white. A painting robot travels around the circle clockwise, starting in sector $1$ and finishing in sector $100$. Upon arriving at a sector, it looks at the two preceding sectors (that is, when in sector $1$, it looks at sectors $100$ and $99$; when in sector $2$, it looks at sectors $1$ and $100$; and so on). If these two sectors are the same color, it leaves the current sector unchanged; otherwise, it reverses the color of the current sector.

It is known that sector $100$ was initially black and remained black after the robot completed its work. What color would sector $100$ be at the end if it had initially been white (with all other sectors colored as before)?

Show the answer

black

Show the solution – Compare the two runs sector by sector

Compare two runs of the robot: run I with sector $100$ initially black, run II with sector $100$ initially white, all other sectors the same. Call a sector different at some moment if it has different colours in the two runs at that moment.

The robot changes the current sector exactly when the two sectors it looks at have different colours. If exactly one of these two sectors is different, then in one run they agree and in the other they disagree, so the robot acts differently in the two runs (it reverses the sector in exactly one run). If none or both of them are different, the robot acts the same way in both runs.

Before the robot starts, only sector $100$ is different. Let $D$ be the set of sectors $i\in\{1,\dots,99\}$ that are different after the robot has finished with sector $i$ (the robot never returns to them). Sector $i$ itself is initially the same in both runs, so it is different afterwards exactly when the robot acted differently on it.

  • Sector $1$: the robot looks at sectors $100$ (different) and $99$ (not yet visited, the same). It acts differently: $1\in D$.

  • Sector $2$: it looks at $1$ (different) and $100$ (different): it acts the same way: $2\notin D$.

  • Sector $i$, $3\le i\le99$: it looks at $i-1$ and $i-2$, so $i\in D$ exactly when exactly one of $i-1,i-2$ is in $D$.

Starting from $1\in D$, $2\notin D$, the rule gives $3\in D$, $4\in D$, $5\notin D$, $6\in D$, $7\in D$, $8\notin D$, …: the pattern in, in, out repeats with period $3$ (if $i-2,i-1$ are “in, out” then $i$ is in; “out, in” gives in; “in, in” gives out). Hence $i\notin D$ exactly when $i\equiv2\pmod 3$. In particular $98\notin D$ and $99\in D$.

Finally the robot arrives at sector $100$, which is still different (it has not been touched). It looks at $99$ (different) and $98$ (the same), so it acts differently in the two runs: it reverses sector $100$ in exactly one of them. A sector that is different and is reversed in exactly one run becomes the same. So at the end sector $100$ has the same colour in both runs.

In run I it ends black, so in run II it also ends black.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Periodic and Cyclic Behavior Modulo $n$

We are given two infinite increasing progressions of positive integers, an arithmetic progression and a geometric progression. It is known that they have at least two common terms. Is it true that their common terms form an infinite geometric progression?

Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – The two smallest common exponents

Yes, it is true.

Let the arithmetic progression be $a,a+d,a+2d,\dots$ ($a,d$ positive integers) and the geometric progression $b,bq,bq^2,\dots$ with $b$ a positive integer and ratio $q>1$.

$q$ is an integer. The ratio $q=\frac{bq}{b}$ of two consecutive terms is a rational number; write $q=\frac uv$ in lowest terms. Then $bq^m=\frac{bu^m}{v^m}$ is an integer for every $m$, so $v^m\mid b$ for every $m$, which forces $v=1$.

A number $bq^m$ belongs to the arithmetic progression exactly when $bq^m\ge a$ and $d\mid bq^m-a$. Let $E$ be the set of exponents $m\ge0$ for which this happens. By assumption $E$ has at least two elements; let $i<j$ be its two smallest elements and $s=j-i$.

From $bq^i\equiv a\equiv bq^j\pmod d$ we get $d\mid bq^i(q^s-1)$. Hence for every $m\ge i$, \[bq^{m+s}-bq^m=q^{m-i}\cdot bq^i(q^s-1)\equiv0\pmod d .\tag{1}\]

  • Forward: if $m\in E$ then $m+s\in E$, since $bq^{m+s}>bq^m\ge a$ and $bq^{m+s}\equiv bq^m\equiv a$ by (1). So $i,\ i+s,\ i+2s,\dots$ all belong to $E$.

  • Backward: if $m\in E$ and $m-s\ge i$, then $m-s\in E$: indeed $bq^{m-s}\ge bq^i\ge a$, and $bq^{m-s}\equiv bq^m\equiv a$ by (1) applied to $m-s$.

Now take any $m\in E$ and write $m-i=\kappa s+\rho$ with $0\le\rho<s$. Applying the backward step $\kappa$ times gives $i+\rho\in E$. But $i\le i+\rho<j$, and $i,j$ are the two smallest elements of $E$; so $\rho=0$. Therefore \[E=\{\,i,\ i+s,\ i+2s,\dots\},\] and the common terms are $bq^i,\ bq^i\cdot q^s,\ bq^i\cdot q^{2s},\dots$: an infinite geometric progression with ratio $q^s$.

Show solution 2 – The order of $q$ modulo a divisor of $d$

Yes, it is true.

Let the progressions be $a+nd$ $(n\ge0)$ and $bq^m$ $(m\ge0)$ with $a,b,d$ positive integers and $q>1$. The ratio $q$ is an integer: it is rational, being the quotient $\frac{bq}{b}$ of two integers, and if $q=\frac uv$ in lowest terms, then $v^m$ divides $b$ for all $m$ (since $\frac{bu^m}{v^m}$ is an integer), so $v=1$.

The term $bq^m$ is common to both progressions exactly when $bq^m\ge a$ and $bq^m\equiv a\pmod d$. Let $i$ be the smallest exponent of a common term. For $m\ge i$ we have $bq^m\ge bq^i\ge a$, and the congruence becomes \[bq^m\equiv bq^i\pmod d\iff d\mid bq^i\,(q^{m-i}-1)\iff e\mid q^{m-i}-1,\qquad e=\frac{d}{\gcd(d,bq^i)} .\] (The last step: $d\mid XY$ is equivalent to $\frac d{\gcd(d,X)}\mid Y$.) Exponents $m<i$ give no common terms by the choice of $i$. So the common terms are $bq^{i+t}$ for exactly those $t\ge0$ with $q^t\equiv1\pmod e$.

There is a second common term, so $q^t\equiv1\pmod e$ for some $t\ge1$. Then $\gcd(q,e)=1$, and the exponents $t\ge0$ with $q^t\equiv1\pmod e$ are exactly the multiples of the order $s$ of $q$ modulo $e$ (the smallest positive such exponent): if $q^t\equiv1$ and $t=\kappa s+\rho$ with $0\le\rho<s$, then $q^\rho\equiv q^t(q^s)^{-\kappa}\equiv1$, so $\rho=0$. (If $e=1$, every $t$ works and $s=1$.)

Hence the common terms are exactly $bq^i,\ bq^{i+s},\ bq^{i+2s},\dots$, an infinite geometric progression with ratio $q^s$.

Let the pirates of a ship be at $p_1,\dots,p_n$ and let \[f(X)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|Xp_i|\] be the average distance from a point $X$ of the island to them.

At the centre: every pirate is on the island, so $|Op_i|\le R$ and $f(O)\le R$.

$k=n^2+2n$ (for $n\ge2$; for $n=1$ the answer is $k=2$)

Show solution 1 – First fit by rows, then one free column

The answer is $k=n^2+2n=(n+1)^2-1$ for $n\ge2$. (For $n=1$ the board has only $2$ cells: a strip of length $2$ always fits, whether it is cut or not, and a strip of length $3$ never fits, so the answer is $k=2$; we assume $n\ge2$ below.)

$k=(n+1)^2$ does not always work. Let Bob cut the strip into $n+1$ pieces of length $n+1\le2n$. A piece longer than $n$ cannot stand vertically (the board has height $n$), so it lies in one row; two such pieces do not fit in one row, since $2(n+1)>2n$. With only $n$ rows, at most $n$ of the pieces can be placed. Any larger $k$ fails as well: cut off $n+1$ pieces of length $n+1$ and cut the rest arbitrarily.

$k\le n^2+2n$ always works. Order the pieces by length, $\ell_1\ge\ell_2\ge\dots$, and place them one by one, horizontally, each one in the first row (from the top) in which it fits, pushed to the left against the pieces already in that row. In this way the free cells of each row always form a block at its right end; let $u_r$ be the number of occupied cells in row $r$.

If all pieces are placed, we are done. Otherwise let $\ell=\ell_{p}$ be the first piece that does not fit. Then every row has fewer than $\ell$ free cells: $u_r>2n-\ell$ for all $r$.

Claim 1: $\ell\le n$. Otherwise every row contains at least one cell, hence at least one of the pieces placed before (all of length $\ge\ell$), and two pieces longer than $n$ do not fit in a row. So $n$ pieces of length $\ge\ell$ are placed, and with the piece $\ell$ the total length is at least $(n+1)\ell\ge(n+1)^2>k$, a contradiction.

Claim 2: the remaining pieces fit in one column. Let $F$ be the number of full rows ($u_r=2n$). The other rows have $u_r\ge2n-\ell+1\ge n+1$. The remaining pieces $\ell_p,\ell_{p+1},\dots$ have total length \[R=k-\sum_r u_r\le n^2+2n-\bigl(2nF+(n-F)(n+1)\bigr)=n-F(n-1).\] Permute the rows so that the $F$ full rows are on top (this keeps everything placed). The last column then has $n-F$ free cells, one below the other, at the bottom.

  • If $F=0$, the last column is completely free and $R\le n$: put the remaining pieces (each of length at most $\ell\le n$) vertically into it, one below the other.

  • If $F\ge1$, then $R\le n-(n-1)=1$. If a piece remains, it is a single cell; it fits into any free cell, and a free cell exists because the placed pieces cover $k-1\le n^2+2n-1<2n^2$ cells.

So all pieces can always be placed when $k\le n^2+2n$, and the largest $k$ is $\boxed{n^2+2n}$.

Show solution 2 – Glue the short pieces in pairs

For $n=1$ the board has $2$ cells: a strip of length $2$ always fits, whether it is cut or not, and a strip of length $3$ never fits; so the answer is $k=2$. From now on $n\ge2$, and we show that the answer is $k=n^2+2n$.

Strips of length $k\ge(n+1)^2$ can fail. Bob cuts off $n+1$ pieces of length $n+1$ (allowed, as $n+1\le2n$) and cuts the rest, if any, into pieces of length $1$. A piece of length $n+1$ is too long to stand vertically on a board of height $n$, so it lies in a row; and one row of length $2n$ cannot hold two of them, because $2(n+1)>2n$. With $n$ rows, at most $n$ of the $n+1$ long pieces can be placed.

Strips of length $k\le n^2+2n$ always fit. Call a piece short if its length is at most $n$, and long otherwise. While there are at least two short pieces, glue two of them end to end into a single piece; its length is at most $2n$, and placing the glued piece on the board places both of its parts. Each gluing lowers the number of pieces, so after finitely many steps at most one short piece is left. Every long piece has at least $n+1$ cells, so the number $g$ of long pieces satisfies \[g(n+1)\le k\le n^2+2n<(n+1)^2,\qquad\text{hence } g\le n .\]

  • If there are at most $n$ pieces in total, Alice puts each of them into a row of its own, starting at the left edge; this is possible since every piece has at most $2n$ cells.

  • Otherwise there are exactly $n$ long pieces and one short piece, of length $s\le n$. Alice puts the long pieces into the $n$ rows, one in each row, against the left edge. If every long piece is shorter than $2n$, the rightmost column is entirely free, and the short piece stands in it vertically. If some long piece has length $2n$, the other $n-1$ long pieces have at least $n+1$ cells each, so \[s\le k-2n-(n-1)(n+1)\le n^2+2n-2n-(n^2-1)=1 ,\] that is, $s=1$; and a free cell exists, because the long pieces occupy at most $k-1\le n^2+2n-1<2n^2$ cells (the last inequality is $(n-1)^2>0$). The short piece goes into a free cell.

In every case all the pieces fit. Hence the largest $k$ is $\boxed{n^2+2n}$ for $n\ge2$ (and $k=2$ for $n=1$).

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Vector Reformulation of Classical Geometry

A convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ has equal diagonals. Similar isosceles triangles $ABP$ and $CDQ$ are constructed externally on the sides $AB$ and $CD$ as bases, and similar isosceles triangles $ADR$ and $BCS$ are constructed externally on the sides $AD$ and $BC$ as bases. Find the angle between the lines $PQ$ and $RS$.

Show the answer

$90^\circ$

Show solution 1 – Complex numbers

The lines $PQ$ and $RS$ are perpendicular: the angle is $90^\circ$ (as long as $P\neq Q$ and $R\neq S$, so that the lines exist).

Use complex numbers and let $A,B,C,D$ go around the quadrilateral counterclockwise. Then the exterior of side $AB$ is to the right of the direction $A\to B$, and a point to the right is obtained by multiplying by $-i$. The apex of an isosceles triangle on base $XY$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$, at distance $\lambda\,|XY|$ from the midpoint, where $\lambda>0$ depends only on the shape of the triangle. So, with $\lambda$ for the triangles on $AB,CD$ and $\mu$ for those on $AD,BC$, \[P=\tfrac{A+B}2-i\lambda(B-A),\quad Q=\tfrac{C+D}2-i\lambda(D-C),\quad R=\tfrac{D+A}2-i\mu(A-D),\quad S=\tfrac{B+C}2-i\mu(C-B).\] (Going counterclockwise, the sides are traversed as $A\to B$, $B\to C$, $C\to D$, $D\to A$.) Put $u=C-A$ and $v=D-B$ (the diagonals). Then \[Q-P=\tfrac{u+v}2-i\lambda(v-u),\qquad S-R=\tfrac{u-v}2-i\mu(u+v).\] Let $s=u+v$ and $t=u-v$. The diagonals are equal, $|u|=|v|$, so $s\perp t$: indeed $\operatorname{Re}(s\bar t)=|u|^2-|v|^2=0$. Also $s\neq0$ (otherwise $A+B=C+D$, and the opposite sides $AB$, $CD$ would have a common midpoint). So $t=i\kappa s$ for a real number $\kappa$. Now \[Q-P=\tfrac s2+i\lambda t=\tfrac s2-\lambda\kappa s=s\Bigl(\tfrac12-\lambda\kappa\Bigr),\qquad S-R=\tfrac t2-i\mu s=i s\Bigl(\tfrac\kappa2-\mu\Bigr).\] So $Q-P$ is a real multiple of $s$ and $S-R$ is a real multiple of $is$. Hence $PQ\perp RS$, and the angle between the lines is $\boxed{90^\circ}$.

Show solution 2 – A rotation and a spiral similarity

We show that $PQ\perp RS$ (when $P\neq Q$ and $R\neq S$, so that the lines exist).

Let $K,L,M,N$ be the midpoints of $AB,BC,CD,DA$. By the midline theorem in triangles $ABC$ and $ACD$, the segments $KL$ and $NM$ are parallel to $AC$ and have length $\tfrac12AC$; in the same way $LM$ and $KN$ are parallel to $BD$ and have length $\tfrac12BD$. Since $AC=BD$, the quadrilateral $KLMN$ is a rhombus, and the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular: $KM\perp LN$. So it suffices to prove $PQ\parallel KM$ and $RS\parallel LN$. Let the vertices $A,B,C,D$ go counterclockwise; then the exterior of every side lies to the right of that side traversed as $A\to B$, $B\to C$, $C\to D$, $D\to A$.

$PQ\parallel KM$. The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ of a convex quadrilateral cross, so they are not parallel. As they have equal lengths, there is a rotation $\rho$ about some point $O$ with $\rho(A)=B$ and $\rho(C)=D$ (it turns the vector $\overrightarrow{AC}$ into $\overrightarrow{BD}$; its angle is not $0^\circ$). Then $OA=OB$ and $OC=OD$, so $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, which is the line $KP$, and on the perpendicular bisector of $CD$, which is the line $MQ$. Moreover $O\neq K$: if $O=K$, then $\rho$ would be the half-turn about $K$, and $\overrightarrow{BD}=-\overrightarrow{AC}$ would make the diagonals parallel.

Let $\sigma$ be the spiral similarity with centre $O$ (a rotation about $O$ followed by a homothety with centre $O$) that maps $A$ to $C$. Two such maps with the same centre commute, so \[\sigma(B)=\sigma(\rho(A))=\rho(\sigma(A))=\rho(C)=D .\] Thus $\sigma$ maps the directed segment $A\to B$ onto $C\to D$. It keeps orientation and ratios of lengths, so it maps the midpoint $K$ of $AB$ to the midpoint $M$ of $CD$, and it maps the apex $P$ of the isosceles triangle $ABP$ on the right of $A\to B$ to the apex of a similar isosceles triangle on $CD$ on the right of $C\to D$, which is $Q$. So $\sigma(K)=M$ and $\sigma(P)=Q$.

The points $O,K,P$ lie on one line and $O\neq K$, so $\overrightarrow{OP}=t\,\overrightarrow{OK}$ for a real number $t$. The map $\sigma$ acts on vectors starting at $O$ by a rotation and a scaling, which respects this relation: $\overrightarrow{OQ}=t\,\overrightarrow{OM}$. Hence \[\overrightarrow{PQ}=\overrightarrow{OQ}-\overrightarrow{OP}=t\bigl(\overrightarrow{OM}-\overrightarrow{OK}\bigr)=t\,\overrightarrow{KM},\] and $PQ\parallel KM$.

$RS\parallel LN$. The segments $CA$ and $BD$ are also equal and not parallel, so there is a rotation $\rho'$ about a point $O'$ with $\rho'(C)=B$ and $\rho'(A)=D$. Then $O'B=O'C$ and $O'A=O'D$: the point $O'$ lies on the line $LS$ (the perpendicular bisector of $BC$) and on the line $NR$ (the perpendicular bisector of $AD$); and $O'\neq L$, since otherwise $\rho'$ is the half-turn about $L$ and $\overrightarrow{BD}=-\overrightarrow{CA}$, again parallel diagonals. Let $\sigma'$ be the spiral similarity with centre $O'$ mapping $C$ to $A$; then $\sigma'(B)=\sigma'(\rho'(C))=\rho'(\sigma'(C))=\rho'(A)=D$. So $\sigma'$ maps the directed segment $B\to C$ onto $D\to A$, the midpoint $L$ to the midpoint $N$, and the apex $S$ (on the right of $B\to C$) to the apex of the similar triangle on the right of $D\to A$, which is $R$. Writing $\overrightarrow{O'S}=t'\,\overrightarrow{O'L}$ with $t'$ real, we get $\overrightarrow{O'R}=t'\,\overrightarrow{O'N}$ and $\overrightarrow{SR}=t'\,\overrightarrow{LN}$, so $RS\parallel LN$.

Therefore $PQ\parallel KM\perp LN\parallel RS$, and the angle between the lines $PQ$ and $RS$ is $\boxed{90^\circ}$.

Problem 4

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Additive Structure and Sums of Integers

We are given a set of $2n+k$ cards, each with a positive integer written on it. It is known that the sum of the numbers on any $n+k$ of the cards is divisible by the sum of the numbers on the remaining $n$ cards. Find the largest possible value of $n$ in terms of $k$.

Show the answer

$n=2k$

Show solution 1 – The $n$ largest cards carry half of the total

Here $k\ge1$ (for $k=0$ any $n$ works with all cards equal). The answer is $n=2k$.

Let $T$ be the total of all $2n+k$ cards. For a set of $n$ cards with sum $s$, the other $n+k$ cards have sum $T-s$, and $s\mid T-s$ is equivalent to $s\mid T$. So the condition says: the sum of any $n$ cards divides $T$. Such a sum $s$ is smaller than $T$, so $s\le T/2$, and $s=T/2$ or $s\le T/3$.

Example for $n=2k$. Take $5k-1$ cards with $1$ and one card with $k+1$ (that is $2n+k=5k$ cards). Then $T=6k$, and a set of $n=2k$ cards has sum $2k$ (only ones) or $2k-1+k+1=3k$; both divide $6k$.

Bound $n\le2k$. Suppose $n>k$ (otherwise $n\le k<2k$). Let $X$ be the set of the $n$ largest cards and $Y$ the other $n+k$ cards. The cards of $X$ are at least as large as the average card, so their sum is at least $\frac{n}{2n+k}T>\frac T3$ (because $3n>2n+k$). Hence the sum of $X$ is $T/2$, and the sum of $Y$ is also $T/2$. Let $\alpha$ be the smallest card in $X$; every card of $Y$ is at most $\alpha$.

Case 1: some card $y\in Y$ is smaller than $\alpha$. Swap $\alpha$ and $y$: the new set of $n$ cards has sum $T/2-(\alpha-y)<T/2$, so it is at most $T/3$, and $\alpha-y\ge T/6$. Then every card of $X$ is at least $\alpha>T/6$, so $T/2=\operatorname{sum}(X)>n\cdot T/6$ and $n<3$, that is, $n\le2\le2k$.

Case 2: all cards of $Y$ equal $\alpha$. Then $T/2=\operatorname{sum}(Y)=(n+k)\alpha$, so $T=2(n+k)\alpha$. Since $Y$ has at least $n$ cards, $n$ cards equal to $\alpha$ form an admissible set, so $n\alpha\mid 2(n+k)\alpha$, i.e. $n\mid 2n+2k$, i.e. $n\mid 2k$. Hence $n\le2k$.

In both cases $n\le2k$, and $n=2k$ is attained. The answer is $\boxed{n=2k}$.

Show solution 2 – Three blocks of the sorted cards

Here $k\ge1$ (for $k=0$ any $n$ works, with all cards equal). The answer is $n=2k$.

An example with $n=2k$. Take $2n+k=5k$ cards: one card $k+1$ and $5k-1$ cards $1$; the total is $6k$. Any $2k$ of the cards contain at most one card $k+1$, so their sum is $2k$ (all ones) or $(2k-1)+(k+1)=3k$. The remaining $3k$ cards then have sum $6k-2k=4k$, divisible by $2k$, or $6k-3k=3k$, divisible by $3k$.

No example with $n\ge2k+1$. Suppose $n\ge2k+1$, and sort the cards: $c_1\le c_2\le\dots\le c_{2n+k}$. Cut the sorted row into three blocks, of $k$, $n$ and $n$ cards, with sums \[r=c_1+\dots+c_k,\qquad p=c_{k+1}+\dots+c_{k+n},\qquad q=c_{k+n+1}+\dots+c_{2n+k}.\] Compare the blocks. Every card of the first two blocks is at most $c_{k+n+1}$, while every card of the last block is at least $c_{k+n+1}$. Hence $p\le n\,c_{k+n+1}\le q$ and, as $k<n$, $r\le k\,c_{k+n+1}<n\,c_{k+n+1}\le q$. Also every card of the middle block is at least $c_{k+1}$ and every card of the first block is at most $c_{k+1}$, so \[p\ge n\,c_{k+1}\ge(2k+1)\,c_{k+1}>2k\,c_{k+1}\ge 2r .\]

The last block. Its $n$ cards have sum $q$, and the other $n+k$ cards have sum $p+r$; so $q\mid p+r$. From $p\le q$ and $r<q$ we get $0<p+r<2q$, so $p+r=q$.

The middle block. Its $n$ cards have sum $p$, and the other cards have sum $q+r=p+2r$; so $p\mid p+2r$, that is, $p\mid 2r$. But $0<2r<p$ (here $r>0$ because $k\ge1$), and a positive integer smaller than $p$ is not a multiple of $p$. This contradiction shows $n\le2k$.

So the largest possible value is $\boxed{n=2k}$.

Problem 5

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods · Combinatorics with Geometric Insight

For which $n$ is it possible to colour all the sides and diagonals of a regular $n$-gon in several colours so that each colour is used for exactly three segments, and the three segments of each colour always form an isosceles triangle?

Show the answer

exactly when $n$ is a power of $3$: $n=3,9,27,\dots$

Show the solution – Counting by lengths, then splitting the vertices into three classes

Number the vertices by the residues $0,1,\dots,n-1$ modulo $n$ in cyclic order. The length of a segment joining $x$ and $y$ is the distance between $x$ and $y$ along the polygon, $\min(|x-y|,\,n-|x-y|)$; two segments are equal exactly when their lengths are equal. A colouring as required is a partition of all $\binom n2$ segments into triangles (with vertices among the vertices of the polygon) that are isosceles. We call such a partition good. The answer is: a good partition exists exactly when $n$ is a power of $3$.

Isosceles triangles. If the vertices of a triangle cut the polygon into arcs of $p,q,r$ steps ($p+q+r=n$), its sides have lengths $\min(p,n-p)$ etc., and two of these are equal only if two of $p,q,r$ are equal (if $p\ne q$ then $p=n-q$ would give $r=0$). So an isosceles triangle has the form $\{v-a,\ v,\ v+a\}$ with apex $v$ and $1\le a<n/2$: two legs of length $a$ and a base of length $\varphi(a)=\min(2a,\,n-2a)$.

Step 1: $n$ is odd. In a good partition, each triangle through a vertex uses two of its $n-1$ segments; so $n-1$ is even.

Step 2: $3\mid n$. Let $n$ be odd and $m=\frac{n-1}2$; the lengths are $1,\dots,m$, and there are exactly $n$ segments of each length. The map $\varphi$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,m\}$ (its values $2a$ for $a\le n/4$ are even and distinct, its values $n-2a$ for $a>n/4$ are odd and distinct). Let $t_a$ be the number of triangles of the partition with legs of length $a$ (an equilateral triangle, when $3a=n$, is counted once and has $\varphi(a)=a$). Counting the segments of length $\varphi(a)$: they are the bases of the $t_a$ triangles with legs $a$, and the legs of the $t_{\varphi(a)}$ triangles with legs $\varphi(a)$: \[t_a+2t_{\varphi(a)}=n\qquad\text{for every }a .\] Put $e_a=t_a-\frac n3$; then $e_{\varphi(a)}=-\frac12e_a$. Since $\varphi$ is a permutation of a finite set, $\varphi^L(a)=a$ for some $L\ge1$, and then $e_a=\bigl(-\frac12\bigr)^Le_a$, so $e_a=0$. Thus $t_a=\frac n3$ is an integer: $3\mid n$.

Step 3: the three classes modulo $3$. Let $n=3q$ ($q$ odd). Residues modulo $3$ are well defined on the vertices; let $C_0,C_1,C_2$ be the three classes. The class $C_j=\{j,\,j+3,\,\dots\}$ consists of the vertices of a regular $q$-gon, and the lengths of its segments in the $n$-gon are $3$ times their lengths in the $q$-gon. A segment inside a class has length divisible by $3$. In an isosceles triangle $\{v-a,v,v+a\}$ the base has length $\equiv\pm2a\pmod 3$; so if one side has length divisible by $3$, then $3\mid a$ and the whole triangle lies inside one class. Consequently, in a good partition of the $n$-gon, the segments inside $C_j$ are partitioned into triangles inside $C_j$, isosceles also in the $q$-gon: if $n=3q$ has a good partition, then $q=1$ or the $q$-gon has a good partition.

Step 4: if $q=1$ or the $q$-gon has a good partition, then $n=3q$ has one. Inside each class $C_j$ use a good partition of the $q$-gon (nothing to do if $q=1$). For the segments between different classes use the triangles \[\{v-a,\ v,\ v+a\}\qquad\text{with } v\equiv0\pmod 3,\ \ 1\le a\le m,\ \ 3\nmid a .\] Their three vertices lie in the three different classes. Every segment between classes is in exactly one of them:

  • a segment $\{x,y\}$ with $x\equiv0$, $y\not\equiv0$: its length $a$ is not divisible by $3$, and it is a leg of the triangle with apex $x$ and legs $a$. It is not a base of any of these triangles, since bases join $v\pm a$, both $\not\equiv0$; and it is a leg only at the apex $x$.

  • a segment $\{x,y\}$ with $x,y\not\equiv0$, so $\{x,y\}\equiv\{1,2\}\pmod3$: it cannot be a leg, so it must be a base $\{w-b,w+b\}$. This forces $2w\equiv x+y$ and $2b\equiv y-x\pmod n$; as $n$ is odd, $w$ and $\pm b$ are uniquely determined, and $w\equiv2(x+y)\equiv0$, $b\equiv\pm2(y-x)\not\equiv0\pmod 3$. So exactly one triangle of the family has this base.

Together with the partitions inside the classes this is a good partition of the $n$-gon.

Conclusion. For $n=3$ the single triangle is equilateral, so $n=3$ is good. By Step 4 and induction, every $n=3^j$ is good. Conversely, if $n$ is good then $n=3q$ (Steps 1–2) and $q=1$ or $q$ is good (Step 3); by induction $n$ is a power of $3$. So the required colouring exists exactly for $n=3,9,27,81,\dots$, that is, for $\boxed{n=3^j\ (j\ge1)}$.

Problem 6

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 10: Double Counting and Counting Identities · Weighted Counting

There is a flat island in the ocean. When a pirate ship arrives at the island, the pirates spread out over the island to rest. A collector has obtained several identical maps of this island from different ships; each map shows where each pirate of that ship rested. The collector decided to mark a pirate “headquarters” on each map (a separate one for each map) so that the average distance from the headquarters to the pirates is the same for all maps. Prove that this can be done, regardless of how many pirates were on each ship and where they settled on the island.

Assume that the distance between two points is the length of the shortest path between them within the island, and that such a path exists for any two points of the island.

Show solution 1 – Average over a second map

Let $d(x,y)$ be the path distance on the island, and let the collector's maps be $P_1,\dots,P_N$. For a map $P$ with pirates at $p_1,\dots,p_n$ (repetitions allowed) let \[f_P(x)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n d(x,p_i)\] be the average distance from a point $x$ of the island to the pirates of $P$. Let $Z$ be the finite set of all pirate positions on all $N$ maps, and put \[m_P=\min_{z\in Z}f_P(z),\qquad M_P=\max_{z\in Z}f_P(z);\] these exist because $Z$ is finite.

$f_P$ takes every value between $m_P$ and $M_P$. Let $f_P(z_1)=m_P$ and $f_P(z_2)=M_P$ with $z_1,z_2\in Z$, and join $z_1$ to $z_2$ by a shortest path, parametrised by arc length $s\in[0,L]$ as $\gamma(s)$. The part of the path between $\gamma(s)$ and $\gamma(s')$ has length $|s-s'|$, so $d(\gamma(s),\gamma(s'))\le|s-s'|$. By the triangle inequality $|d(x,p)-d(y,p)|\le d(x,y)$ for any points $x,y,p$, hence $|f_P(\gamma(s))-f_P(\gamma(s'))|\le|s-s'|$. So $s\mapsto f_P(\gamma(s))$ is continuous on $[0,L]$, and by the intermediate value theorem it takes every value in $[m_P,M_P]$.

Key inequality: $m_P\le M_Q$ for any two of the maps $P$, $Q$. Let $Q$ have pirates $q_1,\dots,q_k$. Counting the $nk$ distances $d(p_i,q_j)$ in two ways, \[\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^k f_P(q_j)=\frac1{nk}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^k d(p_i,q_j)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f_Q(p_i).\] All the points $q_j$ and $p_i$ belong to $Z$, so every term on the left is at least $m_P$ and every term on the right is at most $M_Q$. Hence $m_P\le M_Q$.

The common value. Let $D$ be the largest of the numbers $m_{P_1},\dots,m_{P_N}$, say $D=m_{P_a}$. For every map $P_b$ we have $m_{P_b}\le D$ by the choice of $D$, and $D=m_{P_a}\le M_{P_b}$ by the key inequality. Hence $D\in[m_{P_b},M_{P_b}]$, and some point $h_b$ of the island has $f_{P_b}(h_b)=D$.

The collector marks the point $h_b$ on the map $P_b$ $(b=1,\dots,N)$. On every map the average distance from the headquarters to the pirates is then $D$, as required.

Show solution 2 – Intervals that meet in pairs

Let $d(x,y)$ be the path distance on the island, and let the collector's maps be $P_1,\dots,P_N$ (finitely many). For the map $P_i$ with pirates at $p_1,\dots,p_n$ let $f_i(x)=\frac1n\sum_{s=1}^n d(x,p_s)$, and let $I_i$ be the set of all values $f_i(x)$, $x$ on the island. We need a number lying in all the sets $I_1,\dots,I_N$.

Each $I_i$ is an interval. Let $f_i(x)=\alpha$ and $f_i(y)=\beta$. Walk along a shortest path from $x$ to $y$ and let $z(\rho)$ be the point at path length $\rho$ from $x$. For any fixed point $p$ we have $|d(z(\rho),p)-d(z(\rho'),p)|\le d(z(\rho),z(\rho'))\le|\rho-\rho'|$, so $\rho\mapsto f_i(z(\rho))$ is continuous and takes every value between $\alpha$ and $\beta$. So together with any two values, $I_i$ contains everything between them: $I_i$ is an interval (possibly without its endpoints).

Any two of the intervals meet. Let $P_i$ have pirates $p_1,\dots,p_n$ and $P_j$ have pirates $q_1,\dots,q_k$. The average of all $nk$ distances $d(p_s,q_t)$ can be computed in two ways: \[c=\frac1k\sum_{t=1}^k f_i(q_t)=\frac1n\sum_{s=1}^n f_j(p_s).\] The first expression is an average of numbers of $I_i$, so $c$ lies between the smallest and the largest of them, hence in the interval $I_i$; the second expression shows $c\in I_j$ in the same way. So $I_i\cap I_j\neq\varnothing$.

All the intervals meet. Put $u=\max_i\,\inf I_i$ and $w=\min_i\,\sup I_i$ (if the island is unbounded, $w$ may be $+\infty$), and choose indices with $u=\inf I_a$ and $w=\sup I_b$. A common point $x$ of $I_a$ and $I_b$ satisfies $u\le x\le w$, so $u\le w$.

  • If $u<w$, take any $x$ with $u<x<w$. For every $i$ we have $\inf I_i\le u<x<w\le\sup I_i$, so $I_i$ contains numbers below $x$ and numbers above $x$; being an interval, it contains $x$.

  • If $u=w$, the point $x$ above equals $u$, and we show $u\in I_i$ for every $i$. We know $\inf I_i\le u\le\sup I_i$. If $u\notin I_i$, then the interval $I_i$ lies entirely above $u$ or entirely below $u$. In the first case it misses $I_b\subseteq(-\infty,w]=(-\infty,u]$, in the second it misses $I_a\subseteq[u,\infty)$; both contradict the fact that any two of the sets meet. So $u\in I_i$.

So some number $D$ lies in all the intervals: on each map $P_i$ there is a point $h_i$ with $f_i(h_i)=D$, and the collector marks these points as the headquarters.

Problem 7

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Arithmetic Structure of Products and Powers

Do there exist $100$ distinct positive integers such that both their sum and their product are $60$th powers of positive integers?

Show the answer

Yes

Show solution 1 – Sixty numbers and forty numbers with two multipliers

Yes. The difficulty: if all $100$ numbers are multiplied by the same $c$, the product gains the factor $c^{100}$, which need not be a $60$th power. We use two multipliers, $a$ for $60$ numbers and $b$ for $40$ numbers; then the product gains $a^{60}b^{40}$, and $a^{60}$ is always a $60$th power.

Let \[Y=\sum_{j=1}^{60}j^{20},\qquad Z=\sum_{k=1}^{40}k^{20},\qquad W=60!\cdot40!,\qquad b=W\,Y^{3},\] choose a positive integer $t$ so large that \[a=Y^{59}t^{60}-W\,Y^{2}Z\] satisfies $a>b\cdot40^{20}$ (possible, since $a$ grows like $t^{60}$), and take the $100$ numbers \[a\cdot1^{20},\ a\cdot2^{20},\ \dots,\ a\cdot60^{20}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b\cdot1^{20},\ b\cdot2^{20},\ \dots,\ b\cdot40^{20}.\]

Distinct positive integers. Within each group they are distinct. Every number of the first group is at least $a>b\cdot40^{20}$, which is the largest number of the second group.

The sum. Note $bZ=WY^3Z$, so \[a\,Y+b\,Z=Y^{60}t^{60}-WY^3Z+WY^3Z=(Yt)^{60}.\]

The product. \[a^{60}\,(60!)^{20}\cdot b^{40}\,(40!)^{20}=a^{60}\,W^{20}\,b^{40}=a^{60}\,W^{20}\,W^{40}Y^{120}=\bigl(a\,W\,Y^{2}\bigr)^{60}.\] Both the sum and the product are $60$th powers of positive integers.

Show solution 2 – Monomials of a binomial expansion

Yes. Write \[(x+2)^{60}=\sum_{i=0}^{60}a_i\,x^i,\qquad a_i=\binom{60}{i}2^{60-i},\] and split this sum into $100$ monomials with positive integer coefficients:

  • the $59$ monomials $a_ix^i$ for $i=2,3,\dots,60$;

  • the two monomials $x$ and $(a_1-1)\,x$ (their sum is $a_1x$, and $a_1-1=60\cdot2^{59}-1\neq1$);

  • the $39$ constants $1,2,\dots,38$ and $2^{60}-(1+2+\dots+38)=2^{60}-741$ (their sum is $a_0=2^{60}$, and they are distinct).

For every positive integer $x$ these are $100$ positive integers whose sum is $(x+2)^{60}$, a $60$th power. Their product is $C\,x^{d}$, where $C$ is the product of all the coefficients and \[d=(2+3+\dots+60)+1+1=1829+2=1831 .\]

Making the product a $60$th power. Since $1831=30\cdot60+31$ and $31\cdot29=899=15\cdot60-1$, we have $29d\equiv-1\pmod{60}$; so $29d+1=53100=60\cdot885$. Take $x=C^{29}$. Then the product is \[C\,x^{d}=C^{1+29d}=C^{53100}=\bigl(C^{885}\bigr)^{60}.\]

The numbers are distinct. Among the coefficients are $2$ and $2^{60}-741$, so $x\ge C\ge2\,(2^{60}-741)>2^{60}$; and $x$ is larger than every coefficient, since each coefficient is a factor of $C$ and $C$ has further factors larger than $1$. The constants are less than $2^{60}<x$, so they are smaller than every monomial of degree at least $1$. Two monomials $cx^i$ and $c'x^j$ with $1\le i<j$ satisfy $cx^i<x^{i+1}\le c'x^j$, since $c<x$. Two monomials of the same degree have different coefficients (degree $1$: $1\neq a_1-1$; degree $0$: distinct constants). So the $100$ numbers are distinct.

Thus both the sum and the product are $60$th powers of positive integers.
