Problem 2

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Homothety Centers and Image Tracking

We place an infinite series of rectangles $R_n$, $n\ge1$, in the coordinate plane defined recursively by the following rules: $S_1$ is a $1$ by $1$ square at the bottom left of the first quadrant, $R_1$ is the bottom half of $S_1$, $S_{n+1}$ is the top half of $S_n$, rotated $90^\circ$ clockwise about the top half's bottom right corner, and $R_{n+1}$ is the bottom half of $S_{n+1}$, $n\ge1$. What fraction of the total area of the rectangles $R_n$, $n\ge1$, lies below the line $y=2x/3$?

Show the answer

$\frac{23}{36}$

Show the solution

Solution (Self-similarity about the point $(3,2)$)

Following the rules from $S_1=[0,1]\times[0,1]$ (a clockwise quarter turn about a corner takes a $w\times h$ rectangle to an $h\times w$ one): \[R_1=[0,1]\times[0,\tfrac12],\quad S_2=[1,\tfrac32]\times[\tfrac12,\tfrac32],\quad R_2=[1,\tfrac32]\times[\tfrac12,1],\] \[S_3=[\tfrac32,2]\times[1,\tfrac32],\quad R_3=[\tfrac32,2]\times[1,\tfrac54],\quad S_4=[2,\tfrac94]\times[\tfrac54,\tfrac74],\quad R_4=[2,\tfrac94]\times[\tfrac54,\tfrac32].\] So $S_3$ is the image of $S_1$ under the homothety $h$ with ratio $\frac12$ and centre $Z=(3,2)$ (it sends $(0,0)\mapsto(\frac32,1)$ and $(1,1)\mapsto(2,\frac32)$). The construction rules commute with homotheties of positive ratio, so $S_{n+2}=h(S_n)$ and $R_{n+2}=h(R_n)$ for all $n$. The rotation puts $S_{n+1}$ to the right of $S_n$, its left side on the line containing the right side of $S_n$; so the $R_n$ have disjoint interiors and their areas add.

The line $y=\frac{2x}3$ passes through $O$ and through the centre $Z=(3,2)$, so $h$ maps it to itself and maps the part of $R_n$ below it to the part of $R_{n+2}$ below it. Hence both the areas and the areas below the line shrink by the factor $\frac14$ every two steps, and the required fraction equals the same fraction for $R_1\cup R_2$.

  • $R_1$ (area $\frac12$): the line meets its top side $y=\frac12$ at $x=\frac34$; the part above the line is the triangle with vertices $(0,0),(0,\frac12),(\frac34,\frac12)$, of area $\frac3{16}$. Below: $\frac12-\frac3{16}=\frac5{16}$.

  • $R_2$ (area $\frac14$): for $1\le x\le\frac32$ the line is at height $\frac{2x}3\in[\frac23,1]$, inside the rectangle, so the part below has area $\int_1^{3/2}\left(\frac{2x}3-\frac12\right)dx$; this is a trapezoid with parallel sides $\frac16$ and $\frac12$ and width $\frac12$, area $\frac12\cdot\frac12\left(\frac16+\frac12\right)=\frac16$.

The fraction is $\dfrac{\frac5{16}+\frac16}{\frac12+\frac14}=\dfrac{23/48}{3/4}=\boxed{\dfrac{23}{36}}$.

Problem 3

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Euler Totient and Related Functions

In the coordinate plane, let $X=(1,0)$, $Y=(0,1)$, and $O=(0,0)$. Let $V$ be the set of points $(x,y)$ such that $x$ and $y$ are integers between $1$ and $12$, inclusive. Let \[S=\{\tan\angle POX+\tan\angle POY\mid P\in V\}.\] What is the size of $S$?

Show the answer

$46$

Show the solution

Solution (Count the slopes)

For $P=(x,y)$ with positive coordinates, $\tan\angle POX=\frac yx$ and $\tan\angle POY=\frac xy$, so the element of $S$ is $r+\frac1r$ with $r=\frac yx$. For positive $r,s$, \[r+\tfrac1r=s+\tfrac1s\iff(r-s)\left(1-\tfrac1{rs}\right)=0\iff s=r\ \text{or}\ s=\tfrac1r.\] So the size of $S$ is the number of distinct ratios $\frac yx\ge1$ (each pair $\{r,\frac1r\}$ contains exactly one such ratio). These are the fractions $\frac qp$ in lowest terms with $1\le p\le q\le12$; for each $q$ the admissible $p$ are the integers $1\le p\le q$ coprime to $q$, so there are $\varphi(q)$ of them (for $q=1$ only $p=1$, and $\varphi(1)=1$). Hence \[|S|=\sum_{q=1}^{12}\varphi(q)=1+1+2+2+4+2+6+4+6+4+10+4=\boxed{46}.\]

Problem 4

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Coordinate Optimization of Synthetic Figures

An infinite “T” is formed by attaching the foot of a ray to a point on a line in such a way that the ray and the line are perpendicular. A circle of radius $10$ is placed on the plane of the “T” so that the amount of length of the “T” contained within the circle is maximized. Determine the square of the distance of the circle's center to the branch point of the “T”.

Show the answer

$20$

Show the solution

Solution (Reduce to the axis, then one variable)

Put the line on the $x$-axis and the ray along the positive $y$-axis, branch point $T=(0,0)$. Let the centre be $P=(a,b)$.

For $|a|\le10$ put $h=\sqrt{100-a^2}$; the circle meets the line $x=0$ in the segment from $(0,b-h)$ to $(0,b+h)$, so the length on the ray is the length of $[b-h,b+h]\cap[0,\infty)$ (and it is $0$ if $|a|>10$). The length on the line is $2\sqrt{100-b^2}$ if $|b|<10$ and $0$ otherwise; it depends only on $|b|$.

We may take $b\ge0$. If $b<0$, the ray length is $\max(0,b+h)=\max(0,h-|b|)$, while at height $|b|$ it is $\min(2h,|b|+h)$, which is strictly larger whenever $h>0$. So reflecting the centre in the line strictly improves the total (or both ray lengths are $0$).

We may take $a=0$. For $b\ge0$ the ray length is $\min(2h,b+h)$, which does not decrease as $h$ grows; for $0\le b<10$ it is at most $b+h$, which is strictly less than $b+10$ (the value at $a=0$, $h=10$) when $a\ne0$. So for $0\le b<10$ the total is largest exactly when $a=0$.

If $b\ge10$ the total is at most $20$. For $0\le b<10$ and $a=0$ the ray part is $b+10$ (from $0$ to $b+10$), so the total is \[L(b)=2\sqrt{100-b^2}+b+10,\qquad L'(b)=1-\frac{2b}{\sqrt{100-b^2}}.\] $L'$ is decreasing, and vanishes when $\sqrt{100-b^2}=2b$, i.e. $b^2=20$. The maximum is $L=2\sqrt{80}+\sqrt{20}+10=10+10\sqrt5\approx32.4>20$, at $P=(0,2\sqrt5)$, and by the two reductions (both strict) no other centre attains it. The squared distance from the centre to the branch point is $PT^2=\boxed{20}$.

Problem 5

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Reduction to Known Models

Seven real numbers $m_k$, $k=1,\dots,7$ are chosen uniformly and independently at random from the interval $[-1,1]$. Determine the probability that the seven lines $y=m_kx+k$, $k=1,\dots,7$ exhibit a total of $2$ intersections to the right of the $y$-axis.

Show the answer

$\frac{1}{252}$

Show the solution

Solution (Intersections are inversions)

With probability $1$ the slopes are distinct and no three lines are concurrent, so every pair of lines meets in exactly one point and these points are different. Lines $k<j$ meet where $m_kx+k=m_jx+j$, i.e. at $x=\frac{j-k}{m_k-m_j}$, which is positive exactly when $m_k>m_j$. So the number of intersections with $x>0$ is the number of inversions of the sequence $m_1,\dots,m_7$. By symmetry all $7!$ relative orders are equally likely, so the probability is $I/5040$, where $I$ is the number of permutations of $7$ elements with exactly $2$ inversions.

A permutation with $2$ inversions is obtained from the identity either by moving one element two places within three consecutive positions (patterns $231$ or $312$ on positions $i,i+1,i+2$): $2\cdot5=10$ ways; or by two swaps of adjacent positions $\{i,i+1\}$, $\{j,j+1\}$ with $j\ge i+2$: $\binom62-5=10$ ways. These are all. A permutation with an inversion has one in two adjacent positions, and swapping those two entries removes exactly that inversion. So a permutation $\pi$ with $2$ inversions becomes, after one such swap of positions $\{j,j+1\}$, a permutation with $1$ inversion, which is the identity with one adjacent swap $\{i,i+1\}$ applied; $j\ne i$, since otherwise $\pi$ would be the identity. If $|i-j|=1$, $\pi$ is one of the two patterns $231$, $312$ on three consecutive positions; if $|i-j|\ge2$, $\pi$ is two disjoint adjacent swaps. So $I=20$ and the probability is $\frac{20}{5040}=\boxed{\frac1{252}}$.

Problem 6

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences · Quadratic Residues

How many ordered triples of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ are there with $a,b,c\le100$ such that $a^2+b^2\equiv c^2\pmod{101}$?

Show the answer

$9600$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Parametrise the conic)

Work in $\mathbb F_p$ with $p=101$. First count all solutions $(a,b,c)\in\mathbb F_p^3$ of $a^2+b^2=c^2$.

All solutions. Since $p\equiv1\pmod4$ there is $i$ with $i^2=-1$. The equation is $a^2=(c-b)(c+b)$. Put $u=c-b$, $w=c+b$ (a bijective change of variables since $2$ is invertible): we count $(a,u,w)$ with $uw=a^2$. If $a=0$: $uw=0$ has $2p-1$ solutions. If $a\ne0$ ($p-1$ choices): $u\ne0$ is arbitrary and $w=a^2/u$: $p-1$ solutions. Total $2p-1+(p-1)^2=p^2$.

Remove zero coordinates. $a=0$: $b=\pm c$, $2p-1$ solutions; $b=0$: likewise $2p-1$; $c=0$: $a=\pm ib$, $2p-1$. Any two of $a,b,c$ zero forces the third to be $0$. By inclusion-exclusion the solutions with some zero coordinate number $3(2p-1)-3+1=6p-5$.

Hence the count with $a,b,c\in\{1,\dots,100\}$ is $p^2-6p+5=(p-1)(p-5)=100\cdot96=\boxed{9600}$.

Solution 2 (Count by the value of $c^2-b^2$)

For $t\in\mathbb F_{101}$ let $N(t)$ be the number of $a\in\{1,\dots,100\}$ with $a^2\equiv t$: $N(t)=2$ if $t$ is a nonzero square, $0$ if $t$ is a non-square, and $N(0)=0$. We need $\sum_{b,c\ne0}N(c^2-b^2)$.

Fix $b\ne0$ and substitute $c=b\gamma$, $a=b\alpha$: the count for each $b$ equals the number of $(\alpha,\gamma)$, $\alpha,\gamma\ne0$, with $\alpha^2+1=\gamma^2$, which does not depend on $b$. Write $(\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma+\alpha)=1$: with $s=\gamma-\alpha\ne0$ we get $\gamma+\alpha=s^{-1}$, so $\gamma=\frac{s+s^{-1}}2$, $\alpha=\frac{s^{-1}-s}2$; this gives $100$ pairs, one for each $s\ne0$. Exclude $\alpha=0$ ($s^2=1$: $2$ values of $s$) and $\gamma=0$ ($s^2=-1$: $2$ values, as $-1$ is a square mod $101$); these four are distinct. So each $b$ gives $96$ pairs, and the total is $100\cdot96=\boxed{9600}$.

Solution 3 (Lines through a point of the conic)

Work modulo the prime $p=101$; the numbers $1,\dots,100$ are exactly the nonzero residues. For $c\ne0$ put $u=a/c$ and $v=b/c$. Then $(a,b,c)$ is a solution with $a,b,c\ne0$ exactly when $(u,v)$ is a point of the conic $u^2+v^2=1$ with $u\ne0$, $v\ne0$, and $(a,b)=(cu,cv)$. So the answer is $100$ times the number $K$ of such points.

All points of the conic. The point $(1,0)$ lies on it. A line through $(1,0)$ other than the vertical one is $v=t(u-1)$, with slope $t$. Substituting, \[u^2-1+t^2(u-1)^2=(u-1)\bigl((1+t^2)u+1-t^2\bigr)=0 .\]

  • If $1+t^2\ne0$, the line meets the conic again at the single point \[u=\frac{t^2-1}{t^2+1},\qquad v=t(u-1)=\frac{-2t}{t^2+1},\] which is not $(1,0)$, since $u=1$ would mean $t^2-1=t^2+1$.

  • If $1+t^2=0$ the second factor is the nonzero constant $2$, and the line meets the conic only at $(1,0)$. Since $10^2=100\equiv-1$, this happens for exactly two slopes, $t=\pm10$.

Conversely, a point $(u,v)\ne(1,0)$ of the conic has $u\ne1$ (if $u=1$ then $v^2=0$), so it lies on exactly one of these lines, the one with slope $t=\frac{v}{u-1}$. Hence the points other than $(1,0)$ correspond one-to-one to the $101-2=99$ slopes $t\ne\pm10$.

Points with both coordinates nonzero. The point $(1,0)$ is excluded. For the other points, $v=0$ exactly when $t=0$, and $u=0$ exactly when $t^2=1$, that is, $t=\pm1$. Removing these three slopes leaves $K=99-3=96$.

Therefore the number of triples is $100\cdot96=\boxed{9600}$.

Problem 7

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition

The vertices of a kite are labeled $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ in clockwise order. The interior angles at $B$ and $D$ measure $160^\circ$ and $130^\circ$, respectively. Let $O$ be the intersection of the diagonals of the kite. The line through $B$ perpendicular to line $CD$ intersects line $CD$ at $F$. Let $G$ be the intersection of lines $AB$ and $OF$. Suppose the degree measure of $\angle FGD$ is $t^\circ$, where $0\le t<180$. What is $t$?

Show the answer

$25$

Show the solution

Solution (Two cyclic quadrilaterals)

Since $\angle B\ne\angle D$, the axis of symmetry of the kite is $BD$; so $BA=BC$, $DA=DC$, the diagonals are perpendicular at $O$, $O$ is the midpoint of $AC$, and $\angle A=\angle C=\frac{360^\circ-160^\circ-130^\circ}{2}=35^\circ$. Hence \[\angle ABD=\angle CBD=80^\circ,\quad\angle ADB=\angle CDB=65^\circ,\quad\angle CAD=\angle ACD=25^\circ,\quad\angle BCA=10^\circ.\] The kite is convex and $\angle BCD=35^\circ$, $\angle BDC=65^\circ$ are acute, so $F$ lies between $C$ and $D$; $O$ lies between $B$ and $D$.

$B,O,F,C$ are concyclic, on the circle with diameter $BC$, since $\angle BOC=\angle BFC=90^\circ$. In this cyclic quadrilateral $BOFC$, the exterior angle at $O$ equals the interior angle at $C$: $\angle DOF=\angle BCF=35^\circ$.

$A,G,O,D$ are concyclic. Since $F\ne D$ lies on segment $CD$, it is on the side of $C$ of line $BD$, so the ray from $O$ opposite to ray $OF$ lies on the side of $A$ and makes the angle $\angle DOF=35^\circ$ (vertical angles) with ray $OB$. As $35^\circ<90^\circ=\angle BOA$, this ray lies inside $\angle BOA$ and meets segment $AB$; that point is $G$, and $\angle GOB=35^\circ$. In triangle $GBO$, $\angle GBO=80^\circ$, so $\angle BGO=65^\circ$ and $\angle AGO=115^\circ$. Since $\angle ODA=65^\circ$, the opposite angles of quadrilateral $AGOD$ at $G$ and $D$ add up to $180^\circ$, so it is cyclic.

Therefore $\angle OGD=\angle OAD=25^\circ$ (angles on the same arc $OD$). Since $F$ lies on ray $GO$ beyond $O$, $\angle FGD=\angle OGD$, so $t=\boxed{25}$.

Problem 8

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry · Spatial Coordinate and Vector Methods

The points $(0,56,80)$, $(0,72,80)$, $(0,51,75)$, and $(0,69,75)$ form the vertices of a trapezoid in $xyz$-coordinate space. A point light is placed at the coordinates $(x,y,z)$, with $x>0$. The shadow cast by the trapezoid onto the $xy$-plane is a square with sides parallel to or perpendicular to the axes. What is $x$?

Show the answer

$144$

Show the solution

Solution (Central projection from the light)

Let the light be $L=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$. The shadow of a point $Q$ at height $h>0$ lies on the ray from $L$ through $Q$, beyond $Q$; this ray comes down to height $0$ only if $z_0>h$. So the light must be higher than the trapezoid, $z_0>80$. A point $Q$ at height $h$ casts its shadow at $L+k(Q-L)$ with $k=\frac{z_0}{z_0-h}$ (this point has height $0$). So the top edge (height $80$, from $y=56$ to $72$) and the bottom edge (height $75$, from $y=51$ to $69$), both parallel to the $y$-axis, cast shadows parallel to the $y$-axis, scaled by \[k_1=\frac{z_0}{z_0-80},\qquad k_2=\frac{z_0}{z_0-75}.\] For the shadow to be a square with sides parallel to the axes, these two images must be opposite sides: equal lengths and equal $y$-ranges.

Equal lengths: $16k_1=18k_2$, i.e. $16(z_0-75)=18(z_0-80)$, so $z_0=120$, $k_1=3$, $k_2=\frac83$, and the side is $48$.

Same $y$-range: $y_0+3(56-y_0)=y_0+\frac83(51-y_0)$ gives $y_0=96$; both images then run from $y=-24$ to $y=24$.

Distance between them: their $x$-coordinates are $x_0(1-k_1)=-2x_0$ and $x_0(1-k_2)=-\frac53x_0$, which differ by $\frac{x_0}{3}$. This must equal $48$, so $x_0=\boxed{144}$. Indeed, for $L=(144,96,120)$ the shadow has vertices $(-288,\pm24,0)$ and $(-240,\pm24,0)$, a square of side $48$.

Problem 9

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 34: Complex Numbers as an Olympiad Tool · Roots of Unity

Let $x+yi$ be the root of the polynomial \[8z^7+28z^6+56z^5+70z^4+56z^3+28z^2+8z+1\] where $x$ and $y$ are real numbers, $i=\sqrt{-1}$, and $y$ is maximal. What is $y$? Express your answer in the form $a+b\sqrt c$, where $a$ and $b$ are rational numbers in simplest form and $c$ is a square-free integer.

Show the answer

$\frac12+\frac12\sqrt2$

Show the solution

Solution (Recognise $(z+1)^8-z^8$)

By the binomial theorem the polynomial equals $(z+1)^8-z^8$. Since $z=0$ is not a root, the roots satisfy $\left(\frac{z+1}{z}\right)^8=1$ with $\frac{z+1}z\ne1$, so $1+\frac1z=\omega=e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}{8}$, $k=1,\dots,7$, and \[z=\frac1{e^{i\theta}-1}=\frac{e^{-i\theta/2}}{e^{i\theta/2}-e^{-i\theta/2}}=\frac{\cos\frac\theta2-i\sin\frac\theta2}{2i\sin\frac\theta2}=-\frac12-\frac i2\cot\frac\theta2.\] So $y=-\frac12\cot\frac\theta2$ with $\frac\theta2\in\{\frac\pi8,\frac{2\pi}8,\dots,\frac{7\pi}8\}$. This is largest when $\cot\frac\theta2$ is most negative, at $\frac\theta2=\frac{7\pi}8$, where $\cot\frac{7\pi}8=-\cot\frac\pi8=-(1+\sqrt2)$. Hence \[y=\frac{1+\sqrt2}{2}=\boxed{\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt2}.\]

Problem 10

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Angle Expressions and Trigonometric Identities

Find the smallest positive real number $t$ that maximizes the value of \[\sin(8t^\circ)-\sin(t^\circ)-\sin(7t^\circ).\]

Show the answer

$240$

Show the solution

Solution (Three sines with angles adding to $0$)

Put $\alpha=8t^\circ$, $\beta=-t^\circ$, $\gamma=-7t^\circ$; then $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0$ and the expression is $f=\sin\alpha+\sin\beta+\sin\gamma$. With $u=\frac{\alpha+\beta}2$ and $v=\frac{\alpha-\beta}2$, \[f=2\sin u\cos v-\sin2u=2\sin u\,(\cos v-\cos u).\] Hence $f\le2|\sin u|\,(1+|\cos u|)$. With $c=|\cos u|$, $\left(2\sqrt{1-c^2}(1+c)\right)^2=4(1-c)(1+c)^3$, and by AM-GM on $3(1-c),1+c,1+c,1+c$, \[3(1-c)(1+c)^3\le\left(\frac{3(1-c)+3(1+c)}4\right)^4=\frac{81}{16},\] so $f\le\sqrt{\frac{4\cdot27}{16}}=\frac{3\sqrt3}2$, with equality only if $c=\frac12$ and $\cos v=\pm1$ with the right signs. The two equality cases are $\cos u=\frac12,\ \sin u=-\frac{\sqrt3}2,\ \cos v=-1$ and $\cos u=-\frac12,\ \sin u=\frac{\sqrt3}2,\ \cos v=1$; in both, $\alpha=u+v\equiv120^\circ$ and $\beta=u-v\equiv120^\circ\pmod{360^\circ}$.

So the maximum $\frac{3\sqrt3}2$ is attained exactly when $-t\equiv120$ and $8t\equiv120\pmod{360}$. The first gives $t\equiv240\pmod{360}$, and then $8t=1920\equiv120$ holds. (Check: at $t=240$ the three terms are $\sin1920^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}2$, $-\sin240^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}2$, $-\sin1680^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}2$.) The smallest positive maximiser is $t=\boxed{240}$.

Problem 12

HCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · Dependent and Independent Events

In the street map below, Ally is located at corner $A$ and Bea is located at corner $B$.

Every $3$ minutes, each picks one of the cardinal directions she could go from her current position, uniformly and independently at random, walks in that direction to the first corner or intersection encountered, and stays there until the next direction selection. What is the probability that Ally reaches intersection $\star$ before Bea?

Show the answer

$\frac{13}{19}$

Show the solution

Solution (Parity and two-step chains)

The map is the graph with vertices $A,\star,E$ (top), $B,C,D$ (bottom) and streets $A\star,\ \star E,\ AB,\ ED,\ BC,\ CD,\ \star C$. Colour $A,C,E$ white and $\star,B,D$ black: every street joins two colours. The walkers move independently, one step per $3$ minutes. Ally starts on white, so she can reach $\star$ only after an odd number of steps; Bea starts on black, so only after an even number. So no ties occur, and Ally wins exactly when she reaches $\star$ at step $2k+1$ while Bea has not reached it in her first $2k$ steps.

Bea. From $B$, two steps lead to $\star$ via $A$ with probability $\frac12\cdot\frac12$ and via $C$ with probability $\frac12\cdot\frac13$; otherwise she is at $B$ or $D$ again. By the left-right symmetry of the map, $D$ behaves like $B$. So she survives each pair of steps with probability $1-\frac14-\frac16=\frac7{12}$, and $P(\text{Bea has not reached }\star\text{ in her first }2k\text{ steps})=\left(\frac7{12}\right)^k$.

Ally. At even times she is at $A$ (or the symmetric $E$) or at $C$. One step from $A$ reaches $\star$ with probability $\frac12$, from $C$ with probability $\frac13$. Two steps without reaching $\star$: from $A$ to $A/E$ with probability $\frac14$ and to $C$ with $\frac14$; from $C$ to $A/E$ with $\frac13$ and to $C$ with $\frac13$. Let $p_k,q_k$ be the probabilities that after $2k$ steps Ally has not reached $\star$ and is at $A/E$, respectively $C$, and put $Y_A=\sum_k\left(\frac7{12}\right)^kp_k$, $Y_C=\sum_k\left(\frac7{12}\right)^kq_k$. The answer is $\frac12Y_A+\frac13Y_C$. From $p_0=1,q_0=0$ and the transitions, \[Y_A=1+\tfrac7{12}W,\qquad Y_C=\tfrac7{12}W,\qquad\text{where } W=\tfrac14Y_A+\tfrac13Y_C.\] Substituting, $W=\frac14+\frac{7}{48}W+\frac{7}{36}W$, so $\frac{95}{144}W=\frac14$, $W=\frac{36}{95}$, $Y_A=\frac{116}{95}$, $Y_C=\frac{21}{95}$. The probability is \[\frac12\cdot\frac{116}{95}+\frac13\cdot\frac{21}{95}=\frac{65}{95}=\boxed{\frac{13}{19}}.\]

Problem 13

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 41: Continued Fractions and Rational Approximation · Diophantine Approximation

Let $c$ be a positive real number. Place $2025$ red circles of radius $1/c$ on the coordinate plane, one centered at each ordered pair of integers $(x,y)$ with $-22\le x,y\le22$. Find the largest $c$ for which every line passing through the origin has a common point with at least two red circles. Express your answer as $\sqrt d$ where $d$ is a square-free integer.

Show the answer

$\sqrt{530}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A bad line and Dirichlet approximation)

The circle centred at the origin meets every line through the origin, so the condition is: every line through $O$ passes within distance $\frac1c$ of some lattice point $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ of the grid ($|x|,|y|\le22$). Let $\delta$ be the largest, over all lines through $O$, of the smallest such distance; then the answer is $c=\frac1\delta$.

The line $y=\frac x{23}$ is bad. The distance from $(x,y)$ to it is $\frac{|x-23y|}{\sqrt{530}}$. If $y=0$ then $|x|\ge1$; if $y\ne0$ then $|x-23y|\ge23|y|-|x|\ge23-22=1$. So every grid point other than $O$ is at distance at least $\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$, and $\delta\ge\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$.

Every line is good with radius $\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$. The grid is symmetric in both axes and in the line $y=x$, so it suffices to treat $y=mx$ with $0\le m\le1$; the distance from $(q,p)$ is $\frac{|p-qm|}{\sqrt{1+m^2}}$.

  • If $m\le\frac1{23}$: the point $(1,0)$ is at distance $\frac m{\sqrt{1+m^2}}\le\frac{1/23}{\sqrt{1+1/529}}=\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$ (the function $\frac{m}{\sqrt{1+m^2}}$ is increasing).

  • If $m\ge\frac1{23}$: the $24$ numbers $0$, $\{m\},\{2m\},\dots,\{22m\}$, $1$ lie in $[0,1]$, which is split into $23$ intervals of length $\frac1{23}$; two of them lie in the same interval, and they are not $0$ and $1$. This gives $1\le q\le22$ and an integer $p$ with $|qm-p|\le\frac1{23}$. Since $0\le qm\le q$, we get $0\le p\le q\le22$, so $(q,p)$ is a grid point, and its distance is $\le\frac{1/23}{\sqrt{1+m^2}}\le\frac{1/23}{\sqrt{1+1/529}}=\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$.

Hence $\delta=\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$, and the largest $c$ is $\boxed{\sqrt{530}}$ ($530=2\cdot5\cdot53$ is square-free).

Solution 2 (Neighbouring fractions)

The circle centred at the origin $O$ meets every line through $O$, so the condition says: every line through $O$ passes within distance $\frac1c$ of some grid point $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ with $|x|,|y|\le22$. We show that every such line passes within $\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$ of a grid point, and that one line does not pass closer; then the largest $c$ is $\sqrt{530}$.

Reduction. The grid is symmetric in both axes and in the line $y=x$; these reflections carry every line through $O$ to a line $y=mx$ with $0\le m\le1$. A grid point $(q,p)$ with $0\le p\le q\le22$, $q\ge1$, gives the slope $\frac pq$; let $\mathcal F$ be the set of these slopes, written in lowest terms. It contains $\frac01$ and $\frac11$.

Neighbours in $\mathcal F$. Let $\frac ab<\frac cd$ be consecutive elements of $\mathcal F$ (in lowest terms). We claim that $bc-ad=1$ and $b+d\ge23$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, there are integers $x,y$ with $bx-ay=1$, and replacing $(x,y)$ by $(x+ka,y+kb)$ we may take $22-b<y\le22$; then $y\ge1$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$. Now $\frac xy-\frac ab=\frac1{by}>0$, so $x>0$; and since $a\le b-1$ (because $\frac ab<\frac cd\le1$), $x=\frac{1+ay}{b}\le\frac{1+(b-1)y}{b}\le y$. So $\frac xy\in\mathcal F$ and $\frac xy>\frac ab$, hence $\frac xy\ge\frac cd$. If $\frac xy>\frac cd$, then $\frac cd-\frac ab\ge\frac1{bd}$ and $\frac xy-\frac cd\ge\frac1{dy}$, so \[\frac1{by}=\frac xy-\frac ab\ge\frac1{bd}+\frac1{dy}=\frac{b+y}{bdy},\] which gives $d\ge b+y>22$, impossible. Hence $\frac cd=\frac xy$, so $bc-ad=1$ and $d=y>22-b$, that is, $b+d\ge23$.

Every line is good. Take $0\le m\le1$. If $m\in\mathcal F$ the line passes through a grid point. Otherwise $\frac ab<m<\frac cd$ for consecutive $\frac ab,\frac cd$ in $\mathcal F$. Let $P=(b,a)$, $Q=(d,c)$ (grid points), let $\alpha<\beta$ be their direction angles (both in $[0^\circ,45^\circ]$) and $\varphi$ the direction angle of the line, $\alpha<\varphi<\beta$. The distances from $P$ and $Q$ to the line are \[f(\varphi)=|OP|\sin(\varphi-\alpha),\qquad g(\varphi)=|OQ|\sin(\beta-\varphi);\] as $\varphi$ increases from $\alpha$ to $\beta$ (an interval shorter than $90^\circ$), $f$ increases strictly and $g$ decreases strictly. So $\min(f,g)$ is largest where $f=g$. On the line through $O$ and $P+Q$, whose direction lies strictly between those of $P$ and $Q$, the distances are \[\frac{|\det(P,P+Q)|}{|P+Q|}=\frac{|\det(P,Q)|}{|P+Q|}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|\det(Q,P+Q)|}{|P+Q|}=\frac{|\det(Q,P)|}{|P+Q|},\] which are equal, both $\frac{bc-ad}{|P+Q|}=\frac1{|P+Q|}$. Hence the line $y=mx$ passes within \[\min(f,g)\le\frac1{|P+Q|}=\frac1{\sqrt{(b+d)^2+(a+c)^2}}\le\frac1{\sqrt{23^2+1^2}}=\frac1{\sqrt{530}}\] of $P$ or $Q$, using $b+d\ge23$ and $a+c\ge c\ge1$ (as $\frac cd>0$).

A bad line. Consider the line $y=\frac x{23}$ (the direction of $P+Q$ for the neighbours $\frac01,\frac1{22}$). The distance from a grid point $(x,y)$ to it is $\frac{|x-23y|}{\sqrt{530}}$. The integer $x-23y$ is not $0$ for $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$: if $y=0$ then $x\ne0$, and if $y\ne0$ then $|23y|\ge23>|x|$. So every grid point other than $O$ is at distance at least $\frac1{\sqrt{530}}$ from this line.

Therefore the largest $c$ is $\boxed{\sqrt{530}}$ ($530=2\cdot5\cdot53$ is square-free).

Problem 14

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Partial Fractions and Decomposition in Sums

Let $F_k$ be the Fibonacci sequence, defined recursively by $F_1=F_2=1$ and $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$ for $n>1$. Evaluate \[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{F_nF_{n+1}F_{n+2}F_{n+4}F_{n+5}F_{n+6}}.\] (Note: The denominator does not contain a factor of $F_{n+3}$.)

Show the answer

$\frac1{960}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Partial fractions in three products)

We use Vajda's identity, valid for all integers $n,i,j\ge0$ (with $F_0=0$): \[F_{n+i}F_{n+j}-F_nF_{n+i+j}=(-1)^nF_iF_j.\] Proof. Fix $n,i$. Both sides, as functions of $j$, satisfy $g(j+2)=g(j+1)+g(j)$, so it suffices to check $j=0$ and $j=1$. For $j=0$ both sides are $0$. For $j=1$ let $D_n=F_{n+i}F_{n+1}-F_nF_{n+i+1}$; then $D_0=F_iF_1=F_i$, and \[D_{n+1}=F_{n+i+1}(F_{n+1}+F_n)-F_{n+1}(F_{n+i+1}+F_{n+i})=F_{n+i+1}F_n-F_{n+1}F_{n+i}=-D_n,\] so $D_n=(-1)^nF_i$, as required.

Let $u=F_nF_{n+6}$, $v=F_{n+2}F_{n+4}$, $w=F_{n+1}F_{n+5}$, $\varepsilon=(-1)^n$. By the identity, $v=u+3\varepsilon$ and $w=u+5\varepsilon$. The $n$th term is $\frac1{uvw}$, and partial fractions in $u$ (with $\varepsilon^2=1$) give \[\frac1{uvw}=\frac{1}{15u}-\frac{1}{6v}+\frac{1}{10w}.\] (Check: the coefficients are $\frac1{(v-u)(w-u)}=\frac1{15}$, $\frac1{(u-v)(w-v)}=-\frac16$, $\frac1{(u-w)(v-w)}=\frac1{10}$.)

Sums of $\frac1{F_mF_{m+2k}}$. Since $F_{m+2}-F_m=F_{m+1}$, we have $\frac1{F_mF_{m+2}}=\frac1{F_mF_{m+1}}-\frac1{F_{m+1}F_{m+2}}$, hence $\sum_{m\ge N}\frac1{F_mF_{m+2}}=\frac1{F_NF_{N+1}}$. Next, Vajda's identity with $n=2k-2$, $i=2$, $j=m$ reads $F_{2k}F_{m+2k-2}-F_{2k-2}F_{m+2k}=F_m$; dividing by $F_mF_{m+2k-2}F_{m+2k}$ gives \[\frac{F_{2k}}{F_mF_{m+2k}}-\frac{F_{2k-2}}{F_mF_{m+2k-2}}=\frac1{F_{m+2k-2}F_{m+2k}},\] and adding these equations for $k=1,2,\dots,K$ (the first term on the left telescopes away since $F_0=0$) yields \[\frac{F_{2K}}{F_mF_{m+2K}}=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac1{F_{m+2j}F_{m+2j+2}}.\] Summing over $m$: \[\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1u=\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac1{F_1F_2}+\frac1{F_3F_4}+\frac1{F_5F_6}\right)=\frac18\cdot\frac{143}{120}=\frac{143}{960},\] \[\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1v=\sum_{m\ge3}\frac1{F_mF_{m+2}}=\frac1{F_3F_4}=\frac16,\qquad \sum_{n\ge1}\frac1w=\sum_{m\ge2}\frac1{F_mF_{m+4}}=\frac13\left(\frac1{F_2F_3}+\frac1{F_4F_5}\right)=\frac{17}{90}.\] Therefore the sum is \[\frac{143}{15\cdot960}-\frac1{36}+\frac{17}{900}=\frac{143-400+272}{14400}=\frac{15}{14400}=\boxed{\frac1{960}}.\]

Solution 2 (Supply the missing factor and telescope)

For $n\ge1$ let \[T_n=\frac1{F_nF_{n+1}F_{n+2}F_{n+3}F_{n+4}F_{n+5}},\] the reciprocal of six consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Then \[T_n-T_{n+1}=\frac{F_{n+6}-F_n}{F_nF_{n+1}F_{n+2}F_{n+3}F_{n+4}F_{n+5}F_{n+6}} .\] Express both $F_{n+6}$ and $F_n$ through $F_{n+2}$ and $F_{n+3}$ with the recurrence: \[F_{n+6}=F_{n+5}+F_{n+4}=2F_{n+4}+F_{n+3}=3F_{n+3}+2F_{n+2},\qquad F_n=F_{n+2}-F_{n+1}=2F_{n+2}-F_{n+3}.\] So $F_{n+6}-F_n=4F_{n+3}$, and cancelling $F_{n+3}$, \[T_n-T_{n+1}=\frac{4}{F_nF_{n+1}F_{n+2}F_{n+4}F_{n+5}F_{n+6}} .\] Thus the $n$th term of the series is $\tfrac14(T_n-T_{n+1})$, and the partial sums telescope: \[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{F_nF_{n+1}F_{n+2}F_{n+4}F_{n+5}F_{n+6}}=\frac14\bigl(T_1-T_{N+1}\bigr).\] Since $F_k\ge1$ for all $k\ge1$ and $F_k\ge k-1$, we have $T_{N+1}\to0$. Hence the sum is \[\frac{T_1}4=\frac1{4\cdot F_1F_2F_3F_4F_5F_6}=\frac1{4\cdot1\cdot1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot8}=\boxed{\frac1{960}}.\]

Problem 15

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

Consider the “circle” of quadratic polynomials $x^2-bx+c$ where \[(b+18)^2+(c+15)^2\le512.\] What is the largest root of a quadratic in this collection?

Show the answer

$7$

Show the solution

Solution (Cauchy-Schwarz)

Suppose $r$ is a root of $x^2-bx+c$ with $(b+18)^2+(c+15)^2\le512$. Then $r$ is real and $c=br-r^2$, so \[(b+18)r-(c+15)=r^2+18r-15.\] By Cauchy-Schwarz, the left side is at most $\sqrt{(b+18)^2+(c+15)^2}\,\sqrt{r^2+1}\le\sqrt{512}\sqrt{r^2+1}$ in absolute value. For $r>1$ the right side $r^2+18r-15$ is positive, so squaring, \[(r^2+18r-15)^2-512(r^2+1)=r^4+36r^3-218r^2-540r-287=(r-7)(r^3+43r^2+83r+41)\le0.\] The cubic factor is positive for $r>0$, so $r\le7$. Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz is possible: take $(b+18,c+15)=\lambda(7,-1)$ with $\lambda=\frac{160}{50}=\frac{16}{5}$, i.e. $b=\frac{22}5$, $c=-\frac{91}5$, which lies on the boundary circle ($\lambda^2\cdot50=512$), and then $x^2-\frac{22}5x-\frac{91}5=\frac15(x-7)(5x+13)$ has root $7$. The largest root is $\boxed{7}$.

Problem 16

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Telescoping Sums and Products

Let \[s=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\arctan\frac{8n}{16n^4+5}.\] Determine $\tan s$.

Show the answer

$\frac12$

Show the solution

Solution (Telescoping arctangents)

Factor $16n^4+4=(4n^2+2)^2-(4n)^2=(4n^2+4n+2)(4n^2-4n+2)$. With $a_n=4n^2+4n+2=(2n+1)^2+1$ and $b_n=4n^2-4n+2=(2n-1)^2+1$ we have $a_n-b_n=8n$ and $1+a_nb_n=16n^4+5$, so (both arctangents in $(0,\frac\pi2)$, $a_n>b_n>0$) \[\arctan\frac{8n}{16n^4+5}=\arctan a_n-\arctan b_n.\] Since $b_{n+1}=a_n$, the sum telescopes: $\sum_{n=1}^N=\arctan a_N-\arctan b_1=\arctan\left((2N+1)^2+1\right)-\arctan2$. Letting $N\to\infty$, $s=\frac\pi2-\arctan2$, so \[\tan s=\cot(\arctan2)=\boxed{\tfrac12}.\]

Problem 17

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry · Distance and Angle in Space

Consider a regular icosahedron inscribed in a sphere $S$ of radius $1$. For each vertex $V$ of the icosahedron, let $C(V)$ be the great circle of $S$ that has a pole at $V$. These great circles partition $S$ into spherical polygons. Let $A$ be the area of one of the spherical pentagons in this pattern. Determine $\cos(A)$. Express your answer in the form $\dfrac{a\sqrt d}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime positive integers and $d$ is a square-free integer.

Show the answer

$\frac{41\sqrt5}{125}$

Show the solution

Solution (Lune angles and Girardś theorem)

The $12$ vertices come in $6$ antipodal pairs, and $C(V)=C(-V)$, so there are $6$ great circles. Fix a vertex $V$ and let $W_1,\dots,W_5$ be its neighbours, in cyclic order. The $6$ axes are $V$ and $W_1,\dots,W_5$ (each other vertex is antipodal to one of these). Adjacent vertices make angle $\theta$ at the centre with $\cos\theta=\frac1{\sqrt5}$, and $V\cdot W_i=\frac1{\sqrt5}$.

The region around $V$. Take coordinates with $V=(0,0,1)$. Its five neighbours form a regular pentagon around $V$ at angle $\theta$, so $W_i=\frac1{\sqrt5}(2e_i,1)$, where $e_1,\dots,e_5$ are unit vectors in the $xy$-plane spaced by $72^\circ$ (indeed $W_i\cdot W_{i+1}=\frac{4\cos72^\circ+1}5=\frac1{\sqrt5}$, using $\cos72^\circ=\frac{\sqrt5-1}4$). Consider the region $\{p:\ p\cdot W_i\ge0,\ i=1,\dots,5\}$ containing $V$. Writing $p=(r,z)$ with $r$ in the plane, the conditions are $2e_i\cdot r+z\ge0$. Adding them and using $e_1+\dots+e_5=0$ gives $z\ge0$; and $z=0$ is impossible, since then $e_i\cdot r\ge0$ for all $i$ with sum $0$ forces $e_i\cdot r=0$ for all $i$, so $r=0$. Hence the region lies in $z>0$, and the central projection $p\mapsto s=\frac rz$ onto the plane $z=1$ (which maps great circles to lines) identifies it with $\{s:\ e_i\cdot s\ge-\frac12,\ i=1,\dots,5\}$, the regular pentagon circumscribed about the disc of radius $\frac12$. So the region is a regular spherical pentagon with one side on each of $C(W_1),\dots,C(W_5)$. Its vertices are at distance $\frac1{2\cos36^\circ}$ from the centre of the planar pentagon, that is, at angular distance $\arctan\frac1{2\cos36^\circ}<32^\circ$ from $V$. So $C(V)$ (the circle $z=0$) does not cut it, and it is one of the faces. By the symmetry of the icosahedron the $12$ such faces, one around each vertex, are congruent; they are distinct, since each lies within $32^\circ$ of its own vertex while distinct vertices are at least $\theta\approx63.4^\circ$ apart. At the end we check that there are no other pentagonal faces.

Its angles. At the vertex where $C(W_i)$ and $C(W_{i+1})$ meet, the pentagon is locally the lune $\{p\cdot W_i\ge0,\ p\cdot W_{i+1}\ge0\}$, whose angle is $\varphi=180^\circ-\angle(W_i,W_{i+1})$. Since $W_i,W_{i+1}$ are adjacent, $\cos\varphi=-\frac1{\sqrt5}$.

Area. By Girard's theorem a spherical pentagon on the unit sphere has area (sum of angles) $-3\pi$, so $A=5\varphi-3\pi$ and $\cos A=-\cos5\varphi$. With $x=\cos\varphi=-\frac1{\sqrt5}$ and $\cos5\varphi=16x^5-20x^3+5x$: \[\cos5\varphi=-\frac{16}{25\sqrt5}+\frac{20}{5\sqrt5}-\frac{5}{\sqrt5}=-\frac{16+25}{25\sqrt5}=-\frac{41}{25\sqrt5}.\] Hence $\cos A=\frac{41}{25\sqrt5}=\boxed{\frac{41\sqrt5}{125}}$.

No other pentagons. Two distinct axes among $V,W_1,\dots,W_5$ have dot product $\pm\frac1{\sqrt5}$ (two vertices of the icosahedron that are neither equal nor antipodal make an angle with cosine $\pm\frac1{\sqrt5}$). Three circles $C(X),C(Y),C(Z)$ through a common point would need $X,Y,Z$ to lie in one plane through the centre, that is, a zero Gram determinant; but with off-diagonal entries $\pm\frac1{\sqrt5}$ the Gram determinant is $1-\frac35\pm\frac{2}{5\sqrt5}>0$. So no three circles meet. Each pair of circles meets in $2$ points, giving $2\binom62=30$ vertices; each circle carries $2\cdot5=10$ of them and is cut into $10$ arcs, giving $60$ edges; by Euler's formula there are $2-30+60=32$ faces. Every face has at least $3$ sides: a face bounded by only two circles would be a lune with corners $P$ and $-P$, but any third circle misses $P$ and $-P$, separates them, and so crosses each side of that lune. The $32$ faces have $2\cdot60=120$ sides in total; the $12$ pentagons account for $60$, so the other $20$ faces share $60$ sides, at least $3$ each, and are all triangles. Hence every pentagon in the pattern is one of the $12$ congruent ones, and $A$ is well defined.

Problem 18

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Linear Recurrences

Let \[M=\begin{pmatrix}51&-46&-22\\34&-31&-14\\38&-34&-17\end{pmatrix}.\] Let $v=M^{1000}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Determine the first (top) component of $v$.

Show the answer

$20008001$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 ($M-I$ is nilpotent)

Let $N=M-I=\begin{pmatrix}50&-46&-22\\34&-32&-14\\38&-34&-18\end{pmatrix}$ and $e=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Then \[Ne=\begin{pmatrix}28\\20\\20\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^2e=N\begin{pmatrix}28\\20\\20\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}40\\32\\24\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^3e=N\begin{pmatrix}40\\32\\24\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.\] Since $I$ and $N$ commute, the binomial theorem gives $M^{1000}e=\sum_k\binom{1000}{k}N^ke=e+1000\,Ne+\binom{1000}2N^2e$. The first component is \[1+1000\cdot28+499500\cdot40=1+28000+19980000=\boxed{20008001}.\]

Solution 2 (Interpolate a quadratic)

The characteristic polynomial of $M$ is $(\lambda-1)^3$ (trace $3$, sum of principal $2\times2$ minors $3$, determinant $1$). By Cayley-Hamilton, $(M-I)^3=0$, so the sequence $f(n)=$ first component of $M^ne$, where $e=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, satisfies $(E-1)^3f=0$ ($E$ the shift), i.e. its third differences vanish. So its second differences are constant, and summing twice shows that $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2$ in $n$. Compute $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=51-22=29$, and $Me=\begin{pmatrix}29\\20\\21\end{pmatrix}$, $f(2)=51\cdot29-46\cdot20-22\cdot21=1479-920-462=97$. The quadratic through $(0,1),(1,29),(2,97)$ is $f(n)=20n^2+8n+1$. Hence $f(1000)=20000000+8000+1=\boxed{20008001}$.

Problem 19

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control · Higher-Order Inclusion-Exclusion

Iris arranged $4$ apples, $4$ bananas, and $4$ caramels into three distinct rows by type, to be eaten one at a time as snacks. Snacks of the same type are indistinguishable. To pick a snack, she must obey the following rules:

  • Take a snack only from the right end of a row.

  • Never choose consecutive snacks of the same type.

How many different ways are there for her to have eaten all $12$ snacks?

Show the answer

$1092$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Insert the apples into a banana-caramel word)

Since snacks of a type are identical and each row is eaten from one end, a way of eating is just a word with four letters each of $a,b,c$ and no two equal adjacent letters.

Delete the $a$'s: we get a word in $b,c$ with four of each, made of $r$ runs (maximal blocks of equal letters). Every adjacency inside a run ($8-r$ of them) must receive exactly one $a$ (two $a$'s in the same gap would be adjacent). The remaining $4-(8-r)=r-4$ letters $a$ go into distinct gaps among the other $9-(8-r)=r+1$ gaps (between runs or at the ends). So a $b,c$-word with $r$ runs contributes $\binom{r+1}{r-4}=\binom{r+1}5$ words, and we need $r\ge4$.

The number of $b,c$-words with $r$ runs: for $r=2k$, $k$ runs of each letter, starting with either letter: $2\binom3{k-1}^2$; for $r=2k+1$, $k+1$ runs of one letter and $k$ of the other: $2\binom3k\binom3{k-1}$ (compositions of $4$ into a given number of parts). Thus \[\begin{array}{c|ccccc} r&4&5&6&7&8\\\hline \text{words}&18&18&18&6&2\\ \binom{r+1}5&1&6&21&56&126 \end{array}\] and the total is $18+108+378+336+252=\boxed{1092}$.

Solution 2 (Inclusion-exclusion on glued blocks)

Since snacks of a type are identical and each row is eaten from its right end, a way of eating is just a word (the types in eating order) with four letters each of $a,b,c$ and no two equal neighbours; we count these words. Cut the four copies of a letter into $i$ consecutive blocks ($\binom3{i-1}$ compositions of $4$ into $i$ parts), similarly $j$ and $k$ blocks for $b$ and $c$, and arrange the $i+j+k$ blocks in a row, where for each letter its blocks appear from left to right in the order given by that letter's chosen composition, whatever their sizes: $\frac{(i+j+k)!}{i!\,j!\,k!}$ ways. Each such object is a word together with a chosen set $T$ of its equal adjacencies (the ones inside blocks), $|T|=12-i-j-k$, and every pair (word, subset $T$ of its equal adjacencies) arises exactly once. Weighting by $(-1)^{|T|}$, a word with $e$ equal adjacencies contributes $\sum_T(-1)^{|T|}=(1-1)^e$, which is $1$ if $e=0$ and $0$ otherwise. Hence the answer is \[\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{4}(-1)^{12-i-j-k}\binom3{i-1}\binom3{j-1}\binom3{k-1}\frac{(i+j+k)!}{i!\,j!\,k!}.\] The summand factors: with $g_i=(-1)^{4-i}\binom3{i-1}\frac1{i!}$, i.e. $g_1=-1$, $g_2=\frac32$, $g_3=-\frac12$, $g_4=\frac1{24}$, the sum is $\sum_s s!\,[x^s]G(x)^3$, where $G(x)=-x+\frac32x^2-\frac12x^3+\frac1{24}x^4$. The coefficients of $x^3,x^4,\dots,x^{12}$ in $G(x)^3$ are \[-1,\ \tfrac92,\ -\tfrac{33}4,\ 8,\ -\tfrac92,\ \tfrac{49}{32},\ -\tfrac{61}{192},\ \tfrac5{128},\ -\tfrac1{384},\ \tfrac1{13824};\] multiplied by $3!,4!,\dots,12!$ they give $-6,\ 108,\ -990,\ 5760,\ -22680,\ 61740,\ -115290,\ 141750,\ -103950,\ 34650$, whose sum is $\boxed{1092}$.
