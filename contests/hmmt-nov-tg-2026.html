Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 10: Logical Deduction and Constraint Puzzles · Contradiction and Elimination

Jerry and Sophie are each given a number. They are both told the following information:

  • Their numbers are distinct integers between $2$ and $50$, inclusive.

  • Jerry's number is an integer power of Sophie's number.

However, neither of them can determine the other's number given this information. Compute the sum of their numbers.

Show the answer

$18$

Show the solution – Who can be unsure

Let Sophie's number be $s$ and Jerry's number be $j=s^k$. Since $j\ne s$ and both lie in $[2,50]$, the exponent satisfies $k\ge 2$ (the exponent $1$ gives $j=s$, and exponents $k\le 0$ give $1$ or a non-integer).

Sophie is unsure. Sophie knows $s$, so she cannot determine $j$ exactly when at least two of the powers $s^2,s^3,\dots$ are at most $50$, that is, when $s^3\le 50$. Hence $s\in\{2,3\}$ (for $s=2$ the candidates are $4,8,16,32$; for $s=3$ they are $9,27$).

Jerry is unsure. Jerry knows $j$, so $j$ must be a $k$-th power ($k\ge2$) of at least two different integers in $[2,50]$. The perfect powers up to $50$ are \[4=2^2,\ 8=2^3,\ 9=3^2,\ 16=2^4=4^2,\ 25=5^2,\ 27=3^3,\ 32=2^5,\ 36=6^2,\ 49=7^2,\] and only $16$ has two different bases. So $j=16$.

Now $s\in\{2,3\}$ and $16$ is a power of $s$, so $s=2$. Conversely, for $(s,j)=(2,16)$ Sophie hesitates between $4,8,16,32$ and Jerry between $2$ and $4$, so the pair is consistent. The sum is $2+16=\boxed{18}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 33: Radicals and Irrational Expressions · Operations with Radicals

Across all positive integers $m$ and $n$, compute the least possible integer value of \[(\sqrt{21}+\sqrt{55})^2+(\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{n})^2.\]

Show the answer

$144$

Show the solution – Cancel the irrational part

Expanding, \[(\sqrt{21}+\sqrt{55})^2+(\sqrt m-\sqrt n)^2=76+m+n+2\bigl(\sqrt{1155}-\sqrt{mn}\bigr).\] If this is an integer, then $q=\sqrt{mn}-\sqrt{1155}$ is rational, and squaring $\sqrt{mn}=q+\sqrt{1155}$ gives $mn=q^2+1155+2q\sqrt{1155}$. Since $1155=3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{1155}$ is irrational, so $q=0$ and $mn=1155$. Conversely, $mn=1155$ makes the value equal to the integer $76+m+n$.

So we must minimise $m+n$ subject to $mn=1155$. The factor pairs are \[(1,1155),\ (3,385),\ (5,231),\ (7,165),\ (11,105),\ (15,77),\ (21,55),\ (33,35),\] and the smallest sum is $33+35=68$ (as it must be, since $m+n\ge 2\sqrt{1155}>67$). The least integer value is $76+68=\boxed{144}$.

Problem 3

MGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations · Composite Angle Chases

Let points $A$ and $D$ lie on parallel lines $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$, respectively, and let points $B$ and $C$ lie between $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ such that $AB=BC=CD$, and $B$, $C$ are on the same side of $AD$. Suppose the angle between $\overline{AB}$ and $\ell_1$ is $20^\circ$, the angle between $\overline{CD}$ and $\ell_2$ is $26^\circ$, and $\angle ABD=84^\circ$. Compute the degree measure of $\angle ACD$.

Show the answer

$75^\circ$

Show solution 1 – Angle chase with two isosceles triangles

Step 0: the configuration is the one in the figure. Take $\ell_1$ above $\ell_2$, reflect in a vertical line if necessary so that $A$ is to the right of $B$, and measure directions counterclockwise from the rightward horizontal. Then $\overrightarrow{BA}$ has direction $20^\circ$. As $\angle ABD=84^\circ$, ray $BD$ has direction $104^\circ$ or $-64^\circ$; since $D$ is below $B$, it is $-64^\circ$. Ray $CD$ goes down to $\ell_2$ at $26^\circ$, so its direction is $-26^\circ$ or $-154^\circ$. Direction $-154^\circ$ is perpendicular to $BD$; then $\angle BDC=90^\circ$ and $BC>CD$, which is false. So $\overrightarrow{CD}$ has direction $-26^\circ$. The sum of two vectors of equal length bisects their directions (it is a diagonal of a rhombus), so $\overrightarrow{BD}=\overrightarrow{BC}+\overrightarrow{CD}$ bisects the directions of $\overrightarrow{BC}$ and $\overrightarrow{CD}$; hence $\overrightarrow{BC}$ has direction $2(-64^\circ)-(-26^\circ)=-102^\circ$, and $\overrightarrow{CB}$ has direction $78^\circ$. Likewise $\overrightarrow{CA}=\overrightarrow{CB}+\overrightarrow{BA}$ has direction $\frac{78^\circ+20^\circ}{2}=49^\circ$. Consequently ray $BD$ (direction $-64^\circ$) lies inside angle $ABC$ (directions $20^\circ$ and $-102^\circ$), ray $CA$ (direction $49^\circ$) lies inside angle $BCD$ (directions $78^\circ$ and $-26^\circ$), and $A$, $D$, seen from $B$ in directions $20^\circ$ and $-64^\circ$, both lie on the right of line $BC$ (directions strictly between $-102^\circ$ and $78^\circ$). Let $\beta=\angle ABC$ and $\gamma=\angle BCD$.

Step 1: $\beta+\gamma=226^\circ$. Extend line $BC$ beyond $B$ to meet $\ell_1$ at $X$ and beyond $C$ to meet $\ell_2$ at $Y$. In triangle $ABX$ the angle at $A$ is the angle between $AB$ and $\ell_1$, namely $20^\circ$, and $\angle ABC$ is the exterior angle at $B$, so $\beta=20^\circ+\angle BXA$. Similarly, in triangle $CDY$, $\gamma=26^\circ+\angle CYD$. The lines $\ell_1,\ell_2$ are parallel and $A$, $D$ lie on the same side of the transversal $XY$, so $\angle BXA+\angle CYD=180^\circ$ (co-interior angles). Hence $\beta+\gamma=226^\circ$.

Step 2: the isosceles triangles. Since $BA=BC$, triangle $ABC$ is isosceles and $\angle BCA=\frac{180^\circ-\beta}{2}$. Since $CB=CD$, $\angle CBD=\frac{180^\circ-\gamma}{2}$. Ray $BD$ lies inside angle $ABC$, so \[84^\circ=\angle ABD=\angle ABC-\angle CBD=\beta-90^\circ+\frac{\gamma}{2},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \beta+\frac{\gamma}{2}=174^\circ.\] Subtracting this from $\beta+\gamma=226^\circ$ gives $\gamma=104^\circ$ and then $\beta=122^\circ$.

Step 3. Ray $CA$ lies inside angle $BCD$, so \[\angle ACD=\angle BCD-\angle BCA=104^\circ-\frac{180^\circ-122^\circ}{2}=104^\circ-29^\circ=\boxed{75^\circ}.\]

Show solution 2 – Sums of unit vectors

Take $AB=BC=CD=1$ and measure directions as angles counterclockwise from the direction of the arrows on $\ell_1,\ell_2$. Write $e(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Take $\ell_1$ above $\ell_2$ and reflect in a vertical line if necessary so that $\overrightarrow{BA}=e(20^\circ)$. Since $\angle ABD=84^\circ$, $\overrightarrow{BD}$ has direction $104^\circ$ or $-64^\circ$; as $D$ is below $B$, it is $-64^\circ$. The vector $\overrightarrow{CD}$ points down to $\ell_2$ at $26^\circ$, so it is $e(-26^\circ)$ or $e(-154^\circ)$. The direction $-154^\circ$ is perpendicular to $BD$; then $\angle BDC=90^\circ$ and the hypotenuse $BC$ would be longer than $CD$, which is false. So $\overrightarrow{CD}=e(-26^\circ)$. Let $\overrightarrow{BC}=e(\psi)$.

The key fact: for unit vectors, \[e(\alpha)+e(\beta)=2\cos\tfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}\;e\bigl(\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\bigr),\] so the sum points along one of the two directions $\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}$, $\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}+180^\circ$ (the bisector of the two vectors).

Finding $\psi$. $\overrightarrow{BD}=e(\psi)+e(-26^\circ)$ points along the bisector of directions $\psi$ and $-26^\circ$. It has direction $-64^\circ$, so $\frac{\psi-26^\circ}{2}\equiv-64^\circ\pmod{180^\circ}$, i.e. $\psi\equiv-102^\circ\pmod{360^\circ}$. (Check: $\cos\frac{-102^\circ+26^\circ}{2}=\cos 38^\circ>0$, so the sum really points along $-64^\circ$.)

The angle at $C$. $\overrightarrow{CA}=\overrightarrow{CB}+\overrightarrow{BA}=e(78^\circ)+e(20^\circ)=2\cos29^\circ\,e(49^\circ)$, which has direction $49^\circ$. Also $\overrightarrow{CD}$ has direction $-26^\circ$. Therefore \[\angle ACD=49^\circ-(-26^\circ)=\boxed{75^\circ}.\]

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Closed Forms and Hidden Structure

Let $F_0,F_1,F_2,\dots$ be a sequence of positive integers with $F_0=F_1=F_2=\dots=F_{2025}=1$ and $F_i=F_{i-2025}+F_{i-2026}$ for all positive integers $i\ge 2026$. Compute the number of positive integers $n<2026$ such that there exists a positive integer $k$ satisfying $F_k=n$.

Show the answer

$64$

Show solution 1 – A closed form by induction

Write $L=2025$, so $F_0=\dots=F_L=1$ and $F_i=F_{i-L}+F_{i-L-1}$ for $i\ge L+1$. Every index $n\ge L$ is $n=QL+r$ with $Q\ge1$ and $0\le r\le L-1$.

Claim. For $1\le Q\le L$ and $0\le r\le L-1$, \[F_{QL+r}=2^{Q-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{Q-1}{j}.\] Proof by induction on $Q$. For $Q=1$: $F_L=1=2^0$, and for $r\ge1$, $F_{L+r}=F_r+F_{r-1}=2=1+\binom00$. Let $Q\ge2$. If $r\ge1$, then by the induction hypothesis and Pascal's rule \[F_{QL+r}=F_{(Q-1)L+r}+F_{(Q-1)L+r-1}=2^{Q-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{Q-2}{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-2}\binom{Q-2}{j}=2^{Q-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{Q-1}{j}.\] If $r=0$, then $F_{QL}=F_{(Q-1)L}+F_{(Q-2)L+(L-1)}$. The first term is $2^{Q-2}$. The second is $F_{L-1}=1=2^{0}$ if $Q=2$, and for $Q\ge3$ it is $2^{Q-3}+\sum_{j=0}^{L-2}\binom{Q-3}{j}=2^{Q-3}+2^{Q-3}$ because $L-2\ge Q-3$. Either way $F_{QL}=2^{Q-1}$. $\square$

Reading off the values. Put $m=Q-1$ and $T_r(m)=\sum_{j<r}\binom mj$. As $r$ goes from $0$ to $m+1$, $T_r(m)$ increases strictly from $0$ to $2^m$, and it stays $2^m$ for larger $r$. So for $m\le L-2$ (so that $r=m+1\le L-1$ is an allowed index) the values in block $Q=m+1$ are exactly the $m+2$ numbers $2^m+T_r(m)$ $(0\le r\le m+1)$, running from $2^m$ to $2^{m+1}$; the indices $n<L$ give only the value $1$, which is also the value for $m=0$, $r=0$. Thus the values lying in $[2^m,2^{m+1})$ are the $m+1$ numbers with $0\le r\le m$, and every value has a positive index (the value $1$ is $F_1$).

Values below $2026$: each $m=0,1,\dots,9$ contributes $m+1$ values (all below $1024$), in total $1+2+\dots+10=55$. For $m=10$ we need $1024+T_r(10)\le2025$, i.e. $T_r(10)\le1001$; since $T_8(10)=1+10+45+120+210+252+210+120=968$ and $T_9(10)=1013$, this gives $r=0,\dots,8$: $9$ values. For $m\ge11$ (that is, $12\le Q\le L$) every value is at least $2^{11}=2048$. The Claim does not cover indices beyond $L^2+L-1$, but these terms are also at least $2048$: the $L+1$ consecutive terms with indices $12L,\dots,13L$ are at least $2048$ by the Claim, and every later term $F_i=F_{i-L}+F_{i-L-1}$ is a sum of two earlier terms with indices at least $12L$, so by strong induction it is at least $2048$ too. The answer is $55+9=\boxed{64}$.

Show solution 2 – Generating function

Write $L=2025$ and $G(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}F_nx^n$. The coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1-x^L-x^{L+1})G(x)$ is $F_n-F_{n-L}-F_{n-L-1}$ (terms with negative index are $0$): it is $1$ for $n\le L-1$, it is $F_L-F_0=0$ for $n=L$, and it is $0$ for $n\ge L+1$ by the recurrence. Hence \[G(x)=\frac{1+x+\dots+x^{L-1}}{1-x^L(1+x)}=(1+x+\dots+x^{L-1})\sum_{q\ge0}x^{qL}(1+x)^q,\] so \[F_n=\sum_{q\ge0}\ \sum_{j=0}^{q}\binom qj\,\bigl[\,0\le n-qL-j\le L-1\,\bigr].\] Take $n=QL+r$ with $1\le Q<L$ and $0\le r\le L-1$. For $q=Q$ the bracket holds exactly when $j\le r$; for $q=Q-1$ it holds exactly when $j\ge r+1$; for $q\le Q-2$ we have $n-qL-j\ge 2L+r-(Q-2)>L-1$, and for $q\ge Q+1$ we have $n-qL-j<0$. So \[F_{QL+r}=\sum_{j\le r}\binom Qj+\sum_{j\ge r+1}\binom{Q-1}{j}=\sum_{j\le r}\binom{Q-1}{j}+\sum_{j\le r-1}\binom{Q-1}{j}+\sum_{j\ge r+1}\binom{Q-1}{j}=2^{Q-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{Q-1}{j},\] using Pascal's rule $\binom Qj=\binom{Q-1}j+\binom{Q-1}{j-1}$. For $n<L$, $F_n=1$.

Counting. With $m=Q-1$ and $T_r(m)=\sum_{j<r}\binom mj$, the values for $Q=m+1$ are $2^m+T_r(m)$; as $r$ runs over $0,\dots,m+1$ these increase strictly from $2^m$ to $2^{m+1}$ and then stay at $2^{m+1}$. Hence for $0\le m\le10$ each interval $[2^m,2^{m+1})$ contains exactly $m+1$ values of the sequence (the terms with $Q\ge m+2$ are at least $2^{m+1}$; for $m=0$ this is the value $1=F_1$). Below $1024$ we get $\sum_{m=0}^{9}(m+1)=55$ values. In $[1024,2026)$ we need $T_r(10)\le 1001$: the partial sums $0,1,11,56,176,386,638,848,968$ qualify and the next one, $1013$, does not, giving $9$ more values. For $12\le Q<L$ (i.e. $m\ge11$) every value is at least $2^{11}=2048$. The remaining indices $n\ge L^2$ also give terms at least $2048$: the $L+1$ consecutive terms with indices $12L,\dots,13L$ are at least $2048$ by the formula, and each later term $F_n=F_{n-L}+F_{n-L-1}$ is a sum of two earlier terms with indices at least $12L$, so by strong induction it is at least $2048$. Total: $\boxed{64}$.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 55: Three-Dimensional and Spatial Geometry · Distance and Angle in Space

A right circular cone with apex $A$ has base radius $1$ and height $7$. Two perpendicular planes are tangent to the cone's lateral surface along segments $\overline{AP}$ and $\overline{AQ}$, where $P$ and $Q$ lie on the base of the cone. Compute $PQ$.

Show the answer

$\frac{10}{7}$

Show solution 1 – Normal vectors

Place the base in the plane $z=0$ with centre at the origin and the apex at $A=(0,0,7)$. A point of the base circle is $P=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0)$. The plane tangent to the lateral surface along $AP$ contains the generator $AP$ and the tangent line to the base circle at $P$, whose direction is $(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0)$. The vector \[\mathbf n(\varphi)=(7\cos\varphi,\,7\sin\varphi,\,1)\] is perpendicular to both: $\mathbf n\cdot(P-A)=7\cos^2\varphi+7\sin^2\varphi-7=0$ and $\mathbf n\cdot(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0)=0$. So it is a normal vector of that tangent plane.

Two planes are perpendicular exactly when their normals are. For $P$ at angle $\varphi_1$ and $Q$ at angle $\varphi_2$, \[\mathbf n(\varphi_1)\cdot\mathbf n(\varphi_2)=49\cos(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)+1=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\cos(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)=-\tfrac1{49}.\] $P$ and $Q$ lie on the unit circle, so \[PQ^2=2-2\cos(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)=2+\tfrac{2}{49}=\tfrac{100}{49},\qquad PQ=\boxed{\tfrac{10}{7}}.\]

Show solution 2 – The dihedral angle along the common line

Let $O$ be the centre of the base and $\theta=\frac12\angle POQ$, so $PQ=2\sin\theta$. If $P,Q$ were diametrically opposite, the two tangent planes would be mirror images in the plane through the axis perpendicular to $PQ$, each making the small angle $\arctan\frac17$ with the axis, so they would meet at angle $2\arctan\frac17<90^\circ$; hence $0<\theta<90^\circ$, and the tangent lines to the base circle at $P$ and $Q$ meet at a point $T$ on ray $OT$ bisecting angle $POQ$, with $PT=QT=\tan\theta$.

The first tangent plane contains $AP$ and the tangent line $PT$, the second contains $AQ$ and $QT$, so the planes meet along line $AT$. Moreover $PT\perp OP$ and $PT\perp AO$ (the axis is perpendicular to the base), so $PT\perp$ plane $AOP$ and $\angle APT=90^\circ$; similarly $\angle AQT=90^\circ$.

Let $H$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $AT$. Reflection in the plane $AOT$ swaps $P$ and $Q$ and fixes line $AT$, so $H$ is also the foot from $Q$, and $QH=PH$. The segments $HP$ and $HQ$ lie in the two planes and are perpendicular to their common line, so $\angle PHQ$ is the angle between the planes: $90^\circ$. Hence $PQ=\sqrt2\,PH$.

In right triangle $APT$, $AP=\sqrt{1+49}=5\sqrt2$, $PT=\tan\theta$ and $AT=\sqrt{50+\tan^2\theta}$, so $PH=\frac{AP\cdot PT}{AT}$. The condition $2\sin\theta=\sqrt2\,PH$ becomes \[2\sin\theta=\frac{10\tan\theta}{\sqrt{50+\tan^2\theta}}\iff 50+\tan^2\theta=\frac{25}{\cos^2\theta}=25+25\tan^2\theta\iff\tan^2\theta=\frac{25}{24}.\] Then $\sin^2\theta=\frac{25}{49}$, $\sin\theta=\frac57$, and $PQ=2\sin\theta=\boxed{\frac{10}{7}}$.

Problem 6

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent · Comparing Valuations Across Terms

Given that $a$ and $b$ are integers greater than $1$ such that $\frac{a+b^2}{a^2}$ is an integer, compute the least possible value of $b$.

Show the answer

$35$

Show the solution – Prime-by-prime valuations

We need $a^2\mid a+b^2$. Let $p$ be a prime dividing $a$, with $e=v_p(a)\ge1$ and $f=v_p(b)$.

  • If $2f<e$, then $v_p(a+b^2)=2f<e<2e$, impossible.

  • If $2f>e$, then $v_p(a+b^2)=e<2e$, impossible.

So $2f=e$ for every prime $p\mid a$. Hence $a=c^2$ is a perfect square ($c>1$), $c\mid b$, and for each prime $p\mid c$ we have $v_p(b)=v_p(c)$ exactly. Writing $b=cm$, the last condition says $\gcd(m,c)=1$, and the divisibility becomes \[c^4\mid c^2+c^2m^2\iff c^2\mid m^2+1 .\] (Conversely, $c^2\mid m^2+1$ forces $\gcd(m,c)=1$, and then $a=c^2$, $b=cm$ work.)

So we want the least $b=cm$ with $c\ge2$, $m\ge1$, $c^2\mid m^2+1$. Since $m^2+1\equiv1$ or $2\pmod4$, $c$ is odd; since $-1$ is not a square modulo $3$, $7$ or $11$, none of these divides $c$. Also $m=1$ is impossible ($c^2\mid2$), so $m\ge2$ and $b\ge2c$. If $b<35$ then $c\le17$, so $c\in\{5,13,17\}$:

  • $c=5$, $m\le6$: $m^2+1\in\{2,5,10,17,26,37\}$, none divisible by $25$;

  • $c=13$ or $17$: $m\le2$, and $m^2+1\le5$.

Hence $b\ge35$. Equality: $c=5$, $m=7$, i.e. $a=25$, $b=35$, and indeed $\frac{25+35^2}{25^2}=\frac{1250}{625}=2$. The least $b$ is $\boxed{35}$.

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Permutations and Arrangements

Alice has $8$ balls labeled $1,2,3,\dots,8$ and $8$ boxes labeled $1,2,3,\dots,8$. Compute the number of ways for Alice to place exactly $1$ ball in every box such that exactly $1$ ball is labeled with a strictly higher number than its box.

Show the answer

$247$

Show solution 1 – Cycle structure

Let $\pi(i)$ be the label of the ball in box $i$; $\pi$ is a permutation of $\{1,\dots,8\}$, and we need exactly one $i$ with $\pi(i)>i$ (an excedance).

Every cycle of length at least $2$ contains an excedance. If $M$ is the largest element of such a cycle and $\pi(k)=M$, then $k\ne M$, so $k<M=\pi(k)$.

Hence $\pi$ has exactly one cycle of length $\ge2$; all other points are fixed. Follow this cycle starting from its maximum $M$: each step $x\mapsto\pi(x)$ is either an ascent or a descent, and the step into $M$ is an ascent. As there is only one ascent, all other steps descend, so the cycle visits its elements in decreasing order from $M$ down to its minimum and then returns to $M$. Thus the cycle is completely determined by its set of elements $S$, $|S|\ge2$; conversely, for every such $S$ this cycle has exactly one excedance (minimum $\to$ maximum).

The number of subsets of $\{1,\dots,8\}$ with at least two elements is $2^8-1-8=\boxed{247}$.

Show solution 2 – Filling the boxes in order

Suppose box $i$ is the only box whose ball has a higher label, and its ball is $j>i$. Every other box $k$ must receive a ball with label at most $k$. Fill the boxes $1,2,\dots,8$ in this order.

When we reach box $k\ne i$, the boxes $1,\dots,k-1$ hold $k-1$ balls, all with labels at most $k-1$ except that box $i$ (if $i<k$) holds $j$. Counting labels $\le k$ that are still unused:

  • for $k<i$: exactly $1$ (so box $k$ gets ball $k$);

  • for $i<k<j$: exactly $2$ (labels $\le k$ are $k$ in number, and only $k-2$ of them are used, since box $i$ holds $j>k$);

  • for $k\ge j$ (with $k\ne i$): exactly $1$ (now $j\le k$ is also used).

Box $i$ itself gets $j$, which is unused because boxes $1,\dots,i-1$ hold $1,\dots,i-1$. These counts do not depend on earlier choices, and after box $8$ every ball has been placed. So for each pair $i<j$ there are $2^{\,j-i-1}$ arrangements, and the total is \[\sum_{d=1}^{7}(8-d)\,2^{d-1}=7+12+20+32+48+64+64=\boxed{247}.\]

Problem 8

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry · Direct Similarity

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB=13$, $BC=14$, and $CA=15$. Point $D$ is chosen on segment $BC$. Points $P$ and $Q$ are such that $\angle PBC=90^\circ$, $\angle QCB=90^\circ$, and $APDQ$ is a kite with axis of symmetry $PQ$. Suppose that $D$ is chosen such that $\angle PAQ$ is maximized. Compute $AD$.

Show the answer

$4\sqrt{10}$

Show solution 1 – A similar triangle with a sliding base

Since $APDQ$ is a kite with axis $PQ$, $D$ is the reflection of $A$ in line $PQ$: $PA=PD$, $QA=QD$, and $PQ$ bisects angles $APD$ and $AQD$.

Let $D'$ and $D''$ be the reflections of $D$ in $B$ and in $C$ (on line $BC$). As $PB\perp BC$, $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $DD'$, so $PD'=PD=PA$: $P$ is the circumcentre of triangle $ADD'$. Similarly $Q$ is the circumcentre of $ADD''$.

Let $F$ be the foot of the altitude from $A$; from $13^2-BF^2=15^2-(14-BF)^2$ we get $BF=5$, $AF=12$. Since $D'$ lies on the opposite side of $B$ from $C$ and $F$ lies on segment $BC$, the angle $\angle AD'D$ is acute; an inscribed angle subtending a chord is acute exactly when its vertex and the centre lie on the same side of the chord, so $P$ and $D'$ lie on the same side of $AD$ and the central angle is $\angle APD=2\angle AD'D$. As $PQ$ bisects $\angle APD$, $\angle APQ=\angle AD'D=\angle AD'D''$. In the same way $\angle AQP=\angle AD''D'$. (If $D=B$, then $D'=B$ and the circle centred at $P$ is tangent to $BC$ at $B$; the tangent-chord angle gives the same equality, and similarly if $D=C$.) Therefore triangles $APQ$ and $AD'D''$ are similar and \[\angle PAQ=\angle D'AD''.\]

Now $D'=2B-D$ and $D''=2C-D$, so $D'D''=2BC=28$ for every $D$: a segment of fixed length $28$ slides along line $BC$, and its midpoint $B+C-D$ is the reflection of $D$ in the midpoint of $BC$. Put coordinates along $BC$ with $F$ at $0$, so the segment is $[t-14,\,t+14]$ and $A$ is at height $12$. For a segment $[u,v]$ on the axis seen from $(0,h)$, the angle $\theta$ satisfies $\cot\theta=\frac{h^2+uv}{h(v-u)}$ (dot product over cross product of the vectors to $u$ and $v$). Problem 10

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · Combinatorial Decomposition

Let $N$ denote the number of ways to tile a $2\times 199$ grid with O-tetrominoes, T-tetrominoes, and L-trominoes, shown below. Compute the remainder when $N$ is divided by $128$. (The tiles can be rotated, and tilings that differ by rotation or reflection are considered distinct.)

Show the answer

$44$

Show solution 1 – Recurrences and a generating function

Let $a_n$ be the number of tilings of the $2\times n$ grid, and let $c_m$ be the number of tilings of a $2\times m$ grid with one extra cell attached to the left of its top row (by reflection, the same count holds with the extra cell on the bottom row). Inside a $2$-row strip the T-tetromino must lie as three cells in one row plus the middle cell of the other row, and the L-tromino occupies three cells of a $2\times2$ box.

The first column of a $2\times n$ grid. If an O covers it, $a_{n-2}$ tilings remain. If an L covers both cells of column $1$ and one cell of column $2$ ($2$ ways), the rest is a $2\times(n-2)$ grid with one extra cell on the left: $c_{n-2}$ tilings. Any other tile meets column 1 in a single cell; it is then an L with both cells of column 2 or a T whose row of three starts in column 1, and in both cases it also covers the cell of column 2 in the other row. Then the other cell of column 1 has both neighbours covered and cannot be tiled. So $a_n=a_{n-2}+2c_{n-2}$ $(n\ge2)$, with $a_0=1$, $a_1=0$.

The extra cell $e$. The tile covering $e$ cannot use the cell below $e$. Either it is the L formed by $e$ and the whole next column (leaving a $2\times(m-1)$ grid), or it is the T formed by $e$, the next two cells of the top row and the bottom cell of the first column (leaving a $2\times(m-2)$ grid with one extra cell). So $c_m=a_{m-1}+c_{m-2}$ $(m\ge1)$, with $c_0=0$, $c_{-1}=0$.

With $A(x)=\sum a_nx^n$ and $C(x)=\sum c_mx^m$ these say $A=1+x^2A+2x^2C$ and $C=xA+x^2C$. Hence $C=\frac{xA}{1-x^2}$ and \[A=\frac{1}{1-x^2-\frac{2x^3}{1-x^2}}=\frac{1}{1-x^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{2x^3}{(1-x^2)^2}}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{2^kx^{3k}}{(1-x^2)^{2k+1}}.\] Since $[x^{2M}](1-x^2)^{-(2k+1)}=\binom{M+2k}{2k}$, we get $a_n=\sum_k 2^k\binom{\frac{n-3k}2+2k}{2k}$ over $k$ with $n-3k$ even and nonnegative. For $n=199$, $k$ is odd, and terms with $k\ge7$ are divisible by $128$: \[a_{199}\equiv 2\binom{100}{2}+8\binom{101}{6}+32\binom{102}{10}\pmod{128}.\] By Legendre's formula $v_2\binom{101}{6}=97-4-89=4$ and $v_2\binom{102}{10}=98-8-88=2$, so the last two terms are divisible by $128$. Finally $2\binom{100}2=9900=128\cdot77+44$, so $N\equiv\boxed{44}\pmod{128}$.

Show solution 2 – Fault-free blocks and stars and bars

Cut a tiling along every vertical grid line that no tile crosses. The pieces are blocks, tilings of a $2\times\ell$ grid crossed by no vertical line. We find all blocks. Inside a $2$-row strip a T-tetromino is three cells of one row plus the middle cell of the other row, and an L-tromino is three cells of a $2\times2$ box.

The left column of a block is covered either by an O (and then the O alone is a block of length $2$) or by an L that also covers one cell of the second column ($2$ choices). In the second case the boundary has one protruding cell, and the single uncovered cell of column 2 (the other cell of that column is occupied by the first L) must be covered. Either an L fills it together with the whole next column, which ends the block, or a T fills it with two more cells of its row and the middle cell of the other row, which leaves the same situation two columns further on with the protrusion in the other row. (Any other tile isolates a cell.) Hence the blocks are: the O (length $2$, one way) and, for each $t\ge0$, the block L, $t$ T's, L of length $3+2t$ (two ways, decided by the first L).

So $N$ is a sum over sequences of blocks: blocks of length $2$ (weight $1$) and odd blocks of length $3+2t$ (weight $2$). A sequence with $k$ odd blocks has weight $2^k$, and since $199$ is odd, $k$ is odd. Modulo $128$ only $k=1,3,5$ matter.

For fixed $k$, let the odd blocks have lengths $3+2t_1,\dots,3+2t_k$ and let there be $j$ blocks of length $2$. Then $t_1+\dots+t_k+j=M:=\frac{199-3k}{2}$. Encode the sequence as a word: write $\star$ for each $2$-block and $|\,\star^{t_i}\,|$ for each odd block. This is a word with $M$ stars and $2k$ bars, and every such word decodes uniquely (pair the bars as 1st–2nd, 3rd–4th, ...). So there are $\binom{M+2k}{2k}$ sequences, and \[N\equiv 2\binom{100}{2}+8\binom{101}{6}+32\binom{102}{10}\pmod{128}.\] Counting factors of $2$ (Legendre): $v_2(101!)=97$, $v_2(6!)=4$, $v_2(95!)=89$, so $16\mid\binom{101}6$; and $v_2(102!)=98$, $v_2(10!)=8$, $v_2(92!)=88$, so $4\mid\binom{102}{10}$. Thus $N\equiv 2\cdot4950=9900\equiv\boxed{44}\pmod{128}$.

So the degree-$8$ polynomial \[q(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\binom xk\] satisfies $q(n)=H_n$ for $n=1,\dots,8$, and $q(0)=0$. Then $p-q$ has degree $8$ and vanishes at $2,\dots,8$, so $p(x)-q(x)=(x-2)(x-3)\cdots(x-8)(\alpha x+\beta)$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^8$ and $x^7$ (those of $p$ are $0$): \[[x^8]q=-\frac{1}{8\cdot8!},\qquad [x^7]q=-\frac18\cdot\frac{-(0+1+\dots+7)}{8!}+\frac17\cdot\frac1{7!}=\frac{28}{8\cdot8!}+\frac1{7\cdot7!},\] so $\alpha=\frac{1}{8\cdot8!}$ and $\beta-35\alpha=-[x^7]q$, i.e. $\beta=\frac{7}{8\cdot8!}-\frac{1}{7\cdot7!}$. Finally \[p(0)=q(0)+(-2)(-3)\cdots(-8)\,\beta=-8!\,\beta=-\frac78+\frac87=\boxed{\frac{15}{56}}.\]

Problem 26

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

Sophia has a $5\times 5$ grid of dots, and selects $10$ of them uniformly at random. Given that she selects exactly $2$ dots in each row and each column, compute the probability that some $4$ selected dots form a rectangle with grid-aligned sides.

Show the answer

$\frac5{17}$

Show solution 1 – Cycles in a bipartite graph

Given the condition, the selection is uniform among the $0$-$1$ matrices with all row and column sums $2$. View such a matrix as a graph joining row $i$ to column $j$ when $(i,j)$ is selected. Every vertex has degree $2$, so the graph is a disjoint union of cycles; cycles alternate rows and columns, and a cycle through $k$ rows and $k$ columns needs $k\ge2$. A rectangle of selected dots means two rows sharing the same two columns, i.e. a $4$-cycle ($k=2$).

The number of single cycles through $k$ given rows and $k$ given columns is $\frac{k!\,(k-1)!}{2}$ (list the rows in cyclic order starting at a fixed row, $(k-1)!$ ways, interleave the columns, $k!$ ways, and divide by $2$ for the direction). The rows split into cycles as $5$ or $2+3$:

  • one $10$-cycle: $\frac{5!\,4!}{2}=1440$ matrices;

  • a $4$-cycle and a $6$-cycle: choose its $2$ rows and $2$ columns, $10\cdot10$ ways, then $1\cdot\frac{3!\,2!}2=6$ ways: $600$ matrices.

The total is $2040$ and the rectangle occurs exactly in the $600$, so the probability is $\frac{600}{2040}=\boxed{\frac5{17}}$.

Show solution 2 – Rows as edges between columns

Given the condition, the selection is uniform among the $0$-$1$ matrices with all row and column sums $2$. Let each row be an edge joining the two columns it uses. We get a multigraph on the $5$ columns with $5$ edges labelled by the rows, no loops, and every column of degree $2$. A rectangle means two rows using the same pair of columns: a double edge.

A loopless multigraph with all degrees $2$ is a union of cycles, where a double edge counts as a cycle of length $2$. On $5$ vertices the cycle lengths are $5$ or $2+3$.

  • A $5$-cycle: $\frac{4!}{2}=12$ shapes, and the $5$ row labels can be placed on its edges in $5!=120$ ways, all giving different matrices: $1440$.

  • A double edge plus a triangle: $\binom52=10$ choices of the double edge; $5!$ placements of labels, but exchanging the labels of the two parallel edges gives the same matrix: $60$ each, $600$ in all.

So there are $2040$ matrices, $600$ of them with a rectangle, and the probability is $\boxed{\frac{5}{17}}$.

Problem 27

HNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

Crystal writes two $5$-digit positive integers in her journal using every digit exactly once (where leading zeros are not allowed). Compute the maximum possible sum of the digits of the absolute difference between her numbers.

Show the answer

$40$

Show the solution – Borrows in the column subtraction

Let the numbers be $a>b$ and $D=a-b$. Index digit positions $1$ (units) to $5$, write $a_i,b_i,D_i$ for the digits, and let $\beta_i\in\{0,1\}$ be the borrow out of position $i$ in the column subtraction ($\beta_0=\beta_5=0$). Then \[D_i=a_i-b_i-\beta_{i-1}+10\beta_i .\] A borrow at position $i$ means $a_i-\beta_{i-1}<b_i$, and since $a_i\ne b_i$ this gives $a_i<b_i$.

Fact 1. If $\beta_{i-1}=1$ then $D_i\le8$: without a borrow out, $D_i=a_i-b_i-1\le8$; with one, $a_i<b_i$ and $D_i=9+a_i-b_i\le8$. Also $D_5=a_5-b_5-\beta_4\le8$, because $b_5\ge1$. Hence \[S(D)\le 9+\sum_{i=2}^{4}(9-\beta_{i-1})+8=44-(\beta_1+\beta_2+\beta_3).\]

Fact 2. Summing the column equations, $S(D)=S(a)-S(b)+9k$ with $k=\beta_1+\dots+\beta_4$. As every digit is used once, $S(a)+S(b)=45$, so $S(D)=45-2S(b)+9k$, and $S(b)\ge0+1+2+3+4=10$.

Suppose $S(D)\ge41$.

  • $k\le1$: $S(D)\le45-20+9=34$. Impossible.

  • $k=2$: $S(b)\le11$, so $b$'s digits are $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ or $\{0,1,2,3,5\}$. Then at most one position has $a_i<b_i$ (only $a_i=4<b_i=5$ is possible), but each of the two borrows needs such a position. Impossible.

  • $k=3$: $S(D)=72-2S(b)$ is even and, by Fact 1, at most $44-2=42$; so $S(D)=42$, and Fact 1 must be tight. Then $\beta_4=1$ (three borrows, only two among positions $1$–$3$) and $D_5=8$, i.e. $a_5-b_5=9$, forcing $b_5=0$. Impossible.

  • $k=4$: by Fact 1, $S(D)\le41$, so equality holds throughout, again $D_5=a_5-b_5-1=8$ forces $b_5=0$. Impossible.

Hence $S(D)\le40$, and $82469-13570=68899$ has digit sum $40$. The answer is $\boxed{40}$.

Problem 28

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value · Dependent and Independent Events

One hundred players $P_1,P_2,\dots,P_{100}$ are playing a game. Each player $P_i$ has an unfair coin that lands heads with probability $p_i$. On the $n^{\text{th}}$ turn of the game, player $P_n$ flips their coin, where indices are taken modulo $100$. If it lands heads, the game ends immediately and they win. Otherwise, the game continues with the next player's turn.

Given that each player has a $\frac{1}{100}$ probability of winning and that $p_{100}=\frac67$, compute $p_1$.

Show the answer

$\frac{6}{601}$

Show the solution – Equal first-round chances

Let $q_i=\prod_{j<i}(1-p_j)$ be the probability that the first round reaches $P_i$ ($q_1=1$), and $Q=q_{101}$, the probability that a whole round passes with no heads ($Q<1$ since $p_{100}>0$). Player $P_i$ wins with probability $q_ip_i(1+Q+Q^2+\cdots)=\frac{q_ip_i}{1-Q}$. These are all equal, so $q_ip_i=c$ is the same for every $i$. Then $q_{i+1}=q_i(1-p_i)=q_i-c$, so $q_i=1-(i-1)c$, and \[p_{100}=\frac{c}{q_{100}}=\frac{c}{1-99c}=\frac67\ \Longrightarrow\ 7c=6-594c\ \Longrightarrow\ c=\frac6{601}.\] (Each winning probability is then $\frac{c}{1-q_{101}}=\frac{c}{100c}=\frac1{100}$, as required.) Finally $p_1=\frac{c}{q_1}=\boxed{\frac{6}{601}}$.

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure · Geometric Reflection and Isometry

Convex pentagon $BRYAN$ has area $128$ with $\angle A+\angle B=180^\circ$ and side lengths $BR=2$, $RY=12$, $YA=10$, $AN=11$ and $NB=11$. Compute $\sin(\angle A)$.

Show the answer

$\frac{32}{33}$

Show solution 1 – Rotate triangle NAY onto NB

Let $\rho$ be the rotation about $N$ taking $A$ to $B$ (possible as $NA=NB=11$) and let $Y'=\rho(Y)$. Triangles $NAY$ and $NBY'$ are congruent, so $BY'=AY=10$, $NY'=NY$ and $\angle NBY'=\angle NAY=\angle A$.

List the convex pentagon $B,R,Y,A,N$ counterclockwise. Then triangle $N,B,R$ is counterclockwise while $N,A,Y$ is clockwise; the rotation keeps orientation, so $N,B,Y'$ is clockwise and $Y'$, $R$ lie on opposite sides of line $NB$. As $\angle NBY'+\angle NBR=\angle A+\angle B=180^\circ$, the points $R,B,Y'$ are collinear with $B$ between them, and $RY'=2+10=12=RY$.

So triangles $NRY$ and $NRY'$ have equal sides ($NY=NY'$, $RY=RY'$, $NR$ common) and equal areas. Splitting the convex pentagon from $N$, \[128=[NBR]+[NRY]+[NYA]=[NBR]+[NBY']+[NRY]=[NRY']+[NRY]=2[NRY'].\] So $[NRY']=64=\frac12\cdot12\cdot h$, where $h=\frac{32}3$ is the distance from $N$ to line $RY'$. In right triangle formed by $N$, $B$ and the foot of this distance, $\sin\angle NBY'=\frac{h}{NB}=\frac{32}{33}$. Hence $\sin\angle A=\boxed{\frac{32}{33}}$.

Show solution 2 – Law of cosines and Heron

Let $c=\cos\angle B$ and $s=\sin\angle B$; since $\angle A=180^\circ-\angle B$, $\cos\angle A=-c$ and $\sin\angle A=s$. Split the convex pentagon from $N$ into triangles $NBR$, $NRY$, $NYA$: \[[NBR]=\tfrac12\cdot11\cdot2\,s=11s,\qquad [NYA]=\tfrac12\cdot11\cdot10\,s=55s.\] By the law of cosines, $NR^2=121+4-44c=125-44c$ and $NY^2=121+100+220c=221+220c$. Heron's formula in the form $16K^2=4u^2v^2-(u^2+v^2-w^2)^2$ for triangle $NRY$ ($u=NR$, $v=NY$, $w=RY=12$) gives \[\begin{aligned}16[NRY]^2&=4(125-44c)(221+220c)-(202+176c)^2\\&=(110500+71104c-38720c^2)-(40804+71104c+30976c^2)=69696(1-c^2).\end{aligned}\] As $69696=264^2$, $[NRY]=\frac{264}{4}s=66s$. So the area is $11s+66s+55s=132s=128$, and $\sin\angle A=s=\boxed{\frac{32}{33}}$.

There exists a polynomial $p(x,y)$ with real coefficients and total degree at most $4$ such that $p(m,n)$ is the entry in the $m^{\text{th}}$ row and the $n^{\text{th}}$ column of the following table. \[\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}\hline 4&8&*&?\\ \hline 3&5&9&*\\ \hline 2&3&5&9\\ \hline 0&1&2&2\\ \hline\end{array}\] Compute $p(4,4)$, i.e., the top-right entry in the table.

Show the answer

$30$

Show the solution – Mixed finite differences

Write $p(m,n)$ with row $m$ ($m=1$ is the bottom row) and column $n$. Let $\Delta_x f(x,y)=f(x+1,y)-f(x,y)$ and $\Delta_y$ similarly. For a monomial, $\Delta_x^a\Delta_y^b(x^iy^j)=(\Delta^ax^i)(\Delta^by^j)$, which vanishes if $i<a$ or $j<b$. Mark has a radioactive ball weighing $1$ gram. During the $n^{\text{th}}$ minute, each radioactive ball has a $1/2$ chance of splitting into two radioactive balls, each of weight $2^{-n}$ grams (regardless of the ball's initial weight or when it was initially formed). After $7$ minutes, compute the expected value of the total weight in grams of Mark's radioactive balls.

Show the answer

$\frac{2123}{8192}$

Show solution 1 – Recursion for expectations

Let $W_n$ and $N_n$ be the expected total weight and expected number of balls after $n$ minutes ($W_0=N_0=1$). During minute $n$, a ball of weight $w$ keeps weight $w$ with probability $\frac12$ and otherwise becomes two balls of total weight $2\cdot2^{-n}$; its expected contribution is $\frac w2+2^{-n}$, and its expected number of descendants is $\frac32$. By linearity, \[W_n=\tfrac12W_{n-1}+2^{-n}N_{n-1},\qquad N_n=\tfrac32N_{n-1}=\bigl(\tfrac32\bigr)^n.\] Hence $W_1=1$, $W_2=\frac78$, $W_3=\frac{23}{32}$, $W_4=\frac{73}{128}$, $W_5=\frac{227}{512}$, $W_6=\frac{697}{2048}$, and \[W_7=\frac{697}{4096}+\frac1{128}\cdot\frac{729}{64}=\frac{1394+729}{8192}=\boxed{\frac{2123}{8192}}.\]

Show solution 2 – Sort the final balls by birth time

Each ball present at the end is either the original ball, never split (probability $2^{-7}$, weight $1$), or a ball born in some minute $m\in\{1,\dots,7\}$ that did not split in minutes $m+1,\dots,7$ (weight $2^{-m}$, survival probability $2^{-(7-m)}$, independent of its past).

The expected number of balls at the start of minute $m$ is $(\frac32)^{m-1}$ (each minute multiplies the expected count by $\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot2$). Each of them splits with probability $\frac12$ into two newborns, so the expected number of balls born in minute $m$ is $(\frac32)^{m-1}$. By linearity, the expected final weight is \[2^{-7}+\sum_{m=1}^{7}\Bigl(\tfrac32\Bigr)^{m-1}2^{-m}\,2^{-(7-m)}=2^{-7}\Bigl(1+\sum_{m=1}^{7}\Bigl(\tfrac32\Bigr)^{m-1}\Bigr)=2^{-7}\Bigl(1+2\Bigl(\tfrac{3^7}{2^7}-1\Bigr)\Bigr)=\frac{2\cdot2187-128}{2^{14}}=\boxed{\frac{2123}{8192}}.\]

Problem 33

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Orders and Primitive Behavior

The decimal expansion of $1/179$ has a repeating period of length $178$. Compute the sum of the squares of the digits in this period.

Show the answer

$5049$

Show the solution – Remainders run through all residues

In the long division of $1$ by $179$, the remainder before the $(k+1)$-st digit is $r_k\equiv10^k\pmod{179}$, $0<r_k<179$, and the digit is $\lfloor\frac{10r_k}{179}\rfloor$. The period has length $178$, so $r_0,\dots,r_{177}$ are distinct (a repeated remainder would make the period shorter); hence they are exactly $1,2,\dots,178$. So the required sum is \[\sum_{r=1}^{178}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{10r}{179}\Bigr\rfloor^2 .\] The digit equals $d$ for $\frac{179d}{10}\le r<\frac{179(d+1)}{10}$: this is $r=1,\dots,17$ for $d=0$, then blocks of $18$ ($18$–$35$, $36$–$53$, $54$–$71$, $72$–$89$, $90$–$107$, $108$–$125$, $126$–$143$, $144$–$161$) for $d=1,\dots,8$, and $r=162,\dots,178$ ($17$ values) for $d=9$.
