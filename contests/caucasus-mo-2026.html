Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 26: Double Counting and Combinatorial Proof

$50$ children are standing in a row. Determine if it is possible that among any $5$ consecutive children the number of boys is less by $1$ than the number of girls, and also among any $7$ consecutive children the number of boys is less by $1$ than the number of girls.

Show the answer

No, it is not possible.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the first 35 children twice)

Among any $5$ consecutive children let there be $b$ boys and $g$ girls. Then $b+g=5$ and $g=b+1$, so $b=2$ and $g=3$. In the same way, any $7$ consecutive children consist of $3$ boys and $4$ girls.

Suppose such a row exists and look at the first $35$ children (there are $50\ge 35$ of them). Cut them into $7$ blocks of $5$ consecutive children. Each block contains $2$ boys, so the first $35$ children contain $7\cdot 2=14$ boys. Now cut the same $35$ children into $5$ blocks of $7$ consecutive children. Each block contains $3$ boys, so there are $5\cdot 3=15$ boys. Since $14\ne 15$, this is a contradiction.

Hence such an arrangement is not possible.

Solution 2 (The row would have to be constant)

Number the children $1,2,\dots,50$ and let $x_i=1$ if child $i$ is a boy and $x_i=0$ if it is a girl. As in any such row, $5$ consecutive children contain $2$ boys and $3$ girls (from $b+g=5$, $g=b+1$), and $7$ consecutive children contain $3$ boys and $4$ girls (from $b+g=7$, $g=b+1$).

Compare the windows $i,\dots,i+4$ and $i+1,\dots,i+5$ (for $i\le 45$). Both contain $2$ boys and they share four children, so $x_i=x_{i+5}$ for $1\le i\le 45$. In the same way, comparing two overlapping windows of length $7$ gives $x_i=x_{i+7}$ for $1\le i\le 43$.

For $1\le i\le 43$ we have $i+2\le 45$, so \[x_i=x_{i+7}=x_{(i+2)+5}=x_{i+2}.\] Hence $x_1=x_3=x_5=x_7$ and $x_2=x_4=x_6$. Moreover $x_1=x_6$ (shift by $5$). Therefore $x_1=x_2=\dots=x_7$, so the first five children all have the same sex. This contradicts the fact that they are $2$ boys and $3$ girls.

Hence such an arrangement is not possible.

Solution 3 (Neighbours have different sexes)

Among any $5$ consecutive children let there be $b$ boys and $g$ girls. Then $b+g=5$ and $g=b+1$, so $b=2$. In the same way ($b+g=7$, $g=b+1$) any $7$ consecutive children contain exactly $3$ boys.

Suppose such a row exists and number the children $1,2,\dots,50$. Let $6\le i\le 49$. The seven children $i-5,\dots,i+1$ contain $3$ boys, and the first five of them, $i-5,\dots,i-1$, contain $2$ boys. Hence the two remaining children $i$ and $i+1$ contain exactly one boy: children $i$ and $i+1$ have different sexes. So the children $6,7,\dots,50$ alternate boy, girl, boy, … or girl, boy, girl, …

Children $6$ and $7$ have different sexes, so one of the groups $6,\dots,10$ and $7,\dots,11$ starts with a boy. Since the sexes alternate, that group is boy, girl, boy, girl, boy, with $3$ boys. This contradicts the fact that any $5$ consecutive children contain $2$ boys.

Hence such an arrangement is not possible.

Problem 2

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 7: Congruence, Rigidity, and Isometric Structure

In a trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AD\parallel BC$ diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ are perpendicular. Let $K$ be a point on the segment $AD$ such that $AK+KC=AD+BC$. Prove that $K$ is equidistant from $A$ and $C$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Translate the diagonal BD)

Since $AD\parallel BC$ and $ABCD$ is a trapezoid with vertices in this order, the vectors $\overrightarrow{BC}$ and $\overrightarrow{AD}$ point in the same direction. Let $D'$ be the point on ray $AD$ beyond $D$ with $DD'=BC$. Then $\overrightarrow{DD'}=\overrightarrow{BC}$, so $BCD'D$ is a parallelogram and $CD'\parallel BD$. As $BD\perp AC$, we get $CD'\perp AC$, that is, triangle $ACD'$ has a right angle at $C$.

Next, $AD'=AD+DD'=AD+BC=AK+KC$. The point $K$ lies on segment $AD$, hence on segment $AD'$, so \[KD'=AD'-AK=KC .\] Let $O$ be the midpoint of the hypotenuse $AD'$. In a right triangle the midpoint of the hypotenuse is equidistant from the three vertices, so $OA=OC=OD'$.

Both $K$ and $O$ lie on line $AD'$ and are equidistant from $C$ and $D'$, that is, both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $CD'$. If $K\ne O$, this perpendicular bisector would be the line $AD'$ itself; but then $D'$ would lie on the perpendicular bisector of $CD'$, giving $0=D'D'=D'C$, which is absurd. Hence $K=O$, and \[KA=OA=OC=KC .\] So $K$ is equidistant from $A$ and $C$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $A=(0,0)$ and $D=(d,0)$ with $d=AD>0$. The line $BC$ is parallel to $AD$, so $B=(p,h)$ and $C=(q,h)$ with $h>0$; since $\overrightarrow{BC}$ and $\overrightarrow{AD}$ point the same way, $BC=q-p>0$. Let $K=(k,0)$ with $0\le k\le d$, so $AK=k$.

The diagonals are $\overrightarrow{AC}=(q,h)$ and $\overrightarrow{BD}=(d-p,-h)$. Put $s=d-p$. Perpendicularity gives $qs-h^2=0$, so \[qs=h^2>0 .\] Thus $q$ and $s$ have the same sign. Also $q+s=(q-p)+d=BC+AD>0$, so $q>0$ and $s>0$.

The condition $AK+KC=AD+BC=q+s$ reads \[\sqrt{(q-k)^2+h^2}=q+s-k .\] Squaring (both sides are equal and the left one is nonnegative) gives \[(q-k)^2+h^2=(q-k)^2+2(q-k)s+s^2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad h^2=2(q-k)s+s^2 .\] Substituting $h^2=qs$ and dividing by $s>0$ gives $q=2(q-k)+s$, that is, $k=\frac{q+s}{2}$. Therefore \[KC=q+s-k=\frac{q+s}{2}=k=KA,\] so $K$ is equidistant from $A$ and $C$.

(Since $AK=\frac{AD+BC}{2}$, such a point $K$ exists on segment $AD$ exactly when $BC\le AD$; the problem takes this for granted.)

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure

Find the smallest positive integer $k$ such that the number $100^{100}$ can be represented as a product of $99$ positive integers each of which is not greater than $k$.

Show the answer

$128$

Show the solution

Solution (Replace every 5 by a 4)

Since $100^{100}=2^{200}5^{200}$, every factor in such a representation divides $2^{200}5^{200}$, so it has the form $2^{a}5^{b}$ with integers $a,b\ge 0$.

$k\le 127$ is impossible. Suppose $100^{100}=f_1f_2\cdots f_{99}$ with every $f_i=2^{a_i}5^{b_i}\le 127$. Since $5^{b}\ge 4^{b}$, \[2^{a_i+2b_i}=2^{a_i}4^{b_i}\le 2^{a_i}5^{b_i}=f_i\le 127<128=2^7,\] so $a_i+2b_i\le 6$ for every $i$. Adding over the $99$ factors and using $\sum a_i=200$, $\sum b_i=200$: \[600=200+2\cdot 200=\sum_{i=1}^{99}(a_i+2b_i)\le 6\cdot 99=594,\] a contradiction. Hence $k\ge 128$.

$k=128$ works. Take $6$ factors $128=2^7$, $14$ factors $125=5^3$ and $79$ factors $100=2^25^2$. That is $6+14+79=99$ factors, each at most $128$, and \[2\text{-exponent: }6\cdot 7+79\cdot 2=200,\qquad 5\text{-exponent: }14\cdot 3+79\cdot 2=200 .\] So $128^{6}\cdot 125^{14}\cdot 100^{79}=100^{100}$.

(How to find it: in the bound above, $a+2b=7$ is needed on average a bit more than $6$, and among the numbers $2^a5^b\le 128$ only $128$ has $a+2b=7$; using $x$ copies of $128$, $y$ of $125$ and $z$ of $100$, the equations $7x+2z=200$, $3y+2z=200$, $x+y+z=99$ give $x=6$, $y=14$, $z=79$.)

The smallest $k$ is $\boxed{128}$.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 44: Combinatorial Games and Strategy

Pasha and Vova play a game on a $12\times 12$ checkered board. They take turns, Pasha starts. On his turn, Pasha can choose two empty cells adjacent by side and place a coin heads-up in each of these two cells. Vova, on his turn, can choose any two diagonally adjacent cells and make tails-up each coin already placed in these two cells. The end of the game is announced immediately after Vova's move if Pasha cannot make his next move. Find the greatest possible number of heads-up coins, that Pasha can achieve at the end of the game, regardless of Vova's actions.

Show the answer

$36$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Odd rows for Pasha, a potential for Vova)

Call a coin showing heads a head. Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,12$. Two diagonally adjacent cells lie in neighbouring rows and neighbouring columns, and they have the same colour in the usual chessboard colouring.

Pasha can get $36$ heads. For his first $36$ moves Pasha covers rows $1,3,5,\dots,11$ completely with horizontal dominoes (columns $1$–$2$, $3$–$4$, …, $11$–$12$). During these $36$ rounds every coin lies in an odd row. Two diagonally adjacent cells lie in neighbouring rows, so at most one of the two cells Vova chooses contains a coin, and each Vova move turns at most one head to tails. Also, the even rows are still empty, so Pasha always has a legal move and the game does not end. After these $36$ rounds Pasha has placed $72$ heads and Vova has turned at most $36$ of them, so there are at least $36$ heads.

From then on Pasha moves arbitrarily. In each round he adds $2$ heads and Vova turns at most $2$ heads to tails, so the number of heads never decreases. At the end there are at least $36$ heads.

Vova can keep the number of heads at most $36$. Cut the board into $36$ blocks of size $2\times 2$ (rows $2i-1,2i$ and columns $2j-1,2j$). In every block the two black cells are diagonally adjacent, and so are the two white cells. For a cell $x$ let $x^{*}$ be the other cell of the same colour in the block of $x$; Vova may always choose the pair $\{x,x^{*}\}$.

Every domino consists of one black cell $u$ and one white cell $w$. Vova's strategy: after Pasha puts coins on $u$ and $w$,

  • if the cell $u^{*}$ contains a coin, Vova chooses $\{u,u^{*}\}$;

  • if $u^{*}$ is empty, Vova chooses $\{w,w^{*}\}$.

Call a block open if its two black cells contain no head and at least one of them is empty. Let \[\Phi=(\text{number of heads})+(\text{number of open blocks}).\] At the start $\Phi=0+36=36$. We show that $\Phi$ never increases during a round (Pasha's move followed by Vova's answer). Note that whether a block is open depends only on its black cells.

Let $P$ be the block of $u$. Only the black cells of $P$ can change among all black cells: Pasha puts a coin on $u$, and Vova either flips $u,u^{*}$ (black cells of $P$) or flips $w,w^{*}$ (white cells). So only the openness of $P$ can change.

Case 1: $u^{*}$ contains a coin. Vova turns $u$ and $u^{*}$ to tails, so $w$ is the only new head. If $u^{*}$ was a head, it becomes tails: the number of heads does not change, and $P$ was not open before (it had a head) and is not open after (both black cells are occupied), so $\Phi$ is unchanged. If $u^{*}$ was tails, the number of heads grows by $1$, but $P$ was open before (black cells: $u$ empty, $u^{*}$ tails) and is not open after; again $\Phi$ is unchanged.

Case 2: $u^{*}$ is empty. Vova turns $w$ and $w^{*}$ to tails, so $u$ stays a head and the number of heads grows by $1$ if $w^{*}$ was not a head, and does not change if $w^{*}$ was a head. The block $P$ was open before (both black cells empty) and is not open after ($u$ is a head). Hence $\Phi$ changes by $0$ or $-1$.

So $\Phi\le 36$ after every round. Since the number of heads is at most $\Phi$, at the end of the game there are at most $36$ heads, whatever Pasha does.

Therefore the greatest number of heads Pasha can guarantee is $\boxed{36}$.

Solution 2 (The same pairing, counted round by round)

Call a coin showing heads a head. Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,12$ and colour the board like a chessboard. Two cells adjacent by side have different colours, so every Pasha move covers one black cell and one white cell.

Pasha can get $36$ heads. For his first $36$ moves Pasha covers rows $1,3,\dots,11$ with horizontal dominoes (columns $1$–$2$, $3$–$4$, …, $11$–$12$). Two diagonally adjacent cells lie in neighbouring rows, so during these rounds at most one of the two cells chosen by Vova holds a coin, and Vova turns at most one head to tails per round. The even rows are still empty, so Pasha always has a move. After $36$ rounds there are at least $72-36=36$ heads. Afterwards each round adds $2$ heads and removes at most $2$, so the number of heads never decreases, and at the end there are at least $36$ heads.

Vova can keep the number of heads at most $36$. Cut the board into $36$ blocks of size $2\times2$ (rows $2i-1,2i$ and columns $2j-1,2j$). In each block the two black cells are diagonally adjacent, and so are the two white cells. For a cell $x$ let $x^{*}$ be the other cell of the same colour in the block of $x$; Vova may always choose the pair $\{x,x^{*}\}$. The $36$ pairs $\{x,x^{*}\}$ of black cells are disjoint and cover all black cells.

Suppose Pasha has just covered the black cell $u$ and the white cell $w$. Vova plays as follows.

  • If $u^{*}$ is empty (a first round for the pair $\{u,u^{*}\}$), Vova chooses $\{w,w^{*}\}$.

  • If $u^{*}$ holds a coin (a completing round for the pair $\{u,u^{*}\}$), Vova chooses $\{u,u^{*}\}$.

In a first round Vova touches only white cells, and in a completing round he touches only the two black cells of the pair that has just become full. Hence a coin on a black cell is turned to tails only in the round that completes its pair. In particular, in a completing round the coin on $u^{*}$, placed in an earlier round, is still a head.

Now count the heads in each round. In a completing round Pasha adds the heads $u$ and $w$, and Vova turns the heads $u$ and $u^{*}$ to tails: the number of heads does not change. In a first round Pasha adds $2$ heads and Vova turns the new head $w$ to tails (and possibly also a head on $w^{*}$): the number of heads grows by at most $1$.

Each round covers exactly one black cell, so each black pair has at most one first round, the round in which its first cell is covered. Hence there are at most $36$ first rounds, and after every complete round, in particular when the game ends, there are at most $36$ heads, whatever Pasha does.

Therefore the greatest number of heads Pasha can guarantee is $\boxed{36}$.

Juniors, Day 2

Problem 5

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure

Find the largest positive integer consisting of distinct non-zero digits such that for any two of its neighboring digits their product is a composite number.

Show the answer

$987653241$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (What the condition really says, then greedy)

Let $x\ne y$ be nonzero digits. If both are at least $2$, then $xy$ is a product of two integers greater than $1$, so it is composite. If one of them is $1$, then $xy$ equals the other digit, which is composite exactly when it is $4,6,8$ or $9$. So the condition says only: \[\text{every neighbour of the digit }1\text{ is one of }4,6,8,9 .\] The number has at most $9$ digits, and we will find a valid $9$-digit number, so the answer uses all digits $1,\dots,9$.

The largest possible first five digits of a number with distinct digits are $98765$; if a $9$-digit number does not start with $98765$, then at the first place where it differs from $98765$ it has a smaller digit, so it is smaller than every number starting with $98765$. Let us look for valid numbers starting with $98765$. The digits $1,2,3,4$ fill places $6$–$9$.

  • The digit $1$ cannot be in place $6$, because its neighbour in place $5$ is $5$.

  • If $1$ is in place $7$ or $8$, both of its neighbours are among $2,3,4$, so both would have to be $4$, which is impossible.

Hence $1$ is in place $9$ and $4$ is in place $8$, and places $6,7$ hold $2$ and $3$. The larger choice is $3,2$, giving $987653241$, which is valid (the only neighbour of $1$ is $4$).

The answer is $\boxed{987653241}$.

Solution 2 (Where can the digit 1 stand?)

Two different nonzero digits have a composite product unless one of them is $1$ and the other is a prime $2,3,5,7$ (if both are at least $2$, the product has two factors greater than $1$; if one is $1$, the product is the other digit). So the only restriction is that each neighbour of $1$ must be one of $4,6,8,9$. A valid $9$-digit number exists (for example $987653241$), so the largest valid number uses all nine digits once; call it $N$.

Case 1: the digit $1$ is the last digit. Its left neighbour $d$ is $4,6,8$ or $9$, and the first seven digits are the other seven digits. For a fixed $d$ the best is to write those seven digits in decreasing order. The first seven digits are then \[d=4:\ 9876532,\qquad d=6:\ 9875432,\qquad d=8:\ 9765432,\qquad d=9:\ 8765432,\] and $d=4$ is clearly best. This gives $987653241$.

Case 2: the digit $1$ is the first digit. Then $N<2\cdot 10^{8}$, which is much smaller.

Case 3: the digit $1$ has two neighbours. Both neighbours lie in $\{4,6,8,9\}$. If $N$ started with $98765$, then the remaining digits $1,2,3,4$ would occupy places $6$–$9$, the $1$ would not be last (Case 3), would not be in place $6$ (neighbour $5$), and in place $7$ or $8$ both neighbours would come from $\{2,3,4\}$, which cannot both be $4$. So $N$ does not start with $98765$, and then $N<987650000<987653241$.

So the largest number is $\boxed{987653241}$.

Problem 6

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Masha wrote down $2026$ different positive real numbers in a notebook. She considers the pairs of numbers $a,b$ written in the notebook to be good if the number $\frac{2026-ab}{a+b}$ is equal to one of the remaining $2024$ numbers. Determine if it is possible that among all the pairs of initial numbers there are exactly $2026$ good pairs.

Show the answer

No, it is not possible.

Show the solution

Solution (Good pairs come in triangles)

For positive numbers $a,b,c$, \[c=\frac{2026-ab}{a+b}\iff c(a+b)=2026-ab\iff ab+bc+ca=2026 .\] The last condition is symmetric in $a,b,c$.

Call a set $\{x,y,z\}$ of three numbers from the notebook a triangle if $xy+yz+zx=2026$.

  • Every pair inside a triangle is good: for the pair $\{x,y\}$ we get $\frac{2026-xy}{x+y}=z$, and $z$ is one of the other $2024$ numbers. The same holds for $\{y,z\}$ and $\{z,x\}$.

  • Every good pair $\{a,b\}$ lies in exactly one triangle: if $c=\frac{2026-ab}{a+b}$ is one of the other numbers, then $\{a,b,c\}$ is a triangle; and any triangle containing $a$ and $b$ must have third element $\frac{2026-ab}{a+b}$, which is uniquely determined.

So the good pairs are split into groups of three, one group for each triangle, and the number of good pairs equals $3T$, where $T$ is the number of triangles. In particular it is divisible by $3$. But $2026=3\cdot 675+1$ is not divisible by $3$.

Hence there cannot be exactly $2026$ good pairs: the answer is no.

Problem 7

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

In an $8\times 8$ grid square all $81$ grid points were marked. Igor connected some pairs of marked points with line segments to form an $81$-gon. Prove that there is a cell whose center lies on the boundary of this $81$-gon.

Show the solution

Solution (Parity of x+y along the sides)

Put the square on coordinates so that the marked points are the points $(x,y)$ with integers $0\le x,y\le 8$. The cell centres are the points $\bigl(i+\tfrac12,\,j+\tfrac12\bigr)$ with integers $0\le i,j\le 7$.

Suppose no cell centre lies on the boundary of the $81$-gon. Take any side $PQ$ and let $\Delta x,\Delta y$ be the differences of the coordinates of $Q$ and $P$.

  • If $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are both even, the midpoint of $PQ$ has integer coordinates and lies in the square, so it is a marked point. All $81$ marked points are vertices of the polygon, so a vertex would lie inside the side $PQ$, which is impossible for a polygon.

  • If $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are both odd, the midpoint of $PQ$ has both coordinates of the form $m+\tfrac12$ and lies in the square, so it is a cell centre on the boundary, contrary to our assumption.

Hence on every side exactly one of $\Delta x,\Delta y$ is odd, so the parity of $x+y$ changes from one end of a side to the other.

Now walk once around the polygon. The parity of $x+y$ changes at each of the $81$ sides, so after $81$ sides it has changed an odd number of times, and we cannot return to the starting vertex with the same parity. This contradiction shows that some cell centre lies on the boundary of the $81$-gon.

Problem 8

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

A regular $111$-gon with side length $1$ is given. Prove that there are $2$ diagonals whose lengths differ by $1$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Chord lengths and a sum-to-product formula)

Let the vertices be $A_0,A_1,\dots,A_{110}$ in order on the circumcircle, with radius $R$, and put $\theta=\frac{\pi}{111}$. The chord $A_iA_j$ with $|i-j|=k$ subtends a central angle $2k\theta$, so its length is $2R\sin k\theta$. The side ($k=1$) has length $1$, so $2R\sin\theta=1$ and the chord spanning $k$ steps has length \[d_k=\frac{\sin k\theta}{\sin\theta}.\] For $2\le k\le 109$ these chords are diagonals. Take $k=36$ and $k=38$, for example the diagonals $A_0A_{36}$ and $A_0A_{38}$. By the formula $\sin\alpha-\sin\beta=2\cos\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\sin\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}$, \[d_{38}-d_{36}=\frac{\sin 38\theta-\sin 36\theta}{\sin\theta}=\frac{2\cos 37\theta\,\sin\theta}{\sin\theta}=2\cos\frac{37\pi}{111}=2\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=1 .\] So the diagonals $A_0A_{38}$ and $A_0A_{36}$ differ in length by exactly $1$.

Solution 2 (An equilateral triangle and Ptolemy's theorem)

Let the vertices be $A_0,A_1,\dots,A_{110}$ in order on the circumcircle. Since $111=3\cdot 37$, the vertices $A_0,A_{37},A_{74}$ divide the circle into three equal arcs, so triangle $A_0A_{37}A_{74}$ is equilateral; let $t$ be its side.

The vertex $A_{36}$ lies on the arc $A_0A_{37}$ not containing $A_{74}$, so $A_0A_{36}A_{37}A_{74}$ is a convex cyclic quadrilateral with vertices in this order. Ptolemy's theorem gives \[A_0A_{37}\cdot A_{36}A_{74}=A_0A_{36}\cdot A_{37}A_{74}+A_{36}A_{37}\cdot A_0A_{74},\] that is, $t\cdot A_{36}A_{74}=t\cdot A_0A_{36}+t\cdot 1$, since $A_{36}A_{37}$ is a side of the $111$-gon. Dividing by $t$, \[A_{36}A_{74}=A_0A_{36}+1 .\] Both $A_{36}A_{74}$ (it spans $38$ steps) and $A_0A_{36}$ (it spans $36$ steps) are diagonals, and their lengths differ by $1$.

Seniors, Day 1

Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure

On the sides of a triangle whose area equals $1$ squares are constructed outward. The ends of the sides of the squares sharing a vertex of the triangle are connected by red segments.

  • a) Prove that a triangle can be formed from three red segments;

  • b) Prove that the area of this triangle equals $3$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vectors and a quarter turn)

Label the triangle $ABC$ counterclockwise and let $\rho$ denote the rotation of vectors by $90^\circ$ clockwise. Going around the triangle counterclockwise, the outside of each side is on the right, so the outward squares have the vertices \[A_1=A+\rho(B-A),\ B_2=B+\rho(B-A)\ \text{(on }AB),\qquad B_1=B+\rho(C-B),\ C_2=C+\rho(C-B)\ \text{(on }BC),\] \[C_1=C+\rho(A-C),\ A_2=A+\rho(A-C)\ \text{(on }CA).\] The red segments are $A_1A_2$, $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$. Since $\rho$ is linear, \[\overrightarrow{A_2A_1}=\rho(B-A)-\rho(A-C)=\rho(B+C-2A),\] and in the same way $\overrightarrow{B_2B_1}=\rho(C+A-2B)$ and $\overrightarrow{C_2C_1}=\rho(A+B-2C)$.

a) The three vectors add up to $\rho\bigl((B+C-2A)+(C+A-2B)+(A+B-2C)\bigr)=\rho(0)=0$. Moreover $B+C-2A=2\overrightarrow{AM_a}$ and $C+A-2B=2\overrightarrow{BM_b}$, where $M_a,M_b$ are the midpoints of $BC$, $CA$; the medians $AM_a$ and $BM_b$ are not parallel (they meet at the centroid and are different lines), so the first two vectors are not parallel. Placing the three vectors head to tail starting from any point $X$ we return to $X$ and obtain a non-degenerate triangle whose sides are translated copies of the three red segments.

b) A rotation does not change areas, so the area of this triangle is $\frac12\bigl|(B+C-2A)\times(C+A-2B)\bigr|$, where $\times$ is the planar cross product. With $b=B-A$, $c=C-A$ we have $B+C-2A=b+c$ and $C+A-2B=c-2b$, and \[(b+c)\times(c-2b)=b\times c-2\,b\times b+c\times c-2\,c\times b=b\times c+2\,b\times c=3\,b\times c .\] Since $\frac12|b\times c|=[ABC]=1$, the area equals $3\cdot 1=3$.

Solution 2 (Red segments are doubled medians)

Let $m_a,m_b,m_c$ be the medians of triangle $ABC$ from $A,B,C$, with feet $M_a,M_b,M_c$, and let $G$ be the centroid. On side $AB$ let the square be $ABB_2A_1$, on $BC$ the square $BCC_2B_1$, on $CA$ the square $CAA_2C_1$; the red segments are $A_1A_2$, $B_1B_2$, $C_1C_2$.

Each red segment is twice a median. Rotate the plane by $90^\circ$ about $A$ in the direction taking $A_1$ to $B$ (possible since $AA_1=AB$ and $AA_1\perp AB$). The same rotation takes $A_2$ to the point $C'$ with $AC'=AC$ on the line $AC$ beyond $A$ (since $AA_2\perp AC$ and both squares are outward), i.e. $C'$ is the reflection of $C$ in $A$. Hence $A_1A_2=BC'$. In triangle $BCC'$ the segment $AM_a$ joins the midpoints of $CC'$ and $CB$, so $BC'=2AM_a$. Thus $A_1A_2=2m_a$, and likewise $B_1B_2=2m_b$, $C_1C_2=2m_c$.

A triangle with sides $\tfrac23m_a,\tfrac23m_b,\tfrac23m_c$. Let $G'$ be the reflection of $G$ in $M_a$. The diagonals $GG'$ and $BC$ of $BGCG'$ bisect each other, so it is a parallelogram and $BG'=GC=\tfrac23m_c$. Also $BG=\tfrac23m_b$ and $GG'=2GM_a=\tfrac23m_a$. The point $B$ is not on line $AM_a$, so $BGG'$ is a genuine triangle with sides $\tfrac23m_a,\tfrac23m_b,\tfrac23m_c$. Its area is twice the area of $BGM_a$ (equal bases $GM_a=M_aG'$, same height), and $[BGM_a]=\tfrac13[BAM_a]=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12[ABC]=\tfrac16$ because $GM_a=\tfrac13AM_a$. So $[BGG']=\tfrac13$.

a) Enlarging triangle $BGG'$ by the factor $3$ gives a triangle with sides $2m_a,2m_b,2m_c$, the lengths of the red segments. So a triangle can be formed from the red segments.

b) Any triangle with these side lengths is congruent to the enlarged triangle (SSS), so its area is $3^2\cdot\tfrac13=3$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 29: Valuations and Lifting the Exponent

Peter arranged $n$ positive integers in a circle. For each integer, Basil calculated the product of that number and the $GCD$ of the two numbers following it clockwise. All the numbers Basil calculated turned out to be equal. Are the original numbers necessarily equal, if

  • a) $n=15$;

  • b) $n=16$?

Show the answer

a) No (e.g. $1,2,2$ repeated five times); b) Yes, they must be equal.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Prime exponents around the circle)

Let the numbers be $x_1,\dots,x_n$ in clockwise order, indices taken modulo $n$, and let $K=x_i\gcd(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})$ be the common value.

a) Take $1,2,2$ repeated five times ($n=15$). Each number is followed by two numbers whose greatest common divisor is $2$ if the number is $1$ (followed by $2,2$) and $1$ if the number is $2$ (followed by $2,1$ or $1,2$). All products equal $2$, but the numbers are not all equal. So the answer to a) is no.

b) Let $n=16$, fix a prime $p$ and let $v_i$ be the exponent of $p$ in $x_i$. Since the exponent of $p$ in $\gcd(y,z)$ is the smaller of the exponents in $y$ and $z$, \[v_i+\min(v_{i+1},v_{i+2})=k\qquad\text{for all }i,\] where $k$ is the exponent of $p$ in $K$. Let $m=\min_i v_i$ and suppose $v_j=m$.

Every minimum in the formula is at least $m$, so $v_i\le k-m$ for all $i$. For $i=j-1$ and $i=j-2$ the pair $(v_{i+1},v_{i+2})$ contains $v_j=m$, so the minimum is $m$; hence $v_{j-1}=v_{j-2}=k-m$, and $M=k-m$ is the largest exponent. Suppose $M>m$.

For $i=j$: $\min(v_{j+1},v_{j+2})=k-m=M$, so $v_{j+1}=v_{j+2}=M$. For $i=j+1$: $\min(v_{j+2},v_{j+3})=k-M=m$, and $v_{j+2}=M>m$, so $v_{j+3}=m$. Thus from a position with exponent $m$ we always get exponent $m$ three places further on, with exponent $M$ in the two places between. Repeating, $v_{j+3t}=m$ and $v_{j+3t+1}=M$ for all integers $t\ge 0$.

Since $\gcd(3,16)=1$, there is $t\ge0$ with $3t\equiv 1\pmod{16}$ (for example $t=11$). Then position $j+3t$ is position $j+1$, so $v_{j+1}=m$; but also $v_{j+1}=M>m$. This contradiction shows $M=m$: all the $v_i$ are equal.

This holds for every prime $p$, so all $x_i$ have the same prime factorisation and are equal. The answer to b) is yes.

Solution 2 (Each number divides the one three places on)

Let the numbers be $x_1,\dots,x_n$ in clockwise order, indices taken modulo $n$, and let $K=x_i\gcd(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})$ be the common value. Every $x_i$ divides $K$, and \[y_i:=\frac{K}{x_i}=\gcd(x_{i+1},x_{i+2}).\]

Lemma. If $u$ and $v$ divide $K$, then $\dfrac{K}{\gcd(u,v)}=\operatorname{lcm}\Bigl(\dfrac Ku,\dfrac Kv\Bigr)$.

Proof. Fix a prime $p$ and let $e,\alpha,\beta$ be its exponents in $K,u,v$, so $\alpha,\beta\le e$. The exponent of $p$ on the left is $e-\min(\alpha,\beta)$, and on the right it is $\max(e-\alpha,e-\beta)$; these are equal. $\square$

By the Lemma, \[x_i=\frac{K}{\gcd(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})}=\operatorname{lcm}\Bigl(\frac{K}{x_{i+1}},\frac{K}{x_{i+2}}\Bigr)=\operatorname{lcm}(y_{i+1},y_{i+2}).\] Now $y_{i+1}=\gcd(x_{i+2},x_{i+3})$ and $y_{i+2}=\gcd(x_{i+3},x_{i+4})$ both divide $x_{i+3}$, so $x_{i+3}$ is a common multiple of $y_{i+1}$ and $y_{i+2}$, and therefore $x_i\mid x_{i+3}$ for every $i$. Going around the circle in steps of $3$, \[x_i\mid x_{i+3}\mid x_{i+6}\mid\dots\mid x_{i+3n}=x_i .\] Positive integers that divide each other in a cycle are all equal, so $x_i=x_{i+3}$ for every $i$.

a) Take $1,2,2$ repeated five times ($n=15$). The products are $1\cdot\gcd(2,2)=2$, $2\cdot\gcd(2,1)=2$ and $2\cdot\gcd(1,2)=2$, so all of them equal $2$, but the numbers are not all equal. The answer to a) is no.

b) Let $n=16$. From $x_i=x_{i+3}$ we get $x_i=x_{i+3t}$ for every $t\ge0$. Since $3\cdot 11=33\equiv1\pmod{16}$, taking $t=11$ gives $x_i=x_{i+1}$ for every $i$, so all the numbers are equal. The answer to b) is yes.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 13: Systems, Symmetry, and Multi-Variable Algebra

Positive real numbers $a,b,c$ satisfy \[\sqrt{a^2+ab}+b+c=\sqrt{b^2+bc}+c+a=\sqrt{c^2+ca}+a+b.\] Determine if it follows that $a=b=c$.

Show the answer

Yes, $a=b=c$ follows.

Show the 4 solutions

Solution 1 (Square away the roots)

Subtract $a+b+c$ from each of the three equal expressions: \[D:=\sqrt{a^2+ab}-a=\sqrt{b^2+bc}-b=\sqrt{c^2+ca}-c .\] Since $\sqrt{a^2+ab}>\sqrt{a^2}=a$, we have $D>0$. From $\sqrt{a^2+ab}=a+D$, squaring gives $a^2+ab=a^2+2aD+D^2$, i.e. $a(b-2D)=D^2$. In the same way \[a(b-2D)=b(c-2D)=c(a-2D)=D^2>0,\] so $a,b,c$ are all greater than $2D$.

The system is unchanged under the cyclic shift $a\to b\to c\to a$, so we may assume that $a$ is the largest of the three numbers.

  • From $b(c-2D)=a(b-2D)\ge b(b-2D)$ (as $a\ge b$ and $b-2D>0$) we get $c\ge b$.

  • From $a(b-2D)=c(a-2D)$ we get $ab-2aD=ac-2cD$, i.e. $a(b-c)=2D(a-c)\ge 0$, so $b\ge c$.

Hence $b=c$, and then $2D(a-c)=a(b-c)=0$ gives $a=c$. So $a=b=c$: the answer is yes.

Solution 2 (Ratios and two monotone functions)

As in any solution, subtract $a+b+c$ from the three expressions: the condition becomes \[\sqrt{a^2+ab}-a=\sqrt{b^2+bc}-b=\sqrt{c^2+ca}-c .\] Put $x=\frac ba$, $y=\frac cb$, $z=\frac ac$; these are positive and $xyz=1$. Let \[h(t)=\sqrt{1+t}-1=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t}+1},\qquad \varphi(t)=\frac{h(t)}{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t}+1}\qquad(t>0).\] Then $h$ is strictly increasing and $\varphi$ is strictly decreasing. Since $\sqrt{a^2+ab}-a=a\,h(x)$, the condition says \[a\,h(x)=b\,h(y)=c\,h(z).\] Using $b=ax$ in $a\,h(x)=b\,h(y)$ gives $h(x)=x\,h(y)$, i.e. $x\varphi(x)=x\,h(y)$, so $\varphi(x)=h(y)$. In the same way ($c=by$, $a=cz$) we get $\varphi(y)=h(z)$ and $\varphi(z)=h(x)$.

The new system is invariant under the cyclic shift $x\to y\to z\to x$, so we may assume $x$ is the largest of $x,y,z$. Then \[h(y)=\varphi(x)\le\varphi(y)=h(z)\ \Longrightarrow\ y\le z,\] hence $\varphi(y)\ge\varphi(z)$, that is $h(z)\ge h(x)$, so $z\ge x$. As $x$ is the largest, $z=x$; then $h(z)=h(x)$ gives $\varphi(y)=\varphi(z)$, so $y=z$. Thus $x=y=z$, and $xyz=1$ gives $x=y=z=1$, i.e. $a=b=c$. The answer is yes.

Solution 3 (An odd cycle of a decreasing function)

Subtract $a+b+c$ from the three equal expressions and call the common value $t$: \[t=\sqrt{a^2+ab}-a=\sqrt{b^2+bc}-b=\sqrt{c^2+ca}-c .\] Since $\sqrt{a^2+ab}>\sqrt{a^2}=a$, we have $t>0$. Squaring $\sqrt{a^2+ab}=a+t$ gives $ab=2at+t^2$, that is, $b=2t+\frac{t^2}{a}$. In the same way $c=2t+\frac{t^2}{b}$ and $a=2t+\frac{t^2}{c}$.

Let $F(x)=2t+\frac{t^2}{x}$ for $x>0$. Then $F$ is strictly decreasing, takes positive values, and \[b=F(a),\qquad c=F(b),\qquad a=F(c).\] Let $G(x)=F(F(F(x)))$. If $0<x<y$, then $F(x)>F(y)$, hence $F(F(x))<F(F(y))$, hence $G(x)>G(y)$: so $G$ is strictly decreasing. Moreover \[G(a)=F(F(b))=F(c)=a,\qquad G(b)=F(F(c))=F(a)=b,\] and in the same way $G(c)=F(F(a))=F(b)=c$. A strictly decreasing function has at most one fixed point: if $x<y$ were both fixed, then $x=G(x)>G(y)=y$, which is absurd. Since $a$, $b$, $c$ are fixed points of $G$, they are equal. So the answer is yes.

Solution 4 (The differences shrink)

Subtract $a+b+c$ from the three equal expressions and call the common value $t$: \[t=\sqrt{a^2+ab}-a=\sqrt{b^2+bc}-b=\sqrt{c^2+ca}-c .\] Since $\sqrt{a^2+ab}>\sqrt{a^2}=a$, we have $t>0$. Squaring $\sqrt{a^2+ab}=a+t$ gives $ab=2at+t^2$, that is, $b=2t+\frac{t^2}{a}$. In the same way $c=2t+\frac{t^2}{b}$ and $a=2t+\frac{t^2}{c}$. With $F(x)=2t+\frac{t^2}{x}$ this reads \[b=F(a),\qquad c=F(b),\qquad a=F(c).\] In particular each of $a,b,c$ equals $2t$ plus a positive number, so $a,b,c>2t$.

For $x,y>2t$ we have $xy>4t^2$, so \[|F(x)-F(y)|=\Bigl|\frac{t^2}{x}-\frac{t^2}{y}\Bigr|=\frac{t^2}{xy}\,|x-y|\le\frac14|x-y| .\] Let $D$ be the largest of $|a-b|$, $|b-c|$, $|c-a|$. Applying the estimate to the pairs $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$, $(c,a)$ gives \[|b-c|=|F(a)-F(b)|\le\tfrac D4,\qquad |c-a|=|F(b)-F(c)|\le\tfrac D4,\] and $|a-b|=|F(c)-F(a)|\le\frac D4$. Hence $D\le\frac D4$, so $D=0$ and $a=b=c$. The answer is yes.

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

We are given a sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ of positive integers. At each step, all numbers change simultaneously as follows: the $i$-th number is replaced by the number of indices $j$ such that $a_j=a_i$, plus $i$. (For example the sequence $1,1,4,1,4,2,1,3$ becomes $5,6,5,8,7,7,11,9$.) We then continue the process with the new sequence. Prove that after some time the sequence no longer changes.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Lonely indices spread from the left)

Number the positions $1,\dots,n$. For the sequence at time $t$ let $c_t(i)$ be the number of positions $j$ holding the same value as position $i$ (including $i$ itself). The rule says that the value at position $i$ at time $t+1$ is $i+c_t(i)$. Call $i$ lonely at time $t$ if $c_t(i)=1$.

Observation. If positions $i_1<i_2<\dots<i_r$ hold equal values at time $t+1$, then $i_1+c_t(i_1)=\dots=i_r+c_t(i_r)$, so \[c_t(i_1)>c_t(i_2)>\dots>c_t(i_r)\ge 1\quad\text{and}\quad c_t(i_s)-c_t(i_{s+1})=i_{s+1}-i_s .\]

Lemma 1. If positions $1,\dots,k$ are lonely at time $t$, they are lonely at time $t+1$.

Proof. At time $t+1$ position $i\le k$ holds $i+1$; these values are different for different $i\le k$, and a position $j>k$ holds $j+c_t(j)\ge j+1\ge k+2$, which is larger than all of them. $\square$

Lemma 2. Suppose positions $1,\dots,k-1$ are lonely at time $t$ but $k$ is not, and let $c=c_t(k)\ge 2$. Then either $c_{t+1}(k)\le c-1$, or $c_{t+1}(k)=c$ and $k$ is lonely at time $t+2$.

Proof. Let $D=\{i_1<\dots<i_r\}$ be the set of positions sharing the value of $k$ at time $t+1$. By Lemma 1 the positions before $k$ are lonely at time $t+1$, so $i_1=k$. By the Observation the numbers $c_t(i_1)=c>c_t(i_2)>\dots>c_t(i_r)\ge 1$ are distinct, so $r\le c$, and $c_{t+1}(k)=r$. If $r\le c-1$ we are done. If $r=c$, these $c$ distinct numbers in $\{1,\dots,c\}$ must be $c,c-1,\dots,1$, consecutive differences are $1$, and therefore $D=\{k,k+1,\dots,k+c-1\}$, all with $c_{t+1}=c$.

At time $t+2$ position $k$ holds $k+c$. A position $j<k$ is lonely at time $t+2$ (Lemma 1 twice). A position $j$ with $k<j\le k+c-1$ lies in $D$, so it holds $j+c\ne k+c$. A position $j\ge k+c$ holds $j+c_{t+1}(j)\ge k+c+1$. Hence nobody shares the value of $k$: it is lonely at time $t+2$. $\square$

Conclusion. If at time $t$ not every position is lonely, let $k_t$ be the first position that is not lonely. By Lemma 1, $k_t$ never decreases. While $k_t$ stays equal to some $k$, Lemma 2 shows that each step either decreases $c_t(k)$, a positive integer, or makes $k$ lonely two steps later; so after finitely many steps $k$ becomes lonely and then stays lonely (Lemma 1), and the first non-lonely position moves to the right. Since it cannot pass $n$, at some time $T$ all positions are lonely, i.e. all values are distinct. Then at time $T+1$ the sequence is $2,3,\dots,n+1$, whose values are again distinct, so at every later time it is again $2,3,\dots,n+1$. From time $T+1$ on the sequence no longer changes.

Solution 2 (The largest class and where it starts)

Number the positions $1,\dots,n$. For the sequence at time $t$ let $c_t(i)$ be the number of positions $j$ holding the same value as position $i$ (including $i$ itself); the positions holding one value form a class. The rule says that the value at position $i$ at time $t+1$ is $i+c_t(i)$. Let $m_t$ be the largest class size at time $t$, and let $f_t$ be the smallest position $i$ with $c_t(i)=m_t$.

Step 1: $m_{t+1}\le m_t$. If position $j$ holds the value $v$ at time $t+1$, then $j=v-c_t(j)$ with $1\le c_t(j)\le m_t$, so $j\in\{v-m_t,\dots,v-1\}$. So at most $m_t$ positions hold $v$.

Step 2. Suppose $m_{t+1}=m_t=m$, and let a class of size $m$ at time $t+1$ hold the value $v$. By Step 1 its $m$ positions all lie in the $m$-element set $\{v-m,\dots,v-1\}$, so the class is exactly $\{v-m,\dots,v-1\}$, and $c_t(j)=v-j$ for each of its positions $j$; in particular $c_t(v-m)=m$.

Step 3: $f_{t+1}\ge f_t$ when $m_{t+1}=m_t$. The position $f_{t+1}$ is the smallest position of its class, which has size $m=m_t$; by Step 2 this class is $\{v-m,\dots,v-1\}$ with $f_{t+1}=v-m$ and $c_t(f_{t+1})=m$. By the definition of $f_t$, $f_{t+1}\ge f_t$.

Step 4: $f_{t+2}>f_{t+1}$ when $m_t=m_{t+1}=m_{t+2}=m\ge2$. Let $i=f_{t+1}$. By Step 2 the class of $i$ at time $t+1$ is $\{i,i+1,\dots,i+m-1\}$, all of whose positions have $c_{t+1}=m$; as $m\ge2$, it contains $i+1$. So at time $t+2$ positions $i$ and $i+1$ hold the different values $i+m$ and $i+1+m$. If $f_{t+2}$ were $i$, then by Step 2 (from time $t+1$ to $t+2$) the class of $i$ at time $t+2$ would be $\{i,\dots,i+m-1\}$, which contains $i+1$; this is impossible. Together with Step 3, $f_{t+2}>f_{t+1}$.

Conclusion. The sizes $m_t$ are positive integers that never increase. If $m_t=m\ge2$ stayed constant forever, then by Step 4 the positions $f_{t+1}<f_{t+2}<f_{t+3}<\cdots$ would increase strictly, which is impossible because they are at most $n$. So the largest class size keeps decreasing until it becomes $1$ at some time $T$; then all values are distinct. At time $T+1$ the sequence is $2,3,\dots,n+1$, whose values are again distinct, so at every later time it is again $2,3,\dots,n+1$. From time $T+1$ on the sequence no longer changes.

Problem 5

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Determine if there exist non-constant linear functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ such that the equation $f(x)g(x+1)+f(x+1)g(x)=0$ has no real roots.

Show the answer

No, such functions do not exist.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The discriminant)

Write $f(x)=a(x-p)$ and $g(x)=c(x-q)$ with $a\ne0$, $c\ne0$ (a non-constant linear function has exactly one root). Let $F(x)=f(x)g(x+1)+f(x+1)g(x)$. Then \[\frac{F(x)}{ac}=(x-p)(x+1-q)+(x+1-p)(x-q)=2x^2-2(p+q-1)x+2pq-p-q .\] The quarter-discriminant of this quadratic is \[(p+q-1)^2-2(2pq-p-q)=p^2+q^2+1+2pq-2p-2q-4pq+2p+2q=(p-q)^2+1>0 .\] So the equation $F(x)=0$ always has two real roots, and the answer is no.

Solution 2 (A sign change)

Let $f(x)=a(x-p)$ and $g(x)=c(x-q)$ with $a,c\ne0$, and $F(x)=f(x)g(x+1)+f(x+1)g(x)$; $F$ is a polynomial, hence continuous. Since $f(p)=0$, $f(p+1)=a$ and $f(p-1)=-a$, \[F(p)=f(p)g(p+1)+f(p+1)g(p)=a\,g(p),\qquad F(p-1)=f(p-1)g(p)+f(p)g(p-1)=-a\,g(p).\] So $F(p-1)F(p)=-a^2g(p)^2\le 0$. If the product is $0$, then $F(p)=0$; otherwise $F$ changes sign on $[p-1,p]$ and has a root there by the intermediate value theorem. Either way the equation has a real root, so the answer is no.

Problem 6

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle BAC=30^\circ$. Let $N$ be the center of the circle passing through the midpoints of $AB$, $BC$, and $CA$. Prove that $\angle BNC=90^\circ$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (N lies on the circle with diameter BC)

Let $O$ and $R$ be the circumcentre and circumradius of $ABC$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of $BC$. By the law of sines, \[BC=2R\sin\angle BAC=2R\sin 30^\circ=R .\] The midpoints of the sides form the medial triangle, whose sides are half the sides of $ABC$; so it is similar to $ABC$ with ratio $\frac12$ and its circumradius is $\frac R2$. The point $N$ is its circumcentre and $M$ is one of its vertices, so \[NM=\frac R2=\frac{BC}{2}=MB=MC .\] Thus $N$ lies on the circle with diameter $BC$, and by Thales' theorem $\angle BNC=90^\circ$.

(This requires $N\ne B,C$. The point $N$ coincides with $B$ exactly when $\angle ABC=120^\circ$: $N=B$ means that $B$ is at distance $\frac R2$ from the midpoint of $AB$, i.e. $AB=R=BC$, and then $\angle C=\angle A=30^\circ$ and $\angle B=120^\circ$; conversely, in a triangle with angles $30^\circ,120^\circ,30^\circ$ we have $AB=BC=R$ and $B$ is at distance $\frac R2$ from all three midpoints. Similarly $N=C$ exactly when $\angle ACB=120^\circ$. In these two triangles the angle $BNC$ is not defined. In all other triangles with $\angle A=30^\circ$ the conclusion holds.)

Solution 2 (Vectors from the circumcentre)

Put the origin at the circumcentre $O$ and write $A,B,C$ for the position vectors, so $|A|=|B|=|C|=R$. The point $N'=\frac{A+B+C}{2}$ satisfies \[\Bigl|N'-\tfrac{B+C}{2}\Bigr|=\tfrac{|A|}{2}=\tfrac R2,\] and similarly for the other two midpoints, so $N'$ is equidistant from the three midpoints; hence $N'=N$.

Since $\angle BAC=30^\circ$ is acute, the central angle is $\angle BOC=2\angle BAC=60^\circ$, so $B\cdot C=R^2\cos60^\circ=\frac{R^2}{2}$. Now \[4NB^2=|B-A-C|^2=3R^2-2A\cdot B-2B\cdot C+2A\cdot C,\qquad 4NC^2=|C-A-B|^2=3R^2-2A\cdot C-2B\cdot C+2A\cdot B .\] Adding, $4(NB^2+NC^2)=6R^2-4B\cdot C=4R^2$, so $NB^2+NC^2=R^2$. On the other hand \[BC^2=|B-C|^2=2R^2-2B\cdot C=R^2 .\] Hence $NB^2+NC^2=BC^2$, and by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem $\angle BNC=90^\circ$ (when $N\ne B,C$). ($N=B$ means $A+C=B$, so $|A+C|=R$, $\angle AOC=120^\circ$ and, with $\angle BOC=60^\circ$, $\angle ABC=120^\circ$; similarly for $N=C$.)

Problem 7

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

Let positive integers $a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_{120}$ form an arithmetic progression. Find the greatest possible number of pairs $1\le i<j\le 120$ such that $a_j$ is divisible by $a_i$.

Show the answer

$482$

Show the solution

Solution (Reduce to a coprime progression)

Write $a_i=a+(i-1)d$ with positive integers $a,d$ (the progression is increasing). Dividing all terms by $\gcd(a,d)$ does not change which terms divide which, so we may assume $\gcd(a,d)=1$. Then \[\gcd(a_i,d)=\gcd(a+(i-1)d,\,d)=\gcd(a,d)=1 .\] If $a_i\mid a_j$ with $i<j$, then $a_i\mid a_j-a_i=(j-i)d$, and since $\gcd(a_i,d)=1$, $a_i\mid j-i$. Also $a_i=a+(i-1)d\ge 1+(i-1)=i$.

Fix $i$. The admissible $j$ satisfy $j-i\in\{a_i,2a_i,\dots\}$ and $j-i\le 120-i$, so there are at most \[\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120-i}{a_i}\Bigr\rfloor\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120-i}{i}\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120}{i}\Bigr\rfloor-1\] of them. Summing over $i$, the number of pairs is at most \[\sum_{i=1}^{120}\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120}{i}\Bigr\rfloor-1\Bigr)=S-120,\qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^{120}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120}{i}\Bigr\rfloor .\] For $a_i=i$ (that is, $1,2,\dots,120$) the pairs are those with $i\mid j$, and for each $i$ there are exactly $\lfloor 120/i\rfloor-1$ of them, so the bound is attained.

Computing $S$. $S$ counts pairs of positive integers $(i,j)$ with $ij\le 120$. As $11\cdot11>120$, in each such pair $i\le10$ or $j\le10$, and every pair with $i,j\le 10$ qualifies. By inclusion–exclusion, \[S=2\sum_{i=1}^{10}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{120}{i}\Bigr\rfloor-10^2=2(120+60+40+30+24+20+17+15+13+12)-100=2\cdot351-100=602 .\] The greatest number of pairs is $602-120=\boxed{482}$.

Problem 8

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

In a plane, four vertices of a square and $2026$ points inside this square are marked. No three of the marked points are collinear. Prove that there exist two $1015$-gons with vertices at the marked points, one of which lies strictly inside the other.

Show the solution

Solution (Cut by an almost vertical line)

Place the square as $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(1,1)$, $D=(0,1)$ and let $\mathcal M$ be the set of the $2026$ interior marked points. Note $1015+1015=4+2026$: the inner polygon will use $1015$ interior points, the outer one the four vertices and the other $1011$ interior points.

Two auxiliary orders. Choose $\delta>0$ smaller than every coordinate $x_P$ and every $1-x_P$ ($P\in\mathcal M$) and such that the numbers $\varphi(P)=x_P-\delta y_P$ ($P\in\mathcal M$) are distinct; this is possible because for two different points $P,Q$ the equation $x_P-\delta y_P=x_Q-\delta y_Q$ holds for at most one value of $\delta>0$. Similarly choose $\eta>0$ smaller than every $y_P$ such that the numbers $\psi(P)=y_P+\eta x_P$ are distinct.

List $\mathcal M$ as $P_1,\dots,P_{2026}$ with $\varphi$ increasing, let $S_1=\{P_1,\dots,P_{1015}\}$, $S_2=\{P_{1016},\dots,P_{2026}\}$, and pick $\lambda$ with $\varphi(P_{1015})<\lambda<\varphi(P_{1016})$. The line $\ell:\ x-\delta y=\lambda$ splits the plane into the open half-planes $H_L=\{\varphi<\lambda\}\supset S_1$ and $H_R=\{\varphi>\lambda\}\supset S_2$. As $\varphi(P)>x_P-\delta>0$ and $\varphi(P)<x_P<1-\delta$ for $P\in\mathcal M$, we get $0<\lambda<1-\delta$; hence $A,D\in H_L$ and $B,C\in H_R$, and the segment $DA$ lies in $H_L$.

The inner $1015$-gon. Let $L=P_1$ and $R=P_{1015}$. No other point of $S_1$ is on line $LR$; let $U$ and $W$ be the points of $S_1$ on the two sides of this line. Form the polygon $\mathcal P$: from $L$ through the points of $U$ in increasing order of $\varphi$ to $R$, then back through the points of $W$ in decreasing order of $\varphi$ to $L$. Along each of the two chains $\varphi$ is strictly monotone, so each chain is a non-self-intersecting broken line (a line $\varphi=\text{const}$ meets it at most once). The chain through $U$ lies in the closed half-plane of line $LR$ containing $U$ and meets the line $LR$ only at $L$ and $R$; the same holds for the chain through $W$ on the other side. (One of $U$, $W$ may be empty, say $W$; then its chain is the segment $LR$, and the chain through $U\neq\varnothing$ still meets the line $LR$ only at $L$ and $R$.) So the chains meet only at $L$ and $R$, and $\mathcal P$ is a simple polygon with $1015$ vertices (no three marked points are collinear, so no angle is straight). Being inside the convex hull of $S_1$, the polygon $\mathcal P$ together with its interior lies in $H_L$ and in the open square.

The outer $1015$-gon. Let $\Gamma$ be the broken line from $B$ through the points of $S_2$ in increasing order of $\psi$ to $C$. We have $\psi(B)=\eta<\psi(P)<1+\eta=\psi(C)$ for $P\in\mathcal M$, so $\psi$ strictly increases along $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma$ does not intersect itself. All vertices of $\Gamma$ lie in the convex set $H_R$, so $\Gamma\subset H_R$; and every point of $\Gamma$ except $B$ and $C$ has $0<y<1$. Hence $\Gamma$ meets the sides $AB$, $CD$, $DA$ only at $B$ and $C$, and the closed broken line $\mathcal Q=A\,B\,\Gamma\,C\,D\,A$ is a simple polygon with $4+1011=1015$ vertices.

$\mathcal P$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal Q$. Let $X$ be any point of the open square lying in $H_L$. Moving from $X$ to the left decreases $x-\delta y$, so the horizontal ray from $X$ to the left stays in $H_L$ and in the strip $0<y<1$. Therefore it does not meet $\Gamma$, $AB$ or $CD$, and it crosses $DA$ exactly once, at a point other than $A$ and $D$. So $X$ is an interior point of $\mathcal Q$. All points of $\mathcal P$ and of its interior are such points $X$, so $\mathcal P$ lies strictly inside $\mathcal Q$, and $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal Q$ are the required $1015$-gons.
