Problem 1

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle, denote the feet of the perpendiculars from $A$ to the angle bisectors of $\angle ABC$, $\angle ACB$ by $E$, $F$ respectively. Let $I$ be the incenter of $ABC$. The circumcircles of $BIF$, $CIE$ intersect again at $P$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of the arc $BC$ containing $A$. Prove that $P$, $I$, $N$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of a point is an affine function)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths, $s$ the semiperimeter, $r$ the inradius, and let $A,B,C$ also denote the angles. Put $\omega_1=(BIF)$ and $\omega_2=(CIE)$. These circles are different (they meet in exactly the two points $I$ and $P$).

An affine function. For a point $X$ let $\varphi(X)=\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega_1)-\operatorname{pow}(X,\omega_2)$. If $\omega_i$ has centre $O_i$ and radius $R_i$, then, with vectors, \[\varphi(X)=|X-O_1|^2-R_1^2-|X-O_2|^2+R_2^2=2X\cdot(O_2-O_1)+\text{const},\] so $\varphi$ is affine: $\varphi(\alpha U+\beta V+\gamma W)=\alpha\varphi(U)+\beta\varphi(V)+\gamma\varphi(W)$ whenever $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$. Its zero set is the radical axis of $\omega_1,\omega_2$, which is the line $IP$. So we must show $\varphi(N)=0$.

Three values. Clearly $\varphi(I)=0$. The line $CI$ meets $\omega_1$ at $I$ and $F$ (and $F\ne I$, since $\angle AIC=90^\circ+\frac B2$ is not a right angle). As $\angle ACI=\frac C2<90^\circ$, the foot $F$ lies on ray $CI$ with $CF=b\cos\frac C2$. If the incircle touches $BC$ at $D$, the right triangle $CDI$ gives $CI=\dfrac{CD}{\cos\frac C2}=\dfrac{s-c}{\cos\frac C2}$. Hence \[\operatorname{pow}(C,\omega_1)=CF\cdot CI=b(s-c),\qquad \operatorname{pow}(B,\omega_2)=BE\cdot BI=c(s-b)\] (the second in the same way). Since $C\in\omega_2$ and $B\in\omega_1$, we get $\varphi(C)=b(s-c)$ and $\varphi(B)=-c(s-b)$.

Where $N$ is. As $N$ is on arc $BAC$, we have $NB=NC$ and $\angle BNC=\angle BAC$, so $\angle NBC=\angle NCB=90^\circ-\frac A2=\frac{B+C}2$. Both $N$ and $I$ lie on the same side of $BC$ as $A$, and $\angle ICB=\frac C2<\angle NCB$, so ray $CI$ lies inside $\angle BCN$: $N$ and $B$ are on opposite sides of line $CI$, and $\angle ICN=\frac B2$. Likewise $N$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of $BI$ and $\angle IBN=\frac C2$.

Write $N=\alpha B+\beta C+\gamma I$ with $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$ (barycentric coordinates with respect to triangle $BCI$). Then $|\alpha|=[NCI]/[BCI]$, $|\beta|=[NBI]/[BCI]$, and by the previous paragraph $\alpha<0$ and $\beta<0$. Using $NB=NC$, $CI=r/\sin\frac C2$ and $BI=r/\sin\frac B2$, \[\frac{|\alpha|}{|\beta|}=\frac{\frac12 NC\cdot CI\sin\frac B2}{\frac12 NB\cdot BI\sin\frac C2}=\frac{\sin^2\frac B2}{\sin^2\frac C2} =\frac{(s-a)(s-c)/(ac)}{(s-a)(s-b)/(ab)}=\frac{b(s-c)}{c(s-b)},\] by the half-angle formulas $\sin^2\frac B2=\frac{(s-a)(s-c)}{ac}$, $\sin^2\frac C2=\frac{(s-a)(s-b)}{ab}$.

Conclusion. By affinity, \[\varphi(N)=\alpha\varphi(B)+\beta\varphi(C)+\gamma\varphi(I)=|\alpha|\,c(s-b)-|\beta|\,b(s-c)=0 .\] So $N$ lies on the radical axis $IP$, that is, $P$, $I$, $N$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (Barycentric coordinates)

Use barycentric coordinates with respect to $ABC$, with side lengths $a,b,c$ and semiperimeter $s$. Every circle has an equation \[-a^2yz-b^2zx-c^2xy+(x+y+z)(ux+vy+wz)=0,\] and for a point with normalised coordinates ($x+y+z=1$) the left side is its power with respect to the circle. In particular the power of $C=(0,0,1)$ is $w$, and the power of $B$ is $v$.

The two circles. Let $\omega_1=(BIF)$ with coefficients $u_1,v_1,w_1$ and $\omega_2=(CIE)$ with $u_2,v_2,w_2$. Since $B\in\omega_1$ and $C\in\omega_2$: $v_1=w_2=0$. Next, $w_1=\operatorname{pow}(C,\omega_1)$. The line $CI$ meets $\omega_1$ at $I$ and at $F$; $F$ lies on ray $CI$ (because $\angle ACI<90^\circ$) with $CF=b\cos\frac C2$, and $CI=\frac{s-c}{\cos(C/2)}$ (right triangle formed by $C$, $I$ and the touch point of the incircle on $BC$). Hence $w_1=CF\cdot CI=b(s-c)$, and in the same way $v_2=\operatorname{pow}(B,\omega_2)=BE\cdot BI=c(s-b)$.

The radical axis. Subtracting the two equations, the radical axis is \[(u_1-u_2)x-c(s-b)\,y+b(s-c)\,z=0 .\] It passes through $I=(a:b:c)$, so $(u_1-u_2)a=bc(s-b)-bc(s-c)=bc(c-b)$, and (multiplying by $a$) the radical axis is \[bc(c-b)\,x-ac(s-b)\,y+ab(s-c)\,z=0. \tag{$*$}\]

The point $N$. The midpoint of arc $BAC$ is the second intersection of the external bisector of angle $A$ with the circumcircle $a^2yz+b^2zx+c^2xy=0$. The external bisector consists of the points $(x:b:-c)$; substituting gives $-a^2bc+xbc(c-b)=0$, so \[N=\bigl(a^2:\;b(c-b):\;-c(c-b)\bigr)\] (when $b=c$ this is $(1:0:0)=A$, which is indeed the arc midpoint). Substituting $N$ into $(*)$: \[abc(c-b)\bigl[a-(s-b)-(s-c)\bigr]=abc(c-b)\cdot 0=0,\] because $(s-b)+(s-c)=a$. So $N$ lies on the radical axis of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, which is the line $IP$. Hence $P$, $I$, $N$ are collinear.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ that satisfy \[f(x+f(y))+f(f(f(x))-y)=2x\] for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

Show the answer

$f(x)=x$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Bijectivity, then the function f(x) minus x)

The identity $f(x)=x$ works: $(x+y)+(x-y)=2x$. We show it is the only solution. Write $\mathcal P(x,y)$ for the equation and $f^2=f\circ f$.

Surjective. $\mathcal P\bigl(x,f^2(x)\bigr)$ gives $f\bigl(x+f^3(x)\bigr)=2x-f(0)$, and the right side takes every real value.

Injective. Let $f(y_1)=f(y_2)$. Comparing $\mathcal P(x,y_1)$ and $\mathcal P(x,y_2)$ gives $f(f^2(x)-y_1)=f(f^2(x)-y_2)$ for all $x$. As $f^2$ is surjective, $f(t-y_1)=f(t-y_2)$ for all $t$, so $d=y_1-y_2$ is a period of $f$ (and of $f^2$). Then $\mathcal P(x+d,y)$ has the same left side as $\mathcal P(x,y)$, so $2(x+d)=2x$ and $d=0$.

A key identity. Let $h(x)=f^2(x)-x$. Given $x,y$, choose $y'$ with $f(y')=h(x)-y$. Then $x+f(y')=f^2(x)-y$, and $\mathcal P(x,y')$ reads $f(f^2(x)-y)+f(f^2(x)-y')=2x$. Comparing with $\mathcal P(x,y)$: $f(f^2(x)-y')=f(x+f(y))$, so by injectivity $y'=h(x)-f(y)$. Since $f(y')=h(x)-y$, \[f\bigl(h(x)-f(y)\bigr)=h(x)-y\qquad\text{for all }x,y. \tag{1}\] Put $y=f(z)$: $f\bigl(h(x)-f^2(z)\bigr)=h(x)-f(z)$. With $x=z$ the argument is $-z$, so \[h(z)=f(z)+f(-z). \tag{2}\]

Periods of $g=f-\mathrm{id}$. By the line before (2), $g\bigl(h(x)-f^2(z)\bigr)=\bigl(h(x)-f(z)\bigr)-\bigl(h(x)-f^2(z)\bigr)=f^2(z)-f(z)$, which does not depend on $x$. Let $D$ be the set of differences $h(x_1)-h(x_2)$. For $\delta=h(x_1)-h(x_2)\in D$ and any $w$, choose $z$ with $f^2(z)=h(x_2)-w$; then $w$ and $w+\delta$ are $h(x_2)-f^2(z)$ and $h(x_1)-f^2(z)$, so $g(w+\delta)=g(w)$. Every element of $D$ is a period of $g$.

A symmetry. Let $a=f(0)$ and $c=f^2(0)$. $\mathcal P(0,y)$ gives $f^2(y)=-f(c-y)$, i.e. $y+h(y)=-(c-y)-g(c-y)$: \[h(y)=-c-g(c-y). \tag{3}\] By (2), $h(y)=g(y)+g(-y)$, so $g(y)+g(-y)+g(c-y)=-c$. Writing this also for $c-y$ in place of $y$ and subtracting, $g(-y)=g(y-c)$, that is, \[g(t)=g(-t-c)\qquad\text{for all }t. \tag{4}\] Also $c=h(0)=2g(0)=2a$ by (2).

Values lie in one coset. Every $h(x)$ satisfies $h(x)-c=h(x)-h(0)\in D$. By (3), every value of $g$ lies in $-c-h(\cdot)\subseteq -2c+D$. Put $\gamma=-2c$. Because elements of $D$ are periods of $g$: $g(w+g(t))=g(w+\gamma)$ and $g(w+h(t))=g(w+c)$ for all $w,t$.

The equation in terms of $g$. Substituting $f=\mathrm{id}+g$ into $\mathcal P(x,y)$ gives $g(y)+h(x)+g\bigl(x+y+g(y)\bigr)+g\bigl(x+h(x)-y\bigr)=0$. By the previous paragraph and (4), $g(x+y+g(y))=g(x+y+\gamma)$ and $g(x+h(x)-y)=g(x+c-y)=g(y-x-2c)=g(y-x+\gamma)$. Replacing $y$ by $y-\gamma$: \[g(y-\gamma)+h(x)+g(y+x)+g(y-x)=0\qquad\text{for all }x,y. \tag{5}\] With $x=0$ (and $h(0)=c$): $g(y-\gamma)+c+2g(y)=0$. Subtracting this from (5): $h(x)+g(y+x)+g(y-x)=c+2g(y)$. Now put $y=0$ and use (2): $2h(x)=c+2a=2c$. Hence $h\equiv c$, i.e. $f^2(x)=x+c$.

Finish. From $\mathcal P(0,y)$: $y+c=f^2(y)=-f(c-y)$, so $f(t)=t-2c$ for all $t$. A function $f(x)=x+m$ gives $f(x+f(y))+f(f^2(x)-y)=(x+y+2m)+(x+3m-y)=2x+5m$, so $m=0$. Therefore the only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=x}$.

Solution 2 (Swapping the two inner arguments)

The identity $f(x)=x$ works: $(x+y)+(x-y)=2x$. We show it is the only solution. Write $\mathcal P(x,y)$ for the equation and $f^2=f\circ f$, so that the equation reads $f(x+f(y))+f(f^2(x)-y)=2x$.

Surjectivity. Let $S=f(\mathbb R)$ and fix $x$. As $y$ runs over $\mathbb R$, so does $f^2(x)-y$, so the second term of $\mathcal P(x,y)$ takes every value $s\in S$, and then the first term, which also lies in $S$, equals $2x-s$. Hence $2x-s\in S$ for every real $x$ and every $s\in S$. Fixing one $s\in S$ and letting $x$ vary shows $S=\mathbb R$.

Swapping. Fix $x,y$ and put $u=f^2(x)-y$. Then $f^2(x)-u=y$, so $\mathcal P(x,y)$ and $\mathcal P(x,u)$ read \[f\bigl(x+f(y)\bigr)+f(u)=2x,\qquad f\bigl(x+f(u)\bigr)+f(y)=2x .\] Let $t=x+f(y)$. The first equation gives $f(u)=2x-f(t)$, and substituting this into the second gives \[f\bigl(3x-f(t)\bigr)=2x-f(y)=3x-t .\] For fixed $x$, the number $t=x+f(y)$ takes every real value as $y$ varies, because $f$ is surjective. Hence $f(3x-f(t))=3x-t$ for all real $x,t$.

Finish. Given any $w$ and $t$, choose $x$ with $3x=w+f(t)$; the last identity becomes $f(w)=w+f(t)-t$. So $f(w)-w=f(t)-t$ for all $w,t$, that is, $f(x)=x+c$ for a constant $c$. Then $f^2(x)=x+2c$ and the left side of $\mathcal P(x,y)$ is $(x+y+2c)+(x+2c-y+c)=2x+5c$, so $c=0$. Therefore the only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=x}$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

For a positive integer $n$ let $\sigma(n)$ be the sum of divisors of $n$. Prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $m$ for which there is exactly one positive integer $n$ such that $\sigma(n)=m$.

Show the solution

Solution (Counting the primes p for which p+1 has a second preimage)

This proof uses three standard theorems of analytic number theory, quoted without proof.

  • (T1) (Prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions.) The number $\pi(X;4,1)$ of primes $p\le X$ with $p\equiv1\pmod4$ satisfies $\pi(X;4,1)\sim\frac{X}{2\log X}$; in particular $\pi(X;4,1)\ge\frac{X}{3\log X}$ for all large $X$.

  • (T2) (Chebyshev.) There is a constant $c_0$ with $\pi(Y)\le c_0\frac{Y}{\log Y}$ for all $Y\ge2$.

  • (T3) (Brun's sieve, or any upper-bound sieve for two linear forms.) For every fixed integer $d\ge2$ there is a constant $c_d$ such that the number of primes $q\le Y$ for which $dq+d-1$ is also prime is at most $c_d\frac{Y}{(\log Y)^2}$ for all $Y\ge2$.

Let $p\equiv1\pmod 4$ be a prime. Then $\sigma(p)=p+1$, so $m=p+1$ has the preimage $p$. Call $p$ bad if $\sigma(n)=p+1$ for some $n\ne p$, and good otherwise. For a good $p$, the number $m=p+1$ has exactly one preimage, and different $p$ give different $m$. So it suffices to show that the number of bad $p\le X$ is less than $\frac{X}{6\log X}$ for all large $X$; by (T1) the number of good $p\le X$ then tends to infinity.

The shape of a competitor. Let $\sigma(n)=p+1$ with $n\ne p$; then $n>1$ and $n<\sigma(n)=p+1$, so $n\le p$. Since $p\equiv1\pmod4$, the number $\sigma(n)$ is $\equiv2\pmod 4$. Write $n=2^a\prod q_i^{e_i}$ with distinct odd primes $q_i$. The factor $\sigma(2^a)=2^{a+1}-1$ is odd, and $\sigma(q^e)=1+q+\dots+q^e$ is a sum of $e+1$ odd numbers, so it is even exactly when $e$ is odd. As $\sigma$ is multiplicative and $\sigma(n)$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$, exactly one odd prime $q$ has an odd exponent $e$ in $n$. Hence \[n=q^e\,b,\qquad q\nmid b,\qquad b=2^av^2\ \text{with $v$ odd}.\] Call numbers of the form $2^av^2$ ($a\ge0$, $v$ odd) special. For $T\ge1$, the number of special numbers $b\le T$ is at most $\sum_{a\ge0}\sqrt{T/2^a}=\frac{\sqrt T}{1-2^{-1/2}}<4\sqrt T$.

Each bad $p\le X$ has a competitor $n\le X$ as above, and different $p$ have different competitors (as $\sigma(n)=p+1$ determines $p$). So we count the possible competitors $n=q^eb\le X$.

Case $e\ge3$. Then $b\le X/q^e$, so there are fewer than $4\sqrt{X/q^e}$ choices of $b$ for given $q,e$. Summing over all odd $q\ge3$ and $e\ge3$, the number of such $n$ is less than \[4\sqrt X\sum_{q\ge3}\sum_{e\ge3}q^{-e/2}=4\sqrt X\sum_{q\ge3}\frac{q^{-3/2}}{1-q^{-1/2}}\le\frac{4\sqrt X}{1-3^{-1/2}}\sum_{q\ge3}q^{-3/2},\] which is at most $c_1\sqrt X$ for a constant $c_1$, since $\sum q^{-3/2}$ converges.

Case $e=1$, $b=1$. Then $n=q$ is prime and $q+1=p+1$, so $n=p$: this is not a competitor.

Case $e=1$, $b>1$. Since $q\nmid b$, $\sigma(n)=(q+1)\sigma(b)$. With $d=\sigma(b)\ge3$ this gives $p=d(q+1)-1=dq+d-1$. Fix a number $B\ge2$, to be chosen later.

  • If $b\le B$: there are finitely many such $b$, and for each of them $q\le X$ and $dq+d-1=p$ are both prime, so by (T3) the number of such $n$ is at most $C_B\frac{X}{(\log X)^2}$, where $C_B$ is the sum of the constants $c_{\sigma(b)}$ over special $b\le B$.

  • If $B<b\le\sqrt X$: then $q\le X/b$, and $X/b\ge\sqrt X$, so $\log(X/b)\ge\frac12\log X$. By (T2) there are at most $c_0\frac{X/b}{\log(X/b)}\le\frac{2c_0X}{b\log X}$ choices of $q$ for each $b$. Summing over special $b>B$, the number of such $n$ is at most $\frac{2c_0X}{\log X}\,\varepsilon(B)$, where \[\varepsilon(B)=\sum_{\substack{b>B\\ b\text{ special}}}\frac1b\ \longrightarrow\ 0\quad(B\to\infty),\] because $\sum_{a\ge0}\sum_{v\text{ odd}}\frac1{2^av^2}\le2\sum_{v\ge1}\frac1{v^2}$ converges.

  • If $b>\sqrt X$: then $q\le X/b<\sqrt X$, and for each $q$ there are fewer than $4\sqrt{X/q}$ special $b\le X/q$. Using $\sum_{j\le T}j^{-1/2}\le2\sqrt T$, the number of such $n$ is less than $4\sqrt X\cdot2X^{1/4}=8X^{3/4}$.

Conclusion. Choose $B$ so large that $2c_0\,\varepsilon(B)<\frac1{12}$. Then the number of bad $p\le X$ is at most \[c_1\sqrt X+C_B\frac{X}{(\log X)^2}+\frac{X}{12\log X}+8X^{3/4},\] which is less than $\frac{X}{6\log X}$ for all large $X$, because $\sqrt X$, $X^{3/4}$ and $\frac{X}{(\log X)^2}$ are all small compared with $\frac{X}{\log X}$. By (T1), at least $\frac{X}{3\log X}-\frac{X}{6\log X}=\frac{X}{6\log X}$ primes $p\le X$ with $p\equiv1\pmod 4$ are good, and this tends to infinity. For each good $p$, the number $m=p+1$ has exactly one preimage $n=p$. So there are infinitely many such $m$.

Problem 1

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Let $b,c,d,x,y$ be positive integers. A sequence $a_n$ is defined in the following way: $a_0=x$, $a_1=y$ and for all $n\ge 2$: \[a_n=ba_{n-1}+ca_{n-2}+d.\] Prove that for all positive integers $m$ there exists an index $i$ such that none of the numbers $a_i,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{i+m-1}$ are prime.

Show the solution

Solution (Periodicity modulo one prime per position)

Growth. All terms are positive integers, and for $n\ge2$, $a_n=ba_{n-1}+ca_{n-2}+d\ge a_{n-1}+1+1>a_{n-1}$. So $a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots$ and $a_n\ge a_2\ge 3$ for $n\ge2$.

Lemma. For every prime $p$, the sequence $(a_n \bmod p)_{n\ge2}$ is purely periodic: there is $T\ge1$ with $a_{n+T}\equiv a_n\pmod p$ for all $n\ge2$.

Proof. Case $p\nmid c$. Then $c$ is invertible mod $p$ and $a_{n-2}\equiv c^{-1}(a_n-ba_{n-1}-d)$, so the pair $(a_{n},a_{n+1})\bmod p$ determines the previous pair. There are only $p^2$ pairs, so two of them coincide, say at positions $i<j$; going backwards step by step, the pair at position $0$ equals the pair at position $j-i$, and going forwards, $a_{n+j-i}\equiv a_n$ for all $n\ge0$. Case $p\mid c$, $p\nmid b$. For $n\ge2$, $a_n\equiv ba_{n-1}+d\pmod p$. The map $t\mapsto bt+d$ is a permutation of $\mathbb Z_p$, so $(a_n\bmod p)_{n\ge1}$ is an orbit of a permutation, hence purely periodic. Case $p\mid b$, $p\mid c$. Then $a_n\equiv d$ for all $n\ge2$. $\square$

Construction. Fix $m$. For $t=0,1,\dots,m-1$ let $p_t$ be a prime divisor of $a_{2+t}$ (possible since $a_{2+t}\ge3$) and let $T_t$ be a period of $(a_n\bmod p_t)_{n\ge2}$ from the lemma. Put $L=T_0T_1\cdots T_{m-1}$ and $i=2+L$. For each $t$, $L$ is a multiple of $T_t$, so \[a_{i+t}=a_{2+t+L}\equiv a_{2+t}\equiv0\pmod{p_t},\] while $a_{i+t}>a_{2+t}\ge p_t$ by the growth. So $a_{i+t}$ has a prime divisor smaller than itself and is not prime. Hence none of $a_i,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{i+m-1}$ is prime.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

A finite amount of ants are walking in a circular table of radius $1$, each having speed $1$. Every time two ants meet they choose a direction. One of the ants turns in that direction and the other ant goes in the opposite direction (both at speed $1$). Assume that there are never $3$ or more ants in the same place. The process ends when all ants fall off the table.

  • a) Can the process last forever?

  • b) Is there a constant $C$, independent on the number of ants, such that all ants will fall after at most $C$ units of time?

Show the answer

(a) No; (b) No

Show the solution

Solution (Convex hulls of meeting points and a polygon relay)

The answers are (a) No and (b) No.

Conventions. The table is a closed disk; an ant falls off when it leaves it. Between meetings each ant moves along a straight line at speed $1$. A leg of an ant is a maximal time interval on which its velocity is constant (an ant that reverses along the same line starts a new leg); the velocity changes only at meetings, so a leg starts at time $0$ or at a meeting, and ends at a meeting or when the ant falls off. During a leg the ant traverses a straight segment at speed $1$, and a straight segment inside a disk of radius $\rho$ has length at most $2\rho$, so a leg inside such a disk lasts at most $2\rho$. A process is any motion obeying the rules, with finitely many ants starting anywhere on the table in any directions. Dividing all lengths and times by $\rho$ turns a process on a table of radius $\rho$ into one on a table of radius $1$. For a time $t$ let $\mathcal P(t)$ be the set of positions of the ants on the table and $\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)$ its convex hull.

Part (a). We prove by induction on $N$:

  • $(A_N)$ There is a constant $T_N$ such that every process with at most $N$ ants on a table of radius $\rho$ ends within time $T_N\rho$.

This shows that no process lasts forever. $(A_1)$ holds with $T_1=2$: a single ant has one leg.

Step 0: finitely many meetings in a bounded time. Suppose infinitely many meetings happen before some time. Then their times accumulate at some $t^*$ from one side, say from the left (the other side is the same). Positions are continuous, so ants that meet at times tending to $t^*$ are at the same point at $t^*$. At $t^*$ the ants at a common point come in pairs (never three in one place), and ants at different points stay apart near $t^*$. So for small $h>0$, every meeting in $(t^*-h,t^*)$ is between the two ants of one such pair. Two ants that meet each other and nobody else leave in opposite directions and move apart along straight lines, so they cannot meet again. Thus each pair meets at most once in $(t^*-h,t^*)$, a contradiction.

Step 1: meeting points stay in the hull. If no ant falls off during $[s,t]$ and two ants meet at a point $x$ at a time $s'\in[s,t]$, then $x\in\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)$. Otherwise some unit vector $e$ has $x\cdot e>z\cdot e$ for all $z\in\mathcal P(t)$. Follow a moving point: at time $s'$ it leaves $x$ with the one of the two ants (velocities $w$ and $-w$) whose velocity has non-negative dot product with $e$; it moves with that ant until the ant's next meeting, where it switches again to the departing ant whose velocity has non-negative dot product with $e$, and so on. By Step 0 there are finitely many switches before $t$, and no ant falls off, so at time $t$ the point is the position $z$ of some ant. Its dot product with $e$ never decreased, so $z\cdot e\ge x\cdot e$, a contradiction.

From now on let $N\ge2$, assume $(A_{N'})$ for all $N'<N$, and let $T^*=\max_{N'<N}T_{N'}$.

Step 2: a lemma with two anchors. There is a constant $S_N$ with the following property. Suppose that during a time interval $[t_0,t_1]$ of some process no ant falls off, $N$ ants are on the table and all of them stay in a closed disk $\Delta$ of radius $\rho$, and there are two points $Y,Z$ with $YZ\ge\rho/2$ that lie in $\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)$ for every $t\in[t_0,t_1]$. Then $t_1-t_0\le S_N\rho$.

By scaling and shifting we may take $\rho=1$, $\Delta$ the unit disk centred at the origin, and $t_0=0$. For $t\in[0,t_1]$ let $H(t)$ be the convex hull of $Y$, $Z$ and all meeting points at times in $[0,t]$, and for a unit vector $e$ let $h_t(e)=\max_{z\in H(t)}z\cdot e$.

  • (1) $H(t)\subseteq\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)\subseteq\Delta$, by Step 1 and the assumption on $Y,Z$.

  • (2) For $3\le t\le t_1-3$, $\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)\subseteq H(t+2)$. Indeed, take an ant and its leg $[\alpha,\beta]$ through time $t$. The part of the leg inside $[0,t_1]$ is a segment in $\Delta$, so it lasts at most $2$; as $t\ge3$ and $t\le t_1-3$, this forces $0<\alpha$ and $\beta<t_1$, hence $t-2\le\alpha$ and $\beta\le t+2$. A leg starting after time $0$ starts at a meeting, and a leg ending before $t_1$ ends at a meeting (no ant falls off). So the ant's position at $t$ lies on the segment joining two meeting points of times in $[0,t+2]$, which lies in $H(t+2)$.

  • (3) $h_t(e)$ is non-decreasing in $t$, lies in $[-1,1]$, and $|h_t(e)-h_t(e')|\le|e-e'|$, since $H(t)$ lies in the unit disk.

Suppose no constant $S_N$ works. Fix $k\ge1$ and unit vectors $e_1,\dots,e_m$ such that every unit vector is within $\frac1k$ of one of them. Take an instance with $t_1\ge6+4(2mk+1)$ and the $2mk+1$ disjoint blocks $[3+4i,7+4i]$, $0\le i\le2mk$, all inside $[3,t_1-3]$. By (3) the increases of $h(e_l)$ over disjoint blocks add up to at most $2$, so $h(e_l)$ increases by more than $\frac1k$ on at most $2k$ blocks. Hence some block $[s,s+4]$ has $h_{s+4}(e_l)-h_s(e_l)\le\frac1k$ for all $l$, and then, by the Lipschitz bound, $h_{s+4}(e)-h_s(e)\le\frac3k$ for every unit vector $e$. For $t\in[s,s+1]$, (1) and (2) give $H(s)\subseteq\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)\subseteq H(s+4)$, so the support function $g_t(e)=\max_{z\in\mathcal P(t)}z\cdot e$ satisfies $h_s\le g_t\le h_s+\frac3k$.

Shift time so that $s=0$. For each $k$ we get ants $x^k_1(t),\dots,x^k_N(t)$, $t\in[0,1]$, in the unit disk, none falling off, a function $h^k=h_s$ and points $Y_k,Z_k$ with $Y_kZ_k\ge\frac12$, $Y_k\cdot e\le h^k(e)$, $Z_k\cdot e\le h^k(e)$, and $h^k\le g^k_t\le h^k+\frac3k$. The maps $x^k_i$ are $1$-Lipschitz on $[0,1]$ and the $h^k$ are $1$-Lipschitz on the unit circle, all bounded; by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, along a subsequence $x^k_i\to x_i$ and $h^k\to h$ uniformly, and $Y_k\to Y$, $Z_k\to Z$ with $YZ\ge\frac12$. Then for every $t\in[0,1]$, the support function of $K_t=\operatorname{conv}\{x_1(t),\dots,x_N(t)\}$ is $\lim g^k_t=h$. A compact convex set is determined by its support function ($z\in K$ iff $z\cdot e\le h(e)$ for all $e$), so all $K_t$ equal one set $K$, and $Y,Z\in K$.

Let $p$ be a point of $K$ farthest from $Y$; then $|p-Y|\ge|Z-Y|>0$. Put $e=\frac{p-Y}{|p-Y|}$. If $z\in K$ and $z\cdot e\ge p\cdot e$, then $|z-Y|\ge(z-Y)\cdot e\ge|p-Y|$, so equality holds throughout and $z=p$: $p$ is the only point of $K$ maximising $z\cdot e$. The maximum of $z\cdot e$ over $K=K_t$ is attained at one of the points $x_i(t)$, so the set $G(t)=\{i:x_i(t)=p\}$ is non-empty for every $t$. Choose $t^*\in(0,1)$ with $|G(t^*)|$ smallest and put $G=G(t^*)$. By continuity there are $\tau,\eta>0$ with $[t^*-\tau,t^*+\tau]\subset(0,1)$ and $|x_i(t)-p|\ge\eta$ for $i\notin G$ on this interval. There $G(t)\subseteq G$ and $|G(t)|\ge|G|$, so $G(t)=G$. Also $|G|<N$, because $K\ne\{p\}$.

By uniform convergence, for large $k$, on $[t^*-\tau,t^*+\tau]$ every ant of $G$ in the $k$-th instance is within $\delta_k$ of $p$, where $\delta_k\to0$, and every other ant is farther than $\eta/2$ from $p$. Take $k$ with $\delta_k<\eta/4$ and $T^*\delta_k<2\tau$. Then the ants of $G$ meet only each other on this interval, and they never leave the disk of radius $\delta_k$ about $p$. So, viewed on the table of radius $\delta_k$ about $p$, they form a process with $|G|<N$ ants that lasts at least $2\tau>T^*\delta_k$, contradicting $(A_{|G|})$. This proves Step 2.

Step 3: the first fall. Take a process with $N$ ants on a table of radius $1$ and let $\tau_f$ be the time of the first fall ($\tau_f=\infty$ if there is none). After $\tau_f$ fewer ants remain, which fall within $T^*$ more units. So it suffices to bound $\tau_f$ by a constant depending only on $N$. All times below are less than $\tau_f$.

  • (i) No time interval of length more than $2$ is free of meetings (each ant would have a leg longer than $2$). In particular, if meetings stop at some time, $\tau_f$ is at most $2$ later. So we may assume the first meeting happens at a time $t_O\le2$, at a point $O$.

  • (ii) For $t\ge t_O+3$, every ant has a meeting during $[t_O,t]$: otherwise it has a leg covering $[t_O,t_O+3]$.

  • (iii) Let a meeting at $O$ happen at time $s>t_O$, and let one of its ants arrive on a leg $[\alpha,\beta]$ with $\alpha<s\le\beta$. If $\alpha>t_O$, the leg starts at a meeting, which is not at $O$ (on a leg the ant moves at speed $1$ in a fixed direction, so it is not at $O$ both at time $\alpha$ and at time $s$). So if all meetings during $[t_O,s]$ are at $O$, then $\alpha\le t_O$ and $s\le\alpha+2\le t_O+2$. Hence either every meeting is at $O$, and then all meetings happen by $t_O+2$ and $\tau_f\le t_O+4\le6$ by (i), or there is a first meeting at a point $Q\ne O$; put $d=OQ$, so $0<d\le2$.

Let $r_j=2^jd$ for $j\ge0$ and let $J\ge1$ be least with $r_J>2$; then $r_J\le4$ and $r_0+\dots+r_J<2r_J\le8$. Let $D_j$ be the closed disk with centre $O$ and radius $r_j$. For $1\le j\le J$ let $\sigma_j$ be the time of the first meeting at distance at least $r_{j-1}$ from $O$ (so $\sigma_1$ is the time of the meeting at $Q$), and put $\sigma_{J+1}=\infty$ (no meeting point is farther than $2$ from $O$). Then $\sigma_1\le\sigma_2\le\dots$, and for $t<\sigma_{j+1}$ every meeting during $[t_O,t]$ lies at distance less than $r_j$ from $O$. Write $I_j=[\sigma_j,\sigma_{j+1})$ ($1\le j\le J$) and $I_0=[t_O,\sigma_1)$; every meeting during $I_0$ is at $O$.

Claim 1. If $1\le j\le J$, then all meetings during $I_j$ after time $t_O+3$ happen within $(S_N+T^*)r_j$ of $a_j=\max(\sigma_j,t_O+3)$. Assume $I_j$ contains times after $t_O+3$. The meeting at time $\sigma_j$ is at a point $Z_j$ with $r_j/2\le OZ_j<r_j$ (if $OZ_j\ge r_j$ then $\sigma_{j+1}=\sigma_j$ and $I_j$ is empty). By Step 1, $O$ and $Z_j$ lie in $\operatorname{conv}\mathcal P(t)$ for all $t\in I_j$. Let $t\in I_j$, $t\ge t_O+3$. By (ii) the last meeting of each ant before $t$ is in $D_j$. If the ant is outside $D_j$ at time $t$, then it has left $D_j$ on a leg (a straight segment cannot re-enter the convex set $D_j$), and it keeps this velocity, and so stays outside $D_j$, until its next meeting, which therefore happens outside $D_j$, that is, at a time $\ge\sigma_{j+1}$. So during $I_j$ (after $t_O+3$) the set of ants in $D_j$ can only shrink, ants outside $D_j$ take part in no meeting, and ants in $D_j$ meet only each other. While all $N$ ants are in $D_j$, Step 2 (with $\Delta=D_j$, $Y=O$, $Z=Z_j$) shows that this lasts at most $S_Nr_j$ from $a_j$. After that, fewer than $N$ ants are in $D_j$; viewed on the table $D_j$ they form a process, which by the induction hypothesis ends within $T^*r_j$; afterwards no ant is in $D_j$ and no meeting happens during the rest of $I_j$.

Claim 2. Let $t\ge t_O+3$ lie in $I_j$ and let the next meeting after $t$ happen at time $t'$ during $I_{j'}$. Then $t'-t\le r_j+r_{j'}$. One of the two ants of that meeting had its previous meeting at a time in $[t_O,t]$, by (ii), at a point at distance less than $r_j$ from $O$ (for $j=0$: at $O$). From there it went straight to the new meeting point, at distance less than $r_{j'}$ from $O$, so $t'-t$ is at most $r_j+r_{j'}$.

Now list the non-empty intervals among $I_0,\dots,I_J$ that contain times after $t_O+3$, in time order; their indices increase. Starting from $t_O+3$, by Claim 1 the meetings in the current interval $I_j$ end within $(S_N+T^*)r_j$ of its start, and by Claim 2 the next meeting, which is the start $\sigma_{j'}$ of the next interval in the list, comes at most $r_j+r_{j'}$ later. Adding up, the last meeting happens before \[t_O+3+\sum_{j=0}^J\bigl(S_N+T^*+2\bigr)r_j\ \le\ 5+8\,(S_N+T^*+2),\] and by (i), $\tau_f\le7+8(S_N+T^*+2)$. So $(A_N)$ holds with $T_N=7+8(S_N+T^*+2)+T^*$, which completes the induction. Every process ends after finite time: the answer to (a) is no.

Part (b). Let $N\ge4$ and $\theta=\pi/N$. Call a regular $N$-gon with centre at the centre of the table and circumradius $r$ a layer. Suppose that at some moment the ants meet in pairs exactly at the $N$ vertices of a layer of radius $r$, and that each pair leaves along the tangent at its vertex to the circle of radius $r$, in the two opposite directions. These $N$ tangent lines form a regular $N$-gon of circumradius $r\sec\theta$ (rotated by $\theta$), whose sides touch the circle of radius $r$ at their midpoints, the old vertices. So the two ants leaving a vertex run in opposite directions along one side of the new polygon, and after time $r\tan\theta$ (half a side) the two ants arriving at each new vertex along the two sides through it meet there. Two ants on the same side move apart, and different sides meet only at common endpoints, so there are no other meetings and never three ants in one place. Repeating the rule gives layers of radii $r,\ r\sec\theta,\ r\sec^2\theta,\dots$; the ants stay on the table as long as the radii are at most $1$.

Start with radius $r_0=\frac14$. To make the first meetings, put at time $0$ two ants on the radius through each vertex, at distance $\varepsilon<\frac18$ on either side of the vertex, moving towards it; these $N$ short segments are disjoint, so the first meetings are the $N$ pairs at the vertices, at time $\varepsilon$. Then follow the tangent rule. Since $0<\theta\le1$, we have $\cos\theta\ge1-\frac{\theta^2}2\ge e^{-\theta^2}$, so $\sec\theta\le e^{\theta^2}$. Hence the first $L=\bigl\lfloor\frac{\ln2}{\theta^2}\bigr\rfloor$ layers after the first one have radii at most $\frac14e^{L\theta^2}\le\frac12$, and each of these $L$ steps lasts $r\tan\theta\ge\frac14\theta$. So the process lasts longer than \[L\cdot\frac\theta4>\Bigl(\frac{\ln2}{\theta^2}-1\Bigr)\frac\theta4=\frac{N\ln2}{4\pi}-\frac{\pi}{4N},\] which exceeds any given $C$ when $N$ is large (afterwards, the ants keep following the rule until they fall off). So no constant $C$ independent of the number of ants exists: the answer to (b) is no.

Problem 3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 11: Similarity, Homothety, and Ratio Geometry

Let $ABC$ be a triangle, denote its incircle by $\omega$ and its incenter by $I$. A line $\ell$ tangent to $\omega$ intersects the lines $BC$, $AC$, $AB$ at points $D$, $E$, $F$ respectively, and the circumcircles of $BDF$ and $CDE$ are not congruent. Prove that one of the two centres of similitude of these circles is a point $X$ with $\angle AIX=90^\circ$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Inversion in the incircle)

We solve the edited statement: $\ell$ meets the lines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at $D$, $E$, $F$; the circumcircles $\omega_1=(BDF)$ and $\omega_2=(CDE)$ (centres $O_1,O_2$, radii $R_1\ne R_2$) have a centre of similitude $X$ with $\angle AIX=90^\circ$. Let $r$ be the inradius and let $\omega$ touch $BC$, $CA$, $AB$, $\ell$ at $K$, $N$, $L$, $T$. Vectors are taken from $I$; for instance $\vec K=\overrightarrow{IK}$.

Lemma. Let a circle $k$ with centre $J$ and radius $\rho$ touch the three side lines of a triangle at $K_1,K_2,K_3$, and let the triangle have circumcentre $O$ and circumradius $R$. Put $p=JO^2-R^2$ (the power of $J$ with respect to the circumcircle). Then $|p|=2R\rho$ and \[\overrightarrow{JO}=\frac{p}{2\rho^2}\bigl(\overrightarrow{JK_1}+\overrightarrow{JK_2}+\overrightarrow{JK_3}\bigr).\]

Proof. Invert in $k$. Let $V_1$ be the vertex where the side lines touching $k$ at $K_2,K_3$ meet. Then $V_1K_2=V_1K_3$, so $JV_1$ is the perpendicular bisector of $K_2K_3$ and meets it at its midpoint $M_1$; in the right triangle $JK_2V_1$ the altitude from $K_2$ has foot $M_1$, so $JM_1\cdot JV_1=\rho^2$. Thus $V_1$ is mapped to $M_1$, and likewise the other vertices go to the midpoints $M_2,M_3$ of $K_3K_1$, $K_1K_2$. These midpoints are not collinear, so the circumcircle does not pass through $J$ (otherwise its image would be a line), and its image is the circle through $M_1,M_2,M_3$. Triangle $M_1M_2M_3$ is the image of $K_1K_2K_3$ under the homothety with centre the centroid $G$ of $K_1K_2K_3$ and ratio $-\frac12$; as $K_1K_2K_3$ has circumcentre $J$ and circumradius $\rho$, the image circle has radius $\frac\rho2$ and centre $Z=G-\frac12(J-G)$, so that $\overrightarrow{JZ}=\frac32\overrightarrow{JG}=\frac12(\overrightarrow{JK_1}+\overrightarrow{JK_2}+\overrightarrow{JK_3})$.

On the other hand, for a point $Q$ of the circumcircle let $Q'$ be the second point of the circumcircle on line $JQ$; then $\overrightarrow{JQ'}=\frac{p}{JQ^2}\overrightarrow{JQ}$ (power of a point), so the inverse of $Q$, namely $J+\frac{\rho^2}{JQ^2}\overrightarrow{JQ}$, equals $J+\frac{\rho^2}{p}\overrightarrow{JQ'}$. Hence the image of the circumcircle is its image under the homothety with centre $J$ and ratio $\rho^2/p$: a circle with centre $J+\frac{\rho^2}{p}\overrightarrow{JO}$ and radius $\frac{\rho^2R}{|p|}$. Comparing, $\frac{\rho^2R}{|p|}=\frac\rho2$ and $\frac{\rho^2}{p}\overrightarrow{JO}=\frac12\sum\overrightarrow{JK_i}$, which is the claim. $\square$

The two circles. The side lines of triangle $BDF$ are $BC$, $AB$, $\ell$, touching $\omega$ at $K$, $L$, $T$; those of $CDE$ are $BC$, $AC$, $\ell$, touching at $K$, $N$, $T$. Let $p_1,p_2$ be the powers of $I$ with respect to $\omega_1,\omega_2$. By the lemma, $|p_i|=2R_ir\ne0$ (so $p_1\ne p_2$, as $R_1\ne R_2$) and \[\vec O_1=\frac{p_1}{2r^2}\bigl(\vec K+\vec L+\vec T\bigr),\qquad \vec O_2=\frac{p_2}{2r^2}\bigl(\vec K+\vec N+\vec T\bigr).\]

The point $X$. Define $X$ by $\vec X=\dfrac{p_2\vec O_1-p_1\vec O_2}{p_2-p_1}$. Then \[\vec X=\frac{p_1p_2}{p_2-p_1}\Bigl(\frac{\vec O_1}{p_1}-\frac{\vec O_2}{p_2}\Bigr)=\frac{p_1p_2}{2r^2(p_2-p_1)}\,(\vec L-\vec N)=\frac{p_1p_2}{2r^2(p_2-p_1)}\,\overrightarrow{NL}.\] So $X\ne I$ and $IX\parallel NL$. Since $AL=AN$ and $IL=IN$, the line $AI$ is the perpendicular bisector of $LN$; hence $IX\perp AI$, i.e. $\angle AIX=90^\circ$.

Finally $X$ is a centre of similitude: from the definition, $\vec O_1-\vec X=\frac{p_1}{p_2-p_1}(\vec O_2-\vec O_1)$ and $\vec O_2-\vec X=\frac{p_2}{p_2-p_1}(\vec O_2-\vec O_1)$, so $\overrightarrow{XO_1}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}\overrightarrow{XO_2}$ with $\bigl|\frac{p_1}{p_2}\bigr|=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$. The homothety with centre $X$ and ratio $p_1/p_2$ therefore maps $\omega_2$ onto $\omega_1$. It is the external centre (where the two common tangents meet) when $p_1p_2>0$, that is when $I$ lies inside both circles or outside both, and the internal centre otherwise. This proves the statement.

Solution 2 (Complex numbers with the incircle as unit circle)

We solve the edited statement ($\ell$ meets the lines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at $D$, $E$, $F$). Let $\omega_1=(BDF)$ and $\omega_2=(CDE)$ have centres $O_1,O_2$ and radii $R_1\ne R_2$. Take the incircle as the unit circle, $I=0$, and let $k,n,l,t$ be the points where it touches $BC$, $CA$, $AB$, $\ell$ (so $|k|=|n|=|l|=|t|=1$). The tangents at unit points $u,v$ meet at $\frac{2uv}{u+v}$; thus $A=\frac{2ln}{l+n}$, and $B,D,F$ (resp. $C,D,E$) are the pairwise intersections of the tangents at $k,l,t$ (resp. $k,n,t$).

Circumcentre of a tangential triangle. Let a triangle be formed by the tangents at $u,v,w$ and put \[\lambda=\frac{2uvw}{(u+v)(v+w)(w+u)},\qquad O=\lambda(u+v+w).\] For the vertex $V_w=\frac{2uv}{u+v}$ we get $O-V_w=\frac{2uv\,[w(u+v+w)-(v+w)(w+u)]}{(u+v)(v+w)(w+u)}=\frac{-2u^2v^2}{(u+v)(v+w)(w+u)}$, whose modulus $\frac{2}{|(u+v)(v+w)(w+u)|}$ is symmetric in $u,v,w$. So $O$ is the circumcentre and the circumradius is $|\lambda|$. Moreover $\lambda$ is real: using $\bar u=1/u$ and $\overline{u+v}=\frac{u+v}{uv}$, \[\bar\lambda=\frac{2/(uvw)}{(u+v)(v+w)(w+u)/(u^2v^2w^2)}=\lambda .\]

Conclusion. Hence $O_1=\lambda_1(k+l+t)$ and $O_2=\lambda_2(k+n+t)$ with real $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$, $|\lambda_i|=R_i$; as $R_1\ne R_2$, $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$. Put \[X=\frac{\lambda_2O_1-\lambda_1O_2}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}=\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}\,(l-n).\] Then $O_1-X=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}(O_2-O_1)$ and $O_2-X=\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}(O_2-O_1)$, so $X$ is the centre of the homothety with ratio $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ (of absolute value $R_1/R_2$) taking $\omega_2$ to $\omega_1$: a centre of similitude. Finally \[\frac{X}{A}=\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}\cdot\frac{(l-n)(l+n)}{2ln}=\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{2(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)}\Bigl(\frac ln-\frac nl\Bigr),\] and $\frac ln-\frac nl$ is purely imaginary because $\overline{l/n}=n/l$. So $X/A$ is a nonzero purely imaginary number, i.e. $IX\perp IA$ and $\angle AIX=90^\circ$.

Solution 3 (The two centres lie on a fixed line)

We solve the edited statement: $\ell$ meets the lines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ at $D$, $E$, $F$, and the circles $\omega_1=(BDF)$, $\omega_2=(CDE)$ (centres $O_1,O_2$, radii $R_1\ne R_2$) are assumed non-degenerate.

Coordinates. The lines $BC$ and $\ell$ are two tangents to $\omega$ through $D$, so $D\ne I$ and the line $DI$ bisects an angle between them. Put $D=(0,0)$ and $I=(t,0)$ with $t>0$, and let $\mathbf u=(c,s)$ and $\mathbf v=(c,-s)$ be unit vectors along $BC$ and $\ell$, with $c,s>0$ (the two lines are distinct and symmetric about the $x$-axis). The distance from $I$ to either line is $ts$, so the inradius is $ts$.

Lemma. Let a circle $k$ with centre $(H,K)$ and radius $R$ pass through $D$ and meet $BC$ again at $P_1=b\mathbf u$ and $\ell$ again at $P_2=f\mathbf v$ ($b,f\ne0$), and suppose the line $P_1P_2$ touches $\omega$. Then \[\text{(i) } bf=4tH-t^2,\qquad\text{(ii) line }P_1P_2:\ 2H\xi-2K\eta=4tH-t^2,\qquad\text{(iii) } 2sR=|2H-t|.\] Proof. In running coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$ the circle is $\xi^2+\eta^2-2H\xi-2K\eta=0$. Substituting $P_1$ gives $b^2=2b(cH+sK)$, so $b=2(cH+sK)$; likewise $f=2(cH-sK)$, and $bf=4(c^2H^2-s^2K^2)$. The line $2H\xi-2K\eta=bf$ contains $P_1$ (since $2Hbc-2Kbs=b\cdot f$) and $P_2$ (since $2Hfc+2Kfs=f\cdot b$), and $P_1\ne P_2$; it is a genuine line because $(H,K)\ne(0,0)$ ($D$ lies on $k$, so it is not the centre). So it is the line $P_1P_2$. Its distance from $I=(t,0)$ equals the radius $ts$: \[(2tH-bf)^2=4t^2s^2(H^2+K^2).\] Using $s^2=1-c^2$, the difference of the two sides is $4t^2(c^2H^2-s^2K^2)-4tH\,bf+b^2f^2=bf\,(t^2-4tH+bf)$. As $bf\ne0$, this gives (i), and then (ii). Finally $4s^2R^2=4s^2(H^2+K^2)=4H^2-4(c^2H^2-s^2K^2)=4H^2-bf=(2H-t)^2$, which is (iii). $\square$

Applying the lemma. The circle $\omega_1$ passes through $D$, meets $BC$ again at $B$ and $\ell$ again at $F$, and $BF$ is the line $AB$, which touches $\omega$. Likewise $\omega_2$ meets $BC$ at $C$ and $\ell$ at $E$, and $CE$ is the line $AC$. Let $A=(a,h)$ and $O_i=(H_i,K_i)$. Since $A$ lies on $AB$ and on $AC$, (ii) gives for $i=1,2$ \[2(a-2t)H_i-2hK_i+t^2=0 .\] So both centres lie on the line $\lambda:\ 2(a-2t)\xi-2h\eta+t^2=0$ (a genuine line: if $a-2t$ and $h$ were both $0$, the equation would read $t^2=0$, yet $O_1$ satisfies it). By (iii), $R_i=\frac{|H_i-t/2|}{s}$: the radius is proportional to the distance of the centre from the line $m:\ \xi=\frac t2$, the perpendicular bisector of $DI$.

The centre of similitude. Since $R_1\ne R_2$, we get $|H_1-\frac t2|\ne|H_2-\frac t2|$, so $H_1\ne H_2$. Thus $O_1\ne O_2$, the line $O_1O_2$ is $\lambda$, and it is not parallel to $m$. Let $X=(\frac t2,k_0)$ be the intersection of $\lambda$ and $m$. As $X,O_1,O_2$ lie on $\lambda$, we have $\overrightarrow{XO_1}=\mu\,\overrightarrow{XO_2}$, and comparing first coordinates, $\mu=\frac{H_1-t/2}{H_2-t/2}$, so $|\mu|=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$. The homothety with centre $X$ and ratio $\mu$ maps $O_2$ to $O_1$ and the circle $\omega_2$ to the circle with centre $O_1$ and radius $|\mu|R_2=R_1$, namely $\omega_1$. So $X$ is a centre of similitude of $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.

The right angle. As $X\in\lambda$: $(a-2t)t-2hk_0+t^2=0$, that is, $2hk_0=t(a-t)$. Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

Let $n\ge 3$ be a positive integer and let $a_1,\dots,a_n$ be positive real numbers. The following product is written on the board: \[a_1\times a_2\times\cdots\times a_n\] It is known that replacing any one of the multiplication signs by an addition sign will increase the value of the expression. Prove that replacing all of the multiplication signs by addition signs will also increase the value of the expression.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Merging two neighbours)

Write $P=a_1a_2\cdots a_n$ and $S=a_1+\dots+a_n$. We prove the statement for every $n\ge2$ by induction on $n$. The hypothesis says: for each $k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, $(a_1\cdots a_k)+(a_{k+1}\cdots a_n)>P$.

$n=2$. The hypothesis is $a_1+a_2>a_1a_2$, which is exactly the claim.

Step $n\ge3$. First we find two neighbours with $a_i+a_{i+1}\ge a_ia_{i+1}$.

  • If some $a_j\le1$, take any neighbour $a_{j'}$ of it: $a_ja_{j'}\le a_{j'}<a_j+a_{j'}$.

  • If all $a_j>1$: the hypothesis for $k=1$ says $a_1+R>a_1R$ with $R=a_2a_3\cdots a_n$, i.e. $(a_1-1)(R-1)<1$. Since $a_3,\dots,a_n>1$ we have $R>a_2>1$, so $(a_1-1)(a_2-1)<(a_1-1)(R-1)<1$, i.e. $a_1+a_2>a_1a_2$.

Now replace the two neighbours $a_i,a_{i+1}$ by the single number $a_ia_{i+1}$. The new list has $n-1\ge2$ positive terms, the same product $P$, and its “cut” values $(\text{product of a prefix})+(\text{product of the rest})$ are exactly the original cut values for $k\ne i$. So the new list satisfies the hypothesis, and by induction its sum $S'$ is greater than $P$. Finally $S=S'-a_ia_{i+1}+a_i+a_{i+1}\ge S'>P$, which completes the induction.

Solution 2 (The cut where the prefix product jumps)

Let $P=a_1\cdots a_n$, $S=a_1+\dots+a_n$, $L_k=a_1\cdots a_k$ (with $L_0=1$, $L_n=P$).

Small product. If $P<4$, AM–GM gives $S\ge nP^{1/n}$, and $nP^{1/n}>P$ because $P^{(n-1)/n}<4^{(n-1)/n}\le n$ for $n\ge3$ ($4^{2/3}<3$, and $4^{(n-1)/n}<4\le n$ for $n\ge4$). So $S>P$.

Large product. Let $P\ge4$ and let $r_1\le r_2$ be the roots of $t^2-Pt+P=0$. For $t>0$, $t+\frac Pt>P$ holds iff $t<r_1$ or $t>r_2$. Since the quadratic is positive at $t=1$ and $t=P$ and non-positive at $t=2$, we have $1<r_1\le2\le r_2<P$. The hypothesis says $L_k\notin[r_1,r_2]$ for $1\le k\le n-1$; also $L_0=1<r_1$ and $L_n=P>r_2$.

Let $s$ be the least index with $L_s>r_2$; then $L_{s-1}<r_1$. Put $A=L_{s-1}=a_1\cdots a_{s-1}$ and $B=a_{s+1}\cdots a_n=P/L_s$ (empty products are $1$), and let $\sigma_A,\sigma_B$ be the sums of the same groups. Then $P=Aa_sB$, $A<r_1\le2$ and $B<P/r_2=r_1\le2$.

Claim: a non-empty group with product $X<2$ has sum at least $X$. With $q$ terms, AM–GM gives sum $\ge qX^{1/q}$; for $q=1$ this is $X$, and for $q\ge2$, $X^{1-1/q}<2\le q$.

We need $S=a_s+\sigma_A+\sigma_B>P=a_s+P\bigl(1-\frac1{AB}\bigr)$, i.e. $\sigma_A+\sigma_B>P\bigl(1-\frac1{AB}\bigr)$. If $A>1$, the group of $A$ is non-empty and the hypothesis for $k=s-1$ gives $A+\frac PA>P$, i.e. $P(1-\frac1A)<A\le\sigma_A$. Similarly, if $B>1$ then $P(1-\frac1B)<B\le\sigma_B$.

  • $AB\le1$: the right side is $\le0<\sigma_A+\sigma_B$ (for $n\ge3$ at least one group is non-empty).

  • $A\ge1$, $B\ge1$, $AB>1$: since $1-\frac1{AB}=(1-\frac1A)+\frac1A(1-\frac1B)\le(1-\frac1A)+(1-\frac1B)$, we get $P(1-\frac1{AB})\le P(1-\frac1A)+P(1-\frac1B)$. Each term is $0\le\sigma$ if the product is $1$, and $<\sigma$ if it is $>1$; at least one of $A,B$ exceeds $1$, so the total is $<\sigma_A+\sigma_B$.

  • $A<1<B$, $AB>1$: $1-\frac1{AB}<1-\frac1B$, so $P(1-\frac1{AB})<P(1-\frac1B)<B\le\sigma_B$. The case $B<1<A$ is symmetric.

In all cases $S>P$.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

Define the pseudo-derivative $\Delta:\mathbb{N}_{>1}\to\mathbb{N}$ as follows: for a prime $p$ and a positive integer $a$ let $\Delta(p^a)=ap^{a-1}$ and for coprime integers $m,n$ let $\Delta(mn)=\Delta(m)\cdot\Delta(n)$. Is it true that for any positive integer $k$ there exists a positive integer $n$ for which $\Delta^{(k)}(n)=n$ but $\Delta^{(m)}(n)\ne n$ for every $m<k$?

Comment: $\Delta^{(1)}(n)=\Delta(n)$, $\Delta^{(k+1)}(n)=\Delta\left(\Delta^{(k)}(n)\right)$.

Show the answer

Yes

Show the solution

Solution (A rotating pattern of exponents)

The answer is $\boxed{\text{Yes}}$. If $n=\prod q_i^{e_i}$ (distinct primes $q_i$, $e_i\ge1$), then $\Delta(n)=\prod e_iq_i^{e_i-1}$. In particular, a prime that divides $n$ exactly once contributes the factor $1\cdot q^0=1$.

$k=1$. $\Delta(4)=2\cdot2=4$.

The mechanism ($k\ge2$). Fix distinct odd primes $p_1,\dots,p_k$, and read indices modulo $k$ (so $p_{k+1}=p_1$). For a positive integer $x$ let $x'$ be $x$ with all factors $p_1,\dots,p_k$ removed. Suppose that positive integers $a_1,\dots,a_k$ satisfy

  • (i) $a_i\equiv1\pmod 4$, and $a_i$ and $a_i+1$ are squarefree;

  • (ii) $p_i\mid a_i$, $p_{i+1}\mid a_i+1$, and no other $p_j$ divides $a_i(a_i+1)$;

  • (iii) for $i\ne j$: $\gcd(a_i',a_j')=1$ and $\gcd\bigl((a_i+1)',a_j'\bigr)=1$.

For each $i$ let $c_i=(a_i+1)\prod_{j\ne i}a_j$, let $E_i$ be the exponent vector $(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ with its $i$-th entry increased by $1$, and put \[n_i=c_{i-1}'\cdot\prod_{j=1}^k p_j^{(E_i)_j}.\] The $k$ factors $(a_{i-1}+1)'$ and $a_j'$ ($j\ne i-1$) of $c_{i-1}'$ are squarefree by (i), and pairwise coprime by (iii): the odd numbers $a_j'$ are pairwise coprime, and $(a_{i-1}+1)'$ is coprime to each of them (two different numbers $a_j+1$ never occur together in one $c_i$). So $c_{i-1}'$ is squarefree and coprime to every $p_j$, and each of its primes contributes the factor $1$ to $\Delta$. As every exponent $(E_i)_j$ is at least $1$, \[\Delta(n_i)=\prod_j (E_i)_j\,p_j^{(E_i)_j-1}=c_i\prod_j p_j^{(E_i)_j-1}.\] By (ii) and (i), each $a_j$ ($j\ne i$) contributes to $c_i$ exactly one factor $p_j$ and no other $p$, and $a_i+1$ contributes exactly one factor $p_{i+1}$ and no other $p$. Since $p_i\ne p_{i+1}$, this gives: $p_j$ divides $c_i$ exactly once for $j\notin\{i,i+1\}$, $p_i\nmid c_i$, and $p_{i+1}^2$ divides $c_i$ but $p_{i+1}^3$ does not. So the exponent of $p_j$ in $\Delta(n_i)$ is \[a_j-1+1=a_j\ \ (j\notin\{i,i+1\}),\qquad (a_i+1)-1=a_i\ \ (j=i),\qquad a_{i+1}-1+2=a_{i+1}+1\ \ (j=i+1).\] This is the vector $E_{i+1}$, and the part of $\Delta(n_i)$ free of the $p_j$ is $c_i'$. Thus $\Delta(n_i)=n_{i+1}$ for every $i$ (also when $k=2$, where $i+1$ and $i-1$ coincide modulo $2$).

Consequently $\Delta^{(m)}(n_1)=n_{1+m}$ for all $m\ge1$, and $\Delta^{(k)}(n_1)=n_{k+1}=n_1$. For $1\le m<k$ the exponent of $p_1$ is $a_1+1$ in $n_1$ but $a_1$ in $n_{1+m}$ (since $1+m\not\equiv1\pmod k$), so $\Delta^{(m)}(n_1)\ne n_1$. Hence $n=n_1$ has the required property.

Choosing the $a_i$. We choose $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$ in turn. Suppose $a_1,\dots,a_{i-1}$ are already chosen. Let $S$ be the (finite) set of odd primes dividing $p_1p_2\cdots p_k\prod_{j<i}a_j(a_j+1)$, and let $Q=4\prod_{q\in S}q^2$. Since $p_i\ne p_{i+1}$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives an integer $c$ with $1\le c\le Q$ and \[c\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\qquad c\equiv p_i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p_i^2),\qquad c\equiv p_{i+1}-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p_{i+1}^2),\qquad c\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2)\ \text{for the other }q\in S .\] Every $t\equiv c\pmod Q$ then has these properties: $t\equiv1\pmod4$, so $t$ is odd and $t+1\equiv2\pmod 4$; $p_i$ divides $t$ exactly once and does not divide $t+1$; $p_{i+1}$ divides $t+1$ exactly once and does not divide $t$; and for every other $q\in S$ we have $t\equiv1$ and $t+1\equiv2\pmod q$, so $q\nmid t(t+1)$. Hence $a_i=t$ satisfies (ii). It also satisfies (iii) together with $a_1,\dots,a_{i-1}$: an odd prime dividing $t'$ or $(t+1)'$ and also some $a_j$ or $a_j+1$ with $j<i$ would lie in $S$ but not among the $p$'s, and such primes divide neither $t$ nor $t+1$ (the prime $2$ divides no $a_j'$ and not $t'$). It remains to find such a $t$ for which $t$ and $t+1$ are squarefree. By what we just showed, $4\nmid t(t+1)$ and $q^2\nmid t(t+1)$ for $q\in S$, so we only need $q^2\nmid t$ and $q^2\nmid t+1$ for every odd prime $q\notin S$.

Let $t=c+Qu$ with $u=0,1,\dots,U-1$, where $U$ is a large integer. Fix an odd prime $q\notin S$. It is prime to $Q$, so $q^2\mid c+Qu$ holds for $u$ in a single residue class modulo $q^2$, that is, for at most $\frac U{q^2}+1$ values of $u$; the same holds for $q^2\mid c+Qu+1$. Moreover $t+1\le c+QU$, so only primes $q\le\sqrt{c+QU}$ can occur. Since $\frac1{m^2}<\frac1{m^2-1}=\frac12\bigl(\frac1{m-1}-\frac1{m+1}\bigr)$, the sum of $\frac1{m^2}$ over odd $m\ge3$ is less than $\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$ (the sum telescopes). So the number of bad values of $u$ is at most \[\sum_{q}2\Bigl(\frac U{q^2}+1\Bigr)<2\cdot\frac U4+2\sqrt{c+QU},\] which is less than $U$ once $U$ is large enough. So a good $t$ exists, we take it as $a_i$, and the construction goes through for every $k\ge2$.

Example. For $k=2$ and $p_1=3$, $p_2=5$ one may take $a_1=129=3\cdot43$ (with $130=2\cdot5\cdot13$) and $a_2=3305=5\cdot661$ (with $3306=2\cdot3\cdot19\cdot29$); then \[n_1=2\cdot19\cdot29\cdot43\cdot3^{130}5^{3305}\ \longrightarrow\ 2\cdot13\cdot661\cdot3^{129}5^{3306}\ \longrightarrow\ n_1 .\]

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

On a horizontal line $\ell$ there are $n$ blue points $b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n$. Above that line are given $n$ red points $r_1,\dots,r_n$ such that every line passing through two red points doesn't contain another red or blue point. Ayala and Barvaz play a game. Ayala draws $n$ segments such that every red point is connected to a blue point and vice versa. In every turn, Barvaz can choose two intersecting segments $b_ir_j$, $b_kr_l$ and replace them by the segments $b_kr_j$, $b_ir_l$. Prove that Barvaz can make all of the segments to not intersect each other after at most $\binom{n}{2}$ turns.

Show the solution

Solution (Untangle from the highest red point)

Take $\ell$ as the $x$-axis with the red points above it. A flip replaces two crossing segments $b_ir_j$, $b_kr_l$ by $b_kr_j$, $b_ir_l$. We describe a strategy for Barvaz and show that it uses at most $\binom n2$ flips.

Normalisation. For small $\varepsilon>0$ the map $(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(\frac{x}{1+\varepsilon x},\frac{y}{1+\varepsilon x}\bigr)$ is a projective transformation, defined on a region containing all the given points; it maps segments to segments, maps $\ell$ to itself keeping the order of points on it, and keeps the upper half-plane and all collinearities. So it turns the problem into an equivalent one. Two red points $(x_1,y_1)\ne(x_2,y_2)$ get the same new height only if $y_1-y_2+\varepsilon(y_1x_2-y_2x_1)=0$, which holds for at most one $\varepsilon$. Hence we may assume that the red points have pairwise different heights.

Shadows and good pairs. If a red point $y$ is lower than a red point $x$, the ray from $x$ through $y$ meets $\ell$; call this point the shadow $\pi_x(y)$. It is not a blue point (no line through two red points contains a blue point). Consider two segments of a matching with red ends $x$, $y$ ($x$ higher) and blue ends $\beta_x,\beta_y$; the points $\beta_x,\beta_y,\pi_x(y)$ of $\ell$ are distinct.

Claim 1. The two segments cross if and only if $\beta_x$ lies between $\beta_y$ and $\pi_x(y)$. The line $x\beta_x$ meets $\ell$ only at $\beta_x$, so two points of $\ell$ are on the same side of this line iff they are on the same side of $\beta_x$. The point $y$ lies on the segment from $x$ to $\pi_x(y)$, which meets line $x\beta_x$ only at $x$, so $y$ is on the side of $\pi_x(y)$. If $\beta_x$ is not between $\beta_y$ and $\pi_x(y)$, the points $y,\beta_y$ are on one side and the segments do not cross. Otherwise the segment $y\beta_y$ meets line $x\beta_x$ at a point of height between $0$ and the height of $y$, which is less than that of $x$; such a point lies on the segment $x\beta_x$.

Call the pair good if $\beta_y$ lies between $\beta_x$ and $\pi_x(y)$, and bad otherwise; crossing pairs are bad. For a set $G$ of segments let $\Phi(G)$ be the number of bad pairs in $G$; clearly $\Phi(G)\le\binom{|G|}2$.

Claim 2. If red points $w$ above $q$ are both lower than a red point $r$, then $\pi_r(q)$ lies strictly between $\pi_r(w)$ and $\pi_w(q)$. The point $q$ is inside the segment from $w$ to $\pi_w(q)$; the rays from $r$ through the points of this segment sweep the triangle $r\,w\,\pi_w(q)$ in order and meet $\ell$ along the segment from $\pi_r(w)$ to $\pi_w(q)$, so the ray through $q$ meets it strictly between the ends.

The strategy. Untangle$(G)$, for a set $G$ of current segments: if $|G|\le1$, stop. Otherwise let $r$ be the highest red end in $G$ and $s$ its segment. While $s$ crosses some segment of $G$, flip $s$ with the crossing segment whose red end is highest (the new segment at $r$ is again called $s$). When $s$ crosses nothing, let $b$ be its blue end and let $G_L$, $G_R$ be the segments of $G$ with blue end left, resp. right, of $b$; run Untangle$(G_L)$, then Untangle$(G_R)$. Barvaz runs Untangle on the whole matching.

Claim 3 (one flip lowers $\Phi$). Let $s=br$ (with $r$ highest in $G$) be flipped with $t=b'q$ as in the strategy, giving $s'=b'r$, $t'=bq$. Then $\Phi(G)$ drops by at least $1$.

Proof. Pairs not containing $s$ or $t$ do not change. Reflecting the picture in a vertical line if necessary, $b'$ is left of $b$; by Claim 1 (with $x=r$), $b$ lies between $b'$ and $\hat q=\pi_r(q)$, so $b'<b<\hat q$ on $\ell$. The pair $\{s,t\}$ was bad (crossing), and $\{s',t'\}$ is good, since the blue end $b$ of $q$ lies between the blue end $b'$ of $r$ and $\hat q$. Now let $u=b''w$ be another segment of $G$ and $\hat w=\pi_r(w)$. With $x=r$: good$(s,u)$ $\iff$ $b''$ is between $b$ and $\hat w$; good$(s',u)$ $\iff$ $b''$ is between $b'$ and $\hat w$. We show that $\{s',u\},\{t',u\}$ contain at least as many good pairs as $\{s,u\},\{t,u\}$. If $b''<b'$ or $b''>b$, then $b$ and $b'$ are on the same side of $b''$ and the two conditions for $s,s'$ coincide.

Case A: $w$ is higher than $q$. Put $\tilde q=\pi_w(q)$; good$(t,u)$ $\iff$ $b'$ is between $b''$ and $\tilde q$; good$(t',u)$ $\iff$ $b$ is between $b''$ and $\tilde q$. By the choice of $t$, the segment $u$ (whose red end is higher than $q$) does not cross $s$, so by Claim 1 $b$ is not between $b''$ and $\hat w$. By Claim 2, $\hat q$ is between $\hat w$ and $\tilde q$, and $\hat q>b$, so $\hat w>b$ or $\tilde q>b$.

  • $b''<b'$: then $\hat w<b$ (else $b$ would be between $b''$ and $\hat w$), so $\tilde q>b>b'$ and both $t$-pairs are good.

  • $b''>b$: good$(t,u)\iff\tilde q<b'$, good$(t',u)\iff\tilde q<b$, and the first implies the second.

  • $b'<b''<b$: again $\hat w<b$, so $\tilde q>b$; then $\{t',u\}$ is good and $\{t,u\}$ is bad, which makes up for a possible loss of one good pair among the $s$-pairs.

Case B: $w$ is lower than $q$. Put $\tilde w=\pi_q(w)$; good$(t,u)$ $\iff$ $b''$ is between $b'$ and $\tilde w$; good$(t',u)$ $\iff$ $b''$ is between $b$ and $\tilde w$. If $b''<b'$ or $b''>b$, nothing changes. If $b'<b''<b$, the four conditions read $\hat w<b''$, $\hat w>b''$ (for $s,s'$) and $\tilde w>b''$, $\tilde w<b''$ (for $t,t'$), so the number of good pairs can drop only if $\hat w<b''<\tilde w$. But by Claim 2 (with $q$ above $w$), $\hat w$ lies between $\hat q$ and $\tilde w$; as $\hat w<\tilde w$ this forces $\hat q<\hat w<b''<b$, contradicting $\hat q>b$. $\square$

Claim 4 (splitting). When the loop stops, every segment of $G_L$ lies in the open half-plane on the left of line $s$, and every segment of $G_R$ on the right. Indeed, no red end other than $r$ lies on line $s$ (otherwise the line through $r$ and that red point would contain the blue point $b$), and a segment with blue end left of $b$ and red end on the right of line $s$ would cross this line at a height below its red end, hence below $r$, i.e. on $s$. The blue ends left of $b$ are on the left side as well, and a half-plane is convex. Flips inside $G_L$ only re-pair these endpoints, so $G_L$ stays in its half-plane, and likewise $G_R$. Hence no segment of $G_L$ ever crosses $s$ or a segment of $G_R$, and $s$ is never touched again. (The same argument, applied at every level, shows that the segments outside the group being processed never interact with it.)

Counting. We show by induction on $|G|$ that Untangle$(G)$ makes at most $\Phi(G)$ flips (with $\Phi$ computed at the start) and leaves $G$ without crossings. By Claim 3 each flip of the loop lowers $\Phi(G)$ by at least $1$; as $\Phi\ge0$, the loop stops after at most $\Phi(G)-\Phi_{\mathrm{end}}$ flips. At that moment $\Phi_{\mathrm{end}}\ge\Phi(G_L)+\Phi(G_R)$, because the pairs inside $G_L$ and inside $G_R$ are pairs of $G$. By induction the two recursive calls make at most $\Phi(G_L)+\Phi(G_R)$ flips and remove all crossings inside $G_L$ and $G_R$; by Claim 4 there are no other crossings. The total is at most $\Phi(G)$.

For the whole matching this gives at most $\Phi\le\binom n2$ flips, after which no two segments intersect.

Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods

A grasshopper jumps on the number line. It starts at the point $x=1$ with $E$ energy. At each step, the grasshopper can jump to the right; if it jumps from point $y$ to point $z$, it must spend $\frac{z}{y^2}$ energy. Find the minimum $E$ such that for every $n$, the grasshopper can perform $n$ jumps.

Show the answer

$E=4$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A recursive AM-GM bound)

A run of $n$ jumps is a sequence $1=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n$, with cost $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i+1}/x_i^2$.

$E=4$ suffices. Jump to $x_i=2^i$. The cost is $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{2^{i+1}}{2^{2i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{1-i}=4-2^{2-n}<4$ for every $n$.

Lower bound. Define $f_1=1$ and $f_{j+1}=2\sqrt{f_j}$. Taking $\log_2$, $g_j=\log_2f_j$ satisfies $g_1=0$, $g_{j+1}=1+\frac{g_j}2$, so $g_j=2-2^{2-j}$ and $f_j=2^{\,2-2^{2-j}}$; thus $f_j<4$ and $f_j\to4$.

Claim. For every $y>0$ and every $j$ jumps $y=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_j$, the cost $\sum_{i<j}x_{i+1}/x_i^2$ is greater than $f_j/y$.

Proof by induction on $j$. For $j=1$: $x_1/y^2>y/y^2=1/y$. Step: the first jump costs $x_1/y^2$, and the remaining $j$ jumps start at $x_1$, so by the induction hypothesis the total exceeds \[\frac{x_1}{y^2}+\frac{f_j}{x_1}\ \ge\ 2\sqrt{\frac{x_1}{y^2}\cdot\frac{f_j}{x_1}}=\frac{2\sqrt{f_j}}{y}=\frac{f_{j+1}}y\] by AM–GM. $\square$

So $n$ jumps from $x=1$ always cost more than $f_n$. If $E<4$, choose $n$ with $f_n>E$; then $n$ jumps are impossible. Hence the minimum is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 2 (One weighted AM-GM)

A run of $n$ jumps is $1=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n$ with cost $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}t_i$, where $t_i=x_{i+1}/x_i^2$.

$E=4$ suffices. With $x_i=2^i$ the cost is $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{1-i}=4-2^{2-n}<4$.

Lower bound. For $n=1$ the cost is $x_1>1$. Let $n\ge2$ and take the weights \[\lambda_i=2^{-(i+1)}\ (0\le i\le n-2),\qquad \lambda_{n-1}=2^{-(n-1)},\qquad \textstyle\sum\lambda_i=1 .\] Weighted AM–GM gives $\sum t_i=\sum\lambda_i\frac{t_i}{\lambda_i}\ge\prod\bigl(\frac{t_i}{\lambda_i}\bigr)^{\lambda_i}$. In $\prod t_i^{\lambda_i}$ the exponent of $x_j$ for $1\le j\le n-2$ is $\lambda_{j-1}-2\lambda_j=0$, the exponent of $x_{n-1}$ is $\lambda_{n-2}-2\lambda_{n-1}=-2^{-(n-1)}$ and that of $x_n$ is $2^{-(n-1)}$ (and $x_0=1$). So $\prod t_i^{\lambda_i}=(x_n/x_{n-1})^{2^{-(n-1)}}>1$, and \[\sum t_i>\prod\lambda_i^{-\lambda_i}=2^{\,\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j2^{-j}+(n-1)2^{-(n-1)}}=2^{\,2-2^{2-n}},\] using $\sum_{j=1}^{m}j2^{-j}=2-(m+2)2^{-m}$. The bound $2^{2-2^{2-n}}$ tends to $4$, so for any $E<4$ some $n$ has bound $>E$ and $n$ jumps are impossible. The answer is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 3 (An exact identity for one jump)

The identity. For $0<y<z$, \[\frac z{y^2}-4\Bigl(\frac1y-\frac1z\Bigr)=\frac{z^2-4yz+4y^2}{zy^2}=\frac{(z-2y)^2}{zy^2}\ \ge\ 0 .\tag{$*$}\] So a jump from $y$ to $z$ costs at least $4\bigl(\frac1y-\frac1z\bigr)$, with equality exactly when $z=2y$.

$E=4$ suffices. Jump $1\to2\to4\to\cdots$. The first $n$ jumps cost exactly $4\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr)<4$ by $(*)$ (or directly: $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{1-i}=4-2^{2-n}$).

$E<4$ fails. Suppose $E<4$ and the grasshopper makes $n\ge1$ jumps $1=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n$ with total cost at most $E$. Adding $(*)$ over the jumps, the right sides telescope: \[E\ \ge\ \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i^2}\ \ge\ 4\Bigl(1-\frac1{x_n}\Bigr),\] so $\frac1{x_n}\ge1-\frac E4>0$. Every jump costs $\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_i^2}>\frac1{x_i}\ge\frac1{x_n}\ge\frac{4-E}4$, hence $E>n\cdot\frac{4-E}4$, that is, $n<\frac{4E}{4-E}$. So for $n\ge\frac{4E}{4-E}$ (and for every $n\ge1$ if $E\le0$) the $n$ jumps are impossible. The minimum is $\boxed{4}$.

Problem 2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Let $n>2026^{2026}$. A kangaroo hops on an $n\times n$ grid of squares. In each move, it can jump from a square to a side-adjacent square. In every $2026\times 2026$ subgrid, the kangaroo can visit at most $r$ times in total. Find the minimum $r$ for which the kangaroo can visit at least $\frac{n^2}{10^{100}}$ distinct squares.

Show the answer

$r=4051$

Show the solution

Solution (Sliding windows of 2k-2 moves)

Let $k=2026$. A walk is a sequence of squares $s_0,s_1,\dots,s_L$ ($L$ moves, $L+1$ visits), consecutive squares side-adjacent; visits are counted with multiplicity, including the first square. A window is a $k\times k$ subgrid. We show that the answer is $r=2k-1=\boxed{4051}$.

$r=2k-1$ is enough. Number rows and columns $0,1,\dots,n-1$. The walk traverses the rows $0,k,2k,\dots$ (all multiples of $k$ below $n$) completely, alternately left to right and right to left, and joins consecutive traversed rows $jk$ and $(j+1)k$ by the $k-1$ squares between them in the last column (for even $j$) or the first column (for odd $j$). No square is visited twice. Any $k$ consecutive rows contain exactly one multiple of $k$, so a window meets exactly one traversed row, in $k$ squares. Since $n>k$, a window does not meet both the first and the last column. In one of these columns the connecting squares lie in rows $jk+1,\dots,jk+k-1$ for $j$ of one parity only, so consecutive groups are separated by $k+1$ rows, and a window meets at most one group, in at most $k-1$ squares (connecting squares avoid the traversed rows, and one of the window's $k$ rows is traversed). So every window gets at most $2k-1$ visits. The walk visits at least $\frac nk\cdot n=\frac{n^2}{k}>\frac{n^2}{10^{100}}$ distinct squares.

$r\le2k-2$ is not enough. Suppose every window gets at most $2k-2$ visits. Call $2k-2$ consecutive moves (from $s_i$ to $s_{i+2k-2}$) a block; it has $2k-1$ visits.

  • Every block has at least $k$ moves in one of the four directions. If the columns of $s_i,\dots,s_{i+2k-2}$ spanned at most $k$ values and so did the rows, these squares would lie in one window (shift it inwards if it sticks out of the board, which is possible as $n\ge k$), which would get $2k-1>r$ visits. So, say, two of these squares differ by at least $k$ in their column; between them the walk makes at least $k$ moves in one horizontal direction. (Similarly for rows.)

  • This direction, the dominant direction of the block, is unique, since $2k>2k-2$.

  • Consecutive blocks have the same dominant direction. Blocks starting at $i$ and $i+1$ share $2k-3$ moves. If their dominant directions $\mathbf d\ne\mathbf d'$ differed, the first block would contain at least $k$ moves $\mathbf d$ and at least $k-1$ moves $\mathbf d'$ (those of the second block except possibly its last move), in total $2k-1>2k-2$ moves.

So if $L\ge2k-2$, all blocks have the same dominant direction $\mathbf d$. A block has at least $k$ moves $\mathbf d$ and at most $k-2$ moves $-\mathbf d$, so it advances at least $2$ in the direction $\mathbf d$. Cut the walk into $w=\lfloor L/(2k-2)\rfloor$ disjoint consecutive blocks and fewer than $2k-2$ remaining moves. The blocks together advance at least $2w$ in the direction $\mathbf d$, but a coordinate changes by at most $n-1$ on the board, so $2w\le n-1$. Hence \[L+1\le(w+1)(2k-2)\le\frac{n+1}2(2k-2)=(n+1)(k-1)<nk,\] and this also holds if $L<2k-2$. (For an infinite walk, apply this to every finite beginning.) So the walk visits fewer than $nk$ distinct squares. Finally $n>2026^{2026}>k\cdot10^{100}$, so $nk<\frac{n^2}{10^{100}}$. Hence $r\le2k-2$ does not allow $\frac{n^2}{10^{100}}$ distinct squares, and the minimum is $r=2k-1=4051$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Let $f:\mathbb{Z}[x,y]\to\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ be a function satisfying the following conditions:

  • $f(1)=1$, where $1$ denotes the constant polynomial $1$;

  • For all $P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$, $f(P+Q)=f(P)+f(Q)$;

  • For every $P\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ and for every prime number $r$, there exist $n_0,m_0\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that $r\mid P(n_0,m_0)$ if and only if there exist $n_1,m_1\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that $r\mid f(P)(n_1,m_1)$.

Prove that $f(PQ)=f(P)f(Q)$ for all $P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Value sets modulo primes force f of a square to be a square)

Linearity. Additivity gives $f(0)=2f(0)$, so $f(0)=0$; then $f(-P)=-f(P)$ and $f(kP)=kf(P)$ for every $k\in\mathbb Z$. With $f(1)=1$ we get $f(c)=c$ for every constant polynomial $c$.

Same value sets. For a polynomial $R\in\mathbb Z[x,y]$ and a prime $r$ let $V_r(R)=\{R(u,v)\bmod r:\ u,v\in\mathbb Z\}\subseteq\mathbb Z_r$. Let $c$ be an integer. Apply the third condition to $P-c$, using $f(P-c)=f(P)-c$: some value of $P$ at an integer point is $\equiv c\pmod r$ if and only if some value of $f(P)$ is $\equiv c\pmod r$. Hence \[V_r\bigl(f(P)\bigr)=V_r(P)\qquad\text{for every }P\text{ and every prime }r .\]

Squares. Fix $R\in\mathbb Z[x,y]$, an odd prime $r$ and a point $(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2$, and put $\alpha=f(R)(u,v)$ and $\beta=f(R^2)(u,v)$ (integers). Let $\mathcal Q\subseteq\mathbb Z_r$ be the set of squares modulo $r$, including $0$; it has $\frac{r+1}2$ elements. For every integer $t$, linearity gives $f(R^2-2tR)=f(R^2)-2tf(R)$, and $R^2-2tR=(R-t)^2-t^2$ takes only values in $\mathcal Q-t^2$ modulo $r$. Therefore \[\beta-2t\alpha\in V_r\bigl(f(R^2-2tR)\bigr)=V_r(R^2-2tR)\subseteq \mathcal Q-t^2,\] that is, $(t-\alpha)^2+\delta\in\mathcal Q$ for every integer $t$, where $\delta=\beta-\alpha^2$. As $t$ runs over all integers, $t-\alpha$ runs over all residues modulo $r$, so $\mathcal Q+\delta\subseteq\mathcal Q$. Both sets have $\frac{r+1}2$ elements, so $\mathcal Q+\delta=\mathcal Q$, and by repetition $\mathcal Q+j\delta=\mathcal Q$ for every integer $j$. If $\delta\not\equiv0\pmod r$, the multiples $j\delta$ run over all of $\mathbb Z_r$, and since $0\in\mathcal Q$, every residue $0+j\delta$ would lie in $\mathcal Q$. This is false, because there are $\frac{r-1}2\ge1$ non-squares modulo an odd prime $r$. So $\beta\equiv\alpha^2\pmod r$.

Hence the integer $f(R^2)(u,v)-f(R)(u,v)^2$ is divisible by every odd prime, so it is $0$, for every $(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2$. A polynomial in $\mathbb Z[x,y]$ that vanishes on all of $\mathbb Z^2$ is the zero polynomial: write it as $\sum_i g_i(x)y^i$; for each integer $u$ the polynomial $\sum_i g_i(u)y^i$ in $y$ has infinitely many roots, so every $g_i(u)=0$; then each $g_i$ has infinitely many roots, so $g_i=0$. Therefore \[f(R^2)=f(R)^2\qquad\text{for every }R\in\mathbb Z[x,y].\]

Products. Since $4PQ=(P+Q)^2-(P-Q)^2$, linearity and the previous step give \[4f(PQ)=f\bigl((P+Q)^2\bigr)-f\bigl((P-Q)^2\bigr)=\bigl(f(P)+f(Q)\bigr)^2-\bigl(f(P)-f(Q)\bigr)^2=4f(P)f(Q).\] The coefficients of $f(PQ)$ and $f(P)f(Q)$ are integers whose fourfolds agree, so $f(PQ)=f(P)f(Q)$ for all $P,Q\in\mathbb Z[x,y]$.

Solution 2 (Point evaluations modulo p)

Linearity. Additivity gives $f(0)=0$, $f(-P)=-f(P)$ and $f(kP)=kf(P)$ for $k\in\mathbb Z$; with $f(1)=1$, $f(c)=c$ for every constant $c$.

Fix an odd prime $p$ and a point $(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2$, and define $L(P)\in\mathbb Z_p$ as the residue of $f(P)(u,v)$ modulo $p$. Then $L(P+Q)=L(P)+L(Q)$, $L(kP)=kL(P)$ and $L(1)=1$.

$L(P)$ is a value of $P$. Let $c$ be an integer with $c\equiv L(P)$. Then $f(P-c)=f(P)-c$ vanishes modulo $p$ at $(u,v)$, so by the third condition (in the direction from $f(P-c)$ back to $P-c$) there is an integer point where $P-c$ is divisible by $p$. So $L(P)\equiv P(n,m)\pmod p$ for some integers $n,m$. In particular, if $P$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$ modulo $p$, then $L(P)\in\{0,1\}$; and if $P\equiv0$ at every integer point, then $L(P)=0$, so $L(P)=L(P')$ whenever $P\equiv P'\pmod p$ at every integer point.

Indicators. For $a,b\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}$ let \[E_{ab}=\bigl(1-(x-a)^{p-1}\bigr)\bigl(1-(y-b)^{p-1}\bigr).\] By Fermat's little theorem, at an integer point $(n,m)$ the value of $E_{ab}$ is $\equiv1$ if $n\equiv a$ and $m\equiv b\pmod p$, and $\equiv0$ otherwise. Hence every $P$ agrees modulo $p$, at every integer point, with $\sum_{a,b}P(a,b)E_{ab}$ (both sides depend only on the residues of $n,m$), and so \[L(P)=\sum_{a,b}P(a,b)\,L(E_{ab}). \tag{1}\] Each $L(E_{ab})$ is $0$ or $1$. If two different indicators $E_{ab},E_{a'b'}$ had $L=1$, then $L(E_{ab}+E_{a'b'})=2$; but $E_{ab}+E_{a'b'}$ takes only the values $0,1$ modulo $p$, and $2\not\equiv0,1$ as $p\ge3$. So at most one $L(E_{ab})$ equals $1$. On the other hand $\sum_{a,b}E_{ab}\equiv1$ at every integer point, so $\sum_{a,b}L(E_{ab})=L(1)=1$. Hence exactly one indicator, say $E_{a_0b_0}$, has $L=1$, and by (1) \[L(P)\equiv P(a_0,b_0)\pmod p\qquad\text{for every }P .\] So $L$ is evaluation at a point modulo $p$, and therefore $L(PQ)=L(P)L(Q)$.

Lifting. We have shown $f(PQ)(u,v)\equiv f(P)(u,v)\,f(Q)(u,v)\pmod p$ for every odd prime $p$ and every $(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2$. A fixed integer divisible by every odd prime is $0$, so the polynomial $f(PQ)-f(P)f(Q)$ vanishes at every integer point. A polynomial in $\mathbb Z[x,y]$ vanishing on $\mathbb Z^2$ is zero: writing it as $\sum_ig_i(x)y^i$, for each integer $u$ the polynomial $\sum_ig_i(u)y^i$ has infinitely many roots, so all $g_i(u)=0$; then each $g_i$ has infinitely many roots and is $0$. Therefore $f(PQ)=f(P)f(Q)$.

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Given positive integers $n$, $m$ and a complete graph on $n$ vertices. On each edge of the graph, Ayala writes an integer between $1$ and $m$. Barvaz chooses a vertex of the graph, an integer $k$, and a sequence $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_k$ of integers between $1$ and $m$. Ayala then walks along the vertices of the graph: she starts at the vertex chosen by Barvaz, and at the $i$-th step, she must walk along an edge labeled with the number $a_i$ (she is allowed to traverse the same edge more than once). Ayala is forbidden from returning to the starting vertex. If Ayala cannot make a move, she loses. Find all pairs $(n,m)$ for which Ayala loses.

Show the answer

Ayala loses exactly when $n\le 2m$

Show the solution

Solution (A label used once at every vertex)

We show: Ayala loses (Barvaz can always win) if and only if $n\le2m$. Ayala knows the whole sequence before walking; this does not matter below, because in the first part her moves are forced and in the second part she never needs to look ahead.

$n\le 2m$: Barvaz wins. Fix any labelling. If some vertex $u$ has no edge with some label $\ell$, Barvaz chooses $u$, $k=1$, $a_1=\ell$, and Ayala cannot move. Otherwise every label occurs at every vertex. A vertex has $n-1\le2m-1$ edges, so some label occurs exactly once at it (if all $m$ labels occurred twice, there would be at least $2m$ edges). For each vertex $u$ choose such a label $c(u)$ and let $g(u)$ be the other end of the unique edge at $u$ labelled $c(u)$. Then $g(u)\ne u$, and the map $g$ on the finite vertex set has a cycle $v,\,g(v),\dots,g^{j-1}(v)$ of distinct vertices with $g^j(v)=v$, $j\ge2$.

Barvaz chooses the start $v$, $k=j$ and $a_i=c\bigl(g^{i-1}(v)\bigr)$. By induction, before step $i$ Ayala stands at $g^{i-1}(v)$: the only edge there with label $a_i$ goes to $g^{i}(v)$, which for $i<j$ is not the start, so her move is forced. At step $j$ the only edge with label $a_j$ leads to $g^j(v)=v$, the forbidden start. So she loses.

$n\ge 2m+1$: Ayala wins. Number the vertices by $\mathbb Z_n$ and let $d(i,j)$ be their distance around the cycle ($1\le d\le n/2$). Ayala writes $\min\{d(i,j),m\}$ on edge $ij$. For a vertex $u$ and a label $\ell\le m$, the two vertices $u\pm\ell$ are distinct (as $0<2\ell\le2m<n$), both at distance exactly $\ell$, so both edges get label $\ell$. Thus every label occurs at least twice at every vertex.

Whatever Barvaz chooses, Ayala moves greedily: at the current vertex $x$ there are at least two edges with the required label, and at most one of them leads to the start (none if $x$ is the start). So she can always move and never loses.

Hence Ayala loses exactly for the pairs $\boxed{(n,m)\text{ with }n\le2m}$.

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Find all sequences $a_n$ of real numbers such that $a_1=1$, $a_2=2$, and for all pairwise distinct positive integers $m,n,k$, at least one of the following equations holds: \[a_n+a_m=a_{n+m},\qquad a_n+a_k=a_{n+k},\qquad a_m+a_k=a_{m+k}.\]

$a_n=n$ for all $n$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A minimal counterexample and triangles of bad pairs, computer-assisted for small n)

The sequence $a_n=n$ works, since then all three equations hold. We show it is the only one.

Reformulation. Put $b_n=a_n-n$, so $b_1=b_2=0$. Since $n$ is additive, for $i\ne j$ we have $a_i+a_j=a_{i+j}$ iff $b_i+b_j=b_{i+j}$; call the pair $\{i,j\}$ good in that case and bad otherwise. The hypothesis says: among any three distinct positive integers some pair is good. We must show $b\equiv0$.

Suppose not and let $n$ be the least index with $b_n\ne0$; then $n\ge3$. The condition is unchanged if all $b_i$ are multiplied by the same nonzero number, so we may assume $b_n=1$. Write $u_j=b_{n+j}$ ($j\ge0$, so $u_0=1$), $N=\{j\ge0:u_j\ne0\}$ and $O=\{j\ge1:u_j=1\}$.

(F1) For distinct $x,y<n$: $b_x+b_y=0$, so $\{x,y\}$ is bad iff $x+y\ge n$ and $x+y-n\in N$ (if $x+y<n$ then $b_{x+y}=0$).

(F2) For $x<n$, $\{x,n\}$ is good iff $b_{n+x}=1$, i.e. $x\in O$.

Lemma 1. If $1\le x\le n-1$ and $2x\ne n$, then $x\in O$ or $n-x\in O$. Proof: in the triple $\{x,n-x,n\}$ the pair $\{x,n-x\}$ is bad by (F1) ($0\in N$), so $\{x,n\}$ or $\{n-x,n\}$ is good; apply (F2).

Lemma 2. Let $j,k,l\in N$ and suppose $x=\frac{n+j+k-l}2$, $y=\frac{n+j-k+l}2$, $z=\frac{n-j+k+l}2$ are distinct integers in $[1,n-1]$. This is impossible. Proof: $x+y=n+j$, $x+z=n+k$, $y+z=n+l$, so by (F1) all three pairs of $\{x,y,z\}$ are bad. Two special cases:

  • (2a) ($l=0$) There are no distinct $j,k\ge1$ in $N$ with $j+k<n$ and $j+k\equiv n\pmod 2$. (Then $x=\frac{n+j+k}2<n$, $y=\frac{n+j-k}2>j\ge1$, $z>k\ge1$, and $x,y,z$ are distinct because $j\ne k$ and $j,k\ne0$.)

  • (2b) There are no distinct $a,b,c\in[1,n-1]$ with $n-a,n-b,n-c\in O$, $a+b+c$ even, and each of $a,b,c$ less than the sum of the other two. (Take $j=n-a$, $k=n-b$, $l=n-c$: then $x=n-\frac{a+b-c}2$, $y=n-\frac{a-b+c}2$, $z=n-\frac{-a+b+c}2$ lie in $[1,n-1]$ and are distinct.)

Even $n=2m$, $m\ge9$. Two numbers of the same parity in $[1,m-1]$ have an even sum less than $n$, so by (2a) $N$ contains at most one odd and at most one even number of $[1,m-1]$. Every other $x\in[1,m-1]$ has $u_x=0$, so $x\notin O$ and, by Lemma 1, $n-x\in O$. Removing at most one odd and one even number from $\{1,\dots,8\}$ always leaves one of the triples \[(4,6,8),\ (3,4,5),\ (5,6,7),\ (3,5,6),\ (3,7,8),\ (5,7,8),\ (3,6,7)\] (if the removed even number is not $4,6,8$ use $(4,6,8)$; if it is $4$, use $(5,6,7)$, $(3,7,8)$ or $(3,5,6)$ according as the removed odd number is not $5,7$ / is $5$ / is $7$; if it is $6$, use $(3,4,5)$, $(5,7,8)$ or $(3,7,8)$ according as it is not $3,5$ / is $3$ / is $5$; if it is $8$, use $(3,4,5)$, $(5,6,7)$ or $(3,6,7)$ according as it is not $3,5$ / is $3$ / is $5$). Each listed triple has even sum and satisfies the triangle inequalities, and its numbers $a$ satisfy $n-a\in O$. This contradicts (2b).

Odd $n=2m+1$, $m\ge8$. Two numbers of different parity in $[1,m]$ have an odd sum less than $n$, so by (2a) $N\cap[1,m]$ lies in one parity class.

  • If no even number of $[1,m]$ is in $O$, then $4,6,8\notin O$, so $n-4,n-6,n-8\in O$ by Lemma 1, contradicting (2b) for $(4,6,8)$. Hence some even number is in $O\subseteq N$, and $N\cap[1,m]$ consists of even numbers. So $u_x=0$ for every odd $x\le m$; in particular $u_1=u_5=0$, and by Lemma 1, $n-x\in O$ for odd $x\le m$.

  • Let $c$ be even with $4\le c\le m-1$. If $c\notin O$, then $n-c\in O$, and also $n-(c-1),n-(c+1)\in O$; the triple $(c-1,c,c+1)$ has even sum $3c$ and satisfies the triangle inequalities ($c+1<2c-1$), contradicting (2b). So $c\in O$; in particular $u_4=u_6=1$.

Now look at the triple $\{1,n-1,n+5\}$: $\{1,n-1\}$ is bad, and $\{1,n+5\}$ is bad since $b_1+b_{n+5}=u_5=0\ne1=u_6=b_{n+6}$. Hence $\{n-1,n+5\}$ is good: $b_{2n+4}=b_{n-1}+b_{n+5}=0$. In the triple $\{1,n,n+4\}$: $\{1,n\}$ is bad ($b_{n+1}=u_1=0\ne1$) and $\{1,n+4\}$ is bad ($b_{n+5}=0\ne u_4=1$). Hence $\{n,n+4\}$ is good: $b_{2n+4}=b_n+b_{n+4}=2$. This contradiction finishes the case.

Small $n$. The cases $3\le n\le16$ are finite problems: for each such $n$, the conditions coming from all triples $\{i,j,l\}$ with $i+j,\,i+l,\,j+l\le M_n$ (with $b_1=\dots=b_{n-1}=0$, $b_n=1$) have no real solution, where $M_n=12,13,12,13,16,15,20,18,24,21,28,24,32,27$ for $n=3,\dots,16$. This was checked exactly by computer (an SMT solver over the reals); this part of the proof is computer-assisted; for example, for $n=10$ the triples $\{2,8,9\},\{2,8,10\},\{3,7,8\},\{3,7,9\},\{3,7,11\},\{4,6,10\},\{4,6,11\},\{4,6,12\}$ already give a contradiction.

So $b\equiv0$, and the only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=n}$.

Solution 2 (A repeating tail and its largest value)

The sequence $a_n=n$ works, since then all three equations hold. We show it is the only one.

Put $b_n=a_n-n$, so $b_1=b_2=0$. Since $n$ is additive, for distinct $i,j$ we have $a_i+a_j=a_{i+j}$ if and only if $b_i+b_j=b_{i+j}$; call the pair $\{i,j\}$ good in that case and bad otherwise. The hypothesis says: among any three distinct positive integers, some pair is good.

Suppose that not all $b_n$ are $0$, and let $s$ be the least index with $b_s\ne0$; then $s\ge3$. The pair $\{1,s-1\}$ consists of two distinct indices below $s$, so $b_1+b_{s-1}=0\ne b_s$: it is bad.

The tail repeats. Let $k\ge s$. In the triple $\{1,s-1,k\}$ (three distinct numbers) the pair $\{1,s-1\}$ is bad, so $\{1,k\}$ or $\{s-1,k\}$ is good, that is, \[b_{k+1}=b_k\qquad\text{or}\qquad b_{k+s-1}=b_k .\] So for every $k\ge s$ the value $b_k$ appears again at $k+1$ or at $k+s-1$. Repeating, each value $c$ of the tail $\{b_k:k\ge s\}$ appears at infinitely many indices, with gaps at most $s-1$ between consecutive ones; in particular $c$ appears in every set of $s-1$ consecutive indices beyond its first appearance.

The tail takes finitely many values. If the tail took $s$ distinct values, choose a set of $s-1$ consecutive indices beyond the first appearances of all of them; each of the $s$ values would appear there, which is impossible. So the set $V$ of tail values is finite (with at most $s-1$ elements).

Contradiction. $V$ contains $b_s\ne0$, so it has a nonzero element $c$ of largest absolute value. As $c$ appears infinitely often, choose three distinct indices $i,j,l\ge s$ with $b_i=b_j=b_l=c$. For each pair, say $\{i,j\}$, we have $i+j\ge s$, so $b_{i+j}\in V$, while $b_i+b_j=2c$ has $|2c|>|c|$ and so is not in $V$. Thus all three pairs are bad, contradicting the hypothesis.

Hence $b_n=0$ for all $n$, and the only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=n}$.

Solution 1 (Power of the point U)

Directed lengths along a line are written with a bar, e.g. $\overline{UX}$. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $AP_BP_C$, $H$ the orthocentre of $ABC$, $H_A$ and $P_A$ the reflections of $H$ and $P$ in $BC$; $H_A$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$ (a standard fact). We assume the configuration is non-degenerate (the points in the statement exist, $\angle B,\angle C\ne90^\circ$, and $U\ne A,T$).

Step 0 (Steiner line). For $P$ on the circumcircle, $P_A$, $P_B$, $P_C$ and $H$ lie on one line $s$ (the Steiner line of $P$). So $U=s\cap BC$. Reflecting in $BC$ maps $s$ to the line through $P$ and $H_A$ and fixes $U$; hence $U$ lies on line $PH_A$.

The line through $P$ perpendicular to $AC$ (direction $(\gamma,-h)$) is $\gamma(x-p)-h(y-q)=0$, so $X=\bigl(p-\frac{hq}{\gamma},0\bigr)$; similarly $Y=\bigl(p-\frac{hq}{\beta},0\bigr)$. The power of the origin with respect to the circumcircle is $\beta\gamma=h\cdot y_{H_A}$, so $H_A=\bigl(0,\frac{\beta\gamma}h\bigr)$.

Let $\varphi(x,y)=(x,\kappa y)$. This affine map fixes $BC$ pointwise and sends $P\mapsto T$, $H_A\mapsto A$. It maps lines to lines and keeps ratios of directed lengths on a line. Since $U\in BC$ lies on line $PH_A$, $U=\varphi(U)$ lies on line $TA$, and \[\frac{\overline{UT}}{\overline{UA}}=\frac{\overline{UP}}{\overline{UH_A}}=\frac{\overline{UP_A}}{\overline{UH}},\tag{1}\] the last equality by reflecting in $BC$ (which maps line $PH_A$ to $s$, $P\mapsto P_A$, $H_A\mapsto H$).

Step 2 (a lemma on $B$ and $C$). We show $\overline{BU}\cdot\overline{BY}=BP^2$ and $\overline{CU}\cdot\overline{CX}=CP^2$ (directed along $BC$). The points $P,P_A,P_B$ are at distance $BP$ from $B$ (reflections of $P$ in lines through $B$), so $B$ is the circumcentre of triangle $PP_AP_B$, and $BC$ is the perpendicular bisector of $PP_A$; it meets line $P_BP_A$ at $U$ and line $P_BP$ at $Y$. The lines $P_BP$ and $P_BP_A$ meet the $y$-axis at \[y_Y=\frac{kz_1+\sigma z_2}{z_1+\sigma},\qquad y_U=\frac{kz_1-\sigma z_2}{z_1-\sigma},\qquad y_Uy_Y=\frac{k^2z_1^2-\sigma^2z_2^2}{z_1^2-\sigma^2}=\frac{(k^2+\sigma^2)(z_1^2-\sigma^2)}{z_1^2-\sigma^2}=BP^2,\] using $z_2^2=\sigma^2+k^2-z_1^2$. The same argument with $C$ (triangle $PP_AP_C$) gives the second relation. Consequently, for the circle $\kappa_B$ with centre $B$ through $P$ (which also passes through $P_A,P_B$), \[\operatorname{pow}(U,\kappa_B)=UB^2-BP^2=\overline{UB}^{\,2}-\overline{BU}\cdot\overline{BY}=\overline{UB}\,(\overline{UB}+\overline{BY})=\overline{UB}\cdot\overline{UY},\] and similarly $\operatorname{pow}(U,\kappa_C)=\overline{UC}\cdot\overline{UX}$. Since $U$ lies on $s$, which meets $\kappa_B$ at $P_A,P_B$ and $\kappa_C$ at $P_A,P_C$, \[\overline{UP_A}\cdot\overline{UP_B}=\overline{UB}\cdot\overline{UY},\qquad \overline{UP_A}\cdot\overline{UP_C}=\overline{UC}\cdot\overline{UX}.\tag{2}\]

Step 3 (the common point). Let $Z$ be the second intersection of line $UT$ (that is, line $AT$) with $\Gamma$, so $\overline{UZ}\cdot\overline{UT}=\operatorname{pow}(U,\Gamma)=\overline{UX}\cdot\overline{UY}$. The line $UA$ meets $\omega$ at $A$ and a second point $Z'$ with $\overline{UZ'}\cdot\overline{UA}=\operatorname{pow}(U,\omega)=\overline{UP_B}\cdot\overline{UP_C}$ (as $U\in P_BP_C$). So $Z=Z'$ if and only if \[\overline{UX}\cdot\overline{UY}\cdot\overline{UA}=\overline{UP_B}\cdot\overline{UP_C}\cdot\overline{UT} \iff \overline{UX}\cdot\overline{UY}\cdot\overline{UH}=\overline{UP_B}\cdot\overline{UP_C}\cdot\overline{UP_A}\] by (1). Multiply both sides by $\overline{UP_A}\ne0$. On the left, $\overline{UH}\cdot\overline{UP_A}=\overline{UH_A}\cdot\overline{UP}$ (reflection in $BC$), which is the power of $U$ with respect to the circumcircle, i.e. $\overline{UB}\cdot\overline{UC}$. On the right we get $(\overline{UP_A}\cdot\overline{UP_B})(\overline{UP_A}\cdot\overline{UP_C})$, which by (2) is $\overline{UB}\cdot\overline{UY}\cdot\overline{UC}\cdot\overline{UX}$. Both sides equal $\overline{UX}\cdot\overline{UY}\cdot\overline{UB}\cdot\overline{UC}$, so $Z=Z'$.

Hence the point $Z$ lies on $\Gamma$, on the circumcircle of $AP_BP_C$ and on the line $UT$ (which also passes through $A$), as required.

Solution 2 (Cartesian coordinates with the foot of the perpendicular at the origin)

We assume the configuration is non-degenerate: all named points exist, the circles are defined, and $T\ne P$.

Set-up. Take $BC$ as the $x$-axis and $P=(0,1)$ (unit length $=$ distance from $P$ to $BC$), and write $X=(x,0)$, $Y=(y,0)$, $U=(u,0)$. Put \[S=x+y,\quad Q=xy,\quad W=1+u^2,\quad D=(u+x)(u+y),\quad V=u^2-uS+Q,\quad Z=u^2+Q^2 .\]

  • The reflections of a point of the circumcircle in the three sides are collinear (on the Steiner line, the image of the Simson line under the homothety with centre $P$ and ratio $2$). The reflection of $P$ in $BC$ is $(0,-1)$, so $P_B$, $P_C$ lie on the line through $(0,-1)$ and $U$, namely $\xi-u\eta=u$.

A short computation gives $a-uh-u=-\frac{uW(Q+1)}{D}$, which is nonzero in a non-degenerate configuration (if $Q=-1$, then $A$ lies on $P_BP_C$).

The line $UT$. Its points are $(u(1-\tau),Q\tau)$, $\tau\in\mathbb R$, with $\tau=0$ at $U$ and $\tau=1$ at $T$. Along it, $\gamma$ is a quadratic in $\tau$ with leading coefficient $u^2+Q^2=Z$ and value $\gamma(U)=u^2-uS+Q=V$ at $\tau=0$. One root is $\tau=1$ ($T\in\Gamma$), so by Vieta the other is $\tau_0=V/Z$. Let $M$ be the corresponding point: the second intersection of $UT$ with $\Gamma$ (equal to $T$ if $UT$ is tangent to $\Gamma$).

The quadratic terms of $\kappa$ and $\gamma$ agree, so $\kappa-\gamma$ is a linear function, and along $UT$ it equals $\alpha+\beta\tau$ with $\alpha=(\kappa-\gamma)(U)$ and $\alpha+\beta=(\kappa-\gamma)(T)$. As $\gamma(M)=0$, also $\kappa(M)=0$. So $M$ lies on $\Gamma$, on the circle $(AP_BP_C)$ and on the line $UT$: the three share a common point.

Let $G$ be the centroid of triangle $ABC$. Let $K$, $N$, $M$ be the midpoints of the sides $AB$, $AC$, $BC$, respectively. Let $B_M$, $C_M$, $A_N$, $C_N$, $A_K$, $B_K$ be the midpoints of the segments $BM$, $CM$, $AN$, $CN$, $AK$, $BK$, respectively. Prove that $G$ lies on the radical axis of the circumcircles of triangles $B_MC_NA_K$ and $C_MA_NB_K$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Power of the centroid of a triangle)

Lemma. If triangle $XYZ$ has centroid $G$ and circumcircle $\omega$, then $\operatorname{pow}(G,\omega)=-\frac19\,(XY^2+YZ^2+ZX^2)$.

Proof. For any point $Q$, with vectors from $G$ (so $X+Y+Z=0$): $QX^2+QY^2+QZ^2=3QG^2+GX^2+GY^2+GZ^2$, and $XY^2+YZ^2+ZX^2=3(GX^2+GY^2+GZ^2)$ (expand, using $X+Y+Z=0$). Taking $Q$ to be the circumcentre $O$ (radius $R$): $3R^2=3OG^2+\frac13(XY^2+YZ^2+ZX^2)$, so $\operatorname{pow}(G,\omega)=OG^2-R^2=-\frac19(XY^2+YZ^2+ZX^2)$. $\square$

The two triangles. With position vectors, $M=\frac{B+C}2$ gives $B_M=\frac{B+M}2=\frac{3B+C}4$; similarly \[B_M=\tfrac{3B+C}4,\quad C_N=\tfrac{3C+A}4,\quad A_K=\tfrac{3A+B}4;\] \[C_M=\tfrac{3C+B}4,\quad A_N=\tfrac{3A+C}4,\quad B_K=\tfrac{3B+A}4 .\] Each triangle has centroid $\frac{4A+4B+4C}{12}=G$. Each is non-degenerate: its area is $\bigl(\frac{27}{64}+\frac1{64}\bigr)=\frac7{16}$ of $[ABC]$ (determinant of the coefficient matrix). The second triangle is obtained from the first by exchanging the letters $B$ and $C$.

Sum of squared sides. Let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$. With $u=B-A$, $v=C-A$ we have $|u|=c$, $|v|=b$, $u\cdot v=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}2$, and \[B_M-A_K=\tfrac{2u+v}4,\qquad 16\,A_KB_M^2=4c^2+b^2+2(b^2+c^2-a^2)=6c^2+3b^2-2a^2 .\] The other two sides are obtained by the cyclic shift $A\to B\to C\to A$ (which maps $A_K\to B_M\to C_N\to A_K$), so \[16\,(A_KB_M^2+B_MC_N^2+C_NA_K^2)=(6+3-2)(a^2+b^2+c^2)=7(a^2+b^2+c^2).\] This is symmetric in $a,b,c$, so exchanging $B$ and $C$ gives the same value for triangle $C_MA_NB_K$.

Conclusion. By the lemma, $G$ has power $-\frac{7}{144}(a^2+b^2+c^2)$ with respect to both circumcircles, so $G$ lies on their radical axis.

Solution 2 (Adding the three circle equations)

Place the origin at $G$, so that $A+B+C=0$. A circle through points $Z$ is given by $|Z|^2-2\,O\cdot Z+k=0$, where $O$ is its centre and $k$ is the power of the origin $G$.

As computed from the midpoints, $B_M=\frac{3B+C}4$, $C_N=\frac{3C+A}4$, $A_K=\frac{3A+B}4$ and $C_M=\frac{3C+B}4$, $A_N=\frac{3A+C}4$, $B_K=\frac{3B+A}4$; each triple is non-collinear (its triangle has area $\frac7{16}[ABC]$) and has sum $A+B+C=0$.

Write the equation of the circumcircle of $X_1X_2X_3$ at its three points and add: \[\textstyle\sum|X_i|^2-2\,O\cdot\sum X_i+3k=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=-\frac13\sum|X_i|^2 ,\] because $\sum X_i=0$. So the power of $G$ is $-\frac13$ of the sum of the squared distances from $G$ to the three points. Write $\sum|A|^2$ for $|A|^2+|B|^2+|C|^2$. For the first triangle, \[16\textstyle\sum|X_i|^2=|3A+B|^2+|3B+C|^2+|3C+A|^2=10\sum|A|^2+6\,(A\cdot B+B\cdot C+C\cdot A),\] and for the second, \[16\textstyle\sum|X_i|^2=|3A+C|^2+|3B+A|^2+|3C+B|^2=10\sum|A|^2+6\,(A\cdot C+B\cdot A+C\cdot B),\] the same number. Hence $G$ has equal powers with respect to the two circumcircles, i.e. $G$ lies on their radical axis. (In fact the common power is $-\frac7{144}(a^2+b^2+c^2)$.)

Let $T$ be a tree. Denote by $S(T)$ the sum of the distances between every pair of leaves in $T$. Determine the maximum possible value of $S(T)$ over all trees with $2026$ vertices.

$309371400$

Show the solution

Solution (Counting each edge separately)

Let $T$ have $n=2026$ vertices and $L$ leaves; $2\le L\le n-1$. For an edge $e$, removing $e$ splits the leaves into $\ell_e$ and $L-\ell_e$; the path between two leaves uses $e$ exactly when $e$ separates them. Hence \[S(T)=\sum_{e}\ell_e\,(L-\ell_e).\] Each leaf has exactly one edge, and no edge joins two leaves (as $n\ge3$), so there are $L$ leaf edges, each with $\ell_e=1$, contributing $L-1$ each, and $n-1-L$ other edges, each contributing $\ell_e(L-\ell_e)\le\lfloor L^2/4\rfloor$. Therefore \[S(T)\le F(L):=L(L-1)+(n-1-L)\Bigl\lfloor\frac{L^2}4\Bigr\rfloor .\] Equality. Take a path $v_0v_1\cdots v_d$ with $d=n-1-L$, and attach $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$ leaves to $v_0$ and $\lceil L/2\rceil$ leaves to $v_d$ (for $d=0$ this is a star). It has $L+d+1=n$ vertices and exactly $L$ leaves, and every path edge separates $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$ leaves from $\lceil L/2\rceil$, so $S=F(L)$.

Maximising $F$ for $n-1=2025$. For $L=2k$: $F=2k(2k-1)+(2025-2k)k^2=G(k):=-2k^3+2029k^2-2k$, and $G(k+1)-G(k)=-6k^2+4052k+2025$, which is positive for $1\le k\le675$ (it equals $3375$ at $k=675$ and the quadratic decreases for $k\ge338$) and negative for $k\ge676$ (it is $-679$ at $k=676$). So the even case is largest at $k=676$, $L=1352$.

For $L=2k+1$: $F=(2k+1)2k+(2024-2k)(k^2+k)=H(k):=-2k^3+2026k^2+2026k$, and $H(k+1)-H(k)=-6k^2+4046k+4050$, which is $1350>0$ at $k=675$ and $-2710<0$ at $k=676$; as before the odd case is largest at $k=676$, $L=1353$.

Finally, with $676^2=456976$ and $676^3=308915776$, \[G(676)=H(676)=309\,371\,400 .\] So the maximum of $S(T)$ is $\boxed{309371400}$, attained for example by the double broom with $L=1352$ leaves split $676+676$ and a path of $673$ edges between the two centres (or with $L=1353$, split $676+677$, path of $672$ edges).

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis

Let $M$ be a positive integer. A sequence of positive integers $a_1,a_2,\dots$ is called Almost Fibonacci if \[M\ge|a_n-a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}|\] for every $n\ge 3$. Prove that for every unbounded almost Fibonacci sequence there are infinitely many primes dividing an element of the sequence.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Two terms with almost equal exponents)

The condition is read for $n\ge3$ (as edited). Let $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$ and $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$, so $\varphi+\psi=1$, $\varphi\psi=-1$ and $|\psi|=\frac1\varphi<1$. Put $e_n=a_n-a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}$, so $|e_n|\le M$ for $n\ge3$.

Step 1: $a_n=C\varphi^n+c_n$ with $C>0$ and $(c_n)$ bounded. Let $b_n=a_n-\psi a_{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$. Then \[b_n-\varphi b_{n-1}=a_n-(\varphi+\psi)a_{n-1}+\varphi\psi\,a_{n-2}=e_n\qquad(n\ge3),\] so $\frac{b_n}{\varphi^n}=\frac{b_2}{\varphi^2}+\sum_{j=3}^n\frac{e_j}{\varphi^j}$. The series converges absolutely; call its sum $B$. Then \[|b_n-B\varphi^n|=\varphi^n\Bigl|\sum_{j>n}\frac{e_j}{\varphi^j}\Bigr|\le M\sum_{i\ge1}\varphi^{-i}=\frac M{\varphi-1}=M\varphi .\] Let $C=\frac{B\varphi}{\varphi-\psi}$ and $c_n=a_n-C\varphi^n$. Since $C\varphi^n-\psi C\varphi^{n-1}=C\varphi^{n-1}(\varphi-\psi)=B\varphi^n$, we get $c_n-\psi c_{n-1}=b_n-B\varphi^n$, so $|c_n|\le\frac1\varphi|c_{n-1}|+M\varphi$. By induction $|c_n|\le K_0=\max(|c_2|,M\varphi^3)$ for all $n\ge2$ (note $M\varphi+\frac{K_0}\varphi\le K_0$ because $1-\frac1\varphi=\frac1{\varphi^2}$). If $C<0$ the terms would become negative, and if $C=0$ they would be bounded; so $C>0$. Hence there is $n_0$ with $\frac C2\varphi^n\le a_n\le2C\varphi^n$ for $n\ge n_0$.

Step 2: two irrationality facts. By induction from $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$ (and $\psi^2=\psi+1$), $\varphi^d=F_d\varphi+F_{d-1}$ and $\psi^d=F_d\psi+F_{d-1}$ for $d\ge1$, where $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, $F_{i+1}=F_i+F_{i-1}$ are the Fibonacci numbers. As $F_d\ge1$, both $\varphi^d$ and $\psi^d$ are irrational, and $\varphi^d+\psi^d=F_d+2F_{d-1}$ is an integer.

Consequently, for positive integers $u,v$ and $d\ge1$, the number \[(u-v\varphi^d)(u-v\psi^d)=u^2-uv(\varphi^d+\psi^d)+v^2(-1)^d\] is a nonzero integer, so its absolute value is at least $1$. Since $|u-v\psi^d|\le u+v$, \[\Bigl|\frac uv-\varphi^d\Bigr|\ \ge\ \frac1{v(u+v)} .\tag{1}\]

Step 3: finitely many primes is impossible. Suppose every term is a product of the primes $p_1,\dots,p_s$; let $\Lambda=\log p_1+\dots+\log p_s$ and write $a_n=\prod_lp_l^{\,v_l(n)}$. For $n\ge n_0$, $0\le v_l(n)\le\log_2a_n\le K_1n$ for a constant $K_1$. Fix $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ so small that $2\varepsilon\Lambda<(1-\varepsilon)\log\varphi$.

Let $N\ge n_0$ and consider the $N+1$ vectors $\bigl(i-N,\,v_1(i),\dots,v_s(i)\bigr)$ for $N\le i\le2N$. They lie in the box $[0,N]\times[0,2K_1N]^s$. Cut $[0,N]$ into $\lceil1/\varepsilon\rceil$ intervals and $[0,2K_1N]$ into $\lceil2K_1/\varepsilon\rceil$ intervals, all of length at most $\varepsilon N$; this cuts the box into a number $q$ of cells that does not depend on $N$. For $N\ge q$, two of the vectors, for indices $i<j$, lie in the same cell. Then \[1\le d=j-i\le\varepsilon N,\qquad |v_l(j)-v_l(i)|\le\varepsilon N\ \text{ for all }l.\] Write $\frac{a_j}{a_i}=\frac uv$ with $u=\prod_lp_l^{\max(v_l(j)-v_l(i),0)}$ and $v=\prod_lp_l^{\max(v_l(i)-v_l(j),0)}$; then $u,v\le e^{\varepsilon N\Lambda}$, and by (1) \[\Bigl|\frac{a_j}{a_i}-\varphi^d\Bigr|\ \ge\ \frac1{2e^{2\varepsilon N\Lambda}} .\] On the other hand, by Step 1, $a_j-\varphi^da_i=c_j-\varphi^dc_i$, which has absolute value at most $2K_0\varphi^d$, while $a_i\ge\frac C2\varphi^i\ge\frac C2\varphi^N$. Hence \[\Bigl|\frac{a_j}{a_i}-\varphi^d\Bigr|\le\frac{4K_0}C\,\varphi^{d-N}\le\frac{4K_0}C\,e^{-(1-\varepsilon)N\log\varphi} .\] Comparing, $e^{(1-\varepsilon)N\log\varphi-2\varepsilon N\Lambda}\le\frac{8K_0}C$ for every $N\ge\max(n_0,q)$. By the choice of $\varepsilon$ the left side tends to infinity, a contradiction. Hence infinitely many primes divide terms of the sequence.

Solution 2 (A lower bound for linear forms in logarithms)

This solution quotes a deep theorem. Baker's theorem (in its standard quantitative form): for fixed positive real algebraic numbers $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ there are constants $c,\kappa>0$ such that for all integers $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m$ with $\Lambda=\sum_l\beta_l\log\alpha_l\ne0$, \[|\Lambda|\ge c\,H^{-\kappa},\qquad H=\max(2,|\beta_1|,\dots,|\beta_m|).\]

The condition is read for $n\ge3$. Let $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$, $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$ and $e_n=a_n-a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}$, $|e_n|\le M$.

Growth. Let $b_n=a_n-\psi a_{n-1}$ ($n\ge2$). As $\varphi+\psi=1$ and $\varphi\psi=-1$, $b_n-\varphi b_{n-1}=e_n$ for $n\ge3$, so $b_n\varphi^{-n}=b_2\varphi^{-2}+\sum_{j=3}^ne_j\varphi^{-j}$ converges to some $B$, with $|b_n-B\varphi^n|\le M\sum_{i\ge1}\varphi^{-i}=M\varphi$. Put $C=\frac{B\varphi}{\varphi-\psi}$ and $c_n=a_n-C\varphi^n$; then $c_n-\psi c_{n-1}=b_n-B\varphi^n$, so $|c_n|\le\frac1\varphi|c_{n-1}|+M\varphi$, and by induction $|c_n|\le K_0=\max(|c_2|,M\varphi^3)$. Positivity and unboundedness force $C>0$, so $a_n\ge\frac C2\varphi^n$ and $a_n\le2C\varphi^n$ for $n\ge n_0$.

The linear form. Suppose all terms are products of the primes $p_1,\dots,p_s$, $a_n=\prod_lp_l^{\,v_l(n)}$. For $n\ge n_0$, $0\le v_l(n)\le K_1n$ for a constant $K_1$. Let $\beta_l(n)=v_l(n+1)-v_l(n)$ and \[\Lambda_n=\sum_l\beta_l(n)\log p_l-\log\varphi=\log\frac{a_{n+1}}{\varphi a_n}.\] It is nonzero, since $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}$ is rational and $\varphi$ is irrational. Now $a_{n+1}-\varphi a_n=c_{n+1}-\varphi c_n$, so $\bigl|\frac{a_{n+1}}{\varphi a_n}-1\bigr|\le\frac{K_0(1+\varphi)}{\varphi\cdot\frac C2\varphi^n}=K_2\varphi^{-n}$. For large $n$ this is at most $\frac12$, and $|\log(1+x)|\le2|x|$ for $|x|\le\frac12$, so $|\Lambda_n|\le2K_2\varphi^{-n}$.

Apply Baker's theorem to $\alpha=(p_1,\dots,p_s,\varphi)$ and $\beta=(\beta_1(n),\dots,\beta_s(n),-1)$: here $H\le K_1(n+1)+2$, so $|\Lambda_n|\ge c\,(K_1(n+1)+2)^{-\kappa}$. For large $n$ this contradicts $|\Lambda_n|\le2K_2\varphi^{-n}$, because $\varphi^{-n}$ decreases faster than any power of $n$. Hence infinitely many primes divide terms of the sequence.
