Problem 1

SCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics · Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

Uncle Qiang's house consists of a $2026\times2026$ grid of $1\times1$ rooms. Every room has four walls, exactly one of which contains an outgoing one-way door. If the wall is between two adjacent rooms, then the door leads from one room to the other. If the wall is on the perimeter of the house then the door leads outside. In every room, each of the four walls has at most one door, which leads either into or out of the room. There are no incoming doors from outside the house.

Uncle Qiang wants to redesign the house by performing moves: in a move, he chooses a room whose four walls are not all occupied by doors. He then removes that room's outgoing one-way door and replaces it with a new outgoing one-way door through one of its walls not occupied by a door.

Uncle Qiang would like to perform a series of up to $k$ moves so that it is possible to exit the house from any room by following only outgoing one-way doors. Find the smallest number $k$ such that this is always possible.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Trace the unique route from a room. If the route never leaves the house, what must eventually repeat?
Hint 2: key idea
For a lower bound, look for a legal house containing many disjoint cycles, each needing its own move, and ask how small a cycle can be. For an upper bound, count the doors by direction and try eliminating the least frequent direction. (LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics)
Hint 3: approach
Turn each door of the least frequent direction to its opposite, processing rows or columns from the side toward which the new doors point so the relevant wall is free at each move. Check which directions a cycle could still use, and inspect an endmost room of such a cycle. Compare the number of moves with what your many-cycles example forces.
Common trap
A wall can be blocked by an incoming door from the next room, so the order of the moves matters.
Show the answer

$k=1013^2=1\,026\,169$

Show the solution – A blocking house and turning the rarest direction

Following doors from a room either leads outside or ends in a cycle of rooms (each room has exactly one outgoing door).

At least $1013^2$ moves may be needed. Split the house into $1013^2$ blocks of $2\times2$ rooms, and in each block let the four doors go around the block, each room pointing to the next room of its block. Each interior wall of a block carries one door and no other wall carries a door, so this is a legal house, with $1013^2$ disjoint cycles. A move changes the door of one room only, so a cycle none of whose rooms has been moved is still a cycle. Hence each of the $1013^2$ cycles needs a move in one of its own rooms, and at least $1013^2$ moves are needed.

$1013^2$ moves always suffice. The $2026^2$ outgoing doors point in four directions, so some direction, say south (after rotating the house), is used by at most $2026^2/4=1013^2$ rooms. Go through the rows from north to south, and in each row turn every south-pointing door into a north-pointing door.

Each such move is legal: the room's north wall is free. In the first row it is a perimeter wall, there are no doors from outside, and the room's own door points south. In a later row the only other door that could be in that wall is a south door of the room just above, which has already been turned. Moves in the same row do not touch this wall. So we make exactly one move per south-pointing door, at most $1013^2$ moves.

Afterwards no door points south. Suppose some room cannot exit; then its path ends in a cycle. A cycle never goes north (it could not come back), so it stays in one row and uses only east and west doors. Its westernmost room is entered from and leaves to its eastern neighbour, so the wall between them carries two doors, which is impossible. Hence every room can exit, and the smallest $k$ is $1013^2$.

Problem 2

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure · Monotonicity, Boundedness, and Regularity

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ and polynomials $P$ with non-negative coefficients such that \[f(x+P(xy))=f(x)+xf(y)\] for all positive real numbers $x$ and $y$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Fix $x$ and vary $y$. What does positivity tell you about the two values $f(x+P(xy))$ and $f(x)$, and what would happen if $P$ were constant?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the range of the nonconstant polynomial to compare $f$ at arguments separated by more than $P(0)$. For two fixed values of $y$, take $x$ large so that the corresponding inputs on the left are separated by more than that amount, then compare the two equations. (LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Let $x$ tend to zero with $y=1$ and use monotonicity to test whether $P(0)$ can be positive. Then send $y$ to zero with $x$ fixed and use the bounded total size of the right-hand jumps on a compact interval to constrain the limit of $f$ at zero. Finally set $y=t/x$, extract the limit of $f(Y)/Y$ at infinity, and compare it with the limit along $Y=P(t)$.
Common trap
Monotonicity supplies one-sided limits, and right continuity needs to be established before evaluating the limit at $P(t)$.
Show the answer

$P(t)=t$ and $f(x)=cx$ for any constant $c>0$

Show solution 1 – Monotonicity, then a limit

Answer. $P(t)=t$ and $f(x)=cx$ with $c>0$. These work: $f(x+xy)=cx+cxy=f(x)+xf(y)$.

Conversely, let $(f,P)$ be a solution and write $P(t)=p_0+p_1t+\dots+p_nt^n$ with all $p_i\ge0$ and $p_n>0$. Call the equation $E(x,y)$.

Step 1: $P$ is not constant. If $P\equiv p_0$, then $E(x,y)$ reads $f(x+p_0)=f(x)+xf(y)$, so $f(y)$ does not depend on $y$: $f\equiv K>0$. Then $K=K+xK$, which is false. So $n\ge1$. Hence $P$ is continuous and strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$, $P(t)\to\infty$ as $t\to\infty$, and $P$ maps $(0,\infty)$ onto $(p_0,\infty)$.

Step 2: if $a>0$ and $b>a+p_0$, then $f(b)>f(a)$. By Step 1 there is $s>0$ with $P(s)=b-a$. Then $E(a,s/a)$ gives $f(b)=f(a+P(s))=f(a)+af(s/a)>f(a)$.

Step 3: $f$ is strictly increasing. Let $y>y'>0$. For $x>0$, \[P(xy)-P(xy')=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_ix^i\bigl(y^i-y'^i\bigr)\ge p_nx^n\bigl(y^n-y'^n\bigr),\] which tends to $\infty$ as $x\to\infty$. Choose $x$ with $P(xy)-P(xy')>p_0$. By Step 2, $f(x+P(xy))>f(x+P(xy'))$, and subtracting $E(x,y')$ from $E(x,y)$ gives $x\bigl(f(y)-f(y')\bigr)>0$.

Step 4: $p_0=0$. Since $f$ is increasing and positive, $m=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)$ exists and $m\ge0$; also $m<f(z)$ for every $z>0$. Suppose $p_0>0$. In $E(x,1)$ let $x\to0^+$. The right side $f(x)+xf(1)$ tends to $m$. On the left, $x+P(x)>p_0$, so $f(x+P(x))\ge f(p_0)>f(p_0/2)>m$ for every $x$. This contradicts the limit. Hence $p_0=0$, and now $P$ maps $(0,\infty)$ onto $(0,\infty)$.

Step 5: $m=0$ and $f$ is right-continuous. Fix $x>0$ and let $y\to0^+$ in $E(x,y)$. Then $x+P(xy)$ decreases to $x$, so the left side tends to the right-hand limit $f(x^+)$, while the right side tends to $f(x)+xm$. Thus $f(x^+)-f(x)=xm$ for every $x$. If $m>0$, pick $N$ numbers $1<x_1<\dots<x_N<2$; since $f$ is increasing, \[f(2)-f(1)\ge\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(f(x_k^+)-f(x_k)\bigr)\ge Nm\] for every $N$, which is impossible. So $m=0$ and $f(x^+)=f(x)$ for all $x$.

Step 6: $f(P(t))=Lt$. Fix $t>0$ and apply $E(x,t/x)$: \[f\bigl(x+P(t)\bigr)=f(x)+x\,f(t/x).\] Let $x\to0^+$. The left side tends to $f(P(t)^+)=f(P(t))$ and $f(x)\to m=0$, so $x\,f(t/x)\to f(P(t))$. With $Y=t/x\to\infty$ this says \[\lim_{Y\to\infty}\frac{f(Y)}{Y}=\frac{f(P(t))}{t}.\] The left side does not depend on $t$; call it $L$. Then $L>0$ and $f(P(t))=Lt$ for every $t>0$.

Step 7: conclusion. Put $Y=P(t)$ in $f(Y)/Y\to L$ and let $t\to\infty$: $Lt/P(t)\to L$, so $P(t)/t=p_1+p_2t+\dots+p_nt^{n-1}\to1$. This forces $n=1$ and $p_1=1$, i.e. $P(t)=t$. Since $P$ is now the identity, Step 6 reads $f(t)=Lt$. So $f(x)=cx$ with $c=L>0$ and $P(t)=t$, as claimed.

Show solution 2 – Linear growth and a periodic remainder

Answer. $P(t)=t$ and $f(x)=cx$ with $c>0$. These work: $f(x+xy)=cx+cxy=f(x)+xf(y)$.

Conversely, let $(f,P)$ be a solution, let $b=P(0)\ge0$ be the constant coefficient of $P$, and call the equation $E(x,y)$.

Step 1: $P$ is not constant. If $P\equiv b$, then $E(x,y)$ reads $f(x+b)=f(x)+xf(y)$, so $f(y)$ does not depend on $y$: $f\equiv K>0$, and then $K=K+xK$, which is false. So $P$ has a positive coefficient at some power $t^i$ with $i\ge1$; as all coefficients are non-negative, $P$ is continuous and strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$ with $P(t)\to\infty$, so $P$ takes every value in $(b,\infty)$ at some $t>0$.

Step 2: $f(v)>f(u)$ whenever $v>u+b$. Take $s>0$ with $P(s)=v-u$; then $E(u,s/u)$ gives $f(v)=f(u)+uf(s/u)>f(u)$. In particular, for every $M>0$, \[f(u)<f(M+b+1)\quad\text{for all }u\in(0,M], \tag{1}\] so $f$ is bounded above on every interval $(0,M]$.

Step 3: an upper linear bound. For $0<x<1$, $E(x,1/x)$ and (1) with $M=P(1)+1$ give \[x\,f(1/x)=f\bigl(x+P(1)\bigr)-f(x)<f\bigl(x+P(1)\bigr)<C,\qquad C=f\bigl(P(1)+b+2\bigr).\] Writing $s=1/x$: $f(s)<Cs$ for all $s>1$.

Step 4: $f$ is unbounded. $E(1,t)$ reads \[f\bigl(1+P(t)\bigr)=f(t)+f(1)\qquad(t>0). \tag{2}\] Let $t_0=1$ and $t_{k+1}=1+P(t_k)$. By (2), $f(t_k)=(k+1)f(1)$, which tends to $\infty$.

Step 5: a lower linear bound. Put $L=b+2$. For $0<x<1$ we have $L>x+b$, so $f(x)<f(L)$ by Step 2. By Step 4 choose $K>0$ with $f(K)>f(L)$, and by Step 1 choose $T>0$ with $P(T)>K+b$. For $0<x<1$ we have $x+P(T)>K+b$, so $f(x+P(T))>f(K)$ by Step 2, and $E(x,T/x)$ gives \[x\,f(T/x)=f\bigl(x+P(T)\bigr)-f(x)>f(K)-f(L)=\delta>0.\] Writing $s=T/x$: $f(s)>\lambda s$ for all $s>T$, where $\lambda=\delta/T>0$.

Step 6: $P$ has degree $1$. For $t>1$ with $1+P(t)>T$ (true for all large $t$), Steps 5 and 3 and (2) give \[\lambda\bigl(1+P(t)\bigr)<f\bigl(1+P(t)\bigr)=f(t)+f(1)<Ct+f(1).\] So $P(t)$ is at most a constant times $t$ for large $t$, and $P$ has degree at most $1$; by Step 1 it has degree exactly $1$. Write $P(t)=at+b$ with $a>0$. The sequence of Step 4 is now $t_0=1$, $t_{k+1}=at_k+b+1$, still with $f(t_k)=(k+1)f(1)$.

Step 7: $a=1$. If $a<1$, let $M=\max\bigl(1,\frac{b+1}{1-a}\bigr)$. Then $t_0\le M$, and $t_k\le M$ implies $t_{k+1}\le aM+b+1\le M$; so all $t_k$ lie in $(0,M]$, and by (1) the values $f(t_k)$ are bounded, contradicting $f(t_k)=(k+1)f(1)$. If $a>1$, then $t_{k+1}>at_k$, so $t_k\ge a^k$; for every $k$ with $a^k>T$, Step 5 gives $(k+1)f(1)=f(t_k)>\lambda a^k$, which fails for large $k$ because $a^k/(k+1)\to\infty$. Hence $a=1$ and $P(t)=t+b$.

Step 8: the periodic remainder. Let $p=b+1$, $c=f(1)/p>0$ and $g(x)=f(x)-cx$ for $x>0$. Now (2) reads $f(t+p)=f(t)+f(1)=f(t)+cp$, that is, \[g(t+p)=g(t)\qquad(t>0).\] On $(0,p]$ we have $g(x)>-cx\ge-cp$ (as $f>0$) and $g(x)<f(x)<f(p+b+1)$ by (1). Every $x>0$ is $x'+mp$ with $x'\in(0,p]$ and $m\ge0$ an integer, so $g$ is bounded on $(0,\infty)$: $|g(x)|\le G$ for some constant $G$.

Step 9: conclusion. Since $P(xy)=xy+b$, substituting $f(z)=g(z)+cz$ into $E(x,y)$ gives \[g(x+xy+b)+c(x+xy+b)=g(x)+cx+x\,g(y)+cxy,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad x\,g(y)=g(x+xy+b)-g(x)+cb.\] The right side has absolute value at most $2G+cb$ for all $x,y>0$. Fix $y$ and let $x\to\infty$: then $|g(y)|\le(2G+cb)/x\to0$, so $g(y)=0$. Thus $f(y)=cy$ for all $y>0$, and the last display becomes $0=cb$; as $c>0$, $b=0$. Therefore $P(t)=t$ and $f(x)=cx$ with $c>0$, as claimed.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 54: Advanced Number Theory Synthesis · Deep Structural Classification Problems

For a positive integer $k$, define $d(k)$ to be the number of positive divisors of $k$. Find all sequences $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ of positive integers such that the following two conditions hold:

  • $a_{mn}=a_ma_n$ for all positive integers $m$ and $n$;

  • $d(a_n+1)=d(n+1)$ for all positive integers $n$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare the divisors of a square with those of a nonsquare. Which part of the divisor-count condition could you retain if you recorded only whether each count was odd or even?
Hint 2: key idea
Use strong induction and reduce to a prime input $p$. For an odd prime, look for two multiples of $p$ that become squares after adding $1$, with the second multiple obtained from the first by multiplying by a square built from factors smaller than $p$. (LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Treat $p=2$ using the divisor counts at $2$ and $8$ and the factorization of a sum of cubes. For odd $p$, test $p(p-2)$ and its multiple by $4(p-1)^2$, then factor the difference of squares formed by the resulting square roots after scaling the smaller one. If $a_p<p$, treat the smallest values directly and compare $d(pk+1)$ with $d(a_pk+1)$ for $k$ built from primes below $p$ to set up the same bound in reverse.
Common trap
The odd-prime construction uses $p-2>0$, so the prime $2$ needs a separate argument.
Show the answer

only $a_n=n$ for all $n$

Show the solution – Squares and a Pell-type bound

The sequence $a_n=n$ works. Conversely let $(a_n)$ satisfy the conditions. From $a_1=a_1^2$ we get $a_1=1$. Recall that $d(m)$ is odd exactly when $m$ is a perfect square, so \[a_n+1\text{ is a square}\iff n+1\text{ is a square}.\tag{1}\] We prove $a_n=n$ by strong induction on $n$. If $n>1$ is composite, $n=uv$ with $u,v<n$ and $a_n=a_ua_v=uv$. It remains to treat primes.

$p=2$. $d(a_2+1)=d(3)=2$, so $a_2+1$ is a prime $q$. Also $d(a_8+1)=d(9)=3$, so $a_2^3+1=a_8+1$ is the square of a prime $r$. Now $a_2^3+1=(a_2+1)(a_2^2-a_2+1)$ and $q=a_2+1$ divides $r^2$, so $q=r$ and $a_2^2-a_2+1=q=a_2+1$, giving $a_2=2$.

A lemma. Let $K\ge2$ and let $u,w$ be positive integers with $w^2=K^2u^2-(K^2-1)$. Then $u\le K/2$. Indeed $(Ku-w)(Ku+w)=K^2-1$ with both factors positive integers $D=Ku+w$, $E=Ku-w$; so $2Ku=D+E\le1+(K^2-1)=K^2$ (a sum of two positive integers with a fixed product is largest when one of them is $1$).

An odd prime $p$. Assume $a_m=m$ for all $m<p$, hence for every $m$ whose prime factors are less than $p$. Let $x=a_p$ and $K=2(p-1)$.

Upper bound. $n_1=p(p-2)$ has $n_1+1=(p-1)^2$, and $n_2=4p(p-1)^2(p-2)$ has $n_2+1=(2(p-1)^2-1)^2$ (as $(2m^2-1)^2=4m^2(m^2-1)+1$ with $m=p-1$, $m^2-1=p(p-2)$). By multiplicativity $a_{n_1}=x(p-2)$ and $a_{n_2}=4(p-1)^2(p-2)x$, so by (1) both \[x(p-2)+1=u^2,\qquad K^2(p-2)x+1=w^2\] are squares; then $w^2=K^2u^2-(K^2-1)$ and the lemma gives $u\le p-1$, i.e. $x(p-2)+1\le(p-1)^2=p(p-2)+1$, so $x\le p$.

Lower bound. Suppose $x=v<p$. If $v\in\{1,2\}$, then $d(a_p+1)\in\{d(2),d(3)\}=\{2\}$ while $p+1\ge4$ is even and composite, so $d(p+1)\ge3$: impossible. So $3\le v<p$ and $a_v=v$. For every $k$ whose prime factors are below $p$, the numbers $a_{vk}=vk$ and $a_{pk}=vk$ coincide, so $d(pk+1)=d(vk+1)$ and by the parity of $d$: $pk+1$ is a square iff $vk+1$ is. Take $k=v-2$ and $k=4(v-1)^2(v-2)$: as above $vk+1$ is a square for both, hence so is $pk+1$: \[p(v-2)+1=u^2,\qquad 4(v-1)^2(v-2)p+1=w^2 .\] With $K'=2(v-1)$ we get $w^2=K'^2u^2-(K'^2-1)$, so $u\le v-1$ by the lemma, i.e. $p(v-2)+1\le(v-1)^2=v(v-2)+1$ and $p\le v$, a contradiction.

Hence $a_p=p$, completing the induction. The only sequence is $\boxed{a_n=n}$.

Problem 4

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion · Trigonometric Ceva and Menelaus

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with incenter $I$. Let line $AI$ intersect side $BC$ at $D$. The circumcircle of $ABD$ and the circumcircle of $ACI$ intersect again at point $E$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AE$. Let lines $IM$ and $DE$ intersect at point $P$.

Prove that $\angle BAC+\angle BPD=180^\circ$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $I,D,E,M$ and mark the equal angles given by the two circles. Keep track of which segment $P$ lies on the line of and which two sides of triangle $ADE$ meet $IM$.
Hint 2: key idea
Write the half-angles at $A,B,C$ as $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ and use their sum to relate their tangents. Introduce an angle $\varphi$ whose tangent is the product of those tangents, and test with trigonometric Ceva the rays making angles $\varphi$ at $B$ and $\gamma-\varphi$ at $C$ with $BC$. (LPS VII, Chapter 51: Spiral Similarity and Advanced Transformational Geometry)
Hint 3: approach
Use the Ceva relation to find the split at $A$ and match the resulting angles with both cyclicity conditions for $E$. Apply Menelaus in $ADE$ and the angle-bisector theorem in $ABD$ to get $PD/PE$ and locate $P$ on line $DE$. Express the same ratio by the sine rule in $BDP$ and $BEP$, then study uniqueness in the permitted range of $\angle BPD$ before testing the proposed angle.
Common trap
Use the order of $E,D,P$ before converting an interior angle of $BDE$ into an angle involving $P$.
Show the solution – Pin down $E$ by trigonometric Ceva

Write $\angle A=2\alpha$, $\angle B=2\beta$, $\angle C=2\gamma$, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=90^\circ$, and let $a,b,g$ denote $\tan\alpha,\tan\beta,\tan\gamma$. From $\tan(\alpha+\beta)=\cot\gamma$ we get \[ab+bg+ga=1.\tag{1}\] Let $\varphi$ be the acute angle with $\tan\varphi=abg$. Since $bg<1$ and $ab<1$ by (1), $\varphi<\alpha$ and $\varphi<\gamma$.

Step 1: a point $E'$. Let $E'$ be the point on the same side of $BC$ as $A$ with $\angle E'BC=\varphi$ and $\angle E'CB=\gamma-\varphi$. As $\varphi<2\beta$ and $\gamma-\varphi<2\gamma$, $E'$ lies inside the triangle, and $\angle E'BA=2\beta-\varphi$, $\angle E'CA=\gamma+\varphi$. Put $x=\angle BAE'$, $y=\angle E'AC$, so $x+y=2\alpha$. Trigonometric Ceva gives \[\sin x\,\sin\varphi\,\sin(\gamma+\varphi)=\sin y\,\sin(2\beta-\varphi)\,\sin(\gamma-\varphi).\] Since $x\mapsto\sin x/\sin(2\alpha-x)$ is strictly increasing on $(0,2\alpha)$, at most one $x$ fits, and we claim $x=\alpha+\varphi$, i.e. \[\sin(\alpha+\varphi)\sin\varphi\sin(\gamma+\varphi)=\sin(\alpha-\varphi)\sin(2\beta-\varphi)\sin(\gamma-\varphi).\] Divide by $\cos\alpha\cos\gamma\cos^3\varphi$ and put $t=\tan\varphi=abg$; using $\sin2\beta=\frac{2b}{1+b^2}$, $\cos2\beta=\frac{1-b^2}{1+b^2}$, the claim becomes \[(a+t)\,t\,(g+t)=(a-t)(g-t)\,\frac{2b-t(1-b^2)}{1+b^2}.\] By (1), $a+t=a(1+bg)$, $g+t=g(1+ab)$, $a-t=a(1-bg)=a^2(b+g)$, $g-t=g^2(a+b)$ and $(a+b)(b+g)=1+b^2$. So the left side is $a^2g^2b(1+ab)(1+bg)$ and the right side is $a^2g^2b(2-ag+agb^2)$; they agree because $(1+ab)(1+bg)=1+ab+bg+ab^2g=2-ag+ab^2g$ by (1). Hence \[\angle BAE'=\alpha+\varphi,\qquad \angle E'AC=\alpha-\varphi .\]

Step 2: $E'=E$. Since $\angle BAE'>\alpha=\angle BAD$ and $\angle E'CB<\gamma=\angle ICB$, the point $E'$ lies inside triangle $IDC$.

  • $\angle E'AD=\varphi=\angle E'BD$. The line $DE'$ enters triangle $ADC$ at $D$ and leaves through $AC$, so it separates $A$ from $C$; it also separates $B$ from $C$ (as $D$ is between them). So $A$ and $B$ are on the same side of $DE'$, and $A,B,D,E'$ are concyclic.

  • $\angle E'AI=\varphi$ and $\angle E'CI=\gamma-(\gamma-\varphi)=\varphi$. The line $IE'$ enters triangle $IDC$ at $I$ and leaves through $DC$, so it separates $D$ from $C$; it also separates $A$ from $D$ (as $I$ is between them). So $A,C$ are on the same side of $IE'$, and $A,C,I,E'$ are concyclic.

The two circles are different (otherwise $B,D,C$ would be three collinear points on a circle), so they meet only at $A$ and $E$; as $E'\neq A$, $E'=E$. In particular $\angle EBD=\varphi$ and, in circle $ABDE$, $\angle BED=\angle BAD=\alpha$.

Step 3: where $P$ is. Menelaus in triangle $ADE$ with the line through $I\in AD$ and $M\in AE$: \[\frac{AI}{ID}\cdot\frac{DP}{PE}\cdot\frac{EM}{MA}=1,\quad\text{so}\quad\frac{PD}{PE}=\frac{ID}{IA}=\frac{BD}{BA}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+2\beta)},\] using the bisector $BI$ of triangle $ABD$ and the law of sines there ($\angle ADB=180^\circ-\alpha-2\beta$). The line meets sides $AD,AE$ inside, so $P$ is outside segment $DE$; as $\sin\alpha<\sin(\alpha+2\beta)$ we have $PD<PE$, so the order on the line is $E,D,P$.

Step 4: the angle. In triangle $BDE$, $\angle DBE=\varphi$ and $\angle BED=\alpha$, so $\angle BDP=\alpha+\varphi$. Let $\omega=\angle BPD=\angle BPE$. The law of sines in triangles $BDP$ and $BEP$ gives \[PD=PB\,\frac{\sin(\omega+\alpha+\varphi)}{\sin(\alpha+\varphi)},\qquad PE=PB\,\frac{\sin(\omega+\alpha)}{\sin\alpha},\] and with Step 3, \[\frac{\sin(\omega+\alpha+\varphi)}{\sin(\omega+\alpha)}=\frac{\sin(\alpha+\varphi)}{\sin(\alpha+2\beta)}.\tag{2}\] Here $0<\omega<180^\circ-\alpha-\varphi$, and $\frac{\sin(u+\varphi)}{\sin u}=\cos\varphi+\sin\varphi\cot u$ is strictly decreasing in $u=\omega+\alpha$, so (2) has at most one solution. We show $\omega=180^\circ-2\alpha$ is one (it lies in the range since $\varphi<\alpha$). For this $\omega$, (2) becomes \[\sin(\alpha-\varphi)\sin(\alpha+2\beta)=\sin\alpha\,\sin(\alpha+\varphi).\] Dividing by $\cos\varphi$ and solving for $\tan\varphi$, this is equivalent to \[\tan\varphi=\tan\alpha\cdot\frac{\sin(\alpha+2\beta)-\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+2\beta)+\sin\alpha}=\tan\alpha\cdot\frac{2\cos(\alpha+\beta)\sin\beta}{2\sin(\alpha+\beta)\cos\beta}=\tan\alpha\tan\beta\tan\gamma,\] which is the definition of $\varphi$. Therefore $\angle BPD=180^\circ-2\alpha$, that is, $\angle BAC+\angle BPD=180^\circ$.

Problem 5

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Orders and Primitive Behavior

Find all composite numbers $n$ with the property that if $d<n$ is a positive integer such that $\gcd(d,n)>1$, then \[n\mid d^n+(n-d)^{\gcd(d,n)}.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
For a small composite $n$, list the possible values of $\gcd(d,n)$ as $d$ varies. For each prime divisor of $n$, separate the choices of $d$ divisible by that prime from the choices coprime to it.
Hint 2: key idea
Check the divisibility one prime power at a time. For $n=p^k$, compare the two $p$-adic valuations, while for odd $n$ and a prime power coprime to $d$, write the divisibility as a condition on the order of $d$. (LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure)
Hint 3: approach
For a prime power $n$, test the smallest possible positive valuation of $d$, and for even $n$ with odd part $m>1$, try $d=m+1$. For odd $n$ with a repeated prime and another prime factor, make $d$ a multiple of the complementary factor and a primitive root modulo the full repeated prime power. In the odd squarefree case, compare two different choices of $\gcd(d,n)$ while making $d$ a primitive root at the largest prime, and examine the two-prime case separately using Fermat's theorem.
Common trap
The sign in replacing $(n-d)^{\gcd(d,n)}$ by a power of $d$ depends on the parity of the exponent.
Show the answer

$n=p^k$ with $p$ prime and $2\le k\le p$ (this includes $n=4$), and $n=pq$ with $p<q$ odd primes

Show the solution – Prime by prime

Throughout, $d<n$ and $g=\gcd(d,n)>1$. We check the divisibility one prime power $r^e\,\|\,n$ at a time.

Prime powers $n=p^k$ ($k\ge2$). Here $g>1$ means $p\mid d$; write $v=v_p(d)\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}$, so $g=p^v$ and $v_p(n-d)=v$. Then $v_p(d^n)=nv\ge k$ and $v_p\bigl((n-d)^g\bigr)=vp^v$. If $vp^v\ge k$ the sum is divisible by $p^k$; if $vp^v<k$ the sum has valuation exactly $vp^v<k$. All $v$ occur ($d=p^v$), and $vp^v$ increases with $v$, so the condition holds iff $p\ge k$.

$n=pq$, $p<q$ odd primes: it works. If $g=p$ then $q\nmid d$; modulo $p$ both terms vanish, and modulo $q$, since $p$ is odd and $q\nmid d$, \[d^{pq}+(n-d)^p\equiv d^{pq}-d^p=d^p\bigl(d^{p(q-1)}-1\bigr)\equiv0\] by Fermat. The case $g=q$ is symmetric. So $pq$ divides the sum.

$n$ even, not a power of $2$: fails. Write $n=2^am$ with $m>1$ odd and take $d=m+1<n$. It is even and $d\equiv1\pmod m$, so $g$ is a power of $2$, $g\ge2$. For an odd prime $r\mid m$: $d\equiv1$, $n-d\equiv-1\pmod r$, so the sum is $\equiv1+1=2\not\equiv0\pmod r$.

$n$ odd, $r^e\,\|\,n$ with $e\ge2$ and $m=n/r^e>1$: fails. Let $\rho$ be a primitive root modulo $r^e$ and choose $t\in[1,r^e-1]$ with $mt\equiv\rho\pmod{r^e}$; put $d=mt<n$. Then $g=m$ is odd and $r\nmid d$, and modulo $r^e$ the sum is $d^n-d^m=d^m(d^{n-m}-1)$. Divisibility would need the order $r^{e-1}(r-1)$ of $d$ to divide $n-m=m(r^e-1)$, which is impossible because $r$ divides the order but not $m(r^e-1)$.

$n$ odd, squarefree, with at least three prime factors: fails. Let $q$ be the largest prime factor and $p\ne r$ two others, and let $\rho$ be a primitive root mod $q$. By the Chinese remainder theorem choose $d\in(0,n)$ with $d\equiv0\pmod p$, $d\equiv\rho\pmod q$, $d\equiv1$ modulo the other primes; then $g=p$, and modulo $q$ the sum is $d^n-d^p=d^p(d^{n-p}-1)$, so the condition requires $(q-1)\mid n-p$. In the same way (with $r$ in place of $p$) it requires $(q-1)\mid n-r$. Both together give $(q-1)\mid p-r$, but $0<|p-r|\le q-2-3<q-1$. So the condition fails for one of these $d$.

Conclusion. The remaining composite numbers are exactly $p^k$ with $2\le k\le p$ and $pq$ with distinct odd primes, and these work. (For $p=2$ the prime-power case gives only $n=4$.) The answer is \[\boxed{n=p^k\ \text{with } p \text{ prime and } 2\le k\le p,\quad\text{or}\quad n=pq\ \text{with } p<q \text{ odd primes}.}\]

Problem 6

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

$4$ identical squares can be packed inside a circle of radius $1$ such that their interiors do not overlap. Find the maximum possible side length of these squares.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Try a few symmetric arrangements of the four squares and compute the largest side each allows. Keep the best one as a conjectured maximum.
Hint 2: key idea
Take the conjectured side length as a benchmark. On a well-chosen concentric circle, estimate the angular measure inside any square of benchmark size, since disjoint interiors contribute disjoint sets of angles. (LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments)
Hint 3: approach
Align the axes through the disk center with the sides of one benchmark square and bound its offsets using the farthest vertex. Split according to the signs of the lower coordinates, convert the coordinate bounds on the auxiliary circle into angular intervals, and track when the lower bound on their total length is attained. For a larger square compare with its concentric benchmark copy and examine the angular measure in the equality configuration.
Common trap
Use open square interiors when counting arcs, since boundaries of different squares may touch.
Show the answer

$\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$

Show the solution – Arcs on the circle of radius $\sqrt2-1$

Let $O$ be the centre of the circle and put $s_0=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$.

Construction. Four squares of side $s_0$ forming a $2\times2$ block with centre $O$ make a square of side $2s_0$, whose vertices are at distance $s_0\sqrt2=1$ from $O$. So side $s_0$ is possible.

Set-up for the bound. Let $\Gamma$ be the circle with centre $O$ and radius $r=\sqrt2-1$. For an open square $Q$ let $A(Q)$ be the set of angles $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$ with $(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\in Q$, and $|A(Q)|$ its total length. We use the numbers \[\kappa=\frac{s_0}{2r}=\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.854,\qquad r^2=3-2\sqrt2<\tfrac14,\] and three facts: (F1) $\kappa>\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so $\arcsin\kappa>\frac\pi4$; (F2) $\arcsin\kappa>\frac{5\pi}{16}$, because $\sin^2\frac{5\pi}{16}=\frac{1+\sin(\pi/8)}2$, $\sin\frac\pi8=\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}2<\frac{2\sqrt2-1}4$ (square both sides: $8-4\sqrt2<9-4\sqrt2$), hence $\sin^2\frac{5\pi}{16}<\frac{3+2\sqrt2}8=\kappa^2$; (F3) for $X,Y\in[0,1]$, $\arcsin X+\arcsin Y>\frac\pi2$ iff $X^2+Y^2>1$ (as $\arcsin Y>\frac\pi2-\arcsin X=\arccos X$ iff $Y>\sqrt{1-X^2}$).

Lemma. If a square of side $s_0$ lies in the closed unit disc and $Q$ is its interior, then $|A(Q)|\ge\frac\pi2$, with equality only if $O$ is a vertex of the square.

Proof. Take axes along the sides of the square; reflecting in these axes (which fixes $O$, the disc and $|A(Q)|$) we may assume the centre of the square is in the closed first quadrant. Then $Q=(u,u+s_0)\times(v,v+s_0)$ with $u,v\ge-\frac{s_0}2$, and the far vertex $(u+s_0,v+s_0)$ lies in the disc; expanding with $2s_0^2=1$, \[u^2+v^2+\sqrt2\,(u+v)\le0 .\tag{1}\] So $u+v\le0$, with equality only for $u=v=0$, and $u,v$ are not both positive. Put $p=u+s_0$, $q=v+s_0$; note $p,q\ge\frac{s_0}2$.

Case 1: $u\le0$ and $v\le0$. For $\theta$ in the open first quadrant the lower bounds $r\cos\theta>u$, $r\sin\theta>v$ hold automatically, so $\theta\in A(Q)$ iff $r\cos\theta<p$ and $r\sin\theta<q$, an interval of length $(\alpha_1+\beta_1-\frac\pi2)^+$ where $\alpha_1=\arcsin\min(1,\frac pr)$, $\beta_1=\arcsin\min(1,\frac qr)$. The other quadrants are alike, with $\alpha_2=\arcsin\frac{-u}r$, $\beta_2=\arcsin\frac{-v}r$ (here $-u,-v\le\frac{s_0}2<r$). So \[|A(Q)|=\sum_{i,j\in\{1,2\}}\Bigl(\alpha_i+\beta_j-\frac\pi2\Bigr)^+,\qquad \frac pr+\frac{-u}r=\frac{s_0}r=2\kappa .\]

  • If $p\ge r$ and $q\ge r$: $\alpha_1=\beta_1=\frac\pi2$, and the terms $(1,1),(2,1),(1,2)$ give $|A(Q)|\ge\frac\pi2+\alpha_2+\beta_2\ge\frac\pi2$, with equality only if $u=v=0$.

  • If $p<r$: then $\frac{-u}r>2\kappa-1=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so $\alpha_2>\frac\pi4$, and $\alpha_1\ge\arcsin\kappa>\frac\pi4$. By convexity of $\arcsin$ on $[0,1]$, $\alpha_1+\alpha_2=\arcsin\frac pr+\arcsin\bigl(2\kappa-\frac pr\bigr)\ge2\arcsin\kappa$. If $q\ge r$, the terms $(1,1),(2,1)$ give $|A(Q)|\ge\alpha_1+\alpha_2\ge2\arcsin\kappa>\frac\pi2$. If $q<r$, the same reasoning gives $\beta_1,\beta_2>\frac\pi4$ and $\beta_1+\beta_2\ge2\arcsin\kappa$; all four terms are positive and, by (F2), \[|A(Q)|=2(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\beta_1+\beta_2)-2\pi\ge8\arcsin\kappa-2\pi>\tfrac\pi2 .\]

  • If $p\ge r>q$: symmetric to the previous case, $|A(Q)|>\frac\pi2$.

Case 2: $u>0$ (the case $v>0$ is the mirror image in the line $y=x$). Then $v<0$, and (1) gives $\sqrt2\,(u-|v|)\le-(u^2+v^2)<0$, so $|v|>u$. Also $q\ge\frac{s_0}2$ and (1) give $(u+s_0)^2\le1-\frac{s_0^2}4<1$, so $u<1-s_0=\frac r{\sqrt2}$. As $p>s_0>r$, a point of $\Gamma$ is in $Q$ iff $r\cos\theta>u$ and $v<r\sin\theta<q$. The first condition means $\theta\in(-A_1,A_1)$ with $A_1=\arccos\frac ur\in(\frac\pi4,\frac\pi2)$; there $\sin$ is increasing, so the second means $\theta\in(-\beta_-,\beta_+)$ with $\beta_-=\arcsin\frac{|v|}r$, $\beta_+=\arcsin\min(1,\frac qr)$. Hence \[|A(Q)|=\min(A_1,\beta_-)+\min(A_1,\beta_+),\] the least of the four sums below, each larger than $\frac\pi2$:

  • $A_1+A_1>\frac\pi2$ since $A_1>\frac\pi4$;

  • $A_1+\beta_+>\frac\pi2$ since $\beta_+\ge\arcsin\kappa>\frac\pi4$ (as $\frac qr\ge\kappa$);

  • $\beta_-+A_1>\frac\pi2$ since $\beta_->\arcsin\frac ur=\frac\pi2-A_1$;

  • $\beta_-+\beta_+>\frac\pi2$: clear if $q\ge r$; otherwise, with $w=|v|$, $X=\frac{s_0-w}r$, $Y=\frac wr$, we have $X^2+Y^2=\frac{(s_0-w)^2+w^2}{r^2}\ge\frac{s_0^2/2}{r^2}=\frac{1/4}{r^2}>1$, and (F3) applies.

So $|A(Q)|>\frac\pi2$ in Case 2, which proves the lemma. $\square$

The bound. Suppose four squares of side $s>s_0$ with disjoint interiors $Q_1,\dots,Q_4$ lie in the disc. The sets $A(Q_i)$ are disjoint subsets of $[0,2\pi)$, so $\sum|A(Q_i)|\le2\pi$. Let $Q_i'\subset Q_i$ be the interior of the concentric square of side $s_0$ with parallel sides; by the lemma $|A(Q_i)|\ge|A(Q_i')|\ge\frac\pi2$. Hence all these are equal to $\frac\pi2$, and by the equality case each $Q_i'$ has $O$ as a vertex. In axes along its sides, $Q_i'=(0,s_0)^2$, so $A(Q_i')=(0,\frac\pi2)$ (because $r<s_0$), while $Q_i=(-\delta,s_0+\delta)^2$ with $\delta=\frac{s-s_0}2>0$. The open set $A(Q_i)$ contains $(0,\frac\pi2)$ and also $\theta=0$ (the point $(r,0)$ is in $Q_i$), so $|A(Q_i)|>\frac\pi2$, a contradiction.

Therefore the side cannot exceed $s_0$, and the maximum side length is $\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}}$.
