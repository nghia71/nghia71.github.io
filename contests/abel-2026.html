Problem 1a

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Determine all pairs of positive integers $a, b$ for which both $a^2+2b+1$ and $b^2+2a+1$ are perfect squares.

Show the answer

all pairs $(a,a)$ with $a$ a positive integer

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Squeezing between consecutive squares)

By symmetry we may assume $a\ge b$. Then \[a^2<a^2+2b+1\le a^2+2a+1=(a+1)^2 .\] The only perfect square in the interval $(a^2,(a+1)^2]$ is $(a+1)^2$, so $a^2+2b+1=(a+1)^2$, which gives $b=a$.

Conversely, if $a=b$ then both expressions equal $a^2+2a+1=(a+1)^2$, a perfect square. Hence the pairs are exactly $(a,a)$ for every positive integer $a$.

Solution 2 (Writing the square as a shifted square)

Since $a^2+2b+1>a^2$, we can write $a^2+2b+1=(a+s)^2$ with an integer $s\ge1$. Then \[2b+1=2as+s^2 .\] The left side is odd, so $s$ is odd and $s^2\ge s$; hence $2b+1\ge 2as+s\ge 2a+1$, that is, $b\ge a$. In exactly the same way the condition on $b^2+2a+1$ gives $a\ge b$. Therefore $a=b$, and then (as in Solution 1) both numbers equal $(a+1)^2$. The answer is all pairs $(a,a)$.

Problem 1b

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Write $S(n) = 1^1 + 2^2 + \ldots + n^n$ for every positive integer $n$. Show that there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that the first digit of $S(n)$ equals the first digit of $n$, while simultaneously the final digit of $S(n)$ equals the final digit of $n$.

Show the answer

proof (for example every $n=10^m$ with $m\ge2$ works)

Show the solution

Solution (Powers of ten)

We show that every $n=10^m$ with $m\ge2$ has the property.

Last digit. We claim that $k^k \bmod 10$ depends only on $k \bmod 20$. Modulo $2$, $k^k\equiv k$. Modulo $5$: if $5\mid k$ then $k^k\equiv0$; otherwise $k^4\equiv1 \pmod 5$ by Fermat's little theorem, so $k^k\equiv k^{r}\pmod 5$ where $r\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ is the remainder of $k$ modulo $4$. Both $k\bmod5$ and $k\bmod4$ are determined by $k\bmod20$, so $k^k \bmod 2$ and $k^k\bmod 5$, hence $k^k\bmod10$, depend only on $k\bmod 20$.

The last digits of $k^k$ for $k=1,\dots,20$ are \[1,4,7,6,5,6,3,6,9,0,\ 1,6,3,6,5,6,7,4,9,0,\] whose sum is $94$. Hence every block of $20$ consecutive values of $k$ contributes $4$ modulo $10$, and $S(20t)\equiv4t\pmod{10}$. For $n=10^m$ we have $t=n/20=5\cdot10^{m-2}$, so $S(n)\equiv 20\cdot 10^{m-2}\equiv0\pmod{10}$: the last digit of $S(n)$ is $0$, the last digit of $n$.

First digit. For $1\le k\le n-1$ we have $k^k\le n^k$, so \[n^n< S(n)=n^n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^k\le n^n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}n^k=n^n+\frac{n^n-n}{n-1}<2n^n .\] With $n=10^m$, $n^n=10^{mn}$, so $10^{mn}<S(n)<2\cdot10^{mn}$ and the first digit of $S(n)$ is $1$, the first digit of $n$.

Since this holds for every $m\ge2$, there are infinitely many such $n$.

Problem 2a

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Harald has a stack of $n$ cards, marked with numbers $1, 2, \ldots, n$ in order from top to bottom. Then he reduces the stack repeatedly: Each reduction consists of removing two adjacent cards from the stack, subtracting the number $a$ on the lower card from the number $b$ on the one above, writing the difference $b-a$, on a blank card, and inserting the new card at a freely chosen location in the stack. After $n-1$ such reductions, he is left with just one card. For which positive integers $n$ can the number on this card be zero?

Show the answer

exactly the $n\ge4$ with $n\equiv0$ or $3\pmod 4$

Show the solution

Solution (Parity of the sum, and building blocks)

Necessity. A reduction replaces $b$ and $a$ by $b-a$, and $b-a\equiv b+a\pmod 2$. Hence the parity of the sum of all numbers in the stack never changes. At the start the sum is $1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, and at the end it is the number on the last card. So the last card can be $0$ only if $\frac{n(n+1)}2$ is even, that is, $n\equiv0$ or $3\pmod 4$.

The case $n=3$ also fails. The original cards are never moved, so $1$ stays above $2$ and $2$ stays above $3$. If the first reduction uses the cards $1,2$, it creates $-1$, and the last reduction gives $3-(-1)=4$ or $-1-3=-4$. If it uses $2,3$, it creates $-1$, and the last reduction gives $1-(-1)=2$ or $-1-1=-2$. Zero never appears.

Sufficiency. Block of four. If four cards $x,x+1,x+2,x+3$ lie consecutively in the stack (in this order from the top), we can replace them by one card $0$ at the same place: reduce $x,x+1$ to $-1$ and put it back where the pair was; reduce $x+2,x+3$ to $-1$ and put it back where that pair was; now the two cards $-1,-1$ are adjacent and give $-1-(-1)=0$.

Block of seven. The cards $1,\dots,7$ on top of the stack can be replaced by one card $0$ on top (we show only the top part): \[1,2,3,4,5,6,7\ \to\ -1,3,4,5,6,7\ \to\ -4,4,5,6,7\ \to\ -8,5,6,7\ \to\ -1,-8,7\ \to\ 7,7\ \to\ 0 .\] (Here $1-2=-1$, $-1-3=-4$, $-4-4=-8$; then $5-6=-1$ is inserted on top, $-1-(-8)=7$, and $7-7=0$.)

If $n=4q$, split $1,\dots,n$ into the blocks $\{1,2,3,4\},\{5,6,7,8\},\dots$ and turn every block into a $0$. If $n=4q+3$ with $q\ge1$, turn $1,\dots,7$ into a $0$ and the blocks $\{8,9,10,11\},\dots$ into zeros. In both cases we are left with a stack of zeros, and $0-0=0$ reduces it to a single card $0$.

Hence the answer is all $n\ge4$ with $n\equiv0$ or $3\pmod4$.

Problem 2b

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants

Let $m$ and $n$ be positive integers. An $m \times n$ grid is called oriented if every unit edge has an arrow pointing on one of the two directions along the edge. A move consists of rotating a grid cell, along with the direction of each of the cell's four edges, by $90^\circ$. Two oriented grids are called friends if some sequences of moves will turn the first grid into the second one. Determine the largest possible size of a collection of oriented $m \times n$ grids so that no two of them are friends.

Example: The oriented $3 \times 2$ grid on the right is the result of rotating the shaded grid cell of the left grid $90^\circ$ counterclockwise.

Show the answer

$2mn+m+n+1$ (the number of unit edges plus one), except $6$ when $m=n=1$

Show the solution

Solution (Good edges and three-cycles of labels)

Let $E=m(n+1)+n(m+1)=2mn+m+n$ be the number of unit edges.

Good and bad edges. Colour the cells like a chessboard. Call an edge good if its arrow goes clockwise around the black cell it borders and counterclockwise around the white cell it borders (an edge on the boundary borders only one cell, and we use only that cell). This is consistent: an inner edge borders one black and one white cell, and an arrow going clockwise around one of them goes counterclockwise around the other. Each edge has exactly one good direction, so an oriented grid is the same thing as a labelling of the edges by “good” and “bad”.

What a move does. Rotating a cell $c$ about its centre carries the arrow of each of its four edges to the next edge, and an arrow going clockwise around $c$ still goes clockwise around $c$. All four edges have $c$ as their black (or white) cell, so the label travels with the arrow: a move shifts the four labels around the cell by one step and changes nothing else. In particular the number $G$ of good edges never changes. Since $G$ can be any of $0,1,\dots,E$, the $E+1$ grids with these values of $G$ are pairwise not friends.

The $1\times1$ grid. Here the four labels can only be rotated. For $G=0,1,3,4$ all arrangements are rotations of each other, but for $G=2$ the arrangements “two adjacent good edges” and “two opposite good edges” are not. So there are $4+2=6$ classes, and the answer is $6$.

From now on the grid has at least two cells. We show that any two grids with the same $G$ are friends, so the answer is $E+1$.

Step 1: a three-cycle. Take two cells $c_1,c_2$ sharing an edge $e$; turning the picture, we may let $c_1$ be the left one. Name the other edges $t_1,l_1,b_1$ (top, left, bottom of $c_1$) and $t_2,b_2,r_2$ (top, bottom, right of $c_2$). A counterclockwise move $\rho_1$ of $c_1$ moves the labels $t_1\to l_1\to b_1\to e\to t_1$, and $\rho_2$ moves $t_2\to e\to b_2\to r_2\to t_2$. Do $\rho_1$, then $\rho_2$, then $\rho_1^{-1}$, then $\rho_2^{-1}$ (an inverse is three moves). Following each label: the label of $e$ goes $e\to t_1\to t_1\to e\to t_2$; the label of $t_2$ goes $t_2\to t_2\to e\to b_1\to b_1$; the label of $b_1$ goes $b_1\to e\to b_2\to b_2\to e$; every other label comes back. So the labels of $e,t_2,b_1$ are permuted cyclically ($e\to t_2\to b_1\to e$) and all others are fixed.

Step 2: exchanges. Call two edges $p,q$ linked if, for every labelling, some sequence of moves exchanges the labels of $p$ and $q$ and fixes all other labels. If moves can cycle the labels $p\to q\to r\to p$ (others fixed), then any two of $p,q,r$ are linked. Indeed, to exchange $p$ and $q$ we may assume their labels differ; the label of $r$ equals one of them. If it equals the label of $q$, one cycle does the exchange; if it equals the label of $p$, two cycles do it. Linking is symmetric and transitive (exchange $p,q$, then $q,r$, then $p,q$).

Step 3: transport. If $p,q$ are linked and $\rho$ is a move, then $\rho(p),\rho(q)$ are linked: apply $\rho^{-1}$ (the labels of $\rho(p),\rho(q)$ arrive at $p,q$), exchange the labels of $p$ and $q$, and apply $\rho$.

Step 4: all edges are linked. By Steps 1 and 2, $e,t_2,b_1$ are pairwise linked. The move $\rho_1$ fixes $t_2$ and moves $b_1\to e\to t_1\to l_1$, so by Step 3 every edge of $c_1$ is linked to $t_2$; in the same way ($\rho_2$ fixes $b_1$) every edge of $c_2$ is linked to $b_1$. Hence all edges of two adjacent cells are linked to each other. The cells of the grid are connected through common edges, and two adjacent pairs of cells that share a cell share the four edges of the common cell, so all edges of the grid are linked.

Step 5. Given two labellings with the same number of good edges, there is a permutation of the edges carrying one to the other; it is a product of transpositions, and each transposition (exchange of two labels) can be done by moves. So the two grids are friends.

Hence the largest collection has $\boxed{2mn+m+n+1}$ grids for $(m,n)\ne(1,1)$, and $\boxed{6}$ grids for the $1\times1$ grid.

Problem 3a

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Find all infinite sequences $a_1, a_2, \ldots$ of positive integers, such that for every positive integer $n$ there exists a positive integer $k$ satisfying, \[k = \frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} + \ldots + \frac{a_n}{a_{n+k}}.\]

Show the answer

exactly the constant sequences

Show the solution

Solution (Looking at the smallest term)

Constant sequences work: if $a_n=c$ for all $n$, every fraction equals $1$ and $k=1$ does it.

Conversely, let the sequence satisfy the condition. Its terms are positive integers, so there is a smallest value $m$; let $N$ be the first index with $a_N=m$.

From $N$ on, the sequence is constant. Suppose $a_n=m$ for some $n$. Take $k$ as in the condition. Every fraction $\frac{a_n}{a_{n+j}}=\frac{m}{a_{n+j}}$ is at most $1$, and the $k$ fractions add up to $k$, so each equals $1$; in particular $a_{n+1}=m$. By induction $a_n=m$ for all $n\ge N$.

$N=1$. If $N\ge2$, look at $n=N-1$. Then $a_{N-1}>m$ (as $N$ is the first index with value $m$) and all later terms equal $m$, so for every $k$ \[\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N}}+\dots+\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N-1+k}}=k\cdot\frac{a_{N-1}}{m}>k,\] which contradicts the condition. Hence $N=1$ and $a_n=m$ for all $n$: the sequence is constant.

Problem 3b

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Given $4052$ distinct non-zero integers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{2026}, b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_{2026}$, let $M$ be the number of pairs of indices $(i, j)$ such that $1 \le i < j \le 2026$ and, \[2(a_ib_j + a_jb_i)^2 = (a_i^2+b_i^2)(a_j^2+b_j^2).\] What is the largest value of $M$, given all permitted choices of the 4052 numbers?

Show the answer

$\boxed{1026169}=1013^2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Angles of vectors: the graph is bipartite)

Let $u_i=(a_i,b_i)$, a nonzero vector with direction angle $\theta_i$, and let $r_i=|u_i|$. The vector $v_j=(b_j,a_j)$ (the reflection of $u_j$ in the line $y=x$) has length $r_j$ and direction $90^\circ-\theta_j$. Hence \[a_ib_j+a_jb_i=u_i\cdot v_j=r_ir_j\cos(\theta_i+\theta_j-90^\circ)=r_ir_j\sin(\theta_i+\theta_j),\] and the condition $2(a_ib_j+a_jb_i)^2=r_i^2r_j^2$ becomes $\sin^2(\theta_i+\theta_j)=\frac12$, that is, \[\theta_i+\theta_j\equiv45^\circ\pmod{90^\circ}.\] Let $\varphi_i$ be the remainder of $\theta_i$ modulo $90^\circ$. Build a graph on the indices $1,\dots,2026$, joining $i$ and $j$ when the condition holds; $M$ is its number of edges, and $i\sim j$ exactly when $\varphi_j\equiv 45^\circ-\varphi_i\pmod{90^\circ}$.

The map $\varphi\mapsto45^\circ-\varphi \pmod{90^\circ}$ swaps values in pairs; its only fixed points are $\varphi=22.5^\circ$ and $67.5^\circ$. But $\tan\theta_i=b_i/a_i$ is rational, while for $\theta\equiv22.5^\circ$ or $67.5^\circ \pmod{90^\circ}$ the value $\tan\theta$ is one of $\pm(\sqrt2\pm1)$, which are irrational. So no index is joined to an index with the same $\varphi$, and every edge joins the class of some value $\varphi$ to the class of its partner $45^\circ-\varphi$. Putting, for each pair of partner values, one class on the left and the other on the right, we see that the graph is bipartite with parts $X,Y$, $|X|+|Y|=2026$. Therefore \[M\le|X|\cdot|Y|\le1013^2 .\]

Construction. Take $(a_i,b_i)=(c,2c)$ for $c=1,3,5,\dots,2025$ and $(a_j,b_j)=(3d,-d)$ for $d=4,8,\dots,4052$ (each $1013$ vectors). The $4052$ numbers are distinct and nonzero: the $c$ are odd, the $2c$ are $\equiv2\pmod4$, the $3d$ are positive multiples of $4$, and the $-d$ are negative. For such a pair, $a_ib_j+a_jb_i=-cd+6cd=5cd$, and \[2(5cd)^2=50c^2d^2=(c^2+4c^2)(9d^2+d^2),\] so all $1013^2$ such pairs count. Hence the largest value is $M=1013^2=\boxed{1026169}$.

Solution 2 (Slopes, no triangles, and Mantel)

Put $t_i=b_i/a_i$, a nonzero rational. Dividing the condition by $a_i^2a_j^2$ gives $2(t_i+t_j)^2=(1+t_i^2)(1+t_j^2)$, which rearranges to \[(t_i+t_j)^2=(1-t_it_j)^2 .\] So either $t_i+t_j=1-t_it_j$, i.e. $(1+t_i)(1+t_j)=2$ (“type $P$”), or $t_i+t_j=t_it_j-1$, i.e. $(1-t_i)(1-t_j)=2$ (“type $Q$”).

Join $i$ and $j$ when the condition holds. This graph has no triangle. If $i,j,k$ were pairwise joined, two of the three edges would have the same type, and two edges of a triangle share a vertex; say $ij$ and $ik$ are both of type $P$. Then $(1+t_i)(1+t_j)=2=(1+t_i)(1+t_k)$, and $1+t_i\ne0$, so $t_j=t_k$. The edge $jk$ then says $(1+t_j)^2=2$ or $(1-t_j)^2=2$, impossible for a rational $t_j$. Type $Q$ is the same.

Mantel's bound. In a triangle-free graph with $N$ vertices and $M$ edges, the endpoints of an edge $uv$ have no common neighbour, so $\deg u+\deg v\le N$. Summing over all edges, each vertex $v$ appears $\deg v$ times, so $\sum_v(\deg v)^2\le NM$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\sum_v(\deg v)^2\ge\frac{(2M)^2}{N}$. Hence $4M^2/N\le NM$, i.e. $M\le N^2/4$. With $N=2026$: $M\le1013^2$.

Construction. Take $(a_i,b_i)=(c,2c)$ for $c=1,3,5,\dots,2025$ and $(a_j,b_j)=(3d,-d)$ for $d=4,8,\dots,4052$ ($1013$ vectors of each kind). The $4052$ numbers are distinct and nonzero: the $c$ are odd, the $2c$ are $\equiv2\pmod4$, the $3d$ are positive multiples of $4$, and the $-d$ are negative. The first kind has $t=2$ and the second $t=-\frac13$, and $(1+2)\bigl(1-\frac13\bigr)=2$, so every one of the $1013^2$ mixed pairs is of type $P$ and satisfies the condition. Hence the largest value is $M=1013^2=\boxed{1026169}$.

Problem 4a

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $D$ be an interior point of the triangle $ABC$. Let $E$ be the point on the line segment $AC$ such that $\angle ADE = \angle ABC$, and $F$ be the point on the line segment $AB$ such that $\angle ADF = \angle ACB$. Show that the circumcircles of $ABC$ and $DEF$ are mutually tangent.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The circle passes through $A$; homothety)

Write $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ for the angles of triangle $ABC$ at $A,B,C$, and $\alpha_1=\angle FAD$, $\alpha_2=\angle DAE$, so $\alpha_1+\alpha_2=\alpha$ (the ray $AD$ lies inside the angle $BAC$).

$A,F,D,E$ are concyclic. In triangle $AFD$, $\angle AFD=180^\circ-\alpha_1-\gamma$; in triangle $ADE$, $\angle AED=180^\circ-\alpha_2-\beta$. Hence \[\angle AFD+\angle AED=360^\circ-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)=180^\circ .\] The points $F$ and $E$ lie on different sides of the line $AD$ ($F$ on side $AB$, $E$ on side $AC$), so $AFDE$ is a quadrilateral made of the triangles $AFD$ and $ADE$; its angles at $A$ ($=\alpha$) and at $D$ ($=\gamma+\beta$) are less than $180^\circ$, so it is convex. A convex quadrilateral whose opposite angles add up to $180^\circ$ is cyclic. Hence the circumcircle $\omega$ of $DEF$ passes through $A$.

$EF\parallel BC$. In $\omega$, the angles $AEF$ and $ADF$ stand on the same chord $AF$ from the same side, so $\angle AEF=\angle ADF=\gamma=\angle ACB$. Since $E$ lies on $AC$ and $F$ on $AB$, this gives $EF\parallel BC$.

Tangency. The homothety with centre $A$ and ratio $AB/AF$ maps $F$ to $B$ and, because $EF\parallel BC$, maps $E$ to $C$. So it maps the circle $AFE=\omega$ to the circle $ABC$. Two circles that correspond under a homothety whose centre $A$ lies on both of them are tangent at $A$ (the tangent line at $A$ is mapped to a parallel line through $A$, i.e. to itself). The circles are different, since $D$ is inside triangle $ABC$ and hence not on its circumcircle. So the circumcircles of $ABC$ and $DEF$ are tangent at $A$.

Solution 2 (The tangent line at $A$)

Let $t$ be the tangent to the circumcircle of $ABC$ at $A$. By the tangent–chord angle, $t$ makes the angle $\angle ACB$ with the chord $AB$ (on the side of $AB$ away from $C$) and the angle $\angle ABC$ with the chord $AC$ (on the side away from $B$).

Consider the circle $\omega_1$ through $A,D,F$. The line $t$ makes with the chord $AF$ (which lies along $AB$) the angle $\angle ACB=\angle ADF$, measured on the side of $AF$ away from $D$ (since $D$ is on the same side of $AB$ as $C$). By the converse of the tangent–chord theorem, $t$ is tangent to $\omega_1$ at $A$. In the same way, $t$ makes with the chord $AE$ the angle $\angle ABC=\angle ADE$, so $t$ is tangent at $A$ to the circle $\omega_2$ through $A,D,E$.

Now $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ both pass through $D$ and are tangent to the line $t$ at $A$. There is only one such circle (its centre lies on the perpendicular to $t$ at $A$ and on the perpendicular bisector of $AD$; these lines meet in one point since $D\notin t$). Hence $\omega_1=\omega_2$, so $A,D,E,F$ lie on one circle, which is the circumcircle of $DEF$, and it is tangent to $t$ at $A$. The circumcircle of $ABC$ is also tangent to $t$ at $A$, so the two circles are tangent to each other at $A$.

Problem 4b

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB\ne AC$, and $E$ and $F$ be the points satisfying \[\angle BCE = \angle EAC = \angle CBF = \angle FAB = 90^\circ.\] Let $D$ be the point of intersection of the lines $BC$ and $EF$. Show that the line $AD$ is tangent to the circumcircle of $ABC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Ratio of squares)

By hypothesis $AB\ne AC$ (for $AB=AC$ the line $EF$ would be parallel to $BC$ and $D$ would not exist). By symmetry let $AB<AC$. Write $\beta=\angle ABC$, $\gamma=\angle ACB$.

Lengths $CE$ and $BF$. Since $\angle EAC=90^\circ$, the point $A$ lies on the circle with diameter $CE$; since $CE\perp BC$, this circle is tangent to $BC$ at $C$, so it lies on one side of $BC$: hence $E$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$. In the right triangle $EAC$ the angle at $C$ is $\angle ACE=|90^\circ-\gamma|$, so $AC=CE\cos\angle ACE=CE\sin\gamma$. In the same way $F$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$ and $AB=BF\sin\beta$. Therefore, using the law of sines $AC/AB=\sin\beta/\sin\gamma$, \[\frac{CE}{BF}=\frac{AC\sin\beta}{AB\sin\gamma}=\frac{AC^2}{AB^2}\ne1 .\]

Position of $D$. The segments $CE$ and $BF$ are both perpendicular to $BC$, hence parallel, and lie on the same side of $BC$. So $D=EF\cap BC$ is the centre of a homothety with positive ratio taking $C\mapsto B$, $E\mapsto F$; thus $D$ lies outside the segment $BC$ and \[\frac{DC}{DB}=\frac{CE}{BF}=\frac{AC^2}{AB^2}.\]

The tangent at $A$. By the tangent–chord angle, the tangent at $A$ to the circumcircle makes the angle $\gamma$ with the chord $AB$, on the side of line $AB$ away from $C$. Since $AB<AC$ we have $\gamma<\beta$, so the angles $\gamma$ (at $A$) and $180^\circ-\beta$ (the exterior angle at $B$) add up to less than $180^\circ$; hence that ray of the tangent meets the extension of $CB$ beyond $B$ at a point $T$. Triangles $TAB$ and $TCA$ have the common angle at $T$ and $\angle TAB=\gamma=\angle TCA$, so they are similar and \[\frac{TB}{TA}=\frac{TA}{TC}=\frac{AB}{AC},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\frac{TC}{TB}=\frac{AC^2}{AB^2}.\] On the line $BC$ there is only one point outside the segment $BC$ whose distances to $C$ and $B$ have a given ratio different from $1$. Hence $T=D$, and the line $AD$ is tangent to the circumcircle of $ABC$.

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Put $BC$ on the $x$-axis: $A=(0,h)$ with $h\ne0$, $B=(b,0)$, $C=(c,0)$; then the hypothesis $AB\ne AC$ means $b+c\ne0$.

$E$ lies on the line $x=c$ (as $CE\perp BC$): $E=(c,y)$. The condition $AE\perp AC$ reads $(c,\,y-h)\cdot(c,\,-h)=0$, so $y=h+\frac{c^2}{h}$. Similarly $F=\bigl(b,\ h+\frac{b^2}{h}\bigr)$. The slope of $EF$ is $\frac{(c^2-b^2)/h}{c-b}=\frac{b+c}{h}$, so the line $EF$ is \[y=h+\frac{c^2}{h}+\frac{b+c}{h}(x-c).\] Setting $y=0$ gives $(b+c)(x-c)=-(h^2+c^2)$, so $D=(d,0)$ with \[d=\frac{bc-h^2}{b+c},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad (b+c)\,d=bc-h^2 .\] Now compute the power of $D$ with respect to the circumcircle: since $B,C$ are on the circle and on a line through $D$, \[\overline{DB}\cdot\overline{DC}=(d-b)(d-c)=d^2-(b+c)d+bc=d^2+h^2=DA^2 .\] So the line $DA$ meets the circle at $A$ and at a second point $A'$ with $DA\cdot DA'=DA^2$, i.e. $A'=A$: the line $DA$ meets the circle only at $A$, so it is tangent to the circumcircle of $ABC$.
