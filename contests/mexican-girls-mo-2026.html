Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations · Triangle and Polygon Angle Sums

In a regular pentagon $ABCDE$, two equilateral triangles $FBE$ and $ABG$ are constructed as shown in the figure. Let $H$ be the intersection point of $BF$ and $AG$. Determine the value of $\angle FHG$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $BE$ and trace the rays $BF$ and $AG$ through $H$. Mark how those rays sit relative to the pentagon and the two equilateral triangles.
Hint 2: key idea
Use the pentagon's interior angle and the equal sides of $ABE$ to find its base angle at $B$. Combine that angle with the equilateral angle at $B$ to locate the angle at $B$ in triangle $ABH$. (LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations)
Hint 3: approach
Determine $\angle HBA$ by checking the order of rays from $B$, and read $\angle HAB$ from the equilateral triangle. Use the angle sum of $ABH$ and check which rays at $H$ point in opposite directions.
Common trap
Whether two angles should be added or subtracted depends on the ray order shown in the figure.
Show the answer

$\angle FHG=96^\circ$

Show solution 1 – Angle chase in triangle ABH

Each interior angle of a regular pentagon measures $\frac{(5-2)\cdot180^\circ}{5}=108^\circ$. Triangle $ABE$ is isosceles with $AB=AE$ and $\angle BAE=108^\circ$, so $\angle ABE=\angle AEB=36^\circ$.

In the figure, $F$ lies on the same side of $BE$ as $A$, and $\angle FBE=60^\circ>36^\circ=\angle ABE$, so the ray $BA$ lies inside the angle $FBE$ and \[\angle ABF=\angle FBE-\angle ABE=60^\circ-36^\circ=24^\circ .\] Also $\angle BAG=60^\circ$, since triangle $ABG$ is equilateral.

In triangle $ABH$ (with $H$ on segment $BF$ and on segment $AG$), \[\angle AHB=180^\circ-\angle HBA-\angle HAB=180^\circ-24^\circ-60^\circ=96^\circ .\] The ray $HF$ is opposite to the ray $HB$, and the ray $HG$ is opposite to the ray $HA$, so $\angle FHG$ and $\angle BHA$ are vertical angles. Therefore $\angle FHG=\boxed{96^\circ}$.

Show solution 2 – Directions measured from the line EB

Each interior angle of a regular pentagon is $\frac{(5-2)\cdot180^\circ}{5}=108^\circ$, and triangle $ABE$ is isosceles with $AB=AE$, so $\angle ABE=\angle AEB=\frac{180^\circ-108^\circ}2=36^\circ$.

Place the figure so that $EB$ is horizontal, $B$ to the right of $E$, and $A$ above $EB$. Describe the direction of a ray by the angle it makes, counterclockwise, with the ray pointing from $E$ to $B$.

  • The ray $BF$: $F$ is above $EB$ and $\angle EBF=60^\circ$, so the ray $BF$ points at $180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ$.

  • The ray $BA$ points at $180^\circ-36^\circ=144^\circ$, so the ray $AB$ points at $144^\circ-180^\circ=-36^\circ$.

  • The ray $AG$ is the ray $AB$ turned through $60^\circ$ about $A$. Turning clockwise would give the direction $-96^\circ$, which points down into the pentagon (between the rays $AB$ at $-36^\circ$ and $AE$ at $-144^\circ$); since $G$ lies outside the pentagon, the ray $AG$ is obtained by turning counterclockwise and points at $-36^\circ+60^\circ=24^\circ$.

In the figure $H$ lies between $B$ and $F$ and between $A$ and $G$, so the ray $HF$ has the direction of $BF$ ($120^\circ$) and the ray $HG$ has the direction of $AG$ ($24^\circ$). The angle between them is \[\angle FHG=120^\circ-24^\circ=\boxed{96^\circ}.\]

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 29: Exponents, Powers, and Growth · Laws of Exponents

The great witch loves cubes and is about to play a game. She starts with a cube of side length $1$ and another cube of side length $x$ (where $x>1$). At each turn, the witch performs the following two spells, one after the other:

  • 1. She transforms the smaller cube into a prism that keeps its original base, but whose height is stretched until the prism has exactly the same volume as the larger cube.

  • 2. She transforms the newly created prism into a new cube whose side length is equal to the height of the prism.

Find the side length of the larger cube after $2026$ turns.

Note: A prism is a three-dimensional figure with two congruent and parallel bases joined by parallelogram-shaped lateral faces. For example, a box has the shape of a prism.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Sketch the first prism and label its base dimensions. Write what determines its height and decide where that height appears after the second spell.
Hint 2: key idea
If the two sides before a turn are $s<L$, express the prism's height using its base area and the volume of the larger cube. Check which cube is larger after the turn and translate the height formula into a rule on exponents of $x$. (LPS VI, Chapter 29: Exponents, Powers, and Growth)
Hint 3: approach
Start from the exponent pair for the initial cubes and use the rule to generate the next few pairs. Look for a pattern in the exponents after adding one to each, then verify the pattern by substituting into the update rule. Use the turn count to locate the larger exponent.
Common trap
The cube that was larger before the turn stays in play and becomes the smaller one after the turn.
Show the answer

$x^{2^{2027}-1}$

Show solution 1 – One turn on exponents

Suppose that before a turn the cubes have sides $s<L$. The prism has base $s\times s$ and height $h$ with $s^2h=L^3$, so $h=\dfrac{L^3}{s^2}$, and after the turn the cubes have sides $L$ and $h$. Since $L>s$, $h=L\cdot\bigl(\frac Ls\bigr)^2>L$, so the new larger cube is the one of side $h$.

Claim: after $k$ turns the sides are $x^{2^k-1}$ (smaller) and $x^{2^{k+1}-1}$ (larger). For $k=0$ they are $1=x^0$ and $x=x^1$. If the claim holds after $k$ turns, the next turn produces the side \[\frac{\bigl(x^{2^{k+1}-1}\bigr)^3}{\bigl(x^{2^k-1}\bigr)^2}=x^{3\cdot2^{k+1}-3-2^{k+1}+2}=x^{2^{k+2}-1},\] while the old larger cube $x^{2^{k+1}-1}$ becomes the smaller one. This proves the claim.

After $2026$ turns the larger cube has side $\boxed{x^{2^{2027}-1}}$.

Show solution 2 – Logarithms and a doubling difference

Let $c_0=1$, $c_1=x$, and let $c_{k+1}$ be the side of the cube created in turn $k$. In each turn the smaller cube $s$ and the larger cube $L$ give a prism of height $h=L^3/s^2>L$ (because $L>s$), and the new pair is $(L,h)$. Hence the cubes after turn $k$ are $c_k<c_{k+1}$, and \[c_{k+1}=\frac{c_k^3}{c_{k-1}^2}.\] Put $t=\log x>0$ and $\ell_k=\log c_k$. Then $\ell_0=0$, $\ell_1=t$ and $\ell_{k+1}=3\ell_k-2\ell_{k-1}$, i.e. \[\ell_{k+1}-\ell_k=2(\ell_k-\ell_{k-1}).\] The differences double at each step, so $\ell_{k}-\ell_{k-1}=2^{k-1}t$ and \[\ell_k=t\,(1+2+\cdots+2^{k-1})=(2^k-1)\,t .\] After $2026$ turns the larger cube is $c_{2027}=e^{\ell_{2027}}=x^{2^{2027}-1}$. The answer is $\boxed{x^{2^{2027}-1}}$.

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting · Constructive Counting

In the national park “La Malinche” there are $2026$ trees numbered from $1$ to $2026$ and $2026$ squirrels numbered from $1$ to $2026$, each carrying some hawthorns. In the $k$-th minute, the squirrel numbered $k$ does the following:

  • It chooses its $k$ favorite trees and, among them, selects one tree in which it hides $k$ hawthorns.

  • In each of the remaining $k-1$ favorite trees, it hides one hawthorn.

In how many ways can the $2026$ squirrels hide their hawthorns if, after all $2026$ minutes have elapsed, every tree contains the same number of hidden hawthorns?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Count how many hawthorns squirrel $k$ hides in total. If every tree ends with the same amount, what amount must each tree hold?
Hint 2: key idea
Work backwards from the squirrel with the largest number. Track each tree that receives a large pile and ask how the deposits of higher-numbered squirrels can already bring that tree to its required total. (LPS VI, Chapter 6: Casework, Complement, and Constructive Counting)
Hint 3: approach
Show by descending induction that the trees given large piles by higher-numbered squirrels are already full. Check how that restricts the next squirrel's favourite trees and record the remaining choices for its large pile. Verify that any sequence of these choices fills every tree to the target.
Common trap
Reviewing the choices in descending order is a counting device, regardless of the chronological order of the deposits.
Show the answer

$2026!$

Show solution 1 – The big squirrels decide first

Squirrel $k$ hides $k+(k-1)=2k-1$ tejocotes, so in total $\sum_{k=1}^{2026}(2k-1)=2026^2$ tejocotes are hidden. If all trees end with the same amount, each tree ends with exactly $2026$. Call the tree in which squirrel $k$ hides $k$ tejocotes its special tree $T_k$.

Claim. For every $k$, squirrel $k$ chooses exactly the $k$ trees that are not among $T_{k+1},\dots,T_{2026}$, and these $T_j$ are distinct.

We prove this by downward induction on $k$. Assume it holds for all squirrels $j>k$ (nothing to assume for $k=2026$). Let $S=\{T_{k+1},\dots,T_{2026}\}$, a set of $2026-k$ trees.

  • A tree $T_j\in S$ receives $j$ from squirrel $j$ and $1$ from each squirrel $i$ with $j<i\le2026$ (squirrel $i$ chooses every tree outside $\{T_{i+1},\dots,T_{2026}\}$, and $T_j$ is outside that set). So it already has $j+(2026-j)=2026$ tejocotes.

  • Hence no squirrel $\le k$ may choose a tree of $S$ (it would add at least one more tejocote). Squirrel $k$ must choose $k$ trees outside $S$, and there are exactly $2026-(2026-k)=k$ of them: it chooses all of them, and its special tree $T_k$ is one of them, so $T_k\notin S$.

This proves the claim. The only freedom is the choice of $T_k$ among the $k$ trees outside $\{T_{k+1},\dots,T_{2026}\}$: $k$ options for squirrel $k$.

Conversely, every such sequence of choices works: a tree $T_j$ ends with $j+(2026-j)=2026$, because it is chosen exactly by the squirrels $j,j+1,\dots,2026$ (squirrels below $j$ avoid it). Hence the number of ways is \[2026\cdot2025\cdots2\cdot1=\boxed{2026!}.\]

Show solution 2 – Recursion on the number of squirrels

Generalize: for $n$ trees and $n$ squirrels (squirrel $k$ acting as in the problem, $1\le k\le n$), let $f(n)$ be the number of ways in which all trees end with the same number of hawthorns. We show $f(n)=n\,f(n-1)$ for $n\ge2$ and $f(1)=1$.

Squirrel $k$ hides $k+(k-1)=2k-1$ hawthorns, so altogether $1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$ hawthorns are hidden, and each of the $n$ trees must end with exactly $n$.

For $n=1$ the only squirrel chooses the only tree and hides $1$ hawthorn there: $f(1)=1$.

Let $n\ge2$. Squirrel $n$ has $n$ favorite trees, so it chooses all of them; it hides $n$ hawthorns in one tree $T$ (any of the $n$ trees) and $1$ hawthorn in each other tree. Tree $T$ now has $n$ hawthorns, so no other squirrel may choose it. Every other tree has $1$ hawthorn from squirrel $n$ and must receive exactly $n-1$ more, from squirrels $1,\dots,n-1$, none of which uses $T$. Squirrel $k\le n-1$ chooses $k\le n-1$ trees, so it can always avoid $T$.

So after removing $T$ and squirrel $n$, the squirrels $1,\dots,n-1$ must distribute their hawthorns among the remaining $n-1$ trees so that every tree receives the same amount, namely $n-1$: this is the same problem with $n-1$ trees and squirrels, with $f(n-1)$ solutions. Conversely, each of these solutions together with the action of squirrel $n$ leaves exactly $n$ hawthorns in every tree. Since there are $n$ choices for $T$, \[f(n)=n\,f(n-1),\qquad\text{so}\qquad f(n)=n!\,.\] For $n=2026$ the number of ways is $\boxed{2026!}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure · Digit Conditions and Search

For a positive integer $n$, let $s(n)$ denote the sum of the digits of $n$. For example, $s(12)=1+2=3$. Find all triples of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ such that \[s(a+b)=c,\qquad s(b+c)=a,\qquad s(c+a)=b.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Use cyclic symmetry to label the largest of the three entries as $a$. Compare the size of $b+c$ with the size of $a$.
Hint 2: key idea
A number is congruent to its digit sum modulo $9$. Rewrite the three conditions as congruences and combine them to restrict each variable. (LPS VI, Chapter 21: Digits, Place Value, and Decimal Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Bound the digit sum of $b+c$ in terms of the number of digits of $a$ to rule out large $a$. Use the resulting upper bound on $b+c$ to get a sharper digit-sum bound. Check the original equations on the few candidates allowed by that bound and the congruences.
Common trap
Matching the residues is only a necessary condition, so test the actual digit sums for the remaining candidates.
Show the answer

$(a,b,c)=(9,9,9)$

Show the solution – Remainders mod 9 and a size bound

Check. $s(9+9)=s(18)=9$, so $(9,9,9)$ works.

Divisibility by $9$. A number and its digit sum have the same remainder modulo $9$. Hence \[c\equiv a+b,\qquad a\equiv b+c,\qquad b\equiv c+a\pmod 9 .\] Adding, $a+b+c\equiv 2(a+b+c)$, so $a+b+c\equiv0$. Then $c\equiv a+b\equiv -c$, so $9\mid 2c$ and $9\mid c$; in the same way $9\mid a$ and $9\mid b$.

Size. By symmetry of the system under cyclic shifts we may assume $a\ge b$ and $a\ge c$ (otherwise rename). If $a$ has $d\ge3$ digits, then $b+c\le 2a<2\cdot10^d$ has at most $d+1$ digits, so $a=s(b+c)\le9(d+1)<10^{d-1}\le a$, a contradiction. So $a\le99$ and $b+c\le198$. Every number up to $198$ has digit sum at most $18$, hence $a=s(b+c)\le18$, and $a,b,c\in\{9,18\}$.

Cases. If one of the numbers equals $18$, say $a=18$ after a cyclic shift, then $b+c\in\{18,27,36\}$, whose digit sums are all $9\ne18$. So $a=b=c=9$, and the only triple is $\boxed{(9,9,9)}$.

Problem 5

EAlgebra · LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value · Comparative Constraints

A warrior, with the help of a village, will attack a dragon for $2026$ days. Each day exactly one of the following actions is performed:

  • Attack: Each warrior deals $1$ point of damage to the dragon.

  • Train: Exactly one village girl trains and becomes a warrior. No warrior attacks on that day.

The total damage is the sum of the damage dealt throughout the $2026$ days. What is the maximum amount of total damage that the dragon can receive?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Compare two short schedules with the same number of training days but in different positions. Track the number of warriors available on each attack day.
Hint 2: key idea
Fix the number of training days and bound the damage on any one attack day using all those training days. Then ask whether some schedule reaches that bound on every attack day at once. (LPS VI, Chapter 45: Inequalities and Absolute Value)
Hint 3: approach
Express the bound as the product of the number of attack days and the largest army size available. Note that the two factors have a fixed sum and compare products as the factors become closer. Check how the integer restriction affects the choice of training days.
Common trap
Count the original warrior in the army size, and count damage only on attack days.
Show the answer

$1013\cdot1014=1027182$

Show solution 1 – Train first, then attack

Suppose there are $t$ training days and $2026-t$ attack days. On any attack day the number of warriors is $1$ plus the number of earlier training days, which is at most $1+t$. So the total damage is at most $(1+t)(2026-t)$.

The two factors are positive integers with sum $2027$, so their product is largest when they are as close as possible, namely $1013$ and $1014$: indeed $(1+t)(2026-t)=\bigl(\frac{2027}2\bigr)^2-\bigl(t-\frac{2025}2\bigr)^2$, which is maximal for $t=1012$ or $t=1013$. Thus the damage is at most $1013\cdot1014$.

This is achieved by training on the first $1013$ days and attacking on the remaining $1013$ days with $1014$ warriors. The maximum is $\boxed{1013\cdot1014=1027182}$.

Show solution 2 – Move the training days to the front

An exchange. Suppose that in some schedule an attack day is immediately followed by a training day, and let $w$ be the number of warriors on the attack day. Swap the two days. Now the training comes first, so the attack is made by $w+1$ warriors and deals $w+1$ instead of $w$ damage; after the two days the number of warriors is the same as before, so all later days are unchanged. Thus the swap increases the total damage by $1$.

Each swap moves a training day one place earlier, so after finitely many swaps no attack day is followed by a training day: all training days come first. Hence for every schedule with $t$ training days there is a schedule with all $t$ training days first and at least the same damage.

The best such schedule. With $t$ training days first, each of the remaining $2026-t$ days is an attack by $t+1$ warriors, giving $(t+1)(2026-t)$ damage. Put $u=t+1$, so $1\le u\le 2027$ and the damage is \[u(2027-u)=\Bigl(\frac{2027}{2}\Bigr)^2-\Bigl(u-\frac{2027}{2}\Bigr)^2 .\] This is largest for the integers closest to $\frac{2027}2$, namely $u=1013$ and $u=1014$, both giving $1013\cdot1014$. So the maximum total damage is $\boxed{1013\cdot1014=1027182}$, reached by training on the first $1012$ or $1013$ days.

Problem 6

EGeometry · LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers · Medians and Centroid

Let $ABC$ be a triangle and let $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ be points on the rays $BC$ (with origin $B$), $CA$ (with origin $C$) and $AB$ (with origin $A$), respectively, such that $BC=CX$, $CA=AY$ and $AB=BZ$. Prove that the medians of triangle $ABC$ and the medians of triangle $XYZ$ all pass through the same point.

Note: A ray is a line that starts at a fixed point (called its origin) and extends indefinitely in one direction.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw $X,Y,Z$ beyond the second named point on their respective rays. Mark the midpoint of each segment formed by the ray's starting point and the new point.
Hint 2: key idea
Use position vectors to express each new vertex using the midpoint relation. The common point of the medians of a triangle is given by the average of its three vertex vectors. (LPS VI, Chapter 16: Special Segments and Triangle Centers)
Hint 3: approach
Solve the three midpoint equations for the vectors of $X,Y,Z$. Collect the coefficients of the vectors of $A,B,C$ in the sum for the new triangle's centroid, then compare with the corresponding coefficients for the original centroid.
Common trap
The intended construction uses the new point beyond the second named point on each ray, since the ray's starting point also satisfies the distance condition.
Show solution 1 – Position vectors

Use position vectors. The point $X$ lies on the ray $BC$ with $CX=BC$ and $X\ne B$, so $C$ is the midpoint of $BX$: $X=2C-B$. Similarly $A$ is the midpoint of $CY$ and $B$ is the midpoint of $AZ$: \[X=2C-B,\qquad Y=2A-C,\qquad Z=2B-A .\] The medians of a triangle meet at its centroid, the point $\frac13(\text{sum of the vertices})$. For triangle $XYZ$, \[\frac{X+Y+Z}3=\frac{(2C-B)+(2A-C)+(2B-A)}3=\frac{A+B+C}3,\] which is the centroid $G$ of triangle $ABC$. Hence all six medians pass through $G$.

Show solution 2 – Symmetry after an affine map

Reduction. An affine transformation of the plane (for example a suitable stretch followed by a shear) can map any triangle onto an equilateral triangle. Affine maps send lines to lines and preserve ratios of segments on a line; in particular they preserve midpoints, medians and centroids. The points $X$, $Y$, $Z$ are defined by midpoint conditions ($C$ is the midpoint of $BX$ with $X\ne B$, $A$ is the midpoint of $CY$, $B$ is the midpoint of $AZ$), so the map sends them to the corresponding points of the image triangle. Hence it suffices to prove the statement when $ABC$ is equilateral.

Equilateral case. Let $O$ be the center of the equilateral triangle $ABC$ and let $\rho$ be the rotation about $O$ through $120^\circ$ with $\rho(A)=B$, $\rho(B)=C$, $\rho(C)=A$. Rotations preserve midpoints, so $\rho$ maps the configuration “$C$ is the midpoint of $BX$” to “$A$ is the midpoint of $C\rho(X)$”, i.e. $\rho(X)=Y$; in the same way $\rho(Y)=Z$ and $\rho(Z)=X$. So $\rho$ maps triangle $XYZ$ onto itself, and therefore fixes its centroid. The only fixed point of $\rho$ is $O$, so the centroid of $XYZ$ is $O$, which is also the centroid of $ABC$. Thus all six medians pass through $O$, and by the reduction the same holds for every triangle.

Problem 7

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · AM-GM and Mean Inequalities

Let $x$, $y$, $z$ be positive real numbers such that $xy+yz+zx=3$. Prove that \[\frac{x^2+y^2}{z}+\frac{y^2+z^2}{x}+\frac{z^2+x^2}{y}\ge 6.\]

Hint 1: understand the problem
Look at one numerator and compare the sum of its squares with a product of the same two variables. Keep the cyclic order of all three fractions visible.
Hint 2: key idea
Replace each sum of squares by a lower bound involving the product. Then apply AM-GM to pairs of the resulting quotients, choosing the pairs so their product is the square of one variable. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Write the three paired estimates and track how often each quotient appears when they are added. Compare $(x+y+z)^2$ with a multiple of $xy+yz+zx$ by using the sum of the individual squares. Use this to prepare the lower bound on the sum of the variables.
Common trap
Each quotient appears in two of the paired estimates.
Show solution 1 – AM-GM twice

Denote the left-hand side by $S$. Since $x^2+y^2\ge2xy$, \[S\ge2\Bigl(\frac{xy}z+\frac{yz}x+\frac{zx}y\Bigr).\] By AM-GM, $\frac{xy}z+\frac{yz}x\ge2\sqrt{y^2}=2y$, and similarly $\frac{yz}x+\frac{zx}y\ge2z$, $\frac{zx}y+\frac{xy}z\ge2x$. Adding these three inequalities, \[\frac{xy}z+\frac{yz}x+\frac{zx}y\ge x+y+z .\] Finally $(x+y+z)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2+2(xy+yz+zx)\ge3(xy+yz+zx)=9$, because $x^2+y^2+z^2\ge xy+yz+zx$. So $x+y+z\ge3$ and \[S\ge2(x+y+z)\ge6 .\] Equality holds for $x=y=z=1$.

Show solution 2 – Symmetric functions

Let $p=x+y+z$ and $r=xyz$. Adding $z$ to $\frac{x^2+y^2}z$ gives $\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}z$, so \[S=\frac{x^2+y^2}z+\frac{y^2+z^2}x+\frac{z^2+x^2}y=(x^2+y^2+z^2)\Bigl(\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z\Bigr)-p .\] Here $x^2+y^2+z^2=p^2-2(xy+yz+zx)=p^2-6$ and $\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z=\frac{xy+yz+zx}{xyz}=\frac3r$.

Two bounds. From $p^2=x^2+y^2+z^2+6\ge(xy+yz+zx)+6=9$ we get $p\ge3$. By AM-GM, $3=xy+yz+zx\ge3\sqrt[3]{x^2y^2z^2}$, so $r\le1$.

Since $p^2-6\ge3>0$ and $\frac3r\ge3$, \[S\ge3(p^2-6)-p,\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3(p^2-6)-p-6=3p^2-p-24=(3p+8)(p-3)\ge0 .\] Hence $S\ge6$, with equality for $x=y=z=1$.

Problem 8

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive odd integer. A mirror maze is an $n\times n$ board of cells with glass walls, where each cell contains a double-sided mirror placed along one of the two possible diagonals. Given a mirror maze, we shine a laser at one of its outer walls, and the laser enters the maze horizontally or vertically. Whenever the laser hits a mirror, it always strikes its midpoint and is reflected by $90^\circ$ according to the orientation of the mirror. We say that the maze is perfect if, regardless of the outer wall at which the laser is aimed, the laser travels through an even number of cells before leaving the maze (if the laser visits the same cell several times, that cell is counted several times in the path). Determine the number of ways the mirrors can be arranged to form a perfect maze.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Trace a ray on a small odd board. Record its entry step, its reflections, and its exit step, and see what reversing the direction does to the path.
Hint 2: key idea
A ray alternates horizontal and vertical steps. For a ray entering from the left or right and visiting an even number of cells, use the final step direction to locate the exit walls, then use the column change to restrict the parity of the horizontal step count. (LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination)
Hint 3: approach
Count horizontal and vertical steps separately, including the moves between the exterior and the board. Infer how the row parity changes from entry to exit. Use reversibility to pair the left and right openings and compare the available openings of each row parity.
Common trap
A ray visiting $m$ cells makes $m+1$ steps if the entry and exit steps are both counted.
Show the answer

$0$

Show solution 1 – Two parity counts

Number the rows and columns $1,\dots,n$, and call an opening one of the $4n$ unit segments of the boundary through which the laser can enter.

The laser always leaves, and paths pair the openings. The motion is reversible: a two-sided mirror sends a ray back along its path if the direction is reversed. Hence the state (cell, direction) at each step determines the previous state, so a ray coming from outside can never enter a cycle; as there are finitely many states, it leaves the board. Running a path backwards, the laser entering through the exit opening leaves through the entry opening.

Moves alternate. In every cell the laser turns through $90^\circ$, so its steps alternate between horizontal and vertical. Suppose it enters through the left or right wall (first step horizontal) and passes through $m$ cells. It makes $m+1$ steps (one to enter, $m-1$ between cells, one to leave), and step $j$ is horizontal exactly when $j$ is odd.

If $m$ is even. Then the last step $m+1$ is horizontal, so the laser leaves through the left or right wall; there are $\frac{m+2}2$ horizontal and $\frac m2$ vertical steps. Count positions with the outside columns numbered $0$ and $n+1$. The column changes from $0$ or $n+1$ to $0$ or $n+1$, a change of $0$ or $\pm(n+1)$, which is even because $n$ is odd. Each horizontal step changes the column by $\pm1$, so $\frac{m+2}2$ is even, hence $\frac m2$ is odd. The row changes by $\pm1$ at each of the $\frac m2$ vertical steps, so the row changes by an odd amount: the entry row and the exit row have different parity.

No maze is perfect. In a perfect maze every path starting at a left or right opening has $m$ even, so the $2n$ left and right openings are split into pairs (entry, exit), each pair consisting of one opening in an odd row and one in an even row. Then there would be as many odd-row openings as even-row openings on these two walls. But there are $\frac{n+1}2$ odd rows and $\frac{n-1}2$ even rows, giving $n+1$ odd-row openings and only $n-1$ even-row openings. This contradiction shows that no placement of the mirrors is perfect: the answer is $\boxed{0}$.

Show solution 2 – A left-right path and a top-bottom path would cross

Call an opening one of the $4n$ unit segments of the boundary through which the laser can enter; a laser entering through the left or right wall moves horizontally, one entering through the top or bottom wall moves vertically.

The laser always leaves, and the paths pair up the openings. A double-sided mirror sends a ray back along its path if the direction is reversed, so the motion is reversible: the state (cell, direction) at each step determines the previous state. Hence a ray coming from outside never enters a cycle, and since there are finitely many states, it leaves the board. Running a path backwards, the laser entering through the exit opening leaves through the entry opening. So the paths split the $4n$ openings into $2n$ pairs.

Drawing the paths. The mirror of a cell cuts it into two right triangles, each containing two sides of the cell. A laser entering the cell through the midpoint of a side leaves through the midpoint of the other cell side in the same triangle. In each triangle draw the segment joining the midpoints of its two cell sides; it is parallel to the mirror and stays inside that triangle, so the two segments of a cell are disjoint. A laser path is then drawn as the polygonal line made of the segments it traverses. Knowing one traversed segment (and the direction) determines the whole path, forwards and backwards, so two different paths use no common segment. Two segments of different cells can meet only at the midpoint of a common side, and the two segments through such a midpoint are traversed consecutively by the same path. Therefore the polygonal lines of two different paths are disjoint. Each polygonal line runs inside the board and ends at the midpoints of its two openings.

Parity. In every cell the laser turns through $90^\circ$. So if a path passes through an even number of cells, it leaves moving parallel to its entering direction: a path entering through the left wall leaves through the left wall or the right wall, and a path entering through the top wall leaves through the top or the bottom wall.

No maze is perfect. Suppose the maze is perfect. The $n$ openings of the left wall are paired with openings of the left or the right wall. If all of them were paired among themselves, $n$ would be even; since $n$ is odd, some path joins the left wall to the right wall. In the same way some path joins the top wall to the bottom wall. These are two different paths, so their polygonal lines are disjoint. But a curve inside a square joining its left side to its right side and a curve joining its top side to its bottom side always meet: this is a well-known consequence of the Jordan curve theorem (the first curve separates the top side from the bottom side). This contradiction shows that no placement of the mirrors is perfect, and the answer is $\boxed{0}$.
