Problem 1

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Let $a,b,c$ be distinct positive integers such that $ab+c=c^2$. Prove that $(a-b)^2\ge 4c$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The sum $a+b$ is at least $2c$)

The condition is $ab=c^2-c$. Put $s=a+b$. Then \[(a-b)^2=s^2-4ab=s^2-4c^2+4c,\] so it suffices to show $s\ge2c$; then $(a-b)^2\ge4c^2-4c^2+4c=4c$.

Suppose instead $s\le2c-1$. If $s\le2c-2$, then (as $c\ge2$, because $ab=c(c-1)>0$) \[(a-b)^2=s^2-4c^2+4c\le(2c-2)^2-4c^2+4c=4-4c<0,\] which is impossible. So $s=2c-1$ and $(a-b)^2=(2c-1)^2-4c^2+4c=1$. Then $\{a,b\}=\{c,c-1\}$, so one of $a,b$ equals $c$, contradicting that $a,b,c$ are distinct. Hence $a+b\ge2c$ and $(a-b)^2\ge4c$.

Solution 2 (One number above $c$, one below)

We have $ab=c(c-1)$, and $c\ge2$. Without loss of generality $a>b$. If $b\ge c$, then $b\ge c+1$ (as $b\ne c$) and $ab>c^2>c(c-1)$; so $b\le c-1$. If $a\le c$, then $a\le c-1$ and $ab<(c-1)^2<c(c-1)$; so $a\ge c+1$.

Write $a=c+u$ and $b=c-v$ with integers $u,v\ge1$. Then \[ab=c^2+c(u-v)-uv=c^2-c\iff c\,(u-v+1)=uv .\] As $uv>0$ and $c>0$, the integer $k=u-v+1$ is positive, so $c=\dfrac{uv}{k}\le uv$. Therefore \[(a-b)^2=(u+v)^2\ge4uv\ge4c,\] by AM-GM. (Equality needs $u=v$ and $k=1$, i.e. $c=u^2$, $a=u^2+u$, $b=u^2-u$, e.g. $(a,b,c)=(6,2,4)$.)

Problem 2

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 18: Invariants and Monovariants · Invariant Quantities

There are $m$ foxes and $n$ rabbits sitting in $m+n$ seats around a circular table, where all animals are distinguishable. If a fox and a rabbit are sitting next to each other, they may swap positions with each other. For a fixed starting configuration, determine the number of configurations that can be reached by a sequence of swaps.

Note: Rotations and reflections of a configuration are considered distinct.

Show the answer

$\boxed{\dbinom{m+n}{m}\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}$ for $m,n\ge1$ (and $1$ if $m=0$ or $n=0$)

Show the solution

Solution (A rank invariant modulo $\gcd(m,n)$)

If $m=0$ or $n=0$ no swap is possible and the answer is $1$. Let $m,n\ge1$, $N=m+n$, $g=\gcd(m,n)$. Number the seats $0,1,\dots,N-1$ clockwise; the seats $N-1$ and $0$ are adjacent. Call the set of seats occupied by foxes the pattern.

The cyclic orders. A swap exchanges a fox and a rabbit, so reading only the foxes clockwise around the table gives the same cyclic sequence before and after; the same holds for the rabbits. Fix one fox $F$ and one rabbit $R$. Let $r_F$ be the number of foxes in seats with smaller numbers than $F$'s seat, and define $r_R$ in the same way. Because the cyclic order of the foxes never changes, the pattern together with $r_F$ determines the seat of every fox; similarly for rabbits. So a reachable configuration is determined by its pattern (at most $\binom Nm$ choices), $r_F\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}$ and $r_R\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$.

The invariant. We claim that $r_F+r_R \bmod g$ never changes. A swap of two seats other than the pair $\{N-1,0\}$ does not change which foxes have smaller seat numbers than $F$, so $r_F$ and $r_R$ stay. A swap across the pair $\{N-1,0\}$ moves one animal from seat $N-1$ to seat $0$ and the other from $0$ to $N-1$. If a fox moves from $N-1$ to $0$, it was the fox with the largest seat number and becomes the one with the smallest, so every fox's rank goes up by $1$ modulo $m$; in particular $r_F$ changes by $+1$ modulo $m$. At the same time a rabbit moves from $0$ to $N-1$, so $r_R$ changes by $-1$ modulo $n$. The other direction is the reverse. Since $g$ divides $m$ and $n$, $r_F+r_R$ is unchanged modulo $g$. (For $N=2$ we have $g=1$ and there is nothing to prove.)

Upper bound. For a fixed pattern, the pairs $(r_F\bmod m,\ r_R\bmod n)$ with a prescribed value of $r_F+r_R\bmod g$ number $mn/g$. Hence at most $\binom Nm\cdot\frac{mn}{g}=\binom{N}{m}\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ configurations are reachable.

All of them are reachable.

  • Any pattern can be turned into any other: adjacent fox-rabbit swaps act on the pattern as adjacent transpositions of the letters F and R along the row of seats $0,\dots,N-1$, and these sort any word into any other with the same letter counts.

  • Suppose the foxes occupy seats $0,\dots,m-1$ (a block). Let the fox in seat $m-1$ swap successively with the rabbits in seats $m,m+1,\dots,N-1$; it ends in seat $N-1$ and those rabbits each move back one seat, to $m-1,\dots,N-2$. Now let the rabbit in seat $m-1$ swap successively with the foxes in seats $m-2,m-3,\dots,0$ and then with the fox in seat $N-1$; it ends in seat $N-1$, and those foxes each move forward one seat. In the result the foxes again occupy seats $0,\dots,m-1$, every fox stands in the seat of the next fox clockwise, and every rabbit stands in the seat of the previous rabbit. So $(r_F,r_R)$ has changed by $(+1,-1)$ (modulo $m$ and $n$) and the pattern is the same.

From the start, first reach a block configuration, with some pair $(r_F,r_R)=(x,y)$. Repeating the operation gives $(x+k,\,y-k)$ for every $k$; these are $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ different pairs, namely all pairs in the invariant class. Finally fix a sequence $\sigma$ of seat swaps turning the block pattern into a given pattern $A$. Applied to configurations with the block pattern, $\sigma$ is always legal (it only depends on the pattern) and it is injective (each swap is invertible). So for every pattern $A$ at least $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ configurations are reachable.

Therefore exactly $\boxed{\binom{m+n}{m}\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}$ configurations are reachable (and $1$ if $m=0$ or $n=0$).

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 45: Complex Numbers in Geometry · Complex Reformulation of Olympiad Geometry

Let $ABC$ be an acute scalene triangle with no angle equal to $60^{\circ}$. Let $\omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. Let $\triangle_B$ be the equilateral triangle with three vertices on $\omega$, one of which is $B$. Let $\ell_B$ be the line through the two vertices of $\triangle_B$ other than $B$. Let $\triangle_C$ and $\ell_C$ be defined analogously. Let $Y$ be the intersection of $AC$ and $\ell_B$, and let $Z$ be the intersection of $AB$ and $\ell_C$.

Suppose that the circumcircle of $AYZ$ intersects $\omega$ at $P\neq A$, $BC$ intersects $YZ$ at $D$, and $PA$ intersects $YZ$ at $E$. Prove that $PE=PD$.

Show the solution

Solution (Complex numbers for $OP\perp YZ$, then the Miquel point)

Take $\omega$ as the unit circle, with $A,B,C$ at $a,b,c$, and write $\sigma_1=a+b+c$, $\sigma_2=ab+bc+ca$. We assume the points named in the statement exist and are distinct (this is what the hypotheses guarantee); in particular $\sigma_1\ne0$ (the triangle is not equilateral) and $\sigma_2=abc\,\overline{\sigma_1}\ne0$.

The lines. The vertices of $\triangle_B$ are $b,\,b\varepsilon,\,b\varepsilon^2$ with $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/3}$, so $\ell_B$ is the line perpendicular to $OB$ at the point $-b/2$: its equation is $z\bar b+\bar z b=-1$, i.e. $z+b^2\bar z=-b$. The chord $AC$ has equation $z+ac\,\bar z=a+c$. Subtracting, $(b^2-ac)\bar z=-\sigma_1$ at $Y$; conjugating ($\bar a=1/a$ etc.) gives \[y=\frac{b\,\sigma_2}{b^2-ac},\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad z_0=\frac{c\,\sigma_2}{c^2-ab}\] for $Z$ (here $b^2\ne ac$ and $c^2\ne ab$, since $\ell_B\not\parallel AC$ and $\ell_C\not\parallel AB$). A short computation gives \[y-z_0=\frac{\sigma_2^2\,(c-b)}{(b^2-ac)(c^2-ab)} .\]

The point $P$. Let $p=\sigma_2/\sigma_1$. Since $\overline{\sigma_1}=\sigma_2/(abc)$ and $\overline{\sigma_2}=\sigma_1/(abc)$, we get $\bar p=\sigma_1/\sigma_2=1/p$, so $p$ lies on $\omega$. Next, \[p-y=-\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1(b^2-ac)},\quad p-z_0=-\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1(c^2-ab)},\quad a-y=-\frac{c(a^2+ab+b^2)}{b^2-ac},\quad a-z_0=-\frac{b(a^2+ac+c^2)}{c^2-ab},\] so the cross-ratio \[\frac{(p-y)(a-z_0)}{(p-z_0)(a-y)}=\frac{b\,(a^2+ac+c^2)}{c\,(a^2+ab+b^2)}\] equals its own conjugate (replace $a,b,c$ by their inverses and simplify), hence is real. Thus $p$ lies on the circle $AYZ$. If $p\ne a$, then $p$ is the point $P$ of the statement; if $p=a$, the circle $AYZ$ would be tangent to $\omega$ at $A$, contrary to the existence of $P$. So $P=p$.

Key fact: $OP\perp YZ$. We have \[\frac{p}{y-z_0}=\frac{(b^2-ac)(c^2-ab)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2\,(c-b)},\] and conjugating (using $\overline{\sigma_1\sigma_2}=\sigma_1\sigma_2/(abc)^2$) gives exactly the negative of this number. So $p/(y-z_0)$ is purely imaginary: $OP\perp YZ$, i.e. the tangent $t$ to $\omega$ at $P$ is parallel to $YZ$.

Finishing with directed angles (modulo $180^\circ$). The four lines $AB$, $AC$, $BC$, $YZ$ form a complete quadrilateral; its Miquel point lies on the circles $ABC$, $AZY$, $BZD$, $CYD$. The first two circles meet only at $A$ and $P$, and the Miquel point is not $A$ (otherwise the circle $BZD$ would contain the three collinear points $A,B,Z$). Hence $P$ lies on the circle $BZD$. Then \[\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(DP,DZ)=\angle(BP,BZ)=\angle(BP,BA).\] On the other hand, $E$ lies on $PA$ and on $YZ\parallel t$, so by the tangent-chord angle, \[\angle(ED,EP)=\angle(t,PA)=\angle(BP,BA).\] Thus $\angle(DP,DE)=\angle(ED,EP)$: the angles of triangle $PDE$ at $D$ and at $E$ are equal, and $PE=PD$.

Problem 4

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 3: Foundational Angle and Configuration Chasing · Cyclic Quadrilateral Recognition

Triangle $\triangle ABC$ has circumcircle $\omega$ and circumcenter $O$. Lines $AO$ and $BC$ meet at point $D$. Let $X$ be the $A$-excenter of $\triangle ABD$ and let $Y$ be the $A$-excenter of $\triangle ACD$. Prove that if $X$ lies on $\omega$, then $Y$ also lies on $\omega$.

Show the answer

proof ($X\in\omega$ happens exactly when $\angle BAC=90^\circ$)

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 ($X$ on $\omega$ forces a right angle at $A$)

We assume the triangles $ABD$ and $ACD$ exist, i.e. $D$ exists and $D\ne B,C$; this means $\angle B\ne90^\circ$, $\angle C\ne90^\circ$. Write $\gamma=\min(\angle C,180^\circ-\angle C)$. Then the central angle is $\angle AOB=2\gamma$, and for a point $Q\ne A,B$ of $\omega$, $\angle AQB=\gamma$ if $Q$ and $O$ are on the same side of $AB$ and $\angle AQB=180^\circ-\gamma>90^\circ$ otherwise.

A fact about excenters. In triangle $ABD$ the excenter $X$ opposite $A$ lies on the internal bisector of $\angle BAD$ and on the external bisector at $B$, so $\angle BAX=\frac12\angle BAD$ and $\angle ABX=\angle ABD+\frac12(180^\circ-\angle ABD)=90^\circ+\frac12\angle ABD$. Hence \[\angle AXB=180^\circ-\tfrac12\angle BAD-90^\circ-\tfrac12\angle ABD=\tfrac12\angle ADB<90^\circ .\] Also $X$ lies inside the angle $BAD$, so $X$ and $D$ are on the same side of line $AB$.

If $X\in\omega$ then $\angle BAC=90^\circ$. Since $\angle AXB<90^\circ$, the first paragraph gives $\angle AXB=\gamma$ and $X$, $O$ on the same side of $AB$. So $D$ and $O$ are on the same side of $AB$; as both lie on line $AO$, which meets $AB$ only at $A$, the points $D$ and $O$ lie on the same ray from $A$. Moreover $\angle ADB=2\angle AXB=2\gamma=\angle AOB$. If $D\ne O$, consider triangle $BDO$ (note $B\notin AO$, since otherwise $AB$ would be a diameter and $\angle C=90^\circ$). If $O$ lies between $A$ and $D$, then $\angle AOB$ is an exterior angle of triangle $BOD$ at $O$, so $\angle AOB>\angle ODB=\angle ADB$; if $D$ lies between $A$ and $O$, then $\angle ADB$ is an exterior angle at $D$, so $\angle ADB>\angle DOB=\angle AOB$. Both contradict $\angle ADB=\angle AOB$. Hence $D=O$, so $O$ lies on $BC$, $BC$ is a diameter and $\angle BAC=90^\circ$.

If $\angle BAC=90^\circ$ then $Y\in\omega$. Now $O=D$ is the midpoint of $BC$, the angles $B$, $C$ are acute, and $OA=OC$. By the excenter fact for triangle $ACD$, $\angle AYC=\frac12\angle ADC=\frac12\angle AOC=\angle ABC$. The point $Y$ lies inside the angle $CAD$, so $Y$ is on the same side of $AC$ as $D=O$, which is the midpoint of $BC$ and hence on the same side as $B$. So $B$ and $Y$ see the segment $AC$ under equal angles from the same side, and $A,Y,B,C$ are concyclic: $Y\in\omega$.

Combining the two parts: if $X$ lies on $\omega$, then $\angle BAC=90^\circ$, and then $Y$ lies on $\omega$.

Solution 2 (Lengths along the bisector and the power of $D$)

Let $R$ be the radius of $\omega$ and let $A'$ be the point of $\omega$ diametrically opposite $A$. We assume $D$ exists and $D\ne B,C$, so that the triangles $ABD$, $ACD$ exist; then $\angle B,\angle C\ne90^\circ$, so neither $B$ nor $C$ is $A'$, and $B,C$ are not on line $AO$.

Two facts.

  • Chords from $A$. If $Q\ne A$ is on $\omega$, then $\angle AQA'=90^\circ$ (or $Q=A'$), so $\angle QAA'<90^\circ$ and $AQ=2R\cos\angle QAA'$. Conversely, a ray from $A$ making an angle $\alpha<90^\circ$ with ray $AO$ meets $\omega$ again at exactly one point, at distance $2R\cos\alpha$ from $A$; a ray making an angle $\alpha\ge90^\circ$ with ray $AO$ does not meet $\omega$ again.

  • The excenter distance. In a triangle $APQ$ with $\angle PAQ=\beta$ and semiperimeter $p$, the $A$-excenter $J$ satisfies $AJ=p/\cos(\beta/2)$. Indeed, the excircle touches the lines $AP$, $AQ$, $PQ$ at $T_1$ (beyond $P$), $T_2$ (beyond $Q$) and $T$ (on segment $PQ$). Equal tangents give $AT_1=AT_2$, $PT_1=PT$, $QT_2=QT$, so $AT_1+AT_2=AP+PQ+QA=2p$ and $AT_1=p$. The triangle $AT_1J$ has a right angle at $T_1$ and the angle $\beta/2$ at $A$, so $AJ=AT_1/\cos(\beta/2)$.

Step 1: $D$ lies on ray $AO$. Put $\theta=\angle BAO$; by the first fact (with $Q=B$) $\theta<90^\circ$, and $\theta>0$ since $B\notin AO$. If $D$ were on the ray opposite to $AO$, then $\angle BAD=180^\circ-\theta$, and the bisector of $\angle BAD$ would make the angle $\theta+\frac12(180^\circ-\theta)=90^\circ+\frac{\theta}{2}>90^\circ$ with ray $AO$. The excenter $X$ lies on this bisector and $X\ne A$, so by the first fact $X\notin\omega$, a contradiction. Hence $D$ is on ray $AO$, $\angle BAD=\theta$, and the bisector of $\angle BAD$ makes the angle $\theta/2$ with ray $AO$.

Step 2: $AD+BD=2R$. Write $d=AD$. By the first fact, $AB=2R\cos\theta$, and the point $X$, which is on $\omega$ and on the bisector ray, satisfies $AX=2R\cos\frac{\theta}{2}$. By the second fact applied to triangle $ABD$, \[\frac{AB+BD+d}{2\cos(\theta/2)}=AX=2R\cos\frac{\theta}{2},\qquad\text{so}\qquad AB+BD+d=4R\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}=2R(1+\cos\theta)=2R+AB .\] Hence $d+BD=2R$.

Step 3: $D=O$. From Step 2, $0<d<2R$, so $D$ lies strictly between $A$ and $A'$, inside the disk of $\omega$. A point of line $BC$ inside the disk lies on the chord $BC$, so $D$ is between $B$ and $C$. The power of $D$ with respect to $\omega$, computed along the chords $BC$ and $AA'$, gives \[BD\cdot DC=AD\cdot DA'=d(2R-d)=d\cdot BD,\] so $DC=d=DA$. Thus $D$ is equidistant from $A$ and $C$, and so is $O$: both lie on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$. If $D\ne O$, this perpendicular bisector would be the line $DO=AO$, which passes through $A$; but $A$ is not equidistant from $A$ and $C$. Hence $D=O$, and $AD=CD=R$.

Step 4: $Y\in\omega$. Put $\varphi=\angle CAO=\angle CAD$ (now $D=O$ is on ray $AO$); as in Step 1, $0<\varphi<90^\circ$, and $AC=2R\cos\varphi$. The excenter $Y$ lies on the bisector of $\angle CAD$, which makes the angle $\varphi/2<90^\circ$ with ray $AO$; this ray meets $\omega$ again at the point $Y_0$ with $AY_0=2R\cos\frac{\varphi}{2}$. By the second fact applied to triangle $ACD$, \[AY=\frac{AC+CD+AD}{2\cos(\varphi/2)}=\frac{2R\cos\varphi+2R}{2\cos(\varphi/2)}=\frac{4R\cos^2(\varphi/2)}{2\cos(\varphi/2)}=2R\cos\frac{\varphi}{2}=AY_0 .\] The points $Y$ and $Y_0$ lie on the same ray from $A$ at the same distance from $A$, so $Y=Y_0$, and $Y$ lies on $\omega$.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

Emily has a red sheet of paper. She draws $2026$ circles (not necessarily of equal size) on the piece of paper. She chooses a circle to color black, then cuts the paper around the circumference of all $2026$ circles. She then separates the pieces of paper, into at least $2$ black pieces and some number of red pieces. Is it possible that all black pieces are congruent?

Show the answer

No

Show the solution

Solution (A piece inside the most circles)

The answer is no. Let $\Gamma$ be the black circle; the black region is its open disk, and the pieces are the connected components left after cutting along all circles. Each piece misses every circle, so it lies entirely inside or entirely outside each circle; let $d(Q)$ be the number of circles whose inside contains the piece $Q$.

Call a boundary point $x$ of a piece $Q$ simple if $x$ lies on exactly one circle $c$. Near a simple point the circle $c$ is the only cut, so $Q$ fills one side of $c$ near $x$: we say $Q$ is inside $c$ at $x$ or outside $c$ at $x$.

Step 1: a black piece $Q_0$ with the largest $d$ is inside the circle at each of its simple boundary points. Suppose $Q_0$ is outside $c$ at a simple point $x$. Then $c\ne\Gamma$ (black pieces lie inside $\Gamma$), so $x\notin\Gamma$ and $x$ is in the open black disk. A small disk around $x$ meets no circle other than $c$; its half inside $c$ belongs to a black piece $Q_1$, which is on the same side as $Q_0$ of every circle except $c$, and inside $c$. So $d(Q_1)=d(Q_0)+1$, contradicting the choice of $Q_0$.

Step 2: some black piece $Q$ is outside a circle along a whole arc of simple points. (Circles drawn twice give the same cut, so we treat the drawn circles as distinct.) There are at least two black pieces, so some circle $c\ne\Gamma$ passes through the open black disk; it meets each other circle in at most two points, so we can pick $x_0\in c$ inside the black disk lying on no other circle, and then an open arc $\alpha\ni x_0$ of $c$ that stays in the open black disk and meets no other circle. Near $\alpha$ the only cut is $c$, so the region just outside $c$ along $\alpha$ is connected and uncut: it lies in one black piece $Q$, and every point of $\alpha$ is a simple boundary point of $Q$ at which $Q$ is outside $c$.

Step 3: contradiction. Suppose all black pieces are congruent, and let $\varphi$ be an isometry with $\varphi(Q_0)=Q$. For $x\in\alpha$ the point $y=\varphi^{-1}(x)$ is a boundary point of $Q_0$, so it lies on at least one drawn circle. The circle $\varphi^{-1}(c)$ either is a drawn circle or meets each drawn circle in at most two points; in both cases only finitely many of its points lie on two or more drawn circles. So we can choose $x\in\alpha$ such that $y=\varphi^{-1}(x)$ lies on exactly one drawn circle $c'$, i.e. $y$ is a simple boundary point of $Q_0$. Near $x$ the boundary of $Q$ is an arc of $c$ with $Q$ on the outside; applying $\varphi^{-1}$, near $y$ the boundary of $Q_0$ is an arc of $\varphi^{-1}(c)$ with $Q_0$ outside it. Near $y$ the only cut is $c'$, so this arc lies on $c'$, and a circle sharing an arc with $\varphi^{-1}(c)$ equals it: $c'=\varphi^{-1}(c)$. So $Q_0$ is outside the drawn circle $c'$ at the simple point $y$, contradicting Step 1.

Hence the black pieces cannot all be congruent.
