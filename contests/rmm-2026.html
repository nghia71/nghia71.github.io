Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 39: Graph Theory and Structural Combinatorics

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Alice draws a unit area triangle on the board. Then she draws additional triangles by performing $n$ moves in a row. On each move, she chooses a drawn triangle $\Delta$ with no marked points in its interior, marks a point $P$ in its interior, and draws three smaller triangles by joining $P$ to each vertex of $\Delta$ with a segment.

Once these $n$ moves have been performed, Bob chooses three distinct drawn triangles $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ which contain no marked points in their interiors, such that $\Delta_2$ shares one side with $\Delta_1$ and another with $\Delta_3$. In terms of $n$, determine the largest possible constant $c$ such that Bob can guarantee that the sum of the areas of $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_3$ is at least $c$, regardless of Alice's choices.

Show the answer

$c=\dfrac{3}{2n+1}$

Show the solution

Solution (A closed tour through all the triangles)

Call a drawn triangle small if it has no marked point in its interior. Each move replaces one small triangle by three, so after $n$ moves there are $2n+1$ small triangles; they tile the original triangle, and any two of them that share more than a vertex share a whole side (a new segment from $P$ to a vertex of $\Delta$ is a full side of exactly two of the three new triangles, and the sides of $\Delta$ stay sides). We show that the answer is $c=\dfrac3{2n+1}$.

Alice can keep Bob at $\frac3{2n+1}$. Alice makes all small triangles have area $\frac1{2n+1}$. In her first move she chooses $P$ so that two of the three triangles have area $\frac1{2n+1}$ each; the third has area $\frac{2n-1}{2n+1}$. In every later move she subdivides this “large” triangle, of current area $\frac{2k+1}{2n+1}$ $(k\ge1)$, by a point $P$ such that two of the three parts have area $\frac1{2n+1}$ and the third has area $\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}>0$ (a point with prescribed positive area coordinates exists in any triangle). After $n$ moves the large triangle has area $\frac1{2n+1}$ too. Then any three small triangles have total area exactly $\frac3{2n+1}$, so $c\le\frac3{2n+1}$.

Bob can always get $\frac3{2n+1}$.

Claim. After every move $(n\ge1)$ the small triangles can be arranged in a cyclic order $t_1,t_2,\dots,t_{2n+1}$ such that any two cyclically consecutive triangles share a side.

We use induction on the number of moves. After the first move the three triangles $PAB,PBC,PCA$ share sides in pairs. Suppose the claim holds, and Alice subdivides the small triangle $t=t_i$ by a point $P$. Its neighbours $t_{i-1}$ and $t_{i+1}$ in the cyclic order are different triangles (there are at least three), and they share with $t$ two different sides $s_1$ and $s_2$, because a side of $t$ belongs to at most one other small triangle. Let $t'$ be the new triangle with side $s_1$, let $t'''$ be the new triangle with side $s_2$, and let $t''$ be the third one. Any two of the three new triangles share a side (a segment from $P$ to a vertex of $t$). Hence \[\dots,\ t_{i-1},\ t',\ t'',\ t''',\ t_{i+1},\ \dots\] is a cyclic order of the required kind. (In the figure, $n=3$ and the dashed closed path visits the seven small triangles in such an order.)

Now consider the $2n+1$ triples $(t_{i-1},t_i,t_{i+1})$, indices modulo $2n+1$. Each is a legal choice for Bob: the three triangles are distinct because $2n+1\ge3$, and $t_i$ shares one side with $t_{i-1}$ and another side with $t_{i+1}$ (two different triangles cannot share the same side of $t_i$). Every small triangle lies in exactly three of these triples, so the areas of the $2n+1$ triples add up to $3$. Hence some triple has area at least $\frac3{2n+1}$, and Bob chooses it.

Answer. $\boxed{c=\dfrac{3}{2n+1}}$

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 33: Residues, Quadratic Structure, and Higher Congruences

Let $p\ge 11$ be a prime. Suppose that, if $a$ and $b$ are integers such that $1\le a<b\le p-3$, then $b!-a!$ is not divisible by $p$. Prove that $p-5$ is divisible by $8$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Wilson pairs and the product of all residues)

All congruences are modulo $p$. Put $h=\frac{p-1}2$; note $2\le h\le p-3$. The hypothesis says that $1!,2!,\dots,(p-3)!$ are pairwise distinct modulo $p$.

Step 1: a pairing. For $0\le k\le p-1$, \[k!\,(p-1-k)!\equiv(-1)^{k+1}.\tag{1}\] Indeed, by Wilson's theorem $-1\equiv(p-1)!=k!\prod_{i=1}^{p-1-k}(p-i)\equiv k!\,(-1)^{p-1-k}(p-1-k)!$, and $(-1)^{p-1-k}=(-1)^k$. In particular $(h!)^2\equiv(-1)^{h+1}$.

Step 2: $p\equiv1\pmod4$. Suppose $p\equiv3\pmod 4$. Then $h$ is odd and $(h!)^2\equiv1$, so $h!\equiv\pm1$. If $h!\equiv1$, then $h!\equiv1!$ with $1<h\le p-3$, a contradiction. If $h!\equiv-1$, then, since $2h\equiv-1$ gives $h^{-1}\equiv-2$, \[(h-1)!\equiv h!\cdot h^{-1}\equiv(-1)(-2)=2=2!,\] and $2<h-1=\frac{p-3}2\le p-3$ because $p\ge11$; again a contradiction. Hence $p\equiv1\pmod4$, $h$ is even, and \[(h!)^2\equiv-1 .\tag{2}\]

Step 3: the residue $-1$ is not a factorial. Suppose $k!\equiv-1$ with $1\le k\le p-3$; then $k\ge2$. By (1), $(p-1-k)!\equiv(-1)^{k}$, and $2\le p-1-k\le p-3$. If $k$ is even, $(p-1-k)!\equiv1=1!$, a contradiction. If $k$ is odd, $(p-1-k)!\equiv-1\equiv k!$ forces $p-1-k=k$, i.e. $k=h$, which is even; a contradiction.

Step 4: the product of the factorials. In the product $\prod_{k=2}^{p-3}k!$ pair $k$ with $p-1-k$; only $k=h$ is paired with itself. By (1) a pair of odd indices contributes $1$ and a pair of even indices contributes $-1$. There are $\frac{p-3}2$ even indices in $[2,p-3]$; without $h$ they form $\frac{p-5}4$ pairs. Therefore \[\prod_{k=1}^{p-3}k!\equiv(-1)^{\frac{p-5}4}\,h!\, .\tag{3}\]

Step 5: conclusion. The $p-3$ factorials are distinct non-zero residues, so exactly two non-zero residues are missing; by Step 3 one of them is $-1$; call the other $s$. The product of all non-zero residues is $(p-1)!\equiv-1$, so by (3) \[(-1)\cdot s\cdot(-1)^{\frac{p-5}4}h!\equiv-1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad s\equiv(-1)^{\frac{p-5}4}(h!)^{-1}\equiv(-1)^{\frac{p-1}4}\,h!,\] where we used $(h!)^{-1}\equiv-h!$ from (2). If $p\equiv1\pmod 8$, this gives $s\equiv h!$; but $h!$ is one of the factorials and $s$ is a missing residue. This is impossible, so $p\equiv5\pmod8$, i.e. $8\mid p-5$.

Solution 2 (Counting orbits of a four-element group)

All congruences are modulo $p$. Put $h=\frac{p-1}2$; the hypothesis says that $1!,2!,\dots,(p-3)!$ are pairwise distinct modulo $p$. We first prove three facts (the same as Steps 1–3 of Solution 1).

(a) A pairing. For $0\le k\le p-1$ we have $k!\,(p-1-k)!\equiv(-1)^{k+1}$. Indeed, by Wilson's theorem $-1\equiv(p-1)!=k!\prod_{i=1}^{p-1-k}(p-i)\equiv k!\,(-1)^{p-1-k}(p-1-k)!$, and $(-1)^{p-1-k}=(-1)^k$. For $k=h$ this gives $(h!)^2\equiv(-1)^{h+1}$.

(b) $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $(h!)^2\equiv-1$. If $p\equiv3\pmod4$, then $h$ is odd, $(h!)^2\equiv1$ and $h!\equiv\pm1$. Now $h!\equiv1=1!$ is impossible because $1<h\le p-3$. If $h!\equiv-1$, then $2h\equiv-1$ gives $h^{-1}\equiv-2$ and $(h-1)!\equiv h!\cdot h^{-1}\equiv2=2!$, impossible because $2<h-1\le p-3$ for $p\ge11$. So $p\equiv1\pmod4$, $h$ is even, and $(h!)^2\equiv-1$ by (a).

(c) The residue $-1$ is not one of $1!,\dots,(p-3)!$. Suppose $k!\equiv-1$ with $1\le k\le p-3$; then $k\ge2$, and by (a) $(p-1-k)!\equiv(-1)^k$ with $2\le p-1-k\le p-3$. For even $k$ this says $(p-1-k)!\equiv1=1!$; for odd $k$ it says $(p-1-k)!\equiv-1\equiv k!$, so $p-1-k=k$ and $k=h$, which is even. Both are impossible.

The orbit count. Let $\iota\equiv h!$; by (b) the solutions of $x^2\equiv-1$ are $\pm\iota$.

Let $E=\{k!:k\text{ even},\,2\le k\le p-3\}$ and $U=\{k!:k\text{ odd},\,3\le k\le p-4\}$ (sets of residues). By the hypothesis these have $\frac{p-3}2$ and $\frac{p-5}2$ elements, they are disjoint, neither contains $1=1!$, and by (c) neither contains $-1$. So the non-zero residues are \[\{1,-1\}\ \sqcup\ E\ \sqcup\ U\ \sqcup\ \{s\}\] for one more residue $s$. Consider the maps $\alpha(x)=x^{-1}$ and $\beta(x)=-x^{-1}$ on the non-zero residues; both are involutions. Since $k$ and $p-1-k$ have the same parity, the pairing (a) shows \[\alpha(U)=U,\qquad\beta(E)=E .\] $\alpha$ fixes $1$ and $-1$ and maps $U$ onto itself, hence it maps $E\cup\{s\}$ onto itself. $\beta$ exchanges $1$ and $-1$ and maps $E$ onto itself, hence it maps $U\cup\{s\}$ onto itself.

The residue $s$ equals $-\iota$. The set $U\cup\{s\}$ has $\frac{p-3}2$ elements, an odd number because $p\equiv1\pmod4$ by (b). An involution of a set with an odd number of elements has a fixed point, and the fixed points of $\beta$ are the solutions of $x^2\equiv-1$, namely $\iota$ and $-\iota$. As $\iota=h!\in E$, we get $-\iota\in U\cup\{s\}$. If $-\iota\equiv k!$ with $k$ odd, then $(p-1-k)!\equiv(k!)^{-1}=(-\iota)^{-1}=\iota\equiv h!$ by (a), so $p-1-k=h$ by the hypothesis, and $k=h$, which is even: impossible. Hence $s=-\iota$.

Counting. Let $G=E\cup\{-\iota\}$; it has $\frac{p-3}2+1=\frac{p-1}2$ elements. $G$ is mapped onto itself by $\alpha$ (shown above) and by $\beta$ (because $\beta(E)=E$ and $\beta(-\iota)=-\iota$), hence also by $\alpha\beta:x\mapsto-x$. So $G$ is a union of orbits of the group $\{x,\,-x,\,x^{-1},\,-x^{-1}\}$. An orbit has four elements unless $x=-x$ (never), $x=x^{-1}$ (only $x=\pm1$, not in $G$) or $x=-x^{-1}$ (only $x=\pm\iota$). Thus $G$ consists of the two-element orbit $\{\iota,-\iota\}$ and some four-element orbits: \[\frac{p-1}2\equiv2\pmod 4 ,\] which means $p\equiv5\pmod8$.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 50: Algebraic Modeling Beyond Standard Forms

Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a finite subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Prove that there exist three polynomials $P(x,y,z)$, $Q(x,y,z)$ and $R(x,y,z)$ with real coefficients, such that a triple of real numbers $(a,b,c)$ is in $\mathcal{S}$ if and only if the system of equations \[\begin{cases}P(x,y,z)=a\\ Q(x,y,z)=b\\ R(x,y,z)=c,\end{cases}\] does not have a solution in real numbers $x$, $y$, and $z$.

Show the answer

proof (explicit construction)

Show the solution

Solution (Points on a line first, then a change of coordinates)

We must find a polynomial map $\Phi=(P,Q,R):\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3$ whose image is exactly $\mathbb R^3\setminus\mathcal S$. If $\mathcal S=\emptyset$, take $(P,Q,R)=(x,y,z)$. Let $\mathcal S$ have $m\ge1$ points.

Step 1: the points $(a_1,0,0),\dots,(a_m,0,0)$. Let $a_1,\dots,a_m$ be distinct real numbers and $A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^m(t-a_i)$. Define \[F(x,y,z)=(x,\ u,\ v),\qquad u=yz-1+A(x)\,y-y^3z,\qquad v=y^2u-z .\] Claim: the image of $F$ is $\mathbb R^3\setminus\{(a_i,0,0):1\le i\le m\}$.

Fix a target $(t,\alpha,\beta)$. We need $x=t$ and real $y,z$ with $u=\alpha$, $v=\beta$. If $u=\alpha$, then $v=\beta$ means $z=\alpha y^2-\beta$. Substituting this $z$ into $u=\alpha$ gives \[y(\alpha y^2-\beta)-1+A(t)y-y^3(\alpha y^2-\beta)=\alpha,\] that is, \[G(y):=\alpha y^5-(\alpha+\beta)y^3+(\beta-A(t))\,y+(1+\alpha)=0 .\tag{1}\] Conversely, if $y$ is a real root of (1) and $z=\alpha y^2-\beta$, then $u=\alpha$ and $v=y^2\alpha-z=\beta$. So $(t,\alpha,\beta)$ is in the image if and only if (1) has a real root.

  • If $\alpha\ne0$, $G$ has degree $5$, so it has a real root.

  • If $\alpha=0$ and $\beta\ne0$, then $G(y)=-\beta y^3+(\beta-A(t))y+1$ has degree $3$, so it has a real root.

  • If $\alpha=\beta=0$, then $G(y)=1-A(t)y$, which has a real root if and only if $A(t)\ne0$.

Hence the only points not in the image are $(t,0,0)$ with $A(t)=0$, which proves the claim.

Step 2: moving $\mathcal S$ onto the $x$-axis. For two different points of $\mathcal S$ with difference $(d_1,d_2,d_3)\ne0$, the expression $d_1+\lambda d_2+\lambda^2d_3$ is a non-zero polynomial in $\lambda$ and has at most two roots. So there is a real $\lambda$ for which the numbers $x+\lambda y+\lambda^2z$, $(x,y,z)\in\mathcal S$, are pairwise distinct. The linear map \[\Lambda(x,y,z)=(x+\lambda y+\lambda^2z,\ y,\ z)\] is a bijection of $\mathbb R^3$ with polynomial inverse $\Lambda^{-1}(x,y,z)=(x-\lambda y-\lambda^2z,\,y,\,z)$, and $\Lambda(\mathcal S)=\{(a_i,b_i,c_i):1\le i\le m\}$ with distinct $a_i$. By Lagrange interpolation there are real polynomials $p,q$ with $p(a_i)=b_i$ and $q(a_i)=c_i$ for all $i$. The map \[\Psi(t,u,v)=(t,\ u+p(t),\ v+q(t))\] is a bijection of $\mathbb R^3$ (its inverse subtracts $p(t)$ and $q(t)$) and $\Psi(a_i,0,0)=(a_i,b_i,c_i)$. So the polynomial bijection $\Lambda^{-1}\circ\Psi$ maps $\{(a_i,0,0)\}$ onto $\mathcal S$.

Step 3: the answer. Let $\Phi=\Lambda^{-1}\circ\Psi\circ F$ with $F$ from Step 1 for these $a_1,\dots,a_m$. Its three components $P,Q,R$ are polynomials, and since $\Lambda^{-1}\circ\Psi$ is a bijection, \[\Phi(\mathbb R^3)=\Lambda^{-1}\Psi\bigl(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{(a_i,0,0)\}\bigr)=\mathbb R^3\setminus\mathcal S .\] Thus the system $P=a$, $Q=b$, $R=c$ has no real solution exactly when $(a,b,c)\in\mathcal S$.

Problem 4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure

For any positive integer $m$, let $\varphi(m)$ be the number of positive integers less than or equal to $m$ and coprime to $m$. Define $\varphi_0(m)=m$ and, for each positive integer $k$, $\varphi_k(m)=\varphi(\varphi_{k-1}(m))$. For any integer $n\ge 3$, prove that \[\varphi_0(2^n-3)\cdot\varphi_1(2^n-3)\cdot\varphi_2(2^n-3)\cdot\ldots\cdot\varphi_n(2^n-3)\] has at most $n$ distinct prime divisors.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting factors of two along the chain)

Let $m=2^n-3$ and $m_k=\varphi_k(m)$. Recall $\varphi(N)=\prod_{p^e\,\|\,N}p^{e-1}(p-1)$. Two consequences:

  • $\varphi(N)$ is even for $N\ge3$, and $\varphi(N)\le N/2$ for even $N$;

  • writing $v_2$ for the exponent of $2$ and $s(N)$ for the number of distinct odd primes dividing $N$, \[v_2(\varphi(N))\ \ge\ s(N)\ \ (N\text{ odd}),\qquad v_2(\varphi(N))\ \ge\ v_2(N)-1+s(N)\ \ (N\text{ even}),\tag{1}\] because each odd prime $p\mid N$ contributes the even factor $p-1$.

The chain reaches $1$ within $n$ steps. $m\ge5$ is odd, so $m_1\le m-1<2^n$ and $m_1$ is even. For $k\ge1$ the number $m_k$ is $1$ or even, and $m_{k+1}\le m_k/2$ when $m_k$ is even. Hence $m_k<2^{n+1-k}$ as long as $m_k>1$, so $m_n=1$. Let $K\le n$ be the smallest index with $m_K=1$; then $m_1,\dots,m_{K-1}$ are even.

Bookkeeping. Let $e_k=v_2(m_k)$ and $s_k=s(m_k)$. By (1), $e_1\ge s_0$ and $e_{k+1}\ge e_k-1+s_k$ for $1\le k\le K-1$. Adding these inequalities, \[0=e_K\ \ge\ s_0+s_1+\dots+s_{K-1}-(K-1),\qquad\text{so}\qquad s_0+s_1+\dots+s_{K-1}\le K-1\le n-1 .\] Every odd prime dividing the product $m_0m_1\cdots m_n$ divides some $m_k$ with $k\le K-1$ and is counted in $s_k$. So the product has at most $n-1$ distinct odd prime divisors, and, together with the prime $2$, at most $n$ distinct prime divisors.

Solution 2 (A logarithmic bound for the tree of primes)

For an odd integer $N\ge1$ let $\mathcal C(N)$ be the smallest set of odd primes that contains all prime divisors of $N$ and, with every prime $p$, all odd prime divisors of $p-1$. (So $\mathcal C(1)=\emptyset$.)

Step 1. Every odd prime dividing some $\varphi_k(m)$, $m=2^n-3$, lies in $\mathcal C(m)$. This holds for $k=0$. Since $\varphi(N)=\prod_{p^e\|N}p^{e-1}(p-1)$, an odd prime dividing $\varphi(N)$ divides $N$ or divides $p-1$ for a prime $p\mid N$ (necessarily odd). So if the odd primes of $\varphi_k(m)$ lie in $\mathcal C(m)$, so do those of $\varphi_{k+1}(m)$.

Step 2. $|\mathcal C(N)|\le\log_2N$ for every odd $N\ge1$. We use strong induction on $N$; the case $N=1$ is clear.

If $N=p$ is prime, let $N'$ be the largest odd divisor of $p-1$. Then $\mathcal C(p)=\{p\}\cup\mathcal C(N')$ and $N'\le\frac{p-1}2$, so \[|\mathcal C(p)|\le1+\log_2\frac{p-1}2=\log_2(p-1)<\log_2p .\] If $N=N_1N_2$ with odd $N_1,N_2>1$, then $\mathcal C(N)=\mathcal C(N_1)\cup\mathcal C(N_2)$ and $|\mathcal C(N)|\le\log_2N_1+\log_2N_2=\log_2N$.

Step 3. By Steps 1 and 2 the number of distinct odd primes dividing $\varphi_0(m)\varphi_1(m)\cdots\varphi_n(m)$ is at most $|\mathcal C(m)|\le\log_2(2^n-3)<n$, hence at most $n-1$. Counting also the prime $2$, the product has at most $n$ distinct prime divisors.

Remark. The argument uses only that $m$ is odd and $m<2^n$.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $AB<AC$, let $O$ be its circumcentre and let $XYZT$ be a parallelogram inside triangle $ABC$ such that \[\angle AXB=\angle AZC,\ \angle AZB=\angle AXC,\] \[\angle AYB=\angle ATC,\ \angle ATB=\angle AYC.\] Prove that the diagonals $XZ$ and $YT$ of the parallelogram intersect on the circumcircle of $BOC$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A tangent circle, then coordinates)

Let $A,B,C$ be labelled counterclockwise, let $a$ be the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$ and $m$ the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. $\measuredangle(\ell,\ell')$ denotes the directed angle between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $J$ be the common midpoint of the diagonals $XZ$ and $YT$.

Step 1: $B,C,X,Z$ are concyclic. Since $X$ is inside the triangle, $\angle AXB+\angle BXC+\angle CXA=360^\circ$, and the same holds for $Z$. The two hypotheses on $X,Z$ give $\angle BXC=\angle BZC$. As $X$ and $Z$ lie on the same side of $BC$, the points $B,C,X,Z$ lie on a circle $\gamma$ $(X\ne Z)$. Its centre $G$ lies on $m$.

Step 2: the circle $AXZ$ is tangent to $a$ at $A$. For a point $X$ inside the counterclockwise triangle $ABC$ the rays $XA,XB,XC$ follow each other counterclockwise. So $\measuredangle(XA,XB)=\angle AXB$ and $\measuredangle(ZA,ZC)=-\angle AZC$ modulo $180^\circ$, and the hypothesis $\angle AXB=\angle AZC$ gives \[\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(ZA,ZC).\] Using $a\parallel BC$ and the circle $\gamma$ (which gives $\measuredangle(ZC,ZX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)$), \[\measuredangle(a,AX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)+\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(ZC,ZX)+\measuredangle(ZA,ZC)=\measuredangle(ZA,ZX).\] The points $A,X,Z$ are not collinear (otherwise $\measuredangle(a,AX)=0$ and $X$ would lie on $a$, outside the triangle). By the converse of the tangent–chord theorem, $a$ is tangent to the circle $AXZ$ at $A$.

Step 3: the point $E$. The lines $XZ$ and $a$ are not parallel: otherwise the chord $XZ$ of circle $AXZ$ would be parallel to the tangent at $A$, so $A$ would lie on the perpendicular bisector of $XZ$; but $XZ\parallel BC$ is also a chord of $\gamma$, whose perpendicular bisector is $m$, and $A\in m$ contradicts $AB<AC$. Let $E=XZ\cap a$; $E\ne A$ because $A,X,Z$ are not collinear. By the tangent–secant theorem $EA^2=EX\cdot EZ$, and $E$ lies outside the segment $XZ$, hence outside $\gamma$. So \[EA^2=\operatorname{pow}(E,\gamma).\tag{1}\] Moreover $J$ is the midpoint of the chord $XZ$ of $\gamma$, and the line $XZ$ passes through $E$; so $GJ\perp JE$ or $J=G$. In both cases \[\overrightarrow{JG}\cdot\overrightarrow{JE}=0.\tag{2}\]

Step 4: coordinates. Let $B=(-1,0)$, $C=(1,0)$, $A=(p,q)$ with $q>0$ and $p\ne0$ (as $AB\ne AC$). Then $m$ is the $y$-axis and $a$ is the line $y=q$. Let $G=(0,g)$; then $\gamma$ has radius$^2$ equal to $1+g^2$. For $E=(e,q)$, relation (1) reads $e^2+(q-g)^2-(1+g^2)=(e-p)^2$, i.e. \[e=\frac{p^2-q^2+1+2qg}{2p}.\] With $J=(x,y)$, relation (2) is $x(x-e)+(y-g)(y-q)=0$; inserting $e$, \[x^2+y^2-qy-\frac{p^2-q^2+1}{2p}\,x+g\Bigl(q-y-\frac qp\,x\Bigr)=0.\tag{3}\]

Step 5: two different circles. The pair $Y,T$ satisfies the same hypotheses, so $B,C,Y,T$ lie on a circle $\gamma'$ with centre $(0,g')$, and (3) also holds with $g'$ in place of $g$ (for the same point $J$). We claim $g\ne g'$. If $\gamma=\gamma'$, the chords $XZ$ and $YT$ of $\gamma$ pass through the same point $E$ (it is determined by $g$) and have the same midpoint $J$. If $J\ne G$, both chords lie on the perpendicular to $GJ$ at $J$; if $J=G$, both lie on the line $GE$. Either way $X,Y,Z,T$ would be collinear, which is impossible for a parallelogram.

Subtracting the two copies of (3) and dividing by $g-g'$: \[y=q-\frac qp\,x,\qquad\text{and then}\qquad x^2+y^2-qy-\frac{p^2-q^2+1}{2p}\,x=0.\] Since $y^2-qy=y(y-q)=-\frac qp\,xy$, the second equation is $x\bigl(x-\frac qp\,y-\frac{p^2-q^2+1}{2p}\bigr)=0$. If $x=0$, then $y=q$ and $J$ lies on $a$, impossible for a point inside the triangle. So $x=\frac qp\,y+\frac{p^2-q^2+1}{2p}$; together with $y=q-\frac qpx$ this gives, with $s=p^2+q^2$, \[J=\Bigl(\frac{p(s+1)}{2s},\ \frac{q(s-1)}{2s}\Bigr).\] In particular $s>1$ because $J$ is inside the triangle.

Step 6: $J$ lies on the circle $BOC$. The circumcentre is $O=(0,o)$ with $1+o^2=p^2+(q-o)^2$, i.e. $o=\frac{s-1}{2q}$; in particular $o\ne0$ (indeed $o>0$) since $s>1$. The circle through $B,C,O$ has the equation \[x^2+y^2-1=\frac{o^2-1}{o}\,y \] (it contains $(\pm1,0)$ and $(0,o)$). For the point $J$, \[x^2+y^2-1=\frac{p^2(s+1)^2+q^2(s-1)^2}{4s^2}-1=\frac{s^2+1+2p^2-2q^2}{4s}-1=\frac{(s-1)^2-4q^2}{4s},\] and \[\frac{o^2-1}{o}\,y=\frac{(s-1)^2-4q^2}{4q^2}\cdot\frac{2q}{s-1}\cdot\frac{q(s-1)}{2s}=\frac{(s-1)^2-4q^2}{4s}.\] The two sides agree, so the intersection $J$ of the diagonals lies on the circumcircle of $BOC$.

Remark. $J$ does not depend on the parallelogram: it is the midpoint of the chord cut from the $A$-symmedian by the circumcircle. All admissible parallelograms have this centre, and they are very thin (in the figure the angle between the diagonals is about $3^\circ$).

Solution 2 (A family of circles through the A-Dumpty point)

Let $A,B,C$ be labelled counterclockwise and let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle. Let $a$ be the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$, $m$ the perpendicular bisector of $BC$, $M$ the midpoint of $BC$ and $W=a\cap m$. $\measuredangle(\ell,\ell')$ denotes the directed angle between lines, modulo $180^\circ$. Let $J$ be the common midpoint of the diagonals $XZ$ and $YT$. The circle $BOC$ exists, so $B,O,C$ are not collinear and $\angle BAC\ne90^\circ$. We use the following form of Thales' theorem: a point $P$ lies on the circle with diameter $UV$ if and only if $\overrightarrow{PU}\cdot\overrightarrow{PV}=0$ (this includes $P=U$ and $P=V$).

Step 1: $B,C,X,Z$ are concyclic. Since $X$ is inside the triangle, $\angle AXB+\angle BXC+\angle CXA=360^\circ$, and the same holds for $Z$. The two hypotheses on $X,Z$ give $\angle BXC=\angle BZC$. As $X$ and $Z$ lie on the same side of $BC$, the points $B,C,X,Z$ lie on a circle $\gamma$ $(X\ne Z)$. Its centre $G$ lies on $m$.

Step 2: the circle $AXZ$ is tangent to $a$ at $A$. For a point $X$ inside the counterclockwise triangle $ABC$ the rays $XA,XB,XC$ follow each other counterclockwise. So $\measuredangle(XA,XB)=\angle AXB$ and $\measuredangle(ZA,ZC)=-\angle AZC$ modulo $180^\circ$, and the hypothesis $\angle AXB=\angle AZC$ gives $\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(ZA,ZC)$. Using $a\parallel BC$ and the circle $\gamma$ (which gives $\measuredangle(ZC,ZX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)$), \[\measuredangle(a,AX)=\measuredangle(BC,BX)+\measuredangle(XB,XA)=\measuredangle(ZC,ZX)+\measuredangle(ZA,ZC)=\measuredangle(ZA,ZX).\] The points $A,X,Z$ are not collinear (otherwise $\measuredangle(a,AX)=0$ and $X$ would lie on $a$, outside the triangle). By the converse of the tangent–chord theorem, $a$ is tangent to the circle $AXZ$ at $A$.

Step 3: the point $E$. The lines $XZ$ and $a$ are not parallel: otherwise the chord $XZ$ of circle $AXZ$ would be parallel to the tangent at $A$, so $A$ would lie on the perpendicular bisector of $XZ$; but $XZ\parallel BC$ is also a chord of $\gamma$, whose perpendicular bisector is $m$, and $A\in m$ contradicts $AB<AC$. Let $E=XZ\cap a$; $E\ne A$ because $A,X,Z$ are not collinear. By the tangent–secant theorem $EA^2=EX\cdot EZ$, and $E$ lies outside the segment $XZ$, hence outside $\gamma$. So $EA^2=\operatorname{pow}(E,\gamma)$. Moreover $J$ is the midpoint of the chord $XZ$ of $\gamma$ and the line $XZ$ passes through $E$; so $GJ\perp JE$ or $J=G$. In both cases $\overrightarrow{JG}\cdot\overrightarrow{JE}=0$: \[J\ \text{lies on the circle with diameter } GE.\tag{1}\] The pair $Y,T$ satisfies the same hypotheses, so $B,C,Y,T$ lie on a circle $\gamma'$ with centre $G'\in m$, the point $E'=YT\cap a$ satisfies $E'A^2=\operatorname{pow}(E',\gamma')$, and $J$ lies on the circle with diameter $G'E'$.

Step 4: the point $E_G$. For a point $G$ of $m$ let $\gamma_G$ be the circle with centre $G$ through $B$ and $C$. Use signed coordinates: $t$ for $G$ on $m$ (origin $M$, positive towards $a$), $u$ for a point $P$ of $a$ (origin $W$), $\alpha$ for $A$ on $a$, and put $h=MW$. Here $\alpha\ne0$ because $AB\ne AC$. Since $a\perp m$, $PG^2=PW^2+WG^2$, and $GB^2=GM^2+MB^2$; so \[\operatorname{pow}(P,\gamma_G)-PA^2=u^2+(h-t)^2-t^2-MB^2-(u-\alpha)^2=2\alpha u-\alpha^2+h^2-MB^2-2ht .\] Hence exactly one point $E_G$ of $a$ satisfies $\operatorname{pow}(E_G,\gamma_G)=E_GA^2$, and its coordinate $u=\frac{\alpha^2-h^2+MB^2+2ht}{2\alpha}$ is an affine function of $t$. By Step 3, $E=E_G$ and $E'=E_{G'}$.

Step 5: a point on all the circles with diameter $GE_G$. For a point $P$ and $G\in m$ put $c_G(P)=\overrightarrow{PG}\cdot\overrightarrow{PE_G}$. With unit vectors $\vec e$ along $m$ and $\vec f$ along $a$, $G=M+t\vec e$ and $E_G=W+u(t)\vec f$ with $u(t)$ affine, so \[c_G(P)=\bigl(\overrightarrow{PM}+t\vec e\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\overrightarrow{PW}+u(t)\vec f\bigr)\] is a polynomial in $t$ of degree at most $1$: the only possible $t^2$ term is a multiple of $\vec e\cdot\vec f=0$. Consequently, if $c_G(P)=0$ for two different points $G$ of $m$, then $c_G(P)=0$ for every $G\in m$.

Now let $S$ be the intersection of the tangents to $\Omega$ at $B$ and $C$ (they meet, because $BC$ is not a diameter); $S$ lies on $m$ and outside $\Omega$. The line $SA$ is not tangent to $\Omega$ (the tangents from $S$ touch at $B$ and $C$), so it meets $\Omega$ again at $A'\ne A$. Let $D$ be the midpoint of $AA'$ (the $A$-Dumpty point; $SA$ is the $A$-symmedian). Then $OD\perp AA'$ or $D=O$.

  • $G=O$: $\gamma_O=\Omega$ and $\operatorname{pow}(A,\Omega)=0=AA^2$, so $E_O=A$; and $\overrightarrow{DO}\cdot\overrightarrow{DA}=0$ because $OD\perp AA'$ or $D=O$. So $c_O(D)=0$.

  • $G=S$: $\gamma_S$ has radius $SB$, and $SB^2=\operatorname{pow}(S,\Omega)=SA\cdot SA'$. Let $\ell$ be the perpendicular to $AA'$ at $D$. For $P\in\ell$, Pythagoras gives $PS^2-PA^2=DS^2-DA^2=(DS-DA)(DS+DA)=SA'\cdot SA=SB^2$, because $S$ lies on the line $AA'$ outside the segment $AA'$ and $DA=DA'$. So every $P\in\ell$ satisfies $\operatorname{pow}(P,\gamma_S)=PA^2$. The points with this property form the line $\{P:\ PS^2-PA^2=SB^2\}$, perpendicular to $AS$; it contains $\ell$, so it is $\ell$, and $E_S\in\ell$. Since $\overrightarrow{DS}$ is along $AA'$ and $\overrightarrow{DE_S}$ along $\ell$, $c_S(D)=0$.

As $O$ is inside $\Omega$ and $S$ outside, $O\ne S$. Therefore $c_G(D)=0$ for every $G\in m$: $D$ lies on every circle with diameter $GE_G$.

Step 6: $J=D$. By Steps 3 and 4, $J$ lies on the circles $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ with diameters $GE_G$ and $G'E_{G'}$; by Step 5 so does $D$; and so does $W$, because $\overrightarrow{WG}\parallel m$ and $\overrightarrow{WE_G}\parallel a$.

  • $G\ne G'$. Otherwise $\gamma=\gamma'$ and $E=E'$ by Step 4, so the chords $XZ$ and $YT$ of $\gamma$ pass through the same point $E$ and have the same midpoint $J$. If $J\ne G$, both chords lie on the perpendicular to $GJ$ at $J$; if $J=G$, both lie on the line $GE$ ($J\ne E$ because $E\in a$). Either way $X,Y,Z,T$ would be collinear, which is impossible for a parallelogram.

  • $\kappa\ne\kappa'$. The circle with diameter $GE_G$ meets $m$ exactly in $\{G,W\}$ (a point $P\in m$ with $\overrightarrow{PG}\cdot\overrightarrow{PE_G}=0$ is $G$ or has $PE_G\perp m$, i.e. $P=W$). As $G\ne G'$, the sets $\{G,W\}$ and $\{G',W\}$ differ.

  • $J\ne W$, since $J$ is inside the triangle and $W\in a$.

  • $D\ne W$. Let $A_2$ be the reflection of $A$ in $m$; it lies on $\Omega$ and on $a$, and $A_2\ne A$. So $W$, the midpoint of the chord $AA_2$, is inside $\Omega$, and $W\ne S$; as $S\in m$ and $a\cap m=\{W\}$, $S\notin a$. If $D$ were on $a$, then the line $AD$ would be $a$ and would contain $S$. Hence $D\notin a$.

Two different circles have at most two common points, and $J,D,W$ are common points of $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ with $J\ne W$ and $D\ne W$. Hence $J=D$.

Step 7: conclusion. The tangents give $\angle OBS=\angle OCS=90^\circ$, so the circle with diameter $OS$ passes through $B$ and $C$: it is the circle $BOC$. Since $S$ lies on the line $AA'$ and $OD\perp AA'$ (or $D=O$), $\overrightarrow{DO}\cdot\overrightarrow{DS}=0$, so $D$ lies on this circle. As $J=D$, the diagonals of the parallelogram meet on the circumcircle of $BOC$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $k>1$ be an integer, and let $S$ denote the set of all $(k+1)$-tuples of integers $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_{k+1})$ such that $1\le x_1<\cdots<x_{k+1}\le k^2+1$. If $\sigma$ is a permutation of the numbers $1,2,\ldots,k^2+1$, say that an element $X$ of $S$ is $\sigma$-nice if the sequence $\sigma(x_1),\sigma(x_2),\ldots,\sigma(x_{k+1})$ is monotone. Prove that \[\min_{1\le i\le k}\left\lfloor\frac{x_i}{i}\right\rfloor+\min_{2\le i\le k+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k^2+2-x_i}{k+2-i}\right\rfloor\ge k+1\] if and only if there exists a permutation $\sigma$ such that $X$ is the unique $\sigma$-nice tuple in $S$.

Show the solution

Solution (Chain decompositions and an explicit permutation)

Write $N=k^2+1$, and let $m_1,m_2$ be the two minima in the statement.

Reformulation. The inequality $m_1+m_2\ge k+1$ holds if and only if there are integers $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ with $\alpha+\beta=k-1$ such that \[x_i\ge(\alpha+1)\,i\quad(1\le i\le k),\qquad x_i\le k^2+2-(\beta+1)(k+2-i)\quad(2\le i\le k+1).\tag{$\ast$}\] Indeed, $(\ast)$ says $m_1\ge\alpha+1$ and $m_2\ge\beta+1$, so it implies $m_1+m_2\ge k+1$. Conversely, $m_1\ge1$ and $m_2\ge1$ always (as $x_i\ge i$ and $x_i\le k^2+1-(k+1-i)$); if $m_1+m_2\ge k+1$, take $\alpha+1=\min(m_1,k)$ and $\beta+1=k+1-(\alpha+1)\le m_2$.

In terms of the $k^2-k$ positions outside $X$: $(\ast)$ says that at least $\alpha i$ of them are to the left of $x_i$ $(i\le k)$ and at least $\beta(k+2-i)$ of them are to the right of $x_i$ $(i\ge2)$.

A permutation $\sigma$ is pictured by the points $p_j=(j,\sigma(j))$. Write $p<p'$ if $p'$ is to the right of and above $p$. Increasing subsequences are chains, decreasing subsequences are antichains for this order.

Part 1: a unique nice tuple satisfies $(\ast)$. Let $X$ be the only $\sigma$-nice tuple. Replacing $\sigma(j)$ by $N+1-\sigma(j)$ if necessary (this keeps the positions), we may assume that $\sigma$ is increasing on $X$. So $P_i=p_{x_i}$ $(1\le i\le k+1)$ is the only chain with $k+1$ points and there is no antichain with $k+1$ points.

Decomposition. By Dilworth's theorem the $N$ points can be split into at most $k$ chains. (Directly: label each point by the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending at it. The labels are among $1,\dots,k$, and two points with the same label are comparable, since if the right one were lower, its label would be larger; so each label class is a chain.) A chain has at most $k+1$ points (a longer one would contain several chains of $k+1$ points), and only the chain $\mathcal P=\{P_1,\dots,P_{k+1}\}$ has $k+1$ points. Since $k^2+1=(k+1)+(k-1)k$, the splitting consists of $\mathcal P$ and $k-1$ chains with exactly $k$ points each.

One chain. Let $y_1<y_2<\dots<y_k$ be one of these chains. Fix $j$. The points of $\mathcal P$ below $y_j$ are $P_1,\dots,P_r$ for some $r$, and $P_1<\dots<P_r<y_j<y_{j+1}<\dots<y_k$ is a chain different from $\mathcal P$, so it has at most $k$ points: $r\le j-1$. Thus $P_j\not<y_j$. Similarly the points of $\mathcal P$ above $y_j$ are $P_{r'},\dots,P_{k+1}$, and the chain $y_1<\dots<y_j<P_{r'}<\dots<P_{k+1}$ has at most $k$ points: $r'\ge j+2$. Thus $y_j\not<P_{j+1}$. As $P_j<P_{j+1}$, it follows that $y_j$ is incomparable with both $P_j$ and $P_{j+1}$.

A point incomparable with $P_j$ is either to the left of and above $P_j$ (“type L”) or to the right of and below $P_j$ (“type R”). If $y_j$ is of type L with respect to $P_j$, it is to the left of $P_{j+1}$ and incomparable with it, hence above $P_{j+1}$. If $y_j$ is of type R with respect to $P_j$, it is below $P_{j+1}$, hence to the right of $P_{j+1}$. So: \[\text{type L: } y_j\text{ is left of }x_j\text{ and above }P_{j+1};\qquad\text{type R: } y_j\text{ is right of }x_{j+1}\text{ and below }P_j .\] All points of the chain have the same type: if $y_j$ is of type L, then $y_{j+1}$ is above $y_j$, hence above $P_{j+1}$, so $y_{j+1}$ (incomparable with $P_{j+1}$) is of type L; if $y_{j+1}$ is of type L, then $y_j$ is to the left of $y_{j+1}$, hence to the left of $x_{j+1}$, so $y_j$ is not of type R.

Counting. Let $\alpha$ chains be of type L and $\beta$ of type R; $\alpha+\beta=k-1$. In an L-chain the points $y_1,\dots,y_i$ are to the left of $x_i$ (since $y_j$ is left of $x_j\le x_i$); so at least $\alpha i$ positions outside $X$ are to the left of $x_i$. In an R-chain the points $y_{i-1},\dots,y_k$ are to the right of $x_i$ (since $y_j$ is right of $x_{j+1}\ge x_i$ for $j\ge i-1$); so at least $\beta(k+2-i)$ positions outside $X$ are to the right of $x_i$, for $2\le i\le k+1$. This is $(\ast)$.

Part 2: if $(\ast)$ holds, a permutation exists. Let $\alpha,\beta$ be as in $(\ast)$. List the $k^2-k=(\alpha+\beta)k$ positions outside $X$ from left to right. The first $\alpha k$ of them are divided, from left to right, into groups $G_1,\dots,G_k$ of $\alpha$ consecutive positions; the last $\beta k$ of them are divided, from left to right, into groups $H_1,\dots,H_k$ of $\beta$ positions. By $(\ast)$:

  • at least $\alpha j$ outside positions are left of $x_j$, so all of $G_j$ is to the left of $x_j$ $(1\le j\le k)$;

  • at least $\beta(k+1-j)$ outside positions are right of $x_{j+1}$, so all of $H_j$ is to the right of $x_{j+1}$ $(1\le j\le k)$;

  • every position of a group $G$ is to the left of every position of a group $H$.

Now we fix the order of the values. The values at $x_1<\dots<x_{k+1}$ increase; call these points $P_1,\dots,P_{k+1}$. Between the values of $P_m$ and $P_{m+1}$ (below $P_1$ for $m=0$, above $P_{k+1}$ for $m=k+1$) we put first, lower, the values on $H_{m+1}$ and then, higher, the values on $G_{m-1}$ (when these groups exist), and inside every group the values decrease from left to right. This defines $\sigma$. Summarising: \[\text{values on }G_j\text{ lie between }P_{j+1}\text{ and }P_{j+2};\qquad\text{values on }H_j\text{ lie between }P_{j-1}\text{ and }P_j;\] and in the band between $P_m$ and $P_{m+1}$ the values on $G_{m-1}$ are above those on $H_{m+1}$.

Increasing pairs. Give the weight $\lambda(P_i)=i$, $\lambda=j+\frac12$ to the points of $G_j$ and to the points of $H_j$. We check that whenever $p<p'$, we have $\lambda(p')\ge\lambda(p)+1$.

  • $P_i<P_{i'}$: $i'\ge i+1$. Two points of $G_j$ and $G_{j'}$, or of $H_j$ and $H_{j'}$: comparable only if $j\ne j'$, and then $j<j'$.

  • A point of $G_j$ below $P_i$: its value is above $P_{j+1}$, so $i\ge j+2$, difference $\ge\frac32$. $P_i$ below a point of $G_j$: this point is left of $x_j$, so $i\le j-1$, difference $\ge\frac32$.

  • $P_i$ below a point of $H_j$: its value is below $P_j$, so $i\le j-1$. A point of $H_j$ below $P_i$: it is right of $x_{j+1}$, so $i\ge j+2$. Differences $\ge\frac32$.

  • A point of $G_j$ below a point of $H_{j'}$: the band of $G_j$ must be lower than the band of $H_{j'}$, i.e. $j+1<j'-1$ (in the same band $G$ is above $H$), so $j'\ge j+3$. A point of $H$ is never below a point of $G$, being to its right.

All weights lie in $[1,k+1]$. So an increasing subsequence has at most $k+1$ terms, and if it has $k+1$ terms, its weights are exactly $1,2,\dots,k+1$, all integers; then its points are $P_1,\dots,P_{k+1}$. Hence $X$ is the only increasing $(k+1)$-tuple.

Decreasing pairs. Suppose $p$ is to the left of and above $p'$. Two different groups $G_j,G_{j'}$ $(j<j')$ form only increasing pairs, likewise two groups $H$; the points $P_i$ form only increasing pairs. A point $P_i$ is never to the left of and above a point of $G_j$ (that would need $i\ge j+2$ for the values but $i<j$ for the positions), and a point of $H_j$ is never to the left of and above a point $P_i$ (that would need $i\ge j+2$ and $i\le j-1$) or a point of some $G$. Therefore a decreasing subsequence consists of some points of one group $G_j$, then at most one point $P_i$, then some points of one group $H_{j'}$: at most $\alpha+1+\beta=k$ points.

So $X$ is the unique $\sigma$-nice tuple.

Parts 1 and 2 together with the reformulation prove the statement.
