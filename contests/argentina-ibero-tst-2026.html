Problem 1

ENumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods · Bounding and Size Arguments

Find all pairs of integers $(x,y)$ satisfying \[1+x^2y=x^2+2xy+2x+y.\]

Show the answer

$(0,1),\ (1,-1),\ (2,-7),\ (3,7),\ (-1,-1)$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Solve for y and bound)

The equation is linear in $y$: \[y(x^2-2x-1)=x^2+2x-1 .\] For an integer $x$, $x^2-2x-1=(x-1)^2-2\ne0$, so \[y=\frac{x^2+2x-1}{x^2-2x-1}=1+\frac{4x}{x^2-2x-1},\] and we need $x^2-2x-1\mid4x$.

If $x=0$, then $y=1$. If $x\ne0$, the divisibility forces $|x^2-2x-1|\le4|x|$.

  • $x\ge1$: $x^2-2x-1\le4x$ gives $x^2-6x-1\le0$, so $x\le6$. Checking: $x=1$: $-2\mid4$, $y=-1$; $x=2$: $-1\mid8$, $y=-7$; $x=3$: $2\mid12$, $y=7$; $x=4,5,6$: $7\nmid16$, $14\nmid20$, $23\nmid24$.

  • $x\le-1$: $x^2-2x-1\le-4x$ gives $x^2+2x-1\le0$, so $x\in\{-2,-1\}$. $x=-1$: $2\mid-4$, $y=-1$; $x=-2$: $7\nmid-8$.

The solutions are $\boxed{(0,1),\ (1,-1),\ (2,-7),\ (3,7),\ (-1,-1)}$.

Solution 2 (Reducing the divisor to a divisor of 4)

As in any approach, the equation is $y(x^2-2x-1)=x^2+2x-1$, and $d=x^2-2x-1=(x-1)^2-2$ is never $0$ for integer $x$. So $y=1+\frac{4x}{d}$ is an integer exactly when $d\mid4x$.

If $d\mid4x$, then $d\mid4x\cdot x=4x^2=4d+8x+4$, so $d\mid8x+4$; together with $d\mid8x$ this gives $d\mid4$. Hence $(x-1)^2=d+2\in\{-2,0,1,3,4,6\}$, and as a square it is $0$, $1$ or $4$: $x\in\{-1,0,1,2,3\}$. For these $d=2,-1,-2,-1,2$, and $d\mid4x$ holds in each case, with \[y=1+\frac{4x}{d}=-1,\ 1,\ -1,\ -7,\ 7 .\] The solutions are $\boxed{(0,1),\ (1,-1),\ (2,-7),\ (3,7),\ (-1,-1)}$.

Problem 2

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Periodic and Cyclic Behavior Modulo $n$

Consider the infinite sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ of positive integers defined by \[a_1=43,\qquad a_2=142,\qquad a_{n+1}=3a_n+a_{n-1}\] for every integer $n\ge2$.

  • Prove that $a_n$ and $a_{n+1}$ are relatively prime for every positive integer $n$.

  • For every positive integer $m$, prove that there exist infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that both $a_n-1$ and $a_{n+1}-1$ are divisible by $m$.

Remark: Two integers are said to be relatively prime if their greatest common divisor is $1$.

Show the solution

Solution (Running the recurrence backwards)

Coprimality. Any common divisor of $a_n$ and $a_{n+1}=3a_n+a_{n-1}$ divides $a_{n-1}$. Hence \[\gcd(a_n,a_{n+1})=\gcd(a_{n-1},a_n)=\dots=\gcd(a_1,a_2)=\gcd(43,142)=\gcd(43,13)=1 .\]

Divisibility. The recurrence can be run backwards with $a_{n-1}=a_{n+1}-3a_n$, defining $a_n$ for all integers $n$: \[a_0=142-3\cdot43=13,\quad a_{-1}=43-3\cdot13=4,\quad a_{-2}=13-3\cdot4=1,\quad a_{-3}=4-3\cdot1=1 .\] Fix $m\ge1$ and consider the pairs $p_n=(a_n\bmod m,\ a_{n+1}\bmod m)$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. The pair $p_{n+1}$ is determined by $p_n$ through $(u,v)\mapsto(v,3v+u)$, and $p_n$ is determined by $p_{n+1}$ through $(v,w)\mapsto(w-3v,v)$. There are only $m^2$ possible pairs, so two of $p_0,p_1,\dots,p_{m^2}$ coincide, say $p_i=p_{i+T}$ with $T\ge1$; applying the forward and backward rules repeatedly gives $p_n=p_{n+T}$ for every integer $n$.

In particular $p_{-3+jT}=p_{-3}=(1,1)$ for every $j\ge1$: that is, $m\mid a_n-1$ and $m\mid a_{n+1}-1$ for $n=-3+jT$. For $j>3/T$ these $n$ are positive, so there are infinitely many such $n$.

Problem 3

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Hybrid Combinatorial-Arithmetic Arguments

Tony chooses an integer $n$. Then, Manu fills each cell of a $100\times100$ board with a distinct integer from $1$ to $100^2$, inclusive. If Tony can find two numbers in the same row or the same column that have a common divisor greater than or equal to $n$, he wins the game. Determine the greatest value of $n$ that Tony can choose so that he is guaranteed to win, regardless of how Manu fills the board.

Show the answer

$99$

Show the solution

Solution (Rows of consecutive numbers, columns modulo 101)

Tony wins with $n=99$. The multiples of $99$ up to $10000$ are $99,198,\dots,99\cdot101=9999$: $101$ numbers. They occupy $101$ cells in $100$ rows, so two of them share a row, and their greatest common divisor is at least $99$. (For $n<99$ Tony wins all the more.)

Manu can prevent $n=100$. We give a filling in which any two numbers in a common row or column have $\gcd\le99$.

Rows. Row $q$ ($q=0,1,\dots,99$) contains the block $100q+1,\dots,100q+100$. Two numbers of one row differ by less than $100$, and their gcd divides the difference, so it is at most $99$.

Columns. Use residues modulo the prime $101$. Since $100q+i\equiv i-q\pmod{101}$, block $q$ contains each residue modulo $101$ exactly once except the residue $-q$. So block $0$ contains the residues $1,\dots,100$, and block $q\ge1$ contains exactly one multiple of $101$ and misses the residue $101-q\in\{2,\dots,100\}$. Put a number with nonzero residue $j$ into column $j$ ($j=1,\dots,100$), and put the multiple of $101$ of block $q\ge1$ into column $101-q$. Then every row meets every column exactly once, so this is a valid filling.

Take two numbers $x<y$ in the same column with $g=\gcd(x,y)\ge100$.

  • If both are $\not\equiv0\pmod{101}$, they have the same residue $j$, so $y-x=101t$ with $1\le t\le99$. Then $g\mid101t$; since $g\ge100>t$, we get $101\mid g$, hence $101\mid x$, a contradiction.

  • If $x$ (say) is a multiple of $101$ and $y$ is not, then $101\nmid g$ (as $g\mid y$), so $g\mid x/101\le99$, contradicting $g\ge100$.

(Each column contains at most one multiple of $101$.) So all gcds within a line are at most $99$, and Tony cannot guarantee a win with any $n\ge100$.

The answer is $\boxed{99}$.

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Divisibility Chains and Factor Relations

Find all positive integers $n$ that have an even number of positive divisors (including $1$ and $n$) and whose divisors can be partitioned into pairs such that the sum of the two divisors in each pair is a power of $3$.

Show the answer

$n=2\cdot3^k$ ($k\ge0$)

Show the solution

Solution (Pairs keep the power of 3)

These work. For $n=2\cdot3^k$ the divisors are $3^i$ and $2\cdot3^i$ ($0\le i\le k$), an even number $2(k+1)$ of them, and the pairs $\{3^i,2\cdot3^i\}$ have sums $3^{i+1}$.

No others. Let $n$ work, and write $n=3^em$ with $3\nmid m$. A pair $\{d,d'\}$ has $d+d'=3^j$ with $j\ge1$ (the sum is at least $1+2$). If $v_3(d)<v_3(d')$, then $v_3(d+d')=v_3(d)$, so $j=v_3(d)$ and $3^j\le d<d+d'=3^j$, impossible. Hence the two divisors in a pair have the same exponent of $3$. In particular the divisors not divisible by $3$, which are exactly the divisors of $m$, are paired among themselves with sums powers of $3$. So $m$ itself works, and we show $m=2$.

Since $m$ works, $m$ is paired with some $d\mid m$ with $m+d=3^j$; then $d\mid3^j$ and $3\nmid d$, so $d=1$ and $m=3^j-1$. As $m$ has an even number of divisors, $m\ne1$, so $j\ge1$ and $m$ is even.

Suppose $m>2$. Then $m\ne4$ (as $5$ is not a power of $3$), so $\frac m2\notin\{1,2,m\}$ is a divisor that is paired with some divisor $d\notin\{1,m\}$ (those two are paired with each other), $\frac m2+d=3^i$. Any common divisor of $\frac m2$ and $d$ divides $3^i$ and is prime to $3$, so $\gcd(\frac m2,d)=1$; since $d\mid m=2\cdot\frac m2$, this gives $d\mid2$, so $d=2$. Thus $\frac{3^j-1}2+2=3^i$, i.e. $3^j+3=2\cdot3^i$. Here $j\ge2$ (as $m>2$), so $3^{j-1}+1=2\cdot3^{i-1}$; the left side is $\equiv1\pmod3$, forcing $i=1$ and then $3^{j-1}=1$, $j=1$, a contradiction.

So $m=2$ and $n=\boxed{2\cdot3^k}$ with $k\ge0$.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 40: Vector Geometry and Affine Structure · Dot Product and Metric Recovery

Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram and let $O$ be the intersection point of its diagonals $AC$ and $BD$. Let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of $BO$ and $CD$, respectively. Prove that if triangles $ABC$ and $AMN$ are similar, then $ABCD$ is a square.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Complex numbers)

We read the similarity with vertices in order: $A\to A$, $B\to M$, $C\to N$. Use complex numbers with $A=0$, $B=b$, $D=d$; then $C=b+d$, $O=\frac{b+d}2$, \[M=\frac{B+O}2=\frac{3b+d}4,\qquad N=\frac{C+D}2=\frac b2+d .\] Directly similar. Then $\frac MN=\frac BC$, i.e. $M\,C=N\,B$: \[\frac{(3b+d)(b+d)}4=\Bigl(\frac b2+d\Bigr)b\iff3b^2+4bd+d^2=2b^2+4bd\iff b^2+d^2=0,\] so $d=\pm ib$: $AD\perp AB$ and $AD=AB$, and the parallelogram is a square.

Oppositely similar. Then $\frac MN=\overline{\bigl(\frac BC\bigr)}$. Rotate and scale so that $b=1$ and write $d=x+iy$. The condition is $M(1+\bar d)=N$, i.e. $(3+d)(1+\bar d)=2+4d$, i.e. \[1+x^2+y^2+3\bar d-3d=0\iff (1+x^2+y^2)-6iy=0,\] whose real part $1+x^2+y^2$ is never zero. So this case cannot occur.

Hence $ABCD$ is a square.

Solution 2 (Side lengths and dot products)

Read the similarity as $A\to A$, $B\to M$, $C\to N$, with ratio $\lambda$. Put $\vec b=\vec{AB}$, $\vec d=\vec{AD}$, $p=|\vec b|^2$, $q=|\vec d|^2$, $s=\vec b\cdot\vec d$. Then $\vec{AC}=\vec b+\vec d$, $\vec{AM}=\frac{3\vec b+\vec d}4$, $\vec{AN}=\frac{\vec b+2\vec d}2$, $\vec{MN}=\frac{3\vec d-\vec b}4$, and \[AB^2=p,\ BC^2=q,\ AC^2=p+2s+q,\qquad AM^2=\frac{9p+6s+q}{16},\ MN^2=\frac{p-6s+9q}{16},\ AN^2=\frac{p+4s+4q}4 .\] The similarity gives $\frac{AM^2}{AB^2}=\frac{MN^2}{BC^2}=\frac{AN^2}{AC^2}=\lambda^2$.

From the first equality, $q(9p+6s+q)=p(p-6s+9q)$, i.e. $6s(p+q)=p^2-q^2$, so $6s=p-q$. Then \[\lambda^2=\frac{9p+(p-q)+q}{16p}=\frac58 .\] The last ratio, with $s=\frac{p-q}6$, is \[\frac{p+4s+4q}{4(p+2s+q)}=\frac{\frac53(p+2q)}{\frac83(2p+q)}=\frac{5(p+2q)}{8(2p+q)},\] and it equals $\frac58$ exactly when $p+2q=2p+q$, i.e. $p=q$. Then $s=0$. So $AB=AD$ and $AB\perp AD$: $ABCD$ is a square.

Problem 6

HAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Algebraic Forcing of Function Form

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that, for all nonzero real numbers $x$ and $y$, \[f(x)\ne0\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{f(x)}{f(y)}+\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}-f\!\left(\frac xy-\frac yx\right)=2.\]

Show the answer

$f(x)=x^2$

Show the solution

Solution (Multiplicative up to inversion, then additive)

Check. $f(x)=x^2$ works: $\frac{x^2}{y^2}+\frac{y^2}{x^2}-\bigl(\frac xy-\frac yx\bigr)^2=2$, and $x^2\ne0$ for $x\ne0$.

Uniqueness. Write $E(x,y)$ for the equation. $E(x,x)$ gives $f(0)=0$, and $E(x,-x)$ gives $\frac{f(x)}{f(-x)}+\frac{f(-x)}{f(x)}=2$, so $f(-x)=f(x)$. Let $a=f(1)\ne0$ and $k(x)=f(x)/a$ for $x\ne0$. Then $E(x,y)$ reads \[a\,k\Bigl(\frac xy-\frac yx\Bigr)=\frac{k(x)}{k(y)}+\frac{k(y)}{k(x)}-2 .\tag{1}\]

Step 1: $k(1/x)=1/k(x)$. $E(r,1)$ and $E(1,1/r)$ have the same left side $f(r-\frac1r)$, so $k(r)+\frac1{k(r)}=\frac1{k(1/r)}+k(1/r)$. Hence $k(1/r)=\frac1{k(r)}$ or $k(1/r)=k(r)$. In the second case $E(r,\frac1r)$ gives $f(r^2-\frac1{r^2})=0$, impossible for $r\ne\pm1$ (since $f$ vanishes only at $0$). So $k(1/r)=1/k(r)$ for all $r\ne0$.

Step 2: $k(x)k(y)=k(xy)^{\pm1}$. $E(x,\frac1y)$ and $E(xy,1)$ have the same left side, so by Step 1 \[k(x)k(y)+\frac1{k(x)k(y)}=k(xy)+\frac1{k(xy)},\qquad\text{i.e. } k(x)k(y)\in\Bigl\{k(xy),\frac1{k(xy)}\Bigr\}.\tag{2}\] Also, by (1) with $y=1$: $a\,k(r-\frac1r)=\frac{(k(r)-1)^2}{k(r)}$; so $k(r)\ne1$ for $r\ne\pm1$.

Step 3: $k>0$ and $a>0$. By (2) the sign of $k$ is multiplicative, so $k(x^2)$ has the sign of $k(x)^2$, i.e. $k>0$ on positive numbers, and by evenness everywhere. Then $a\,k(\frac32)=\frac{(k(2)-1)^2}{k(2)}>0$ gives $a>0$.

Step 4: $k$ is multiplicative. Let $g(u)=\ln k(e^u)$ for real $u$. By (2), $g(u+v)=\pm\bigl(g(u)+g(v)\bigr)$, and $g(u)\ne0$ for $u\ne0$.

  • $g(2u)=2g(u)$: if $g(2u)=-2g(u)$ with $u\ne0$, then $|g(3u)|=|g(2u)+g(u)|=|g(u)|$ and $|g(4u)|=|2g(2u)|=4|g(u)|$, but also $|g(4u)|=|g(3u)+g(u)|\le2|g(u)|$, a contradiction.

  • If $g(u+v)=-(g(u)+g(v))$ with $g(u)+g(v)\ne0$, compute $g(u+2v)$ in two ways: $|g(u+2v)|=|g(u+v)+g(v)|=|g(u)|$ and $|g(u+2v)|=|g(u)+2g(v)|$. So $g(v)=0$ or $g(u)+g(v)=0$; the latter is excluded, and $g(v)=0$ means $v=0$, giving $g(u)=-g(u)$, $g(u)=0$, again excluded.

(If $g(u)+g(v)=0$, both signs give $g(u+v)=0=g(u)+g(v)$.) So $g$ is additive, i.e. $k(xy)=k(x)k(y)$ for all $x,y>0$, and by evenness for all $x,y\ne0$.

Step 5: $k(x)=x^2$ and $a=1$. For $t>0$ let $\sigma(t)=k(\sqrt t)>0$; it is multiplicative and $k(t)=\sigma(t)^2$. Writing $r=\sqrt t$, $k(r-\frac1r)=\frac{k(t-1)}{k(r)}$, so (1) with $y=1$ becomes $a\,k(t-1)=(\sigma(t)-1)^2$, i.e. \[\sqrt a\;\sigma(|t-1|)=|\sigma(t)-1|\qquad(t>0,\ t\ne1).\tag{3}\] With $p=\sigma(2)\ne1$: $t=2$ gives $\sqrt a=|p-1|$; $t=4$ and $t=3$ give $|p^2-1|=\sqrt a\,\sigma(3)$ and $\sigma(3)=1\pm\sqrt a\,p$. Hence $|p+1|=1\pm|p-1|p$, so $p=\pm|p-1|p$, i.e. $|p-1|=1$, $p=2$, and $a=1$.

Now (3) says $\sigma(t+1)=1\pm\sigma(t)$ for $t>0$. Suppose $\sigma(t+1)=1-\sigma(t)$ for some $t>0$. By multiplicativity and (3) at $1+\frac1t$, $\sigma(t+1)=\sigma(t)\sigma(1+\frac1t)=\sigma(t)\bigl(1\pm\frac1{\sigma(t)}\bigr)=\sigma(t)\pm1$; comparing, $\sigma(t)=0$ or $\sigma(t)=1$, i.e. $t=1$ (recall $k\ne1$ off $\pm1$), but $\sigma(2)=2\ne0$. So $\sigma(t+1)=\sigma(t)+1$ for all $t>0$, and then \[\sigma(x+y)=\sigma(y)\,\sigma\!\Bigl(\frac xy+1\Bigr)=\sigma(y)\Bigl(\sigma\!\Bigl(\frac xy\Bigr)+1\Bigr)=\sigma(x)+\sigma(y).\] An additive function with positive values is increasing ($\sigma(y)=\sigma(x)+\sigma(y-x)>\sigma(x)$ for $y>x$), hence linear; with $\sigma(1)=1$ we get $\sigma(t)=t$. So $k(x)=x^2$ for $x>0$, by evenness for all $x\ne0$, and $f(x)=a\,k(x)=x^2$; also $f(0)=0$.

The only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=x^2}$.
