Problem A1

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 1: Variables, Expressions, and Formula Language

A single square table can seat exactly four people, with one person on each side. If $2026$ of these tables are pushed together in a single straight row, how many people can be seated at this combined, long rectangular table?

Show the answer

$4054$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Count the sides)

The long table has two long sides, each made of $2026$ table sides (one person each), and two short ends (one person each). That is $2\cdot2026+2=\boxed{4054}$ people.

Solution 2 (Count what is lost)

Separately the tables seat $4\cdot2026$ people. Each of the $2025$ joins hides two sides, losing $2$ seats: $8104-2\cdot2025=\boxed{4054}$.

Problem A2

Number Theory · LPS VI, Chapter 3: Divisibility and Prime Factorization

For a positive integer $n\ge2$ we will define $n\dagger$ recursively as follows:

  • $2\dagger=2$,
  • $(n+1)\dagger=(n+1)\times(n\dagger\div p)$, where $p$ is the smallest prime that divides $n\dagger$.

For example, $3\dagger=3\times(2\dagger\div2)=3$. Determine the value of $8\dagger$.

Show the answer

$280$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Compute step by step)

  • $3\dagger=3$ (given); smallest prime factor $3$.
  • $4\dagger=4\cdot(3\div3)=4$; smallest prime $2$.
  • $5\dagger=5\cdot(4\div2)=10$; smallest prime $2$.
  • $6\dagger=6\cdot(10\div2)=30$; smallest prime $2$.
  • $7\dagger=7\cdot(30\div2)=105$; smallest prime $3$.
  • $8\dagger=8\cdot(105\div3)=280$.

\[\boxed{8\dagger=280}\]

Solution 2 (Track the prime factorisation)

Each step multiplies by $n+1$ and removes one copy of the smallest prime. In factorised form: $2\to3\to2^2\to2\cdot5\to2\cdot3\cdot5\to3\cdot5\cdot7\to2^3\cdot5\cdot7$. (At $7\dagger$ the factor $2$ from $30$ is removed and $7$ is added; at $8\dagger$ the $3$ is removed and $2^3$ is added.) So $8\dagger=8\cdot35=\boxed{280}$.

Problem A3

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 4: Angle Chasing and Line Configurations

The diagram shows an equilateral triangle $ABC$ with squares drawn on every one of its sides. Find the measure of angle $ABD$ in degrees.

Show the answer

$75^\circ$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (An isosceles triangle)

In the diagram each square is drawn on the side of the triangle facing the third vertex. $D$ is a vertex of the square on $CA$, next to $A$, so $AD=AC=AB$ and $\angle CAD=90^\circ$, with $D$ on the same side of $CA$ as $B$. Hence $\angle BAD=90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ$. Triangle $ABD$ is isosceles with $AB=AD$, so \[\angle ABD=\frac{180^\circ-30^\circ}2=\boxed{75^\circ}.\]

Solution 2 (Coordinates)

Take $C=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $A=(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2)$. Turning $\overrightarrow{AC}=(-\frac12,-\frac{\sqrt3}2)$ by $90^\circ$ towards $B$ gives $\overrightarrow{AD}=(\frac{\sqrt3}2,-\frac12)$, so $D=\left(\frac{1+\sqrt3}2,\frac{\sqrt3-1}2\right)$. Then \[\overrightarrow{BD}=\left(\tfrac{\sqrt3-1}2,\tfrac{\sqrt3-1}2\right)\ \text{points at }45^\circ,\qquad\overrightarrow{BA}=\left(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\right)\ \text{points at }120^\circ,\] so $\angle ABD=120^\circ-45^\circ=\boxed{75^\circ}$.

Problem A4

Combinatorics · LPS VI, Chapter 38: Probability and Expected Value

Alice, Barb, and Cate let fate decide whether to buy ice cream. Each flips a fair coin and writes $0$ for Heads, $1$ for Tails. Let $a,b,c$ be the values that Alice, Barb, and Cate obtain, respectively. They then calculate $1-a-b-c+ab+ac+bc-abc$. If the answer is zero, the girls buy ice cream. What is the likelihood that on this toss the girls will buy ice cream?

Show the answer

$\tfrac78$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Factor)

$1-a-b-c+ab+ac+bc-abc=(1-a)(1-b)(1-c)$. This is $0$ unless $a=b=c=0$ (three heads), which has probability $\frac18$. So they buy ice cream with probability $1-\frac18=\boxed{\tfrac78}$.

Solution 2 (Inclusion–exclusion reading)

With $0/1$ values, $1-a-b-c+ab+ac+bc-abc$ is the inclusion–exclusion count of "none of the three got tails": it equals $1$ if nobody got tails and $0$ otherwise. So the expression is zero exactly when at least one coin shows tails: probability $1-\left(\frac12\right)^3=\boxed{\tfrac78}$.

Problem B1

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 28: Right Triangles and the Pythagorean Framework

A $5\times5$ rectangular grid of dots is placed so that adjacent dots are $1$ unit apart. The dot in the top right corner is removed as in the diagram below. A square is drawn whose vertices are grid points in the diagram, and the area of the square is $A$ square units. What is the sum of all possible values of $A$?

(Note: congruent squares in different locations or with different orientations have the same area $A$. This number should be counted in the sum only once regardless of the number of congruent squares that have this area.)

Show the answer

$39$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Side vectors)

A lattice square has a side vector $(p,q)$ with $p,q\ge0$, not both $0$; the next side is $(-q,p)$, the area is $p^2+q^2$, and the square's bounding box is a square of side $p+q$ units. In a $5\times5$ grid, $p+q\le4$.

  • Upright squares $(p,0)$: areas $1,4,9,16$. The $4\times4$ square needs all four corners of the grid, including the removed dot, so $16$ is impossible; $1,4,9$ fit away from that corner.
  • Tilted squares with $p,q\ge1$, $p+q\le4$: $(1,1)\to2$, $(1,2)\to5$, $(1,3)\to10$, $(2,2)\to8$. For $p+q\le3$ place the box away from the top-right corner. For $p+q=4$ the box is the whole grid, but a tilted square uses only one point on each side of the box, not the corners, so the missing corner does not matter.

Sum: $1+4+9+2+5+8+10=\boxed{39}$.

Solution 2 (List areas as sums of two squares)

The possible areas are the numbers $p^2+q^2$ with $p+q\le4$, and each value is counted once: $1=1^2$, $2=1^2+1^2$, $4=2^2$, $5=1^2+2^2$, $8=2^2+2^2$, $9=3^2$, $10=1^2+3^2$, $16=4^2$. Each of these except $16$ really occurs: upright squares of side $1,2,3$ fit in the bottom-left corner, and for $p,q\ge1$ the square with vertices $(q,0),(p+q,q),(p,p+q),(0,p)$ avoids all four corners of its bounding box, so even for $p+q=4$ it does not use the missing dot. Only $16$ needs that corner (the only $4\times4$ upright square is the grid's own boundary), so the sum is $1+2+4+5+8+9+10=\boxed{39}$.

Problem B2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Waldo has solved several quadratic equations of the form $x^2-ax+b=0$, where $0<b<20$. He observed that each of the equations had two distinct positive integer roots. If no two equations shared a root, what is the maximal possible number of equations that Waldo solved?

Show the answer

$3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Vieta and a pigeonhole bound)

By Vieta the roots $r<s$ satisfy $rs=b\le19$. If $r\ge4$ then $s\ge5$ and $rs\ge20$, too big. So every equation has its smaller root in $\{1,2,3\}$. Different equations share no root, so their smaller roots are different, and there are at most $3$ equations.

Three are possible: roots $\{1,4\},\{2,5\},\{3,6\}$, i.e. $x^2-5x+4$, $x^2-7x+10$, $x^2-9x+18$ (with $b=4,10,18$). Answer $\boxed{3}$.

Solution 2 (Count products)

List the pairs $r<s$ with $rs\le19$ by the smaller root: smaller root $1$ (partners $2,\dots,19$), $2$ (partners $3,\dots,9$), $3$ (partners $4,5,6$), and none with smaller root $\ge4$ ($4\cdot5=20$). The equations use disjoint pairs, so at most one pair from each of the three groups, at most $3$. The choice $(1,4),(2,5),(3,6)$ works: $\boxed{3}$.

Problem B3

Geometry · LPS VI, Chapter 20: Similarity and Proportional Geometry

Three identical mutually tangent circles are drawn inside an equilateral triangle of area $10$ square units and are each tangent to two of the sides of the triangle. A new triangle is formed with the centres of the circles as vertices. Determine the area (in square units) of the new triangle.

Show the answer

$10-5\sqrt3$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Side lengths)

Let the radius be $r$. The triangle of centres is equilateral with side $2r$. Along a side of the big triangle: from a vertex to the nearest tangency point is $r\cot30^\circ=r\sqrt3$ (the centre lies on the angle bisector), and between the tangency points of two neighbouring circles is $2r$. So the big side is $2r+2\sqrt3\,r$. The two triangles are similar with ratio $\frac{2r}{2r+2\sqrt3r}=\frac1{1+\sqrt3}$, so the area is \[\frac{10}{(1+\sqrt3)^2}=\frac{10}{4+2\sqrt3}=\frac{10(4-2\sqrt3)}{4}=\boxed{10-5\sqrt3}\approx1.34.\]

Solution 2 (Inradii)

Both triangles have the same centre. Each side of the small triangle is parallel to a side of the big one at distance $r$ (the centres are $r$ from the side they touch). So if the big triangle has inradius $\rho$, the small one has inradius $\rho-r$. The small triangle has side $2r$ and inradius $\frac{2r}{2\sqrt3}=\frac r{\sqrt3}$, so $\rho-r=\frac r{\sqrt3}$ and $\rho=r\cdot\frac{\sqrt3+1}{\sqrt3}$. The ratio of inradii is $\frac{1}{1+\sqrt3}$, the area ratio is its square, and the area is $\frac{10}{(1+\sqrt3)^2}=\boxed{10-5\sqrt3}$.

Problem B4

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure

An $N$-fold set is a collection of $N+1$ distinct positive integers such that if any one of the elements is removed, the sum of the remaining elements is not divisible by $N$. For example, $\{2,3,5,6\}$ is a $3$-fold set because none of the sums $2+3+5$, $2+3+6$, $2+5+6$, $3+5+6$ is divisible by $3$. What is the smallest possible sum of the elements of a $110$-fold set?

Show the answer

$6244$

Show the solution

Solution (One residue class is missing)

Let $S$ be the sum and $r$ the remainder of $S$ mod $110$. Removing $x$ leaves a multiple of $110$ exactly when $x\equiv S\equiv r\pmod{110}$. So a $110$-fold set is a set of $111$ distinct positive integers none of which is $\equiv r$, where $r=S\bmod110$.

Lower bound. Fix $r$. The smallest possible sum of $111$ distinct positive integers avoiding the class of $r$ is the sum of the $111$ smallest such integers:

  • $3\le r\le109$: $\{1,\dots,112\}\setminus\{r\}$, sum $6328-r$;
  • $r=1$: $\{2,\dots,110,112,113\}$, sum $6329$; $r=2$: $\{1,\dots,113\}\setminus\{2,112\}$, sum $6327$;
  • $r=0$: $\{1,\dots,112\}\setminus\{110\}$, sum $6218$.

The actual sum $S$ is at least this and $S\equiv r\pmod{110}$. For $r=0$ the next multiple of $110$ from $6218$ is $6270$. For $3\le r\le109$, $6328-r\equiv58-r$, and $S\equiv r$ needs an increase of $(2r-58)\bmod110$: for $r\le84$, $S\ge6328-r\ge6244$; for $85\le r\le109$, $S\ge6328-r+(2r-168)=6160+r\ge6245$. For $r=1,2$, $S>6300$. So $S\ge6244$, with equality only possible for $r=84$.

Construction. $\{1,2,\dots,112\}\setminus\{84\}$ has sum $6328-84=6244=56\cdot110+84$, and no element is $\equiv84\pmod{110}$ ($84$ is removed and $194>112$). So it is a $110$-fold set and the answer is $\boxed{6244}$.

Check. A set with sum at most $6244$ has largest element at most $6244-(1+2+\cdots+110)=139$; a computer search over all sets with elements below $140$ (dynamic programming on count and sum mod $110$, for each excluded class) confirms that $6244$ is the minimum.

Problem C1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation

Let $T_n=1+2+3+\cdots+n$ be the $n$th triangular number for $n\ge1$. For example, $T_1=1$, $T_2=1+2=3$, $T_3=1+2+3=6$.

  • (a) Prove that $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ for all $n\ge1$.
  • (b) Show that the expression $2T_nT_{n-1}$ is a triangular number $T_k$ and find $k$ in terms of $n$.
  • (c) Find the sum $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{2026}\frac1{T_n}$.
  • (d) Suppose there exist positive reals $a,b,c,d$ such that $\sqrt{aT_n+b}=cn+d$ for all positive integers $n$. Express $a,b,c$ in terms of $d$.
Show the answer

$\text{(b) }k=n^2-1;\quad\text{(c) }\tfrac{4052}{2027};\quad\text{(d) }a=8d^2,\ b=d^2,\ c=2d$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Solution)

(a) Method 1 (pairing). Write the sum forwards and backwards: \[2T_n=(1+n)+(2+(n-1))+\cdots+(n+1)=n(n+1),\] so $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$. Method 2 (induction). $T_1=1=\frac{1\cdot2}2$, and if $T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ then $T_{n+1}=\frac{n(n+1)}2+(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2$.

(b) For $n\ge2$, \[2T_nT_{n-1}=2\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}2\cdot\frac{(n-1)n}2=\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}2=\frac{(n^2-1)\bigl((n^2-1)+1\bigr)}2=T_{n^2-1}.\] So $k=n^2-1$. (Example: $2T_3T_2=36=T_8$.)

(c) $\frac1{T_n}=\frac2{n(n+1)}=2\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)$, so the sum telescopes: \[\sum_{n=1}^{2026}\frac1{T_n}=2\left(1-\frac1{2027}\right)=\frac{4052}{2027}.\]

(d) Squaring, $a\frac{n(n+1)}2+b=c^2n^2+2cdn+d^2$ for every positive integer $n$. Two quadratics that agree at infinitely many points are identical, so \[\frac a2=c^2,\qquad\frac a2=2cd,\qquad b=d^2.\] Then $c^2=2cd$ and $c>0$ give $c=2d$, so $a=2c^2=8d^2$, $b=d^2$. Check: $\sqrt{8d^2T_n+d^2}=d\sqrt{4n^2+4n+1}=d(2n+1)=cn+d$. \[\boxed{k=n^2-1,\quad\textstyle\sum=\frac{4052}{2027},\quad a=8d^2,\ b=d^2,\ c=2d}\]

Solution 2 (Another way to (b) and (d): the identity $8T_n+1=(2n+1)^2$)

Both (b) and (d) come from the identity $8T_n+1=4n^2+4n+1=(2n+1)^2$.

(b) A number $m$ is triangular, $m=T_k$, exactly when $8m+1=(2k+1)^2$. Here $8\cdot2T_nT_{n-1}+1=4n^2(n^2-1)+1=(2n^2-1)^2$, so $2T_nT_{n-1}=T_k$ with $2k+1=2n^2-1$, $k=n^2-1$.

(d) $\sqrt{8T_n+1}=2n+1$, so scaling by $d$: $\sqrt{8d^2T_n+d^2}=2dn+d$. This shows the values found in Solution 1 really work; that they are the only ones follows from the coefficient comparison there.

Problem C2

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration

The Atrium of the First Nations University of Canada is tiled with the following design, called a star-blanket design. It is made up of small rhombi, with all sides exactly $10$ cm long. There are two points labeled $A$ and $B$ in two corners of the design.

Assume that a path on the design from a grid point to another grid point travels only along the edges.

  • (a) How long is the shortest path from $A$ to $B$?
  • (b) How many different shortest paths are from $A$ to $B$?
  • (c) For how many pairs of grid points $P$ and $Q$ of the star-blanket design, does there exist a shortest path between $P$ and $Q$ that goes through the center of the design?
Show the answer

$\text{(a) }200\text{ cm};\quad\text{(b) }63504;\quad\text{(c) }19440$

Show the solution

Solution

Set-up. Measure in edges ($10$ cm). Let $O$ be the centre and number the eight rays from $O$ (along the edges of the star's large rhombi) $0,1,\dots,7$ counterclockwise, with $B$ between rays $0,1$ and $A$ between rays $4,5$. The design consists of $8$ large rhombi $R_0,\dots,R_7$, where $R_k$ lies between rays $k$ and $k+1$ (indices mod $8$) and is cut into a $5\times5$ grid; its grid points are "$i$ steps along ray $k$, then $j$ steps parallel to ray $k+1$", $0\le i,j\le5$. There are $1+8\cdot5+8\cdot25=241$ grid points.

Coordinates. Give the grid point $(i,j)$ of $R_k$ the vector $v=i\,e_k+j\,e_{k+1}$ in $\mathbb Z^8$, where $e_0,\dots,e_7$ are the unit vectors. This is well defined: two rhombi share only a ray (where both descriptions give the same multiple of one $e_k$) or the centre ($v=0$). Every edge changes exactly one coordinate by $1$. Write $|v|$ for the sum of the coordinates. Then $d(O,P)=|v_P|$, and for any two points \[d(P,Q)\ \ge\ \sum_k\bigl|v_P^{(k)}-v_Q^{(k)}\bigr|\qquad(\ell^1\text{ distance}),\] because each edge changes this sum by at most $1$. Call the set of nonzero coordinates of $v$ its support: a point on ray $k$ has support $\{k\}$, an interior point of $R_k$ has support $\{k,k+1\}$.

(a) $v_A=5e_4+5e_5$ and $v_B=5e_0+5e_1$, so $d(A,B)\ge20$, and going in to $O$ and out again takes $10+10=20$ edges. The shortest path is $20$ edges, i.e. $\boxed{200\text{ cm}}$.

(b) Along a path of length $20$ the $\ell^1$ distance to $v_B$ must drop by $1$ at every step, so each step lowers coordinate $4$ or $5$ or raises coordinate $0$ or $1$, and no other coordinate ever becomes positive. No grid point has a positive coordinate in $\{4,5\}$ and one in $\{0,1\}$ at the same time (supports are $\{k\}$ or $\{k,k+1\}$). So coordinates $4$ and $5$ must both reach $0$, i.e. the path reaches $O$, before coordinates $0,1$ start to grow. The first half is a monotone path in $R_4$ from $A$ to $O$ and the second a monotone path in $R_0$ from $O$ to $B$, each chosen in $\binom{10}5=252$ ways. Number of shortest paths: $252^2=\boxed{63504}$.

(c) The route through $O$ has length $|v_P|+|v_Q|$. If the supports of $P$ and $Q$ are disjoint, this equals the $\ell^1$ distance, which is a lower bound, so a shortest path through $O$ exists. If the supports share a ray $k$, with coordinates $c,c'>0$ there, let $t=\min(c,c')\ge1$ and go from $P$ to the point $t$ steps along ray $k$ and on to $Q$, staying inside the rhombi of $P$ and $Q$: the length is $|v_P|+|v_Q|-2t$, shorter than through $O$. So a pair qualifies exactly when one point is $O$ or the supports are disjoint. Counting unordered pairs of distinct points:

  • $O$ with any other point: $240$;
  • two points on different rays: $\binom82\cdot5^2=700$;
  • a ray point and an interior point of one of the $6$ rhombi not touching that ray: $8\cdot5\cdot6\cdot25=6000$;
  • interior points of two rhombi that are neither equal nor neighbours: $\left(\binom82-8\right)\cdot25^2=12500$.

Total $240+700+6000+12500=\boxed{19440}$ (unordered pairs, counting a pair with an endpoint at the centre). A breadth-first search over all $241$ grid points confirms $200$ cm, $63504$ paths and these pair counts.

Note. The problem does not say whether a pair with $P$ or $Q$ at the centre counts, or whether $(P,Q)$ and $(Q,P)$ are different. Leaving out pairs with an endpoint at the centre gives $19200$; counting ordered pairs doubles the numbers ($38880$ or $38400$).

Problem C3

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 32: Trigonometric Geometry and Metric Conversion

Consider a square $ABCD$ with center $K$. A point $P$ lies in the interior of the segment $KB$, and let $Q$ be a point on the circumcircle of $\triangle APC$, distinct from $A$ such that $AC=QC$.

  • (a) Let $\angle ACP=\theta$. Express $\angle QAP$ in terms of $\theta$.
  • (b) Suppose that $QP=4$ cm and $AC=3$ cm, determine the length $AP$ (in cm).
  • (c) Now suppose that $P$ varies arbitrarily across the interior of segment $KB$. Let $QP$ intersect $AC$ at point $X$. Determine the locus of $X$ as $P$ varies.
Show the answer

$\text{(a) }3\theta;\quad\text{(b) }\sqrt{13}-2\text{ cm};\quad\text{(c) the open segment from }A\text{ to the point }X_0\text{ of }AC\text{ with }AX_0=\tfrac23AC$

Show the solution

Solution (Angles, Ptolemy, and a formula for $X$)

Since $KB$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, $PA=PC$ and $\angle PAC=\angle PCA=\theta$. As $P$ runs over the interior of $KB$, $0<\theta<45^\circ$, and $\angle APC=180^\circ-2\theta$.

(a) Triangle $ACQ$ is isosceles with $CA=CQ$, so $\angle CAQ=\angle CQA$. If $Q$ were on the same arc $AC$ as $P$, then $\angle CQA=\angle CPA=180^\circ-2\theta>90^\circ$ and triangle $ACQ$ would have two obtuse angles. So $Q$ is on the other arc, $\angle CQA=180^\circ-\angle CPA=2\theta$, and $\angle CAQ=2\theta$. Since $P$ and $Q$ are on opposite sides of $AC$, \[\angle QAP=\angle QAC+\angle CAP=2\theta+\theta=\boxed{3\theta}.\]

(b) Method 1 (Ptolemy). $APCQ$ is a cyclic quadrilateral in this order, so $AC\cdot PQ=AP\cdot CQ+PC\cdot QA$. With $c=\cos\theta$: $AP=PC=\frac{AC}{2c}=\frac3{2c}$ and $QA=2\cdot AC\cos2\theta=6\cos2\theta$. So \[12=\frac3{2c}\bigl(3+6(2c^2-1)\bigr)\iff8c=12c^2-3\iff12c^2-8c-3=0,\] $c=\frac{2+\sqrt{13}}6$ (the positive root; it gives $\theta\approx21^\circ$, in range), and $AP=\frac3{2c}=\frac9{2+\sqrt{13}}=\boxed{\sqrt{13}-2}$.

Method 2 (law of sines). In triangle $APQ$: $\angle QAP=3\theta$ and $\angle AQP=\angle ACP=\theta$ (same arc $AP$). So $\frac{QP}{AP}=\frac{\sin3\theta}{\sin\theta}=3-4\sin^2\theta=4c^2-1$. With $AP=\frac3{2c}$: $\frac{8c}3=4c^2-1$, the same equation, so $AP=\sqrt{13}-2$.

(c) The points lie on the circle in the order $A,P,C,Q$ (from (a), $P$ and $Q$ are on opposite arcs $AC$), so the chords $AC$ and $PQ$ cross inside the circle: $X$ is an interior point of both segments. In triangle $APQ$, $\angle APQ=180^\circ-3\theta-\theta=180^\circ-4\theta$. In triangle $AXP$: $\angle XAP=\theta$, $\angle APX=180^\circ-4\theta$, so $\angle AXP=3\theta$ and by the law of sines $AX=AP\frac{\sin4\theta}{\sin3\theta}$. Taking $KA=KC=1$, $AP=\frac1{\cos\theta}$, and expanding $\sin4\theta=\sin3\theta\cos\theta+\cos3\theta\sin\theta$ gives \[AX=\frac{\sin4\theta}{\cos\theta\sin3\theta}=1+\tan\theta\cot3\theta,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\overrightarrow{KX}=\tan\theta\cot3\theta\;\overrightarrow{KC}\] (at $\theta=30^\circ$, $\cot3\theta=0$ and $X=K$). With $t=\tan\theta=KP/KB\in(0,1)$: $\tan\theta\cot3\theta=\frac{1-3t^2}{3-t^2}=3-\frac8{3-t^2}$, which decreases continuously from $\frac13$ (as $P\to K$) to $-1$ (as $P\to B$). So $X$ moves along $AC$ from the point $X_0$ with $KX_0=\frac13KC$ (that is, $AX_0=\frac23AC$) to $A$, both ends excluded. The locus is the open segment $AX_0$.

Problem C4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence

Given any set $D$ and a function $f:D\to D$, call $f$ an involution if $f(f(x))=x$ for all $x\in D$.

  • (a) Prove that if a function $g:D\to D$ is given such that $f$ and $f\circ g$ are involutions, then so is $g\circ f$.
  • (b) Let $D=\{1,2,\ldots,2025\}$, and let $g:D\to D$ be defined via \[g(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x=2025,\\x+1&\text{else.}\end{cases}\] How many involutions $f$ on $D$ are there for which $f\circ g$ is also an involution?
  • (c) Let $D=\{1,2,\ldots,2025\}$, and let $g$ be a function from $D$ to itself such that there are exactly $2025$ involutions $f$ for which $f\circ g$ is also an involution. What is the maximum number of fixed points of $g$: that is, values $x\in D$ such that $g(x)=x$?
Show the answer

$\text{(b) }2025;\quad\text{(c) }0$

Show the solution

Solution

Write $fg$ for $f\circ g$ and $\mathrm{id}$ for the identity.

(a) $fg$ is an involution: $fgfg=\mathrm{id}$. Then \[(gf)(gf)=f\,(f\,g f g)\,f=f\,\mathrm{id}\,f=ff=\mathrm{id},\] using $ff=\mathrm{id}$ twice. So $gf$ is an involution.

(b) Work mod $2025$ (so $g(x)=x+1$). For an involution $f$: $fgfg=\mathrm{id}\iff gfg=f$ (multiply by $f$ on the left). So $f(x)=g(f(g(x)))=f(x+1)+1$, i.e. $f(x+1)=f(x)-1$, which means $f(x)\equiv c-x\pmod{2025}$ for a constant $c$. Conversely each of these $2025$ maps is an involution ($c-(c-x)=x$) and satisfies $gfg=f$. Answer $\boxed{2025}$.

(c) If $fg$ is an involution it is a bijection, so $g$ is a bijection; then the condition is $fgf=g^{-1}$. Such an $f$ maps each cycle of $g$ onto a cycle of the same length, reversing its direction. For a cycle of length $L$: $f$ maps it to itself in $L$ ways (the "reflections" of (b); just $1$ way if $L=1$), or swaps it with another cycle of length $L$, in $L$ ways (the choice on the first cycle fixes $f$ on the second). Hence the number of good $f$ is \[N(g)=\prod_LF_L,\qquad F_L=\sum_k\binom{m_L}{2k}(2k-1)!!\,L^{m_L-k},\] where $m_L$ is the number of $L$-cycles and $(2k-1)!!$ (with $(-1)!!=1$) counts the ways to pair up $2k$ chosen cycles. ($F_1$ is the number of involutions of the $m_1$ fixed points.)

We need $N(g)=2025$, which is odd, so every $F_L$ is odd.

  • $L=1$: the number of involutions of $m$ points is even for $m\ge2$ (they satisfy $I_m=I_{m-1}+(m-1)I_{m-2}$ with $I_2=2$, $I_3=4$, and induction keeps them even). So $m_1\le1$.
  • $L$ even: every term has a factor $L^{m_L-k}$ with $m_L-k\ge1$ (if $m_L\ge1$), so $F_L$ is even. So no even cycles.
  • $L$ odd $\ge3$: $F_L\equiv I_{m_L}\pmod2$, so $m_L\le1$.

So $g$ has at most one fixed point and otherwise distinct odd cycle lengths $\ell_1<\dots<\ell_r$ (all $\ge3$), and $N(g)=\ell_1\cdots\ell_r=2025$.

If $g$ had a fixed point, then $\ell_1+\dots+\ell_r=2024$ while $\ell_1\cdots\ell_r=2025$. For $r=1$ that says $2024=2025$; for $r\ge2$ the product exceeds the sum by much more than $1$ (for two lengths, $\ell_1\ell_2-\ell_1-\ell_2=(\ell_1-1)(\ell_2-1)-1\ge2\cdot4-1=7$; appending another length $L\ge3$ to lengths with product $P$ raises "product minus sum" by $(L-1)P-L>0$). So $g$ has no fixed point. The cycle $g$ of part (b) shows $N(g)=2025$ is possible.

The maximum number of fixed points is $\boxed{0}$.
