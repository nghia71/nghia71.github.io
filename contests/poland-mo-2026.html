Problem 1

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments

Positive integers $a,b,n$ satisfying $2a=bn$ are given. We divide the set $\{1,2,\dots,2a\}$ into $n$ pairwise disjoint sets, each with $b$ elements. Prove that we can divide these $n$ subsets into two groups such that the union of the sets in the first group contains at least $\frac a2-\frac b4$ numbers that are less than or equal to $a$ and the union of the sets in the second group contains at least $\frac a2-\frac b4$ numbers that are greater than or equal to $a$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sort by the number of small elements and cut in the middle)

Call the numbers $1,\dots,a$ small and $a+1,\dots,2a$ large; there are $a$ of each. A number $>a$ is in particular $\ge a$, so it suffices to make the second group contain at least $\frac a2-\frac b4$ large numbers.

For each of the $n$ sets $S_j$ let $l_j$ be its number of small elements; it has $b-l_j$ large elements, and $\sum_j l_j=a$. Number the sets so that $l_1\ge l_2\ge\dots\ge l_n$, let $k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, and put $S_1,\dots,S_k$ into the first group and the rest into the second.

Let $F=l_1+\dots+l_k$ (small numbers in the first group). Each of $l_1,\dots,l_k$ is at least each of $l_{k+1},\dots,l_n$, so $(n-k)F\ge k(a-F)$, i.e. $F\ge\frac{ka}{n}=\frac{kb}2$ (using $a=\frac{bn}2$). Since $k\ge\frac{n-1}2$, \[F\ge\frac{(n-1)b}{4}=\frac a2-\frac b4 .\] The second group contains $G=\sum_{j>k}(b-l_j)=(n-k)b-(a-F)$ large numbers, so with $F\ge\frac{kb}2$ and $k\le\frac n2$, \[G\ge(n-k)b-\frac{nb}2+\frac{kb}2=\frac{(n-k)b}2\ge\frac{nb}4=\frac a2 .\] Both groups satisfy the requirement. (If $n=1$, then $k=0$, the first group is empty and $\frac a2-\frac b4=0$, so the claim holds trivially; for $n\ge2$ both groups are nonempty.)

Solution 2 (Sort, then deal the sets out alternately)

Call $1,\dots,a$ small and $a+1,\dots,2a$ large. Since every large number is $\ge a$, it is enough to find two groups where the first contains at least $\frac a2-\frac b4$ small numbers and the second at least $\frac a2-\frac b4$ large numbers.

Let $l_j$ be the number of small elements of the $j$-th set, ordered so that $l_1\ge l_2\ge\dots\ge l_n$; then $\sum l_j=a$ and the $j$-th set has $b-l_j$ large elements. Deal the sets like cards: sets with odd index go to the first group, sets with even index to the second, except that when $n$ is odd the last set $S_n$ goes to the second group.

$n=2m$ even. The first group has $F=l_1+l_3+\dots+l_{2m-1}\ge l_2+l_4+\dots+l_{2m}=a-F$, so $F\ge\frac a2$. The second group has $\sum_{i=1}^m(b-l_{2i})=mb-(a-F)=F\ge\frac a2$ large numbers (as $mb=a$).

$n=2m+1$ odd. The first group consists of $S_1,S_3,\dots,S_{2m-1}$, and $F=l_1+l_3+\dots+l_{2m-1}\ge l_2+l_4+\dots+l_{2m}=a-l_n-F$, so $F\ge\frac{a-l_n}2$. The smallest value $l_n$ is at most the average $\frac an=\frac b2$, hence $F\ge\frac a2-\frac b4$. The second group ($m+1$ sets) has \[(m+1)b-(a-F)=(m+1)b-\frac{(2m+1)b}2+F=\frac b2+F\ge\frac b2+\frac a2-\frac b4\ge\frac a2\] large numbers. In both cases the requirement is met.

Problem 2

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods

Positive integers $a,b$ are given. Prove that $a\mid b-1$ if and only if \[\left\lfloor\frac ab\cdot n\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac ab\cdot\left(n-\frac12\right)\right\rfloor\] for all $n=1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac b2\right\rfloor$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Translate to remainders of $an$ modulo $b$)

Reformulation. For $n\ge1$ write $an=bQ_n+r_n$ with $0\le r_n<b$. Then $\lfloor\frac{an}b\rfloor=Q_n$ and \[\frac ab\Bigl(n-\frac12\Bigr)=Q_n+\frac{r_n-a/2}{b},\qquad\text{where }\frac{r_n-a/2}{b}<1 .\] So the two floors are equal if and only if $r_n\ge\frac a2$. The statement becomes: $a\mid b-1$ if and only if \[r_n\ge\frac a2\quad\text{for all }1\le n\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac b2\Bigr\rfloor. \tag{C}\] If $b=1$, both sides hold ($a\mid0$, and (C) is empty). Assume $b\ge2$. We shall use the fact: if $1\le d\le e$ then $(e\bmod d)<\frac e2$ (if $d\le\frac e2$ the remainder is $<d\le\frac e2$; otherwise it equals $e-d<\frac e2$).

$a\mid b-1$ implies (C). Then $b\equiv1\pmod a$. Suppose $r_n<\frac a2$ for some $n\le\frac b2$. Then $Q_n\le\frac{an}b\le\frac a2$ and $an=bQ_n+r_n\equiv Q_n+r_n\pmod a$, so $a\mid Q_n+r_n$. But $Q_n+r_n\ge1$ (as $an>0$) and $Q_n+r_n<\frac a2+\frac a2=a$, a contradiction.

(C) implies $a\mid b-1$. Suppose $a\nmid b-1$ (so $a\ge2$); we find $n\le\lfloor b/2\rfloor$ with $r_n<\frac a2$.

  • $a\ge b$: take $n=1$; $r_1=a\bmod b<\frac a2$ by the fact.

  • $a<b$: write $b=qa+\rho$ with $q\ge1$, $0\le\rho<a$; by assumption $\rho\ne1$.

    • $\rho=0$: take $n=q=\frac ba\le\frac b2$; then $r_n=0<\frac a2$.

    • $\rho\ge2$: let $j=\lfloor a/\rho\rfloor\ge1$ and $n=jq+1$. Then \[an=j(qa)+a=j(b-\rho)+a=jb+(a-j\rho),\qquad 0\le a-j\rho=(a\bmod\rho)<\rho<b,\] so $r_n=a\bmod\rho<\frac a2$ by the fact. Moreover $j\le\frac a\rho\le\frac a2$, so $n\le\frac{aq}2+1\le\frac{aq+\rho}2=\frac b2$.

In each case (C) fails. This proves the equivalence.

Solution 2 (Swap the roles: residues of $mb$ modulo $a$)

Reformulation. For real $u<v$, $\lfloor u\rfloor\ne\lfloor v\rfloor$ exactly when some integer $m$ satisfies $u<m\le v$. So the two floors at $n$ differ if and only if there is an integer $m$ with \[\frac ab\Bigl(n-\frac12\Bigr)<m\le\frac ab\,n,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad n-\frac12<\frac{mb}a\le n .\] Then $\frac{mb}a>\frac12>0$, so $m\ge1$, and $n$ is the integer in $\bigl[\frac{mb}a,\frac{mb}a+\frac12\bigr)$, namely $n=\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil$, with $\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil-\frac{mb}a<\frac12$. Conversely, every $m\ge1$ with $\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil-\frac{mb}a<\frac12$ makes the floors differ at $n=\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil\ge1$. Let $\rho_m$ be the remainder of $mb$ modulo $a$. If $\rho_m=0$ then $\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil-\frac{mb}a=0$; otherwise it equals $1-\frac{\rho_m}a$, which is $<\frac12$ iff $\rho_m>\frac a2$. Call $m\ge1$ bad if \[\Bigl\lceil\frac{mb}a\Bigr\rceil\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac b2\Bigr\rfloor\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_m\in\{0\}\cup\Bigl(\frac a2,a\Bigr).\] We have shown: the floors are equal for all $1\le n\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$ if and only if no $m$ is bad. So we must prove that $a\mid b-1$ if and only if no $m$ is bad.

If $a\mid b-1$, no $m$ is bad. Let $m$ satisfy $\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$. Then $\frac{mb}a\le\frac b2$, so $1\le m\le\frac a2<a$. Since $b\equiv1\pmod a$, we get $\rho_m=m\in\bigl[1,\frac a2\bigr]$, which is neither $0$ nor $>\frac a2$. So $m$ is not bad.

If $a\nmid b-1$, some $m$ is bad. Then $a\ge2$ and $b\ne1$, so $b\ge2$. Write $b=qa+\rho$ with $q\ge0$ and $0\le\rho<a$; by assumption $\rho\ne1$. Note that $\rho_m$ is the remainder of $m\rho$ modulo $a$, since $mb=mqa+m\rho$.

  • $\rho=0$ or $\rho>\frac a2$: take $m=1$; $\rho_1=\rho$ has the required form. If $q=0$, then $\lceil\frac ba\rceil=1\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$ as $b\ge2$. If $q\ge1$ and $\rho=0$, then $\lceil\frac ba\rceil=q$ and $b=qa\ge2q$, so $q\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$. If $q\ge1$ and $\rho>\frac a2$, then $\rho\ge2$ (as $\rho>\frac a2\ge1$), $\lceil\frac ba\rceil=q+1$, and $b=qa+\rho\ge2q+2$, so $q+1\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$.

  • $2\le\rho\le\frac a2$ (so $a\ge4$): take $m=\bigl\lfloor\frac a{2\rho}\bigr\rfloor+1$. Then $m>\frac a{2\rho}$ and $m\le\frac a{2\rho}+1$, so \[\frac a2<m\rho\le\frac a2+\rho\le a .\] Hence $\rho_m=m\rho\in(\frac a2,a)$, or $\rho_m=0$ if $m\rho=a$. Moreover $0<m\rho\le a$ gives $\lceil\frac{mb}a\rceil=mq+\lceil\frac{m\rho}a\rceil=mq+1$. If $q=0$ this is $1\le\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$. If $q\ge1$, then $m\le\frac a{2\rho}+1\le\frac a4+1$, and using $a\ge4$ and $\rho\ge2$, \[mq+1\le\frac{qa}4+q+1\le\frac{qa}4+\frac{qa}4+\frac\rho2=\frac b2,\] so the integer $mq+1$ is at most $\lfloor\frac b2\rfloor$.

In every case some $m$ is bad. This proves the equivalence.

Problem 3

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 26: Inequalities: Symmetry, Homogenization, and Advanced Structure

A positive integer $n$ and positive real numbers $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n$ with $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=1$ are given. Prove that \[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2\right)^2\sqrt n\ge\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^3.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (The largest variable)

Write $S_2=\sum x_i^2$, $S_3=\sum x_i^3$ and let $x_{\max}$ be the largest $x_i$.

First, $S_3=\sum x_i\cdot x_i^2\le x_{\max}S_2$, and $x_{\max}^2\le S_2$, so $S_3\le S_2^{3/2}$.

Second, by Cauchy-Schwarz (or QM-AM), $1=\bigl(\sum x_i\bigr)^2\le n\sum x_i^2=nS_2$, so $\sqrt{nS_2}\ge1$.

Therefore \[S_3\le S_2^{3/2}\le S_2^{3/2}\sqrt{nS_2}=\sqrt n\,S_2^2,\] which is the claim. (Equality needs $x_{\max}^2=S_2$, i.e. $n=1$; for $n=1$ both sides equal $1$.)

Solution 2 (Homogenise and use Cauchy-Schwarz twice)

With $S_k=\sum x_i^k$, the claim is the homogeneous inequality $S_1S_3\le\sqrt n\,S_2^2$ (both sides have degree $4$; here $S_1=1$). By Cauchy-Schwarz, \[S_1=\sum 1\cdot x_i\le\sqrt{n}\sqrt{S_2},\qquad S_3=\sum x_i\cdot x_i^2\le\sqrt{S_2}\sqrt{S_4}.\] Moreover $S_4=\sum x_i^4\le\bigl(\sum x_i^2\bigr)^2=S_2^2$, because expanding the square adds only nonnegative cross terms $2x_i^2x_j^2$. Multiplying, \[S_1S_3\le\sqrt n\,S_2\sqrt{S_4}\le\sqrt n\,S_2^2,\] and with $S_1=1$ this is $\bigl(\sum x_i^2\bigr)^2\sqrt n\ge\sum x_i^3$.

Problem 4

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 5: Polynomial Structure and Algebraic Identities

A function $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ satisfies $f(x)=f\!\left(\frac1x\right)$ for all real numbers $x\ne0$. Assume that there exist polynomials $P,Q$ with real coefficients such that $f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ for all real numbers $x$. Prove that there exist polynomials $R,S$ with real coefficients such that \[f(x)=\frac{R\left(x+\frac1x\right)}{S\left(x+\frac1x\right)}\] for all real numbers $x\ne0$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Make numerator and denominator symmetric)

Lemma. For every $k\ge0$ there is a polynomial $T_k$ with real (in fact integer) coefficients such that $x^k+x^{-k}=T_k\bigl(x+\frac1x\bigr)$ for all $x\ne0$. Indeed $T_0=2$, $T_1(t)=t$, and $x^{k+1}+x^{-k-1}=\bigl(x+\frac1x\bigr)(x^k+x^{-k})-(x^{k-1}+x^{-k+1})$ gives $T_{k+1}=tT_k-T_{k-1}$. Consequently, if $L(x)=\sum_{k=-d}^dc_kx^k$ with $c_{-k}=c_k$ (a symmetric Laurent polynomial), then $L(x)=c_0+\sum_{k=1}^dc_k(x^k+x^{-k})$ is a polynomial in $t=x+\frac1x$.

Proof. Since $f=P/Q$ for all real $x$, $Q$ has no real zero. For $x\ne0$ put \[N(x)=P(x)\,Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr),\qquad D(x)=Q(x)\,Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)\ne0 .\] Then $\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=f(x)$. Clearly $D(1/x)=D(x)$, and also \[N\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)=P\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)Q(x)=f\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)Q(x)=f(x)\,Q(x)\,Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)=P(x)\,Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)=N(x).\] $N$ and $D$ are Laurent polynomials. If a Laurent polynomial $L$ with exponents in $[-d,d]$ satisfies $L(x)=L(1/x)$ for all real $x\ne0$, then the polynomial $x^d\bigl(L(x)-L(1/x)\bigr)$ has infinitely many zeros, so it is the zero polynomial, i.e. $c_k=c_{-k}$ for all $k$. Hence, by the Lemma, $N(x)=R(x+\frac1x)$ and $D(x)=S(x+\frac1x)$ for polynomials $R,S$ with real coefficients, and for every real $x\ne0$ \[f(x)=\frac{R\left(x+\frac1x\right)}{S\left(x+\frac1x\right)},\qquad S\Bigl(x+\frac1x\Bigr)=D(x)\ne0 .\]

Solution 2 (Reciprocal polynomials)

For $k\ge0$ the identity $x^k+x^{-k}=T_k(x+\frac1x)$ holds with polynomials $T_0=2$, $T_1=t$, $T_{k+1}=tT_k-T_{k-1}$ (from $(x+\frac1x)(x^k+x^{-k})=(x^{k+1}+x^{-k-1})+(x^{k-1}+x^{1-k})$).

If $P=0$ take $R=0$, $S=1$. Otherwise cancel the greatest common divisor of $P$ and $Q$; we may then assume $P,Q$ coprime, and $Q$ still has no real zero (it divides the original denominator, which has none, because $f=P/Q$ at every real $x$). Let $d=\max(\deg P,\deg Q)$ and define $P^*(x)=x^dP(1/x)$, $Q^*(x)=x^dQ(1/x)$, which are polynomials of degree $\le d$. Since $Q(0)\ne0$, $\deg Q^*=d$.

From $f(x)=f(1/x)$ we get $P(x)Q^*(x)=P^*(x)Q(x)$ for all real $x\ne0$, hence as polynomials. As $\gcd(P,Q)=1$, $Q\mid Q^*$; write $Q^*=QH$, so $P^*=PH$. If $\deg Q<d$, then $\deg P=d$ and $\deg H=d-\deg Q\ge1$, so $\deg P^*=\deg(PH)>d$, impossible. Thus $\deg Q=d$, $H=\lambda$ is a constant, and $Q^*=\lambda Q$, $P^*=\lambda P$. Applying $*$ twice gives back $Q$ (as $\deg Q=d$), so $\lambda^2=1$.

If $\lambda=-1$, then $Q(1)=Q^*(1)=-Q(1)$, so $Q(1)=0$; impossible. So $\lambda=1$. If $d$ were odd, $Q(-1)=(-1)^dQ^*(-1)=-Q(-1)$ would give $Q(-1)=0$; impossible. So $d=2e$ is even and $Q^*=Q$, $P^*=P$ mean that the coefficients of $x^{e+k}$ and $x^{e-k}$ agree in $P$ and in $Q$. Therefore \[x^{-e}Q(x)=q_e+\sum_{k=1}^e q_{e+k}(x^k+x^{-k})=S\Bigl(x+\frac1x\Bigr),\qquad x^{-e}P(x)=R\Bigl(x+\frac1x\Bigr)\] with $S=q_e+\sum_k q_{e+k}T_k$ and $R$ defined in the same way from the coefficients of $P$. For real $x\ne0$, $S(x+\frac1x)=x^{-e}Q(x)\ne0$ and $f(x)=\frac{x^{-e}P(x)}{x^{-e}Q(x)}=\frac{R(x+1/x)}{S(x+1/x)}$.

Solution 3 (Write every polynomial as $A(t)+xB(t)$ and multiply by the conjugate)

For real $x\ne0$ put $t=x+\frac1x$; then $x^2=tx-1$.

Step 1: reduction. For every polynomial $P$ with real coefficients there are polynomials $A,B$ with real coefficients such that $P(x)=A(t)+xB(t)$ for all real $x\ne0$. It suffices to show this for $x^k$, by induction on $k$: $x^0=1+x\cdot0$, and if $x^k=A_k(t)+xB_k(t)$, then \[x^{k+1}=xA_k(t)+x^2B_k(t)=xA_k(t)+(tx-1)B_k(t)=-B_k(t)+x\bigl(A_k(t)+tB_k(t)\bigr).\] Replacing $x$ by $\frac1x$ does not change $t$, so also $P(\frac1x)=A(t)+\frac1xB(t)$; and $\frac1x=t-x$.

Step 2: conjugate. Since $f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ for every real $x$, $Q$ has no real zero. Write $P(x)=A(t)+xB(t)$ and $Q(x)=C(t)+xD(t)$ as in Step 1, and for $x\ne0$ multiply numerator and denominator of $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ by $Q(\frac1x)=C+\frac Dx=(C+tD)-xD$ (here $A,B,C,D$ stand for $A(t),\dots$), which is nonzero. Using $x^2=tx-1$, \begin{align*} Q(x)Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)&=(C+xD)(C+tD-xD)=C^2+tCD+x\,tD^2-x^2D^2\\ &=C^2+tCD+D^2,\\ P(x)Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)&=(A+xB)(C+tD-xD)=AC+tAD+BD+x\,(BC-AD). \end{align*} So with the polynomials $W=C^2+tCD+D^2$, $U=AC+tAD+BD$, $V=BC-AD$ we have, for all real $x\ne0$, \[f(x)=\frac{U(t)+xV(t)}{W(t)},\qquad W(t)=Q(x)Q\Bigl(\frac1x\Bigr)\ne0 .\]

Step 3: the $x$-part vanishes. The identity of Step 2 holds for every $x\ne0$, so we may apply it at $\frac1x$, which has the same $t$: $f(\frac1x)=\frac{U(t)+\frac1xV(t)}{W(t)}$. Since $f(x)=f(\frac1x)$, we get $\bigl(x-\frac1x\bigr)V\bigl(x+\frac1x\bigr)=0$ for all $x\ne0$. For $x>1$ the first factor is nonzero, so $V(t)=0$ for every $t=x+\frac1x$ with $x>1$, i.e. for every $t>2$ (the map $x\mapsto x+\frac1x$ is continuous and increasing on $(1,\infty)$, from $2$ to $\infty$). A polynomial with infinitely many zeros is zero, so $V=0$.

Conclusion. Taking $R=U$ and $S=W$, both polynomials with real coefficients, \[f(x)=\frac{R\left(x+\frac1x\right)}{S\left(x+\frac1x\right)}\qquad\text{for all real }x\ne0,\] and the denominator never vanishes there.

Problem 5

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure

An acute triangle $ABC$ with $AB<AC$ is given. Its incircle touches sides $BC,CA,AB$ at $D,E,F$ respectively. Points $X,Y$ lie on the line $EF$ such that $\angle XBC=\angle YCB=90^\circ$. Point $M$ is the midpoint of segment $BC$. The line symmetric to $BX$ with respect to $MX$ and the line symmetric to $CY$ with respect to $MY$ meet at $Z$. Prove that $BC\perp ZD$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Second tangents to the circle on diameter $BC$; coordinates)

Let $a,b,c$ be the side lengths, $s$ the semiperimeter, so $BD=BF=s-b$, $CD=CE=s-c$; let $R=\frac a2$ and let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $BC$ (centre $M$, dashed in the figure).

Step 1: where $X$ and $Y$ are. As $\angle B<90^\circ$, the ray from $B$ perpendicular to $BC$ on the side of $A$ lies on the other side of line $AB$ than $C$, making the angle $90^\circ-\angle B$ with $BA$. The ray from $F$ that continues $EF$ beyond $F$ also lies on that side (since $E$ is on the side of $C$) and makes the angle $\angle AFE=90^\circ-\frac{\angle A}2$ with $FB$ (vertical angles; $AE=AF$). These angles add up to less than $180^\circ$, so the rays meet; their meeting point is $X$. In triangle $BFX$ the angle at $X$ is $\angle B+\frac{\angle A}2$, and the law of sines gives \[BX=\frac{(s-b)\cos\frac A2}{\sin(B+\frac A2)},\qquad\text{similarly}\qquad CY=\frac{(s-c)\cos\frac A2}{\sin(C+\frac A2)} .\] Since $(B+\frac A2)+(C+\frac A2)=180^\circ$, the sines are equal and $\dfrac{BX}{CY}=\dfrac{s-b}{s-c}$. Both $X$ and $Y$ lie on the same side of $BC$ as $A$.

Step 2: the two reflected lines. $BX\perp MB$, so $BX$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $B$. The reflection in line $MX$ maps $\omega$ to itself (its centre $M$ is on the mirror), so it maps $BX$ to the tangent to $\omega$ at the image $U$ of $B$; this is the second tangent from $X$ ($U\ne B$, since $B$ is not on line $MX$). Likewise the line symmetric to $CY$ with respect to $MY$ is the second tangent $YV$ from $Y$. So $Z$ is the intersection of the tangents to $\omega$ at $U$ and $V$.

Step 3: coordinates. Take $M$ as origin, $BC$ as $x$-axis, $B=(-R,0)$, $C=(R,0)$, $X=(-R,p)$, $Y=(R,q)$ with $p=BX>0$, $q=CY>0$. The tangent at $(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta)$ is $x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=R$. With $t=\tan\frac\theta2$, $\cos\theta=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}$, $\sin\theta=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}$:

  • it passes through $X$ iff $-R(1-t^2)+2pt=R(1+t^2)$, i.e. $t=\frac Rp$ (the point $B$ corresponds to $\theta=180^\circ$);

  • it passes through $Y$ iff $R(1-t^2)+2qt=R(1+t^2)$, i.e. $t=\frac qR$ (the solution $t=0$ is $C$).

So $U,V$ have angles $\theta_1,\theta_2\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ with $\tan\frac{\theta_1}2\tan\frac{\theta_2}2=\frac qp>0$. The point $\frac{R}{\cos\delta}(\cos\mu,\sin\mu)$, $\mu=\frac{\theta_1+\theta_2}2$, $\delta=\frac{\theta_1-\theta_2}2$, lies on both tangents (substituting gives $\frac{R\cos(\mu-\theta_i)}{\cos\delta}=R$), and $\cos\delta\ne0$; so it is $Z$. Hence \[x_Z=R\,\frac{\cos\mu}{\cos\delta}=R\,\frac{1-\tan\frac{\theta_1}2\tan\frac{\theta_2}2}{1+\tan\frac{\theta_1}2\tan\frac{\theta_2}2}=R\,\frac{p-q}{p+q},\qquad y_Z=R\,\frac{\sin\mu}{\cos\delta}>0 .\]

Step 4: conclusion. By Step 1, $\frac pq=\frac{s-b}{s-c}$, so \[x_Z=\frac a2\cdot\frac{(s-b)-(s-c)}{(s-b)+(s-c)}=\frac a2\cdot\frac{c-b}{a}=\frac{c-b}2,\] while $D$ has abscissa $BD-R=(s-b)-\frac a2=\frac{c-b}2$. So $Z$ and $D$ have the same abscissa, $Z\ne D$ (as $y_Z>0$), and $ZD\perp BC$.

Solution 2 (Brianchon and the diagonals of a trapezoid)

Let $\omega$ be the circle with diameter $BC$ and centre $M$, and $s$ the semiperimeter, so $BD=s-b$, $CD=s-c$.

$Z$ is the meet of the second tangents. $BX\perp BM$ means that $BX$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $B$. Reflecting in line $XM$ (which passes through the centre) maps $\omega$ to itself and the tangent $XB$ to the other tangent $XU$ from $X$; similarly the reflection of $CY$ in $MY$ is the second tangent $YV$ from $Y$. Thus $Z=XU\cap YV$. Being the intersection of two tangents, $Z$ lies outside $\omega$, whereas $D$ (an interior point of the diameter $BC$) lies inside; so $Z\ne D$.

The ratio $BX:CY$. Since $\angle B$ is acute, $X$ is the meeting point of the ray from $B$ perpendicular to $BC$ (on the side of $A$) with the extension of $EF$ beyond $F$; in triangle $BFX$ the angles at $B$ and $F$ are $90^\circ-B$ and $\angle AFE=90^\circ-\frac A2$, so the angle at $X$ is $B+\frac A2$. The law of sines gives $BX=\frac{(s-b)\cos(A/2)}{\sin(B+A/2)}$, and in the same way $CY=\frac{(s-c)\cos(A/2)}{\sin(C+A/2)}$. As $\sin(B+\frac A2)=\sin(180^\circ-C-\frac A2)=\sin(C+\frac A2)$, \[\frac{BX}{CY}=\frac{s-b}{s-c}=\frac{BD}{DC}. \tag{1}\]

Brianchon. The tangents to $\omega$ at $B$ and $C$ are parallel (both perpendicular to $BC$); in the projective plane they meet at a point $I_\infty$ at infinity. Consider the hexagon $X\,Z\,Y\,C\,I_\infty\,B$ circumscribed about $\omega$: its sides lie on the tangents at $U$, $V$, $C$, $C$, $B$, $B$ (the vertices $C$ and $B$ are the points where two consecutive sides lie on the same tangent). Brianchon's theorem for six tangent lines $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_6$ stays true when $\ell_3=\ell_4$ and $\ell_5=\ell_6$, the vertex $\ell_3\cap\ell_4$ (and $\ell_5\cap\ell_6$) then being the point of contact; it is the projective dual of Pascal's theorem with two tangents, and it holds in the projective plane, so a vertex may be a point at infinity. By Brianchon's theorem the three main diagonals $XC$, $ZI_\infty$ and $YB$ are concurrent. The line $ZI_\infty$ is the line through $Z$ parallel to $BX$, i.e. perpendicular to $BC$. Hence the perpendicular from $Z$ to $BC$ passes through $L=XC\cap YB$.

The foot of $L$. In the trapezoid $BXYC$ ($BX\parallel CY$) the triangles $LBX$ and $LYC$ are similar, so $\frac{BL}{LY}=\frac{BX}{CY}$. Projecting perpendicularly onto $BC$ (which maps $B\mapsto B$, $Y\mapsto C$) the foot $K$ of $L$ satisfies $\frac{BK}{KC}=\frac{BL}{LY}=\frac{BX}{CY}$, and by (1) this equals $\frac{BD}{DC}$. So $K=D$.

Therefore the perpendicular to $BC$ through $Z$ passes through $D$, i.e. $ZD\perp BC$.

Problem 6

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

An integer $n\ge2$ is given. Wojtek has $n$ bamboos $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_n$ in his garden. All bamboos have a constant rate of growth: every day the bamboo $B_i$ grows by $a_i>0$ meters. Every day at midnight Wojtek can choose one bamboo and cut it to the ground, making its height $0$. The bamboo doesn't stop growing and its rate of growth doesn't change. At the beginning, all the bamboos have height equal to $0$. Assume that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=1$. Prove that Wojtek can guarantee that the height of every bamboo will always be at most $2$ meters.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Round to powers of two, then split days into even and odd)

Rounding. Since $n\ge2$, every $a_i<1$, so $\frac2{a_i}>2$. Let $p_i=2^{k_i}$ be the largest power of $2$ with $p_i\le\frac2{a_i}$; then $k_i\ge1$ and $2p_i>\frac2{a_i}$, i.e. \[a_ip_i\le2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac1{p_i}<a_i,\quad\text{so}\quad\sum_{i=1}^n2^{-k_i}<\sum a_i=1. \tag{1}\] If bamboo $i$ is cut on all days of a residue class modulo $p_i$, it is never more than $p_i$ days without a cut (also at the start, if the representative is chosen in $\{1,\dots,p_i\}$), so its height never exceeds $a_ip_i\le2$. On a day that no bamboo needs, Wojtek cuts anything. So it suffices to prove:

Lemma. If $k_1,\dots,k_n\ge0$ are integers with $\sum2^{-k_i}\le1$, there are residues $r_i$ such that the classes $r_i\bmod 2^{k_i}$ are pairwise disjoint.

Proof by induction on $K=\max k_i$. If some $k_i=0$, then $2^{-k_i}=1$ forces $n=1$, and the single class is all integers. Otherwise all $k_i\ge1$. Sort so that $k_1\le\dots\le k_n$, and let $j$ be the largest index with $s_j=2^{-k_1}+\dots+2^{-k_j}\le\frac12$. If $j<n$, then $s_j$ and $\frac12$ are both multiples of $2^{-k_{j+1}}$ (as $k_1,\dots,k_j\le k_{j+1}$ and $k_{j+1}\ge1$), and $s_j+2^{-k_{j+1}}>\frac12$; hence $s_j=\frac12$. In all cases the indices split into a group $G_0=\{1,\dots,j\}$ and $G_1=\{j+1,\dots,n\}$ with $\sum_{i\in G_g}2^{-k_i}\le\frac12$, i.e. $\sum_{i\in G_g}2^{-(k_i-1)}\le1$. By the induction hypothesis (maximum $K-1$) there are residues $r_i'$ with disjoint classes $r_i'\bmod2^{k_i-1}$ inside each group. Put $r_i=2r_i'+g$ for $i\in G_g$. The class $r_i\bmod2^{k_i}$ is $\{2t+g:\ t\equiv r_i'\pmod{2^{k_i-1}}\}$; classes in different groups have different parity, and within a group they are disjoint because the classes of the $t$'s are. $\square$

By (1) the Lemma applies to $k_1,\dots,k_n$; cutting bamboo $i$ on the days of its class keeps every bamboo at height at most $2$ forever.

Solution 2 (Round to powers of two, then an explicit schedule by reversing binary digits)

Rounding. As $n\ge2$, each $a_i<1$. Let $p_i=2^{k_i}$ be the largest power of two not exceeding $\frac2{a_i}$ (so $k_i\ge1$). Then $a_ip_i\le2$, and $p_i>\frac1{a_i}$ gives $\sum_i2^{-k_i}<\sum_ia_i=1$. It is enough to give disjoint residue classes $r_i\bmod2^{k_i}$: cutting bamboo $i$ exactly on the days $\equiv r_i\pmod{2^{k_i}}$ (on other days cut anything) lets it grow for at most $p_i$ days between cuts (and before the first cut, choosing $1\le r_i\le p_i$), so its height stays at most $a_ip_i\le2$.

Explicit classes. Sort $k_1\le k_2\le\dots\le k_n$ and let $K=k_n$. Define \[N_i=\sum_{j<i}2^{K-k_j}\qquad(i=1,\dots,n).\] Each term $2^{K-k_j}$ with $j<i$ is divisible by $2^{K-k_i}$ (as $k_j\le k_i$), so $N_i$ is a multiple of $2^{K-k_i}$, and since $\sum_j2^{K-k_j}<2^K$ the blocks \[J_i=\{N_i,N_i+1,\dots,N_i+2^{K-k_i}-1\}\] are disjoint and lie in $\{0,1,\dots,2^K-1\}$. Writing numbers in this range with exactly $K$ binary digits, $J_i$ is the set of numbers whose first $k_i$ digits form a fixed string $w_i$ (the binary form of $N_i/2^{K-k_i}$) and whose last $K-k_i$ digits are arbitrary.

Let $\mathrm{rev}$ reverse the order of the $K$ digits; it is a bijection of $\{0,\dots,2^K-1\}$. The image $\mathrm{rev}(J_i)$ is the set of numbers whose last $k_i$ digits form the reversed string of $w_i$, i.e. all numbers in $\{0,\dots,2^K-1\}$ congruent to some fixed $r_i$ modulo $2^{k_i}$. These images are pairwise disjoint because the $J_i$ are and $\mathrm{rev}$ is a bijection.

Schedule: on day $t$, if $t\bmod 2^K\in\mathrm{rev}(J_i)$, cut bamboo $i$. Since $2^{k_i}\mid2^K$, bamboo $i$ is cut exactly on the days $t\equiv r_i\pmod{2^{k_i}}$, at most one bamboo is assigned to each day, and by the first paragraph every bamboo always has height at most $2$ metres.

Solution 3 (Round to powers of two, then assign residues greedily by increasing period)

Rounding. Since $n\ge2$ and all $a_i>0$, every $a_i<1$, so $\frac2{a_i}>2$. Let $p_i=2^{k_i}$ be the largest power of two with $p_i\le\frac2{a_i}$; then $k_i\ge1$, and $2p_i>\frac2{a_i}$ gives $\frac1{p_i}<a_i$. Hence \[a_ip_i\le2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{p_i}<\sum_{i=1}^na_i=1. \tag{1}\]

Reduction. Suppose we have residues $r_i$ such that the classes $\{t\ge1:\ t\equiv r_i\pmod{p_i}\}$ are pairwise disjoint. Wojtek cuts bamboo $i$ at the midnights of the days of its class (on a day belonging to no class he cuts anything). Each class has a member among $1,\dots,p_i$ and consecutive members differ by $p_i$, so bamboo $i$ never grows for more than $p_i$ days without a cut, and its height never exceeds $a_ip_i\le2$ by (1). It remains to find the residues.

Greedy choice. Renumber so that $p_1\le p_2\le\dots\le p_n$, and choose $r_1,r_2,\dots$ one after another. Suppose $r_1,\dots,r_{i-1}$ are chosen with disjoint classes, and let $p=p_i$. For $j<i$, $p_j$ is a power of two not larger than $p$, so $p_j\mid p$; therefore the class of $r_j$ modulo $p_j$ is the union of exactly $\frac p{p_j}$ residue classes modulo $p$. Together the earlier bamboos occupy at most \[\sum_{j<i}\frac p{p_j}=p\sum_{j<i}\frac1{p_j}<p\] residues modulo $p$, by (1). So some residue $r_i$ modulo $p$ is free; its class modulo $p$ is disjoint from the class of each $r_j$, $j<i$, because that class is a union of other residues modulo $p$. Continuing up to $i=n$ gives the required pairwise disjoint classes, and with the schedule above every bamboo always has height at most $2$ metres.
