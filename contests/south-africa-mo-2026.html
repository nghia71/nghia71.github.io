Problem 1

EGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Tangency and Tangent Geometry

$ABCDEF$ is a hexagon with all side-lengths equal to $1$ and all angles equal to each other. What is the radius of the circle passing through $A$ and tangent to $CD$ and $DE$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Draw the long diagonal $DA$ of the hexagon and mark the two sides the circle must touch. Sketch perpendiculars from a potential centre to those sides.
Hint 2: key idea
A centre equidistant from the two side lines lies on an angle bisector at $D$. Use the requirement that the tangencies lie on the side segments to decide which bisector ray is relevant. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Express the radius from the perpendicular to a side using the distance of the centre from $D$. Since the circle passes through $A$, express the radius a second way using that same distance along $DA$. Compare the equations and check whether the perpendicular feet lie on the unit sides.
Common trap
A circle tangent to the extended side lines can fail the requirement of tangency to the side segments.
Show the answer

$4\sqrt3-6$

Show solution 1 – The centre lies on the long diagonal $DA$

An equilateral hexagon with equal angles is regular; each angle is $120^\circ$, and the long diagonal $DA$ has length $2$ and bisects the angle $CDE$.

A circle tangent to the sides $CD$ and $DE$ has its centre $Q$ on the bisector of $\angle CDE$, that is, on ray $DA$. (A centre on the other bisector, the line through $D$ perpendicular to $DA$, would give a circle touching line $CD$ or line $DE$ on the extension beyond $D$, not on the side; a centre on the opposite ray of $DA$ would give a circle touching both lines beyond $D$.) Let $DQ=t$. The distance from $Q$ to line $DC$ is $t\sin60^\circ=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}t$, and this is the radius $r$; the foot of the perpendicular is at distance $t\cos 60^\circ=t/2$ from $D$.

The circle passes through $A$, so $r=QA=2-t$ (if $Q$ is between $D$ and $A$). Thus $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}t=2-t$, so \[t=\frac{2}{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}=\frac{4}{2+\sqrt3}=4(2-\sqrt3),\qquad r=2-t=\boxed{4\sqrt3-6}\approx0.93.\] The points of tangency are at distance $t/2=2(2-\sqrt3)\approx0.54<1$ from $D$, so they lie on the sides $CD$ and $DE$, as required.

(If $Q$ were beyond $A$, then $r=t-2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}t$ gives $t=4(2+\sqrt3)$ and $r=6+4\sqrt3$, but then the points of tangency are about $7.5$ units from $D$, far outside the sides; this circle is tangent only to the extended lines. If the problem meant the lines $CD$ and $DE$, this would be a second answer; a centre on the other bisector or on the opposite ray of $DA$ gives no circle through $A$ at all, since there the radius is less than the distance to $A$.)

Show solution 2 – A homothety from $D$

The hexagon is regular (all sides $1$, all angles $120^\circ$). Its centre $O$ is the midpoint of the long diagonal $DA$ ($DA=2$, $DO=1$), and the distance from $O$ to every side is the apothem $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. So the circle $\gamma$ with centre $O$ and radius $\tfrac{\sqrt3}2$ is tangent to the sides $CD$ and $DE$ (at their midpoints).

Every circle tangent to both sides $CD$, $DE$ is the image of $\gamma$ under a homothety with centre $D$ (its centre lies on the bisector $DA$, and the ratio of the distances to $D$ equals the ratio of the radii). The line $DA$ meets $\gamma$ at the points $U,V$ with $DU=1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $DV=1+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The homothety must send one of them to $A$, where $DA=2$:

  • sending $V$ to $A$: ratio $k=\dfrac{2}{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}=\dfrac{4}{2+\sqrt3}=4(2-\sqrt3)$; the image of $\gamma$ has radius $k\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=2\sqrt3(2-\sqrt3)=4\sqrt3-6$, and its points of tangency are at distance $k\cdot\tfrac12\approx0.54$ from $D$, on the sides;

  • sending $U$ to $A$: ratio $\dfrac{4}{2-\sqrt3}$, about $14.9$; the points of tangency are then about $7.5$ from $D$, outside the sides $CD$, $DE$.

Hence the required radius is $\boxed{4\sqrt3-6}$.

Problem 2

EAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods · Cauchy-Schwarz and Titu's Lemma

What is the smallest value of $a$ for which the system of equations \[\begin{aligned}\underbrace{x+x+\dots+x}_{a\text{ times}}+y&=20\\ \underbrace{x^2+x^2+\dots+x^2}_{a\text{ times}}+y^2&=26\end{aligned}\] has at least one real solution for $x,y$?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Rewrite each equation using multiplication by $a$. Also picture the first equation as the sum of a list of $a+1$ real numbers, with one entry possibly different from all the others.
Hint 2: key idea
Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to that list and a list of ones. Compare the square of the first equation's sum with the sum of squares in the second equation to get a bound on the length of the list. (LPS VII, Chapter 1: Inequalities: Core Classical Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Round the necessary bound to get the first candidate integer $a$. At that candidate, eliminate $y$ using the linear equation and check the discriminant of the resulting equation in $x$.
Common trap
Cauchy–Schwarz gives a necessary bound, so verify that the first candidate actually allows real solutions.
Show the answer

$a=15$

Show solution 1 – Cauchy-Schwarz

Here $a$ is a positive integer and the system is $ax+y=20$, $ax^2+y^2=26$. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the $a+1$ numbers $x,\dots,x,y$, \[400=(\underbrace{x+\dots+x}_{a}+y)^2\le(a+1)(\underbrace{x^2+\dots+x^2}_{a}+y^2)=26(a+1),\] so $a+1\ge\tfrac{400}{26}\approx15.4$, i.e. $a\ge15$.

For $a=15$ a real solution exists: substituting $y=20-15x$ into $15x^2+y^2=26$ gives $240x^2-600x+374=0$, whose discriminant $600^2-4\cdot240\cdot374=360000-359040=960$ is positive. So the smallest value is $\boxed{a=15}$.

Show solution 2 – Discriminant of the resulting quadratic

From the first equation $y=20-ax$. Substituting into the second, \[ax^2+(20-ax)^2=26\iff a(a+1)x^2-40ax+374=0.\] This quadratic in $x$ (its leading coefficient $a(a+1)$ is positive) has a real root iff its discriminant is non-negative: \[1600a^2-4\cdot374\,a(a+1)\ge0\iff 400a\ge374(a+1)\iff 26a\ge374\iff a\ge\tfrac{187}{13}\approx14.4.\] So the system is solvable exactly for integers $a\ge15$, and the smallest value is $\boxed{a=15}$ (for $a=15$ the discriminant is $960>0$).

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · State-Based Construction

In a certain country, elections are held every $5$ years. To make counting the votes more efficient, the constitution of this country requires that exactly two parties contest the elections. On the day of the election, all $n$ citizens of the country stand in a line. An official visits each person in turn and asks which party they are voting for. If, at any point during this process, one of the parties leads by more than $2$ votes, that party is instantly declared the winner of the election and no more votes are counted. (Leading by exactly two votes does not end the election.) It so happens that during the $2026$ election, the official is forced to ask every citizen who they are voting for, as no party is ever in the lead by more than $2$ votes. This is true even once all $n$ of the votes have been counted.

How many sequences of $n$ votes are there such that this occurs?

Hint 1: understand the problem
Record a vote for the first party as $+1$ and one for the second as $-1$. Draw the running lead for a short sequence and mark the allowed band.
Hint 2: key idea
Group admissible prefixes by whether the absolute value of their current lead is zero, one, or two. For each group, count which next votes stay in the allowed band. (LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction)
Hint 3: approach
Write the one-vote transitions for the three counts and their initial values. Use the parity of the prefix length to identify which groups can occur, then combine two successive transitions into a simpler recurrence. Use that recurrence to prepare the totals for odd and even lengths.
Common trap
A lead of exactly two is allowed, and the running-lead restriction still applies after the final vote.
Show the answer

$2\cdot3^{(n-1)/2}$ if $n$ is odd, and $4\cdot3^{(n-2)/2}$ if $n$ is even

Show solution 1 – States and a recursion

Record the votes as a sequence of $+1$ (first party) and $-1$ (second party), and let $s_k$ be the sum of the first $k$ terms (the lead of the first party after $k$ votes), $s_0=0$. We must count sequences of length $n$ with $|s_k|\le2$ for all $k$.

Let $a_k$, $b_k$, $c_k$ be the numbers of admissible sequences of length $k$ with $s_k=0$, $s_k=\pm1$, $s_k=\pm2$, respectively. A sequence ending at $0$ can be continued in $2$ ways (to $\pm1$); one ending at $\pm1$ can go to $0$ or to $\pm2$; one ending at $\pm2$ can only go back to $\pm1$. Hence \[a_{k+1}=b_k,\qquad b_{k+1}=2a_k+c_k,\qquad c_{k+1}=b_k,\] with $a_0=1$, $b_0=c_0=0$. Since $s_k\equiv k\pmod 2$, after an odd number of votes only $b$ is non-zero and after an even number only $a,c$ are. From the recursion, $b_{k+2}=2a_{k+1}+c_{k+1}=3b_k$, and $b_1=2$, so $b_{2m+1}=2\cdot3^m$. For even length, $a_{2m+2}+c_{2m+2}=2b_{2m+1}=4\cdot3^m$.

Therefore the number of sequences is \[\boxed{2\cdot3^{(n-1)/2}\ \text{ for odd } n,\qquad 4\cdot3^{(n-2)/2}\ \text{ for even } n.}\]

Show solution 2 – Look at the lead after every odd vote

Write the votes as $\pm1$ and let $s_k$ be the lead after $k$ votes ($s_0=0$); we need $|s_k|\le2$ for all $k$. Since $s_k\equiv k\pmod2$, after an odd number of votes $s_k=\pm1$, and after an even number $s_k\in\{-2,0,2\}$.

From an odd time to the next odd time. Suppose $s_k=1$ ($k$ odd). The next two votes can be $+-$ (via $2$, back to $1$), $-+$ (via $0$, to $1$) or $--$ (via $0$, to $-1$); the pair $++$ would reach $3$. So there are exactly $3$ admissible continuations, and all of them end again at $\pm1$. The case $s_k=-1$ is symmetric.

The first vote can be either ($2$ ways, giving $s_1=\pm1$). Hence for odd $n=2m+1$ the count is $2\cdot3^m$. For even $n=2m+2$, take any admissible sequence of length $2m+1$ (ending at $\pm1$) and add a last vote: both choices keep $|s|\le2$, so the count is $2\cdot2\cdot3^m$. The answer is \[\boxed{2\cdot3^{(n-1)/2}\ (n\text{ odd}),\qquad 4\cdot3^{(n-2)/2}\ (n\text{ even}).}\]

Problem 4

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Basic Congruence Manipulation

Let $n\geqslant 2$ be an integer. Let $r_n$ denote the remainder when $n^{n-1}-1$ is divided by $(n-1)^3$. Prove that

  • for each $n\geqslant 2$, $r_n$ is divisible by $m^2$, for some integer $m\geqslant 2$;

  • there exist infinitely many $n\geqslant 2$ for which $r_n$ is equal to $m^2$, for some integer $m\geqslant 2$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Name $n-1$ by a single variable and rewrite the base and modulus in terms of it. Check what happens when the modulus is one.
Hint 2: key idea
Expand the power by the binomial theorem and keep only terms that can matter modulo the cube of the new variable. Pay special attention to the factor of two in the second binomial coefficient. (LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Reduce the surviving terms separately for odd and even values of the new variable, then check whether each expression is in the required remainder range. Inspect the resulting factors for a square divisor and look for a parity case that makes the remainder itself a square.
Common trap
A representative equal to the modulus must be reduced again before being called the remainder.
Show the answer

proof ($r_n=(n-1)^2$ for even $n\ge4$; $r_n=(n-1)^2\cdot\frac{n+1}{2}$ for odd $n\ge5$; $r_2=r_3=0$)

Show solution 1 – Binomial expansion of $(1+k)^k$

Put $k=n-1\ge1$, so $n^{n-1}-1=(1+k)^k-1$ and $r_n$ is its remainder modulo $k^3$. For $k=1$ ($n=2$) the modulus is $1$ and $r_2=0$. Let $k\ge2$. By the binomial theorem, \[(1+k)^k-1=\binom k1k+\binom k2k^2+\sum_{j\ge3}\binom kjk^j\equiv k^2+\frac{k-1}{2}\,k^3\pmod{k^3},\] because every term with $j\ge3$ is a multiple of $k^3$, and $\binom k2k^2=\tfrac{k(k-1)}2k^2=\tfrac{k-1}{2}k^3$.

  • If $k$ is odd, $\tfrac{k-1}2$ is an integer, so $(1+k)^k-1\equiv k^2\pmod{k^3}$; as $0<k^2<k^3$, we get $r_n=k^2=(n-1)^2$.

  • If $k$ is even, $\tfrac{k-1}{2}k^3=(k-1)\cdot\tfrac{k^3}{2}\equiv\tfrac{k^3}2\pmod{k^3}$ because $k-1$ is odd. So $(1+k)^k-1\equiv k^2+\tfrac{k^3}{2}=k^2\cdot\tfrac{k+2}{2}\pmod{k^3}$. For $k\ge4$ this number is less than $k^3$, so $r_n=k^2\cdot\tfrac{k+2}{2}$; for $k=2$ it equals $8=k^3$, so $r_3=0$.

First claim. For $n\ge4$, $r_n$ is divisible by $m^2$ with $m=n-1\ge3$. For $n=2,3$, $r_n=0$, which is divisible by $2^2$.

Second claim. For every even $n\ge4$ (i.e. odd $k\ge3$), $r_n=(n-1)^2$ is the square of $m=n-1\ge3$. There are infinitely many such $n$.

Show solution 2 – Factor out $n-1$ and expand a geometric sum

Let $k=n-1$. For $n=2$, $r_2=0$ (the modulus is $1$); let $k\ge2$. Then \[n^{n-1}-1=(n-1)\,S,\qquad S=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}n^i=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1+k)^i.\] Modulo $k^2$ we have $(1+k)^i\equiv1+ik$ (the other binomial terms contain $k^2$), so \[S\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1+ik)=k+k\cdot\frac{k(k-1)}2\pmod{k^2}.\] Multiplying a congruence modulo $k^2$ by $k$ gives one modulo $k^3$: \[n^{n-1}-1=kS\equiv k^2+k^2\cdot\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\pmod{k^3}.\] If $k$ is odd, $\frac{k(k-1)}2=k\cdot\frac{k-1}2$ is a multiple of $k$, so the second term vanishes modulo $k^3$ and $r_n=k^2$. If $k$ is even, $\frac{k(k-1)}{2}=\frac k2(k-1)\equiv\frac k2\pmod k$ (as $k-1$ is odd and $\frac k2(k-1)-\frac k2=\frac k2(k-2)$ is a multiple of $k$), so $n^{n-1}-1\equiv k^2\big(1+\frac k2\big)\pmod{k^3}$, which gives $r_n=k^2\cdot\frac{k+2}{2}$ for $k\ge4$ and $r_3=0$ for $k=2$.

Hence for $n\ge4$ the remainder $r_n$ is a multiple of $(n-1)^2$ with $n-1\ge3$, and $r_2=r_3=0$ is a multiple of $4$; this proves the first claim. For all even $n\ge4$ we get $r_n=(n-1)^2$, a perfect square $m^2$ with $m=n-1\ge3$, which proves the second claim.

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure · Power of a Point

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle and $H$ be a point inside it. Let $AH$ intersect $BC$ at $D$, $BH$ intersect $AC$ at $E$, and $CH$ intersect $AB$ at $F$, and let $\Gamma$ be the circle passing through $D$, $E$, and $F$. Suppose that the midpoints of $AH$, $BH$, and $CH$ lie on $\Gamma$.

Prove that $AH\perp BC$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Mark the three midpoints on the cevians through $H$. Identify pairs of points on $\Gamma$ whose connecting chords pass through $H$.
Hint 2: key idea
Compare the chord products at $H$ and use the midpoint relations to replace the shorter factors by half the corresponding cevian lengths. Apply the converse of the intersecting chords theorem to look for circles through pairs of original vertices. (LPS VII, Chapter 15: Circles, Power, and Radical Structure)
Hint 3: approach
Use the three new circles to transfer an angle from $D$ to $E$, then through $F$ and back to $D$. Track where supplementary angles come from collinear points. Compare the two angles at $D$ on opposite sides of the line $AH$.
Common trap
Rays toward opposite ends of a side give supplementary angles, which matters when transferring the angle through $E$ and $F$.
Show solution 1 – Power of $H$, then three cyclic quadrilaterals

Let $A_1,B_1,C_1$ be the midpoints of $AH,BH,CH$. Because $H$ is inside the triangle, it lies between $A$ and $D$, so on line $AD$ the order is $A,A_1,H,D$; thus the chord $A_1D$ of $\Gamma$ passes through $H$, and $H$ is inside $\Gamma$. The same holds for the chords $B_1E$ and $C_1F$. By the intersecting chords theorem for $\Gamma$, \[HA_1\cdot HD=HB_1\cdot HE=HC_1\cdot HF,\qquad\text{so}\qquad AH\cdot HD=BH\cdot HE=CH\cdot HF,\] since $HA_1=\tfrac12AH$, and so on.

From $AH\cdot HD=BH\cdot HE$, where the segments $AD$ and $BE$ cross at $H$, the converse of the intersecting chords theorem gives that $A,B,D,E$ are concyclic. Likewise $B,C,E,F$ and $C,A,F,D$ are concyclic.

Now use inscribed angles (in each case the two points lie on the same side of the chord):

  • circle $ABDE$: $\angle ADB=\angle AEB$;

  • circle $BCEF$: $\angle BEC=\angle BFC$, hence $\angle AEB=180^\circ-\angle BEC=180^\circ-\angle BFC=\angle AFC$;

  • circle $CAFD$: $\angle AFC=\angle ADC$.

Thus $\angle ADB=\angle ADC$. These two angles add up to $180^\circ$, so each is $90^\circ$, that is, $AH\perp BC$.

Show solution 2 – A homothety and the law of cosines

Let $A_1,B_1,C_1$ be the midpoints of $AH,BH,CH$ and let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. The homothety $h$ with centre $H$ and ratio $2$ maps $A_1,B_1,C_1$ to $A,B,C$, so it maps $\Gamma$ (the circle through $A_1,B_1,C_1$) onto $\Omega$. Hence $D'=h(D)$ lies on $\Omega$; it lies on line $AD$, on the other side of $H$ from $A$, so it is the second intersection of $AD$ with $\Omega$, and $HD'=2HD$. Similarly for $E'=h(E)$, $F'=h(F)$. The power of the interior point $H$ with respect to $\Omega$ gives $HA\cdot HD'=HB\cdot HE'=HC\cdot HF'$, so \[AH\cdot HD=BH\cdot HE=CH\cdot HF.\] By the converse of the intersecting chords theorem, the quadruples $(A,B,D,E)$, $(B,C,E,F)$, $(C,A,F,D)$ are concyclic. The power of the outside vertex with respect to each of these circles gives \[CD\cdot CB=CE\cdot CA,\qquad AF\cdot AB=AE\cdot AC,\qquad BF\cdot BA=BD\cdot BC.\] Since $D,E,F$ lie on the sides, $BC=BD+DC$, $CA=CE+EA$, $AB=AF+FB$. Therefore \[BA^2+BC^2-CA^2=BA(BF+FA)+BC(BD+DC)-CA(CE+EA)=2\,BD\cdot BC,\] because $BF\cdot BA=BD\cdot BC$, $FA\cdot BA=EA\cdot CA$ and $DC\cdot BC=CE\cdot CA$. By the law of cosines the left side equals $2\,BA\cdot BC\cos B$, hence $BD=BA\cos B$. In the acute triangle the foot of the altitude from $A$ is the point of segment $BC$ at distance $BA\cos B$ from $B$; so $D$ is that foot and $AD\perp BC$, i.e. $AH\perp BC$.

Problem 6

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods · Substitution and Special Inputs

Find all functions $f\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)$ that satisfy \[yf(x+y)+f\big(1+yf(x)\big)=1\] for all $x,y>0$.

Hint 1: understand the problem
Fix the sum $x+y$ while varying $y$ over the permitted range. What does positivity of the second term alone tell you about the size of the first?
Hint 2: key idea
Use the fixed-sum substitution to bound $tf(t)$ from above. For a lower bound above $1$, choose $y$ to prescribe the second argument and express the resulting estimate in terms of $xf(x)$. (LPS VII, Chapter 30: Functional Equations: Core Olympiad Methods)
Hint 3: approach
Take the supremum of the quantities $xf(x)$ in the lower estimate and compare it with what the estimate implies at a fixed argument above $1$. Use the matching bounds to determine the function on that range. For an arbitrary $x$, choose $y$ so that both function arguments in the equation lie in the range already determined.
Common trap
A supremum can be approached by a sequence of values even if no input attains it.
Show the answer

$f(x)=\dfrac1x$

Show solution 1 – An upper bound and a supremum argument

Check. For $f(x)=1/x$: $\dfrac{y}{x+y}+\dfrac{1}{1+y/x}=\dfrac{y}{x+y}+\dfrac{x}{x+y}=1$.

Upper bound. All values of $f$ are positive, so the equation gives $yf(x+y)<1$. For $t>0$ and any $0<y<t$, take $x=t-y$: $f(t)<1/y$. Letting $y\to t$ gives $f(t)\le1/t$, i.e. $g(t)=tf(t)\le1$ for all $t>0$.

Lower bound for arguments above $1$. Using $f(x+y)\le\frac1{x+y}$ in the equation, \[f\big(1+yf(x)\big)=1-yf(x+y)\ge1-\frac{y}{x+y}=\frac{x}{x+y}.\] Fix $x$ and $u>1$ and choose $y=\frac{u-1}{f(x)}>0$, so that $1+yf(x)=u$. Then \[f(u)\ge\frac{x}{x+\frac{u-1}{f(x)}}=\frac{g(x)}{g(x)+u-1}\qquad(u>1,\ x>0).\tag{$*$}\] Let $G=\sup_{x>0}g(x)$; $0<G\le1$. The function $s\mapsto\frac{s}{s+u-1}$ is increasing, so taking the supremum over $x$ in $(*)$ gives $f(u)\ge\frac{G}{G+u-1}$, i.e. $g(u)\ge\frac{uG}{G+u-1}$. If $G<1$, then for $u=2$ we would get $g(2)\ge\frac{2G}{G+1}>G$, contradicting the definition of $G$. So $G=1$, and then $f(u)\ge\frac{1}{u}$ for $u>1$. With the upper bound, $f(u)=\frac1u$ for all $u>1$.

All arguments. Let $x>0$ be arbitrary and take $y>1$. Then $x+y>1$ and $1+yf(x)>1$, so both values in the equation are known: $\frac{y}{x+y}+\frac{1}{1+yf(x)}=1$, which gives $1+yf(x)=\frac{x+y}{x}$, i.e. $f(x)=\frac1x$.

Hence the only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=\dfrac1x}$.

Show solution 2 – Substitute so that the inner argument equals $x$

Check. $f(x)=1/x$ works: $\frac{y}{x+y}+\frac{x}{x+y}=1$.

Step 1: $f(t)\le\frac1t$. Since $f>0$, the equation gives $yf(x+y)<1$. For $t>0$ and $0<y<t$, put $x=t-y$: $f(t)<\frac1y$; letting $y\to t$, $f(t)\le\frac1t$.

Step 2: $f(x)=\frac1x$ for $x>1$. Fix $x>1$ and choose $y=\frac{x-1}{f(x)}>0$, so that $1+yf(x)=x$. The equation becomes \[yf(x+y)+f(x)=1,\qquad\text{so}\qquad f(x)=1-yf(x+y)\ge1-\frac{y}{x+y}=\frac{x}{x+y}\] by Step 1. Substituting $y=\frac{x-1}{f(x)}$ and writing $g=xf(x)>0$: \[f(x)\ge\frac{x}{x+\frac{x-1}{f(x)}}=\frac{xf(x)}{xf(x)+x-1}\iff g+x-1\ge x\iff g\ge1.\] So $xf(x)\ge1$, and with Step 1, $f(x)=\frac1x$ for all $x>1$.

Step 3: all $x>0$. Take any $x>0$ and $y>1$. Then $x+y>1$ and $1+yf(x)>1$, so by Step 2 the equation reads $\frac{y}{x+y}+\frac{1}{1+yf(x)}=1$, which gives $1+yf(x)=1+\frac yx$ and $f(x)=\frac1x$.

The only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=\dfrac1x}$.

Show solution 3 – Reading off the behaviour at infinity

Check. $f(x)=1/x$ works: $\frac{y}{x+y}+\frac{1}{1+y/x}=\frac{y}{x+y}+\frac{x}{x+y}=1$.

Step 1: $f(t)\le\frac1t$. Since $f>0$, the equation gives $yf(x+y)<1$. For $t>0$ and $0<y<t$ put $x=t-y$: then $f(t)<\frac1y$. Letting $y\to t$ gives $f(t)\le\frac1t$ for every $t>0$.

Step 2: $tf(t)\to1$ as $t\to\infty$. Fix $x>0$. By Step 1, $0<f(1+yf(x))\le\frac{1}{1+yf(x)}\to0$ as $y\to\infty$, so the equation gives $yf(x+y)\to1$. With $t=x+y$ this says $(t-x)f(t)\to1$, and since $xf(t)\le\frac xt\to0$, also $tf(t)\to1$.

Step 3: a limit that does not depend on $x$. Fix $x>0$ again and write $t=x+y$, $u=1+yf(x)$. The equation says $1-yf(t)=f(u)$, so \[y\bigl(1-yf(t)\bigr)=yf(u)=\frac{y}{u}\cdot uf(u).\] As $y\to\infty$ we have $u\to\infty$, so $uf(u)\to1$ by Step 2, and $\frac yu=\frac{y}{1+yf(x)}\to\frac1{f(x)}$. Hence $y(1-yf(t))\to\frac1{f(x)}$. On the other hand, using $y=t-x$, \[y\bigl(1-yf(t)\bigr)-t\bigl(1-tf(t)\bigr)=-x+(t^2-y^2)f(t)=-x+x\,tf(t)+x\,yf(t)\longrightarrow -x+x+x=x,\] because $tf(t)\to1$ and $yf(t)=tf(t)-xf(t)\to1$. Therefore the limit \[L=\lim_{t\to\infty}t\bigl(1-tf(t)\bigr)\] exists and equals $\frac1{f(x)}-x$. The left side does not involve $x$, so $\frac{1}{f(x)}=x+L$ for every $x>0$, that is, \[f(x)=\frac{1}{x+L}.\] By Step 1, $x+L\ge x$, so $L\ge0$.

Step 4: $L=0$. Substitute $f(x)=\frac1{x+L}$. Then $yf(x+y)=\frac{y}{x+y+L}$ and $1+yf(x)=\frac{x+y+L}{x+L}$, so the equation becomes \[\frac{y}{x+y+L}+\frac{1}{\frac{x+y+L}{x+L}+L}=1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{1}{\frac{x+y+L}{x+L}+L}=\frac{x+L}{x+y+L}=\frac{1}{\frac{x+y+L}{x+L}}.\] The two denominators differ by $L$, so $L=0$.

Hence the only solution is $\boxed{f(x)=\dfrac1x}$.
