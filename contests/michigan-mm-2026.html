Individual Round

Problem 3

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 2: Fundamental Counting and Structured Enumeration · Constraint-Based Enumeration

Anakin Gridwalker wants to walk on the coordinate plane from $(0,0)$ to $(4,5)$. Each minute, he can move either $1$ unit to the right or $1$ unit upward. Darth Vader, Anakin's antimatter clone, starts at $(4,5)$ and exactly mirrors Anakin's movements: Darth Vader moves down whenever Anakin moves up, and Darth Vader moves left whenever Anakin moves right. If Anakin and Darth Vader ever come into contact, either by meeting at a vertex at the end of a minute or by traversing the same edge during the same minute, they annihilate each other, which would be very bad. How many ways can Anakin complete this walk without annihilating himself?

Show the answer

$90$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Complement: only one dangerous edge)

Let $P_t$ be Anakin's position after $t$ minutes ($0\le t\le 9$); every walk has $4$ right moves and $5$ up moves, so it lasts $9$ minutes, and the coordinates of $P_t$ add up to $t$. Because Darth Vader undoes each of Anakin's moves in the opposite direction, his position after $t$ minutes is $(4,5)-P_t$.

Meeting at a vertex. This would need $P_t=(4,5)-P_t$, that is $P_t=(2,\tfrac52)$, which is not a lattice point. So this never happens.

Using the same edge in the same minute. During minute $t$ Anakin goes from $P_{t-1}$ to $P_t$ and Vader goes from $(4,5)-P_{t-1}$ to $(4,5)-P_t$. Their moves point in opposite directions, so they share the edge only if they run along it towards each other, i.e. $P_{t-1}=(4,5)-P_t$. Adding coordinates gives $(t-1)+t=9$, so $t=5$ and $P_4+P_5=(4,5)$. If move $5$ is to the right, $P_4=(a,b)$ and $P_5=(a+1,b)$ give $2a+1=4$, impossible; if it is upward, $2a=4$ and $2b+1=5$, so $P_4=(2,2)$ and $P_5=(2,3)$.

Hence a walk is fatal exactly when it uses the edge from $(2,2)$ to $(2,3)$ (which can only be its fifth move). There are $\binom{4}{2}=6$ walks from $(0,0)$ to $(2,2)$ and $\binom{4}{2}=6$ walks from $(2,3)$ to $(4,5)$, so $36$ fatal walks. Out of all $\binom{9}{4}=126$ walks, $126-36=\boxed{90}$ are safe.

Solution 2 (Counting by the position at the halfway point)

Let $P_t$ be Anakin's position after $t$ minutes. The coordinates of $P_t$ add up to $t$, and the walk lasts $9$ minutes. Vader's position after $t$ minutes is $(4,5)-P_t$, since he undoes each of Anakin's moves.

They can never be at the same vertex: that would require $P_t=(2,\frac52)$. They can meet on an edge only if, during some minute $t$, Anakin goes from $P_{t-1}$ to $P_t$ while Vader goes from $P_t$ to $P_{t-1}$, that is $P_{t-1}+P_t=(4,5)$. The coordinate sums give $2t-1=9$, so $t=5$. Thus everything is decided by the fifth move: if $P_4=(a,b)$ with $a+b=4$, the walk is fatal exactly when $P_5=(4,5)-P_4=(4-a,5-b)$, which must be one step from $(a,b)$; this happens only for $(a,b)=(2,2)$ with an upward fifth move.

Now count safe walks according to $P_4$, multiplying the number of ways to reach $P_4$ by the number of allowed ways to finish:

  • $P_4=(0,4)$: $1$ way to get there, $\binom{5}{1}=5$ ways to finish: $5$.

  • $P_4=(1,3)$: $\binom41=4$ ways, then $\binom{5}{2}=10$: $40$.

  • $P_4=(2,2)$: $\binom42=6$ ways; the fifth move must be to the right, to $(3,2)$, and then $\binom41=4$ ways to reach $(4,5)$: $24$.

  • $P_4=(3,1)$: $\binom43=4$ ways, then $\binom{5}{1}=5$: $20$.

  • $P_4=(4,0)$: $1$ way, then $1$ way: $1$.

The total is $5+40+24+20+1=\boxed{90}$.

Problem 5

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 21: Arithmetic Functions and Multiplicative Structure · Divisor and Sum Functions

There are $100$ rotatable handles lined up on a wall, labeled $1$ through $100$. They all initially point straight upwards. There are also $100$ people, labeled $1$ through $100$. One by one, the $n$th person goes up to each handle with a label that is a multiple of $n$ and turns it $90$ degrees clockwise from its current position. After all $100$ people have completed this process, how many handles will be pointing straight downwards?

Show the answer

$43$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting by the square part)

Person $d$ turns handle $n$ exactly when $d\mid n$, so handle $n$ is turned $\tau(n)$ times, where $\tau(n)$ is the number of positive divisors of $n$. Four quarter turns bring a handle back to its starting position, so handle $n$ points down exactly when $\tau(n)\equiv 2\pmod 4$.

If $n=p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then $\tau(n)=(e_1+1)\cdots(e_k+1)$. This product is $\equiv2\pmod4$ exactly when one factor is $\equiv 2\pmod 4$ and all others are odd, i.e. exactly one exponent is $\equiv1\pmod4$ and all other exponents are even. Equivalently $n=p^{j}m^2$ with $p$ prime, $p\nmid m$ and $j\equiv1\pmod 4$. For $n\le100$ we have $j\in\{1,5\}$, and $j=5$ only for $p=2$, $m=1$ ($n=32$; $3^5>100$ and $2^5\cdot 9>100$).

Count by $m$ (with $p\nmid m$ and $pm^2\le100$):

  • $m=1$: the $25$ primes up to $100$, plus $n=32$: $26$.

  • $m=2$: odd primes $p\le25$: $3,5,7,11,13,17,19,23$: $8$.

  • $m=3$: $p\le 11$, $p\ne3$: $2,5,7,11$: $4$.

  • $m=4$: $p\le6$, $p\ne2$: $3,5$: $2$.

  • $m=5$: $p\le4$: $2,3$: $2$.

  • $m=6$: $p\le2$ but $p\nmid 6$: none.

  • $m=7$: $p\le 2$: $p=2$ ($n=98$): $1$.

  • $m\ge8$: $pm^2\ge128$: none.

In total $26+8+4+2+2+0+1=\boxed{43}$ handles point downwards.

Solution 2 (Counting by the special prime)

Handle $n$ is turned once by each person whose label divides $n$, so $\tau(n)$ times in all, where $\tau(n)$ counts the positive divisors of $n$. It points down exactly when $\tau(n)\equiv2\pmod4$.

With $n=\prod p_i^{e_i}$ we have $\tau(n)=\prod (e_i+1)$, and this is $\equiv 2\pmod 4$ exactly when one exponent $e$ satisfies $e+1\equiv2\pmod4$ (so $e\equiv1\pmod 4$) and all the others are even. So $n=p^{e}m^2$ with a unique prime $p$ (call it the special prime), $e\equiv1\pmod 4$ and $p\nmid m$. Different $(p,e,m)$ give different $n$, so we may count, for each prime $p\le100$, the integers $m\ge1$ with $p\nmid m$ and $p^e m^2\le100$:

  • $p=2$: $e=1$ and odd $m$ with $m^2\le50$: $m=1,3,5,7$; $e=5$ and $m=1$ ($n=32$). Total $5$.

  • $p=3$: $m^2\le 33$, $3\nmid m$: $m=1,2,4,5$: $4$.

  • $p=5$: $m^2\le20$: $m=1,2,3,4$: $4$.

  • $p=7$: $m^2\le14$: $m=1,2,3$: $3$.

  • $p=11$: $m^2\le9$: $m=1,2,3$: $3$.

  • $p=13,17,19,23$: $m^2\le 7, 5, 5, 4$: $m=1,2$ each: $8$.

  • $p=29,31,37,41,43,47$: $m^2<4$: $m=1$ each: $6$.

  • $p=53,59,61,67,71,73,79,83,89,97$: $m=1$ each: $10$.

($e=5$ is impossible for $p\ge3$ since $3^5>100$.) The total is $5+4+4+3+3+8+6+10=\boxed{43}$.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 23: Recurrence and Combinatorial Construction · Recursive Counting

Anakin Pathwalker starts in the top left square of a $2\times 10$ grid. From there, he may move up, down, left, or right to adjacent squares provided he stays within the grid. He may not revisit any squares, including his starting square. A complete path is a path that continues until there are no legal moves remaining. How many different complete paths can Anakin walk?

Show the answer

$767$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A first-move recursion)

Number the columns $1,\dots,n$ and write $T_i$, $B_i$ for the top and bottom squares of column $i$. Let $f(n)$ be the number of complete paths in a $2\times n$ grid that start at a corner; by symmetry all four corners give the same number. Clearly $f(1)=1$ and $f(2)=2$ (the paths $T_1B_1B_2T_2$ and $T_1T_2B_2B_1$). Let $n\ge3$ and start at $T_1$.

First move down, to $B_1$. The only move is then to $B_2$, and from there the path never returns to column $1$ (both squares used). What remains is exactly a complete path of the $2\times(n-1)$ grid on columns $2,\dots,n$ started at its corner $B_2$: $f(n-1)$ paths.

First move right, to $T_2$. Now $B_1$ can only be entered from $B_2$, and after entering $B_1$ the walker is stuck. Consider the part of the path inside columns $2,\dots,n$; it starts at the corner $T_2$ of that $2\times(n-1)$ grid.

  • If the walker arrives at $B_2$ from $B_3$, then $T_2$ and $B_3$ are used, so inside columns $2,\dots,n$ he is stuck at $B_2$; in the big grid he must step to $B_1$ and stop.

  • If the walker goes $T_2\to B_2$ at once, he may continue to $B_3$ (and then $B_1$ is never reachable again) or step to $B_1$ and stop.

  • If he never visits $B_2$, then $B_1$ is irrelevant.

So the complete paths starting $T_1T_2$ are: one for each complete path of the $2\times(n-1)$ grid from $T_2$ (extended by $B_1$ when it ends at $B_2$ after coming from $B_3$), plus the single extra path $T_1T_2B_2B_1$. (For $n\ge3$ this extra path is different from all the others, because the subgrid path $T_2B_2$ is not complete when $B_3$ exists.) That gives $f(n-1)+1$ paths.

Therefore $f(n)=2f(n-1)+1$ for $n\ge 3$, i.e. $f(n)+1=2\,(f(n-1)+1)$. Since $f(2)+1=3$, we get $f(n)+1=3\cdot2^{n-2}$, and $f(10)=3\cdot 2^8-1=\boxed{767}$.

Solution 2 (Snakes and hairpins)

Write $T_i$, $B_i$ for the top and bottom squares of column $i$. Call a position fresh if the walker stands in a corner square of the block of columns $c,\dots,n$, all squares of that block are unused except his own, and he can never again use a square of columns $1,\dots,c-1$. Let $f(m)$ be the number of ways to finish a complete path from a fresh position in a block of $m$ columns. The start is fresh with $m=n$.

Suppose the walker is fresh at $T_c$ (the case $B_c$ is symmetric). Exactly one of the following happens.

  • He moves down to $B_c$. If $m=1$ the path ends. Otherwise he must move on to $B_{c+1}$, which is fresh for a block of $m-1$ columns: $f(m-1)$ ways.

  • He runs right along the top row to $T_b$ for some $b$ with $c<b\le c+m-1$, and then moves down to $B_b$. (He must turn down somewhere, because at the last column $B$ is still free.) From $B_b$ he either runs back left along the bottom row; then he is forced all the way to $B_c$ and stops there ($1$ way). Or, if $b$ is not the last column, he moves right to $B_{b+1}$; the squares $B_c,\dots,B_{b-1}$ are now cut off forever, so $B_{b+1}$ is fresh for a block of $m-(b-c)-1$ columns.

Writing $j=b-c\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}$, we obtain, for $m\ge2$, \[f(m)=f(m-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\bigl(1+f(m-j-1)\bigr),\qquad f(0):=0,\ f(1)=1 .\] Computing step by step: \[f(1)=1,\ f(2)=1+1=2,\ f(3)=2+(1+1)+1=5,\ f(4)=5+3+2+1=11,\] and in general $f(m)=f(m-1)+(m-1)+\bigl(f(1)+\dots+f(m-2)\bigr)$, which gives \[f(5)=23,\ f(6)=47,\ f(7)=95,\ f(8)=191,\ f(9)=383,\ f(10)=\boxed{767}.\]

Problem 7

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 47: Probabilistic Method and Random Structure · Expectation Arguments

Let $A$ and $B$ be two points chosen uniformly and independently at random from the interior of the unit cube centered at the origin with faces parallel to the coordinate planes (the set $\{(x,y,z): |x|<\tfrac12,\ |y|<\tfrac12,\ |z|<\tfrac12\}$). What is the probability that line segment $AB$ intersects the first octant (the set $\{(x,y,z): x>0,\ y>0,\ z>0\}$)?

Show the answer

$\dfrac{5}{16}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Expected number of octants)

With probability $1$ no coordinate of $A$ or $B$ is $0$, and the coordinate planes that the segment crosses (it may miss some) are crossed at different times; we ignore the null events where this fails.

Along the segment from $A$ to $B$ each coordinate is a linear function of the parameter, so it changes sign at most once, and it changes sign exactly when that coordinate of $A$ and of $B$ have opposite signs. Each sign change moves the segment into a new octant (a sign vector it has not had before, since no coordinate changes sign twice). So the number of octants that $AB$ meets is $1+X$, where $X$ is the number of coordinates in which $A$ and $B$ have opposite signs. Each coordinate has opposite signs with probability $\frac12$, so $\mathbb E[1+X]=1+\frac32=\frac52$.

The reflections $x\mapsto -x$, $y\mapsto-y$, $z\mapsto -z$ preserve the cube and the uniform distribution, and they permute the eight octants transitively, so $AB$ meets each octant with the same probability $q$. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of octants met is $8q$. Hence $8q=\frac52$ and $q=\boxed{\tfrac{5}{16}}$.

Solution 2 (Crossing times)

Ignore the probability-zero events where a coordinate vanishes or two crossing times coincide. Call a coordinate (say $x$, with values $a$ at $A$ and $b$ at $B$) of type $PP$, $NN$, $NP$ or $PN$ according to the signs of $a$ and $b$; each type has probability $\frac14$, independently for the three coordinates. The point $A+t(B-A)$, $0\le t\le1$, has positive $x$-coordinate:

  • for all $t$ (type $PP$); never (type $NN$);

  • exactly for $t>\tau$ (type $NP$) or exactly for $t<\tau$ (type $PN$), where $\tau=\frac{|a|}{|a|+|b|}$ is the crossing time.

Given the types, $|a|$ and $|b|$ are independent and uniform on $(0,\frac12)$, so all crossing times are independent with one common continuous distribution; hence all orders of them are equally likely.

The segment meets the first octant iff there is no $NN$ coordinate and every $NP$ crossing happens before every $PN$ crossing. If there are $k$ coordinates of type $NP$ and $l$ of type $PN$, the probability of the right order is $1/\binom{k+l}{k}$. Each of the $4^3=64$ type patterns has probability $\frac1{64}$; we sum the weights of the $27$ patterns without $NN$ (with $i$ coordinates of type $PP$):

  • $(i,k,l)=(3,0,0)$: $1$ pattern, weight $1$;

  • $(2,1,0),(2,0,1)$: $6$ patterns, weight $1$;

  • $(1,2,0),(1,0,2)$: $6$ patterns, weight $1$; $(1,1,1)$: $6$ patterns, weight $\frac12$;

  • $(0,3,0),(0,0,3)$: $2$ patterns, weight $1$; $(0,2,1),(0,1,2)$: $6$ patterns, weight $\frac13$.

The total weight is $1+6+6+3+2+2=20$, so the probability is $\frac{20}{64}=\boxed{\tfrac{5}{16}}$.

Problem 9

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 19: Triangle Centers and Classical Triangle Geometry · Centroid, Incenter, Circumcenter, and Orthocenter

Let $ABCD$ be a trapezoid with $AB\parallel CD$ where $AB=14$, $CD=42$, and $AD=26$. A circle with center $O$ is internally tangent to every side of $ABCD$. If the perpendicular bisectors of $AO$ and $BO$ intersect at a point $P$, what is the distance from $P$ to $AB$?

Show the answer

$4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Coordinates)

Since the circle touches all four sides, tangent lengths give $AB+CD=AD+BC$, so $BC=14+42-26=30$. The point $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AO$ and $BO$, so $P$ is the circumcenter of triangle $AOB$.

Let the height of the trapezoid be $h$, and let $x$ and $y$ be the signed horizontal overhangs of the longer base $CD$ beyond $A$ and $B$, so that $D=(-x,h)$ and $C=(14+y,h)$ when $A=(0,0)$, $B=(14,0)$. Then $x+y=42-14=28$ whatever their signs, and $26^2-x^2=h^2=30^2-y^2$. Hence $y^2-x^2=224$, so $y-x=224/28=8$, giving $x=10$, $y=18$ (both positive), $h=24$. So $A=(0,0)$, $B=(14,0)$, $D=(-10,24)$, $C=(32,24)$. The circle touches both parallel sides, so its radius is $12$ and $O=(t,12)$. Its distance to line $AD$, which is $12x+5y=0$, must be $12$: $|12t+60|/13=12$, so $t=8$ (the other root lies outside). Thus $O=(8,12)$; as a check, the distance from $O$ to line $BC$, $4x-3y=56$, is $|32-36-56|/5=12$.

The circumcenter $P$ of $A$, $B$, $O$ lies on $x=7$; write $P=(7,s)$. From $PA=PO$: $49+s^2=1+(12-s)^2$, so $24s=96$ and $s=4$. The distance from $P$ to $AB$ is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 2 (Half-angles and the circumradius)

Tangent lengths give $AB+CD=AD+BC$, so $BC=30$. Drop perpendiculars from $A$ and $B$ to $CD$, and let $x$ (beside $D$) and $y$ (beside $C$) be the signed overhangs of $CD$ beyond their feet, positive when $CD$ sticks out. Then $x+y=42-14=28$ whatever their signs, and $26^2-x^2=30^2-y^2$, so $x=10$, $y=18$ (both positive), and the height is $24$.

Then $\cos\angle DAB=-\frac{10}{26}=-\frac5{13}$ and $\sin\angle DAB=\frac{12}{13}$, so \[\tan\frac{\angle DAB}{2}=\frac{\sin\angle DAB}{1+\cos\angle DAB}=\frac{12/13}{8/13}=\frac32 ;\] similarly $\cos\angle ABC=-\frac{18}{30}=-\frac35$, $\sin\angle ABC=\frac45$, and $\tan\frac{\angle ABC}{2}=\frac{4/5}{2/5}=2$.

The center $O$ of the inscribed circle lies on the angle bisectors at $A$ and $B$, so in triangle $AOB$ the angles at $A$ and $B$ are $\alpha=\frac12\angle DAB$ and $\beta=\frac12\angle ABC$, and $\angle AOB=180^\circ-\alpha-\beta$. Hence \[\tan\angle AOB=-\tan(\alpha+\beta)=-\frac{\frac32+2}{1-3}=\frac74 ,\] so $\angle AOB$ is acute and $\cot\angle AOB=\frac47$.

The perpendicular bisectors of $AO$ and $BO$ meet at the circumcenter $P$ of triangle $AOB$. Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$; then $PM\perp AB$ and the central angle $\angle APB=2\angle AOB$, so $\angle APM=\angle AOB$ (and $P$ lies on the same side of $AB$ as $O$, because $\angle AOB$ is acute). Therefore \[PM=AM\cot\angle AOB=7\cdot\frac47=\boxed{4}.\]

Problem 10

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 14: Inclusion-Exclusion and Overcounting Control · Complementary Counting

Kermit the Frog wants to visit every lily pad on Lilyline Pond, which has $7$ lily pads lined up in a row from left to right. Kermit starts by choosing one of the $7$ lily pads uniformly at random to begin on. Then, Kermit makes $6$ jumps one by one, each time jumping uniformly at random to a lily pad that he has not yet visited. What is the probability that Kermit makes exactly two jumps to the left?

Show the answer

$\dfrac{397}{1680}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Gluing increasing runs)

Number the pads $1,\dots,7$ from left to right. Kermit's route is a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ of $\{1,\dots,7\}$ (all $7!=5040$ orders are equally likely), and a left jump is a descent, a place where $\pi_i>\pi_{i+1}$. Let $A_k$ be the number of permutations of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with exactly $k$ descents; here $n=7$.

One descent. For each subset $S$ of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ write the elements of $S$ in increasing order followed by the rest in increasing order. This gives $2^n$ words. A permutation with exactly one descent arises exactly once (the cut must be at the descent); the identity arises $n+1$ times (cut anywhere); no other permutation arises. So $A_1=2^n-(n+1)$.

Two descents. For each ordered triple of disjoint sets with union $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (possibly empty), write the three sets one after another, each in increasing order: $3^n$ words, i.e. permutations together with two cut positions $0\le i\le j\le n$ such that the permutation increases inside each piece. A permutation with exactly two descents arises once (both cuts at the descents). A permutation with exactly one descent, at position $d$, arises for every pair $i\le j$ that contains $d$: the pairs $(i,d)$ with $i\le d$ and $(d,j)$ with $j\ge d$, counted with $(d,d)$ once, i.e. $n+1$ times. The identity arises $\binom{n+2}{2}$ times. So \[3^n=A_2+(n+1)A_1+\binom{n+2}{2}.\] For $n=7$: $A_1=128-8=120$ and $A_2=2187-8\cdot120-36=1191$. The probability is $\frac{1191}{5040}=\boxed{\tfrac{397}{1680}}$.

Solution 2 (Inserting the largest pad)

Number the pads $1,\dots,7$ from left to right. The order in which Kermit visits the pads is a uniformly random permutation of $\{1,\dots,7\}$, and a jump to the left is a descent of the permutation (a place where a larger number is followed by a smaller one). Let $E(n,k)$ be the number of permutations of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with exactly $k$ descents.

Every permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ comes from a unique permutation of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ by inserting $n$ into one of the $n$ gaps (including the two ends). Inserting $n$ at the very end, or inside an existing descent $a>b$ (giving $a<n>b$), keeps the number of descents; inserting it at the front or inside an ascent $a<b$ (giving $a<n>b$, where the old ascent is replaced by a descent after $n$) adds one descent. A permutation with $k$ descents has $k$ descents and $n-2-k$ ascents among its $n-2$ adjacent pairs, so \[E(n,k)=(k+1)\,E(n-1,k)+(n-k)\,E(n-1,k-1).\] Starting from $E(1,0)=1$:

  • $n=2$: $1,1$;  $n=3$: $1,4,1$;  $n=4$: $1,11,11,1$;

  • $n=5$: $1,26,66,26,1$;  $n=6$: $1,57,302,302,57,1$;

  • $n=7$: $E(7,2)=3\cdot302+5\cdot57=906+285=1191$.

Hence the probability is $\frac{1191}{7!}=\frac{1191}{5040}=\boxed{\tfrac{397}{1680}}$.

Problem 2

ECombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 27: Bijections and Structural Equivalence · Explicit Bijections

An integer $n$ is called puny if, out of all of the possible distinct integers that can be formed by permuting the digits of $n$, at least half of the integers are strictly greater than $n$. For example, $1111$ would not be puny, as there is only one permutation, itself, and it is not strictly greater than itself, but $1234$ would be puny. How many $4$-digit puny integers not containing the digit $0$ are there?

Show the answer

$3276$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Ranking inside each digit class)

Group the $9^4$ four-digit numbers with nonzero digits into classes of numbers with the same multiset of digits. A class with $m$ members consists exactly of the distinct numbers obtained by permuting the digits of any of its members. List a class increasingly; the member in position $r$ has $m-r$ larger members, so it is puny iff $m-r\ge \frac m2$, i.e. $r\le\frac m2$. Hence a class contains $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ puny numbers.

The class sizes are $\frac{4!}{\text{product of factorials of the multiplicities}}$, i.e. $24$, $12$, $6$, $4$ or $1$. Only the $9$ classes $\{aaaa\}$ have odd size ($m=1$, no puny member). Every other class contributes exactly half of its members, so the number of puny numbers is \[\frac{9^4-9}{2}=\frac{6552}{2}=\boxed{3276}.\]

Solution 2 (The digit-complement bijection)

Let $N$ be the set of four-digit numbers whose digits are all nonzero, and for $n\in N$ let $K(n)$ be the set of distinct numbers obtained by permuting its digits; $n$ is puny iff more than or exactly half of $K(n)$ exceeds $n$.

Define $\varphi(n)=11110-n$; it replaces every digit $d$ by $10-d$, so $\varphi$ is a bijection of $N$ onto itself, it reverses order, and it maps $K(n)$ onto $K(\varphi(n))$. Thus if $K(n)$ has $m$ members of which $g$ exceed $n$ and $s=m-1-g$ are smaller than $n$, then exactly $s$ members of $K(\varphi(n))$ exceed $\varphi(n)$.

Suppose $m$ is even. If $n$ is puny then $g\ge\frac m2$, so $s\le \frac m2-1<\frac m2$ and $\varphi(n)$ is not puny. If $n$ is not puny then $g\le\frac m2-1$, so $s\ge\frac m2$ and $\varphi(n)$ is puny. Thus $\varphi$ pairs puny numbers with non-puny numbers. The size $m=\frac{24}{\text{product of factorials of multiplicities}}$ is even except for the $9$ numbers $aaaa$, which are not puny and are mapped among themselves.

So the remaining $9^4-9=6552$ numbers split evenly, and there are $\boxed{3276}$ puny numbers.

Problem 6

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 49: Combinatorial Number Theory and Structure · Sequences with Arithmetic Constraints

Call a set of integers $S$ balanced if it has the following two properties:

  • 1. $S$ contains at least two integers.

  • 2. For any two integers $a$ and $b$ in $S$, if $a+b$ is even, then $\frac{a+b}{2}$ is also in the set.

How many subsets of the set $\{1,2,3,\dots,10\}$ are balanced?

Show the answer

$66$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting by difference and length)

Claim. A finite set $S$ of at least two integers is balanced iff its elements form an arithmetic progression with odd common difference.

Write $S=\{s_1<s_2<\dots<s_k\}$, $k\ge2$, balanced. Two consecutive elements $s_i,s_{i+1}$ cannot have an even sum, since their average would be an element strictly between them; so they have opposite parities. Then $s_i$ and $s_{i+2}$ have the same parity, so their average lies in $S$, strictly between them; the only candidate is $s_{i+1}$. Thus $s_{i+1}-s_i=s_{i+2}-s_{i+1}$ for all $i$: $S$ is an arithmetic progression, and its difference is odd. Conversely, if $S=\{a,a+d,\dots,a+(k-1)d\}$ with $d$ odd and $(a+id)+(a+jd)$ is even, then $(i+j)d$ is even, so $i+j$ is even and the average $a+\frac{i+j}{2}d$ is in $S$.

Counting. A progression with difference $d$ and $L\ge2$ terms inside $\{1,\dots,10\}$ has $10-(L-1)d$ possible starting values when $(L-1)d\le 9$. Hence

  • $d=1$: $9+8+\dots+1=45$;

  • $d=3$: $7+4+1=12$; $d=5$: $5$; $d=7$: $3$; $d=9$: $1$.

The number of balanced subsets is $45+12+5+3+1=\boxed{66}$.

Solution 2 (Counting by the two extreme elements)

First we show that the balanced sets are exactly the arithmetic progressions (of at least two terms) with odd difference. Let $S=\{s_1<\dots<s_k\}$ be balanced. If two consecutive elements had the same parity, their average would be an element of $S$ strictly between them, which is impossible; so consecutive elements alternate in parity. Then $s_i+s_{i+2}$ is even, and the average of $s_i$ and $s_{i+2}$ is an element strictly between them, hence equal to $s_{i+1}$. So all gaps are equal, and they are odd. Conversely, in a progression $a+jd$ with $d$ odd, $(a+id)+(a+jd)$ is even only if $i+j$ is even, and then the average $a+\frac{i+j}2d$ belongs to the progression.

Now a balanced subset of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ is determined by its smallest element $s$, its largest element $t>s$, and an odd divisor $d$ of $g=t-s$ (the common difference); every such choice gives a valid set. There are $10-g$ pairs $(s,t)$ with $t-s=g$. Writing $\delta(g)$ for the number of odd divisors of $g$, the count is \[\sum_{g=1}^{9}(10-g)\,\delta(g)=9\cdot1+8\cdot1+7\cdot2+6\cdot1+5\cdot2+4\cdot2+3\cdot2+2\cdot1+1\cdot3 ,\] which is $9+8+14+6+10+8+6+2+3=\boxed{66}$.

Problem 8

MCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Extremal Element Selection

Let $c_n$ be the number of ways to place the integers from $1$ through $n^2$, inclusive, in an $n$ by $n$ grid such that every row and every column forms an arithmetic sequence. What is $c_1+c_2+\dots+c_{100}$?

Show the answer

$809$

Show the solution

Solution (Following the small numbers)

For $n=1$ there is one grid, and for $n=2$ every row and column has only two entries, so all $4!=24$ placements work: $c_1=1$, $c_2=24$. Let $n\ge3$ and $N=n-1$; write $g(i,j)$ for the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ ($0\le i,j\le N$). The entries are distinct, so every row and column is a strictly monotone arithmetic sequence and its smallest and largest terms are at its ends.

Step 1: $1$ and $n^2$ are in corners. The number $1$ is the smallest entry of its row and of its column, so it is at an end of both; the same holds for $n^2$. Using the $4$ rotations of the square we put $1$ at $(0,0)$; the factor $4$ is restored at the end.

Step 2: $2$ is next to $1$. If $2$ is in row $0$, that row is $1,1+d,1+2d,\dots$ with an integer $d\ge1$, and $1+jd=2$ forces $d=j=1$: $2$ is at $(0,1)$. Similarly, if $2$ is in column $0$ it is at $(1,0)$. Otherwise $2$ is the smallest entry of its row and of its column, so it is at an end of both, i.e. at the corner $(N,N)$. Then $n^2$ is at $(0,N)$ or $(N,0)$, say $(0,N)$ (the other case is the mirror image). Column $N$ runs from $n^2$ down to $2$ in $N$ equal steps, so $N=n-1$ divides $n^2-2=(n-1)(n+1)-1$, forcing $n-1\mid1$, impossible for $n\ge3$. So $2$ is at $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$; using the reflection in the diagonal through $(0,0)$ we take $2$ at $(1,0)$ (factor $2$ at the end). Then column $0$ is $1,2,\dots,n$.

Step 3: where is $n+1$? It is not in column $0$. Suppose it is at $(i,j)$. If $j\ge2$, the entry $g(i,1)$ lies strictly between $g(i,0)=i+1\le n$ and $n+1$, so it is one of $1,\dots,n$, all of which are in column $0$: impossible. So $j=1$. All entries of column $1$ exceed $n$, so $n+1$ is the smallest one and sits at an end: $i=0$ or $i=N$.

If $i=N$, row $N$ is $n,n+1,\dots,2n-1$. The corner entries $1$, $n$, $2n-1$ are less than $n^2$, so $n^2$ is at $(0,N)$ and row $0$ is $1,1+(n+1),\dots$; its entry $n+2\le 2n-1$ already occurs in row $N$. Contradiction.

So $i=0$, and row $0$ is $1,n+1,2n+1,\dots,Nn+1$.

Step 4: the grid is forced. Since row $i$ is arithmetic, $g(i,j)=g(i,0)+j\,(g(i,1)-g(i,0))$, and columns $0$ and $1$ are arithmetic, so $g(i,j)=1+i+nj+s\,ij$ for some constant $s$. The four corners are $1$, $n$, $Nn+1$ and $g(N,N)=n^2+sN^2$; the first three are less than $n^2$, and $n^2$ is in a corner, so $s=0$ and $g(i,j)=1+i+nj$. This grid (column $j$ is $jn+1,\dots,jn+n$) is valid.

Thus for $n\ge3$ exactly one grid has $1$ at $(0,0)$ and $2$ at $(1,0)$, and its $8$ images under the symmetries of the square are distinct (they move $1$ and $2$ to different places), so $c_n=8$. Therefore \[c_1+c_2+\dots+c_{100}=1+24+98\cdot8=\boxed{809}.\]

Problem 9

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 12: Exponential Congruences and Order Structure · Orders and Primitive Behavior

Let $S$ be a set of integers with the property that for any two distinct integers $m$ and $n$ in $S$, we have that $mn^{254}-nm^{254}$ is not divisible by $2026$. What is the maximum possible number of integers that can be contained in $S$?

Show the answer

$4$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Square roots modulo a prime)

Write $mn^{254}-nm^{254}=mn\,(n^{253}-m^{253})$ and $2026=2\cdot1013$, where $1013$ is prime. The expression is always even: if $m$ or $n$ is even the factor $mn$ is even, and if both are odd then $n^{253}-m^{253}$ is even. So the condition says exactly that for distinct $m,n\in S$, \[1013\nmid m,\qquad 1013\nmid n,\qquad n^{253}\not\equiv m^{253}\pmod{1013}.\] Thus $|S|$ is at most the number of different residues $x^{253}$ with $1013\nmid x$, and this bound is attained by choosing one integer for each residue.

Let $p=1013$; note $1012=4\cdot253$. For $p\nmid x$, Fermat gives $(x^{506})^2=x^{1012}\equiv1$, and modulo a prime $y^2\equiv 1$ forces $y\equiv\pm1$ (since $p\mid(y-1)(y+1)$). So $x^{506}\equiv\pm1$.

  • If $x^{506}\equiv1$, then $(x^{253})^2\equiv1$ and $x^{253}\equiv\pm1$.

  • If $x^{506}\equiv-1$: since $45^2=2025=2\cdot1013-1\equiv-1$, we get $(x^{253})^2\equiv 45^2$, so $p\mid (x^{253}-45)(x^{253}+45)$ and $x^{253}\equiv\pm45$.

So $x^{253}\in\{1,-1,45,-45\}$, at most $4$ values. All four occur: $1^{253}=1$, $(-1)^{253}=-1$, and since $45^4\equiv1$, $45^{253}=(45^4)^{63}\cdot45\equiv45$ and $(-45)^{253}\equiv-45$. Hence $S=\{1,-1,45,-45\}$ works, and the maximum is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 2 (A primitive root)

Since $2026=2\cdot 1013$ with $1013$ prime and $mn^{254}-nm^{254}=mn(n^{253}-m^{253})$, first note this number is always even ($mn$ is even unless $m,n$ are both odd, and then $n^{253}-m^{253}$ is even). So the condition is that $1013$ divides none of $m$, $n$, $n^{253}-m^{253}$, for every two distinct $m,n\in S$. Hence the maximum size of $S$ equals the number of distinct values of $x^{253}\bmod 1013$ for $x\not\equiv0$.

Let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p=1013$, so every nonzero residue is $g^k$ with $0\le k<1012$, and $g^a\equiv g^b$ iff $a\equiv b\pmod{1012}$. Then $(g^k)^{253}=g^{253k}$, and since $1012=4\cdot253$, \[g^{253k}\equiv g^{253k'}\iff 1012\mid 253(k-k')\iff 4\mid k-k'.\] So $x^{253}$ takes exactly $4$ distinct values, namely $g^{0},g^{253},g^{506},g^{759}$, obtained from $k\equiv0,1,2,3\pmod4$. Taking $S=\{1,g,g^2,g^3\}$ (as integers) gives a valid set with $4$ elements, and no valid set has more. The answer is $\boxed{4}$.

Solution 2 (Inversion maps the line to a circle)

The glow-up is the inversion in the unit circle centered at $O$; it is its own inverse, so $L'$ is the image of $L$ under this inversion.

$352$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Subtract $x^2$)

The polynomial $f(x)-x^2$ has degree $3$ (the $x^3$ term of $f$ is untouched) and vanishes at $4,5,6$, so $f(x)=x^2+C(x-4)(x-5)(x-6)$, where $C\ne0$ is the leading coefficient of $f$, an integer. Conversely every integer $C\neq0$ gives such a cubic with integer coefficients.

Solution 2 (Finite differences)

Let $s=f(7)$. For a cubic, the fourth finite difference vanishes: \[f(8)-4f(7)+6f(6)-4f(5)+f(4)=0,\] so $f(8)=4s-216+100-16=4s-132$, which increases with $s$.

Problem 17

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Telescoping Sums and Products

Alice and Bob are playing a game with infinite rounds, starting at round $1$. In round $n$, Alice and Bob each flip identical independent coins that show heads with probability $p_n$, where $p_n$ satisfies \[p_n(1-p_n)=\frac{5}{17\cdot 3^n+15}.\] In each round, if one player's coin shows heads and the other shows tails, the player with heads wins. Otherwise, they continue to the next round. What is the probability that Alice eventually wins?

Show the answer

$\dfrac{5}{44}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Probability that the game never ends)

(Such $p_n$ exist, since $\frac5{17\cdot3^n+15}\le\frac5{66}<\frac14$.) All coin flips are independent (across rounds as well as between the players). In round $n$ the coins agree with probability \[q_n=p_n^2+(1-p_n)^2=1-2p_n(1-p_n)=1-\frac{10}{17\cdot3^n+15}=\frac{17\cdot 3^n+5}{17\cdot3^n+15}=\frac{17+5\cdot3^{-n}}{17+5\cdot3^{1-n}}.\] The product telescopes: By independence, $\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_n$ is the probability that rounds $1,\dots,N$ are all ties, and it telescopes: \[\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_n=\frac{17+5\cdot3^{-N}}{17+5}\longrightarrow\frac{17}{22}\qquad(N\to\infty),\] so the game goes on forever with probability $\frac{17}{22}$ and ends with probability $\frac5{22}$. In each round the outcomes “Alice heads, Bob tails” and “Alice tails, Bob heads” have the same probability $p_n(1-p_n)$, so by symmetry Alice and Bob are equally likely to be the winner. Alice wins with probability $\frac12\cdot\frac5{22}=\boxed{\tfrac5{44}}$.

Solution 2 (Summing round by round)

All coin flips are taken independent, across rounds as well as between the players. Let $q_k=1-2p_k(1-p_k)$ be the probability that round $k$ is a tie, and $r_n=q_1q_2\cdots q_n$ ($r_0=1$), the probability that rounds $1,\dots,n$ are all ties. We claim $r_n=\dfrac{17\cdot3^n+5}{22\cdot 3^n}$. This holds for $n=0$, and \[q_{n+1}=1-\frac{10}{17\cdot3^{n+1}+15}=\frac{17\cdot 3^{n+1}+5}{17\cdot3^{n+1}+15}=\frac{17\cdot 3^{n+1}+5}{3(17\cdot3^{n}+5)},\] so $r_{n+1}=r_nq_{n+1}=\frac{17\cdot3^{n+1}+5}{22\cdot3^{n+1}}$.

Alice wins in round $n$ when rounds $1,\dots,n-1$ are ties and in round $n$ she gets heads and Bob tails: \[r_{n-1}\,p_n(1-p_n)=\frac{17\cdot3^{n-1}+5}{22\cdot 3^{n-1}}\cdot\frac{5}{3(17\cdot 3^{n-1}+5)}=\frac{5}{22\cdot 3^{n}}.\] These events are disjoint, so Alice wins with probability \[\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{5}{22\cdot3^n}=\frac5{22}\cdot\frac12=\boxed{\tfrac5{44}}.\]

$21$

Show the 2 solutions

Problem 3

Show the answer

$82$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Fill freely, then complete)

Both conditions only depend on the entries modulo $4$, and each residue class modulo $4$ contains exactly two of $1,\dots,8$ ($r$ and $r+4$). So $n=2^{32}\,m$, where $m$ is the number of fillings of the $8\times4$ grid with residues modulo $4$ satisfying the conditions.

So $m=4^{21}\cdot2^7=2^{49}$ and \[n=2^{32}\cdot2^{49}=2^{81},\] which has $\boxed{82}$ positive divisors.

Solution 2 (Roots of unity filter)

For a grid $(v_{ij})$ ($1\le i\le8$, $1\le j\le4$, entries in $\{1,\dots,8\}$) use \[[\text{row } i\text{ even}]=\frac12\sum_{\varepsilon_i=\pm1}\varepsilon_i^{\,\sum_j v_{ij}},\qquad [\text{column } j\equiv0\ (4)]=\frac14\sum_{\omega_j^4=1}\omega_j^{\,\sum_i v_{ij}} .\] Multiplying all twelve filters and summing over all grids, \[n=\frac{1}{2^8\,4^4}\sum_{\varepsilon,\omega}\ \prod_{i,j}\ \sum_{v=1}^{8}(\varepsilon_i\omega_j)^v .\] Each $z=\varepsilon_i\omega_j$ is a fourth root of unity, and $\sum_{v=1}^{8}z^v$ equals $8$ if $z=1$ and $0$ otherwise (it is $(1+z^4)(z+z^2+z^3+z^4)$, and $z+z^2+z^3+z^4=0$ for $z\ne1$). So a term is nonzero only if $\varepsilon_i\omega_j=1$ for all $i,j$, i.e. all $\varepsilon_i$ equal some $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$ and all $\omega_j=\varepsilon$. There are two such choices, each contributing $8^{32}$. Hence \[n=\frac{2\cdot 8^{32}}{2^8\cdot 2^8}=2^{1+96-16}=2^{81},\] and $n$ has $\boxed{82}$ divisors.

Power Round

Problem 1(h)

MNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 8: Modular Arithmetic and Congruence Methods · Periodic and Cyclic Behavior Modulo $n$

Let $k$ be a positive integer and let $a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{k-1}$ and $c_0,c_1,\dots,c_{k-1}$ be integer constants. Define the sequence $g$ by $g(0)=a_0$, $g(1)=a_1$, $\dots$, $g(k-1)=a_{k-1}$, and \[g(i)=c_0\,g(i-k)+c_1\,g(i-k+1)+\dots+c_{k-1}\,g(i-1)\qquad\text{for all } i\ge k.\] If $g(0)=a_0=0$ and $c_0=1$, prove that for all positive integers $n$, there exist infinitely many terms of $g$ that are divisible by $n$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pigeonhole and running backwards)

Fix $n$ and work modulo $n$. Consider the state vectors \[s_i=\bigl(g(i),g(i+1),\dots,g(i+k-1)\bigr)\bmod n,\qquad i\ge0 .\] There are only $n^k$ possible states, so by the pigeonhole principle $s_a=s_b$ for some $a<b$. Let $T=b-a$.

Going backwards. Because $c_0=1$, the recurrence can be solved for its oldest term: \[g(i-k)=g(i)-c_1g(i-k+1)-\dots-c_{k-1}g(i-1)\qquad(i\ge k).\] So $s_{i-1}$ is determined by $s_i$ (modulo $n$) for every $i\ge 1$. If $a=0$, then $s_0=s_T$ already. If $a\ge1$, then $s_a=s_b$ gives $s_{a-1}=s_{b-1}$, and repeating this $a$ times gives $s_0=s_{T}$. Going forward, $s_{i+1}$ is determined by $s_i$, so $s_0=s_T$ gives $s_T=s_{2T}$, then $s_{2T}=s_{3T}$, and so on: $s_{jT}=s_0$ for all $j\ge0$.

The first coordinate of $s_{jT}$ is $g(jT)$, so $g(jT)\equiv g(0)=0\pmod n$ for every $j\ge0$. These are infinitely many terms divisible by $n$.

Solution 2 (The state map is a permutation)

Fix $n$ and let $X=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^k$, a finite set. Define $\Phi:X\to X$ by \[\Phi(x_0,x_1,\dots,x_{k-1})=\bigl(x_1,\dots,x_{k-1},\,x_0+c_1x_1+\dots+c_{k-1}x_{k-1}\bigr),\] using $c_0=1$. With $s_i=(g(i),\dots,g(i+k-1))\bmod n$, the recurrence says exactly $s_{i+1}=\Phi(s_i)$, so $s_i=\Phi^i(s_0)$.

$\Phi$ is injective: if $\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)$ then $x_1=y_1,\dots,x_{k-1}=y_{k-1}$, and comparing last coordinates gives $x_0=y_0$. An injective map of a finite set to itself is a permutation. The permutations of $X$ form a finite group, so $\Phi$ has a finite order $T\ge1$ with $\Phi^T=\mathrm{id}$ (among $\Phi,\Phi^2,\dots$ two powers coincide, $\Phi^a=\Phi^b$ with $a<b$, and composing with the inverse permutation $a$ times gives $\Phi^{b-a}=\mathrm{id}$).

Then $s_{jT}=\Phi^{jT}(s_0)=s_0$ for all $j\ge0$, and comparing first coordinates, $g(jT)\equiv g(0)=0\pmod n$. So $n$ divides the infinitely many terms $g(0),g(T),g(2T),\dots$.
