Problem 1

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 4: Divisibility and Prime Structure · Prime Factorization and Structure

For a positive integer $n$, call $n$ a good number if and only if every prime factor of $n$ is not greater than $\sqrt{n}$. Show that there exist $2026$ coprime good numbers such that the difference between any two of them is a good number.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Sixth powers minus one)

We prove the stronger statement that the $2026$ numbers can be chosen pairwise coprime.

Let $N=2026^6$ and $M=N!$, so every prime $q\le N$ divides $M$. For $i=1,2,\dots,2026$ put \[x_i=iM,\qquad a_i=x_i^6-1 .\]

Each $a_i$ is good. For $x\ge2$, \[x^6-1=(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1),\] so every prime factor of $x^6-1$ is at most $x^2+x+1$. Moreover $(x^2+x+1)^2=x^4+2x^3+3x^2+2x+1\le x^6-1$ for $x\ge2$ (at $x=2$ it reads $49\le63$, and $x^6$ grows faster). Hence every prime factor of $a_i$ is at most $\sqrt{a_i}$.

The differences are good. For $i>j$, \[a_i-a_j=M^6\,(i^6-j^6).\] Every prime factor of $M$ is at most $N$, and every prime factor of $i^6-j^6$ is less than $2026^6=N$. On the other hand $a_i-a_j\ge M^6$, so $\sqrt{a_i-a_j}\ge M^3>N$. Thus every prime factor of $a_i-a_j$ is less than $\sqrt{a_i-a_j}$.

The $a_i$ are pairwise coprime. Let a prime $q$ divide both $a_i$ and $a_j$ ($i\ne j$). Then $q$ divides \[j^6a_i-i^6a_j=j^6(i^6M^6-1)-i^6(j^6M^6-1)=i^6-j^6,\] which is a nonzero integer of absolute value less than $N$; so $q<N$ and therefore $q\mid M$. But then $a_i=(iM)^6-1\equiv-1\pmod q$, a contradiction.

So $a_1,\dots,a_{2026}$ are pairwise coprime good numbers whose pairwise differences are good numbers.

Solution 2 (Squares of widely spaced primes)

We prove the stronger statement that the $2026$ numbers can be chosen pairwise coprime.

Since there are infinitely many primes, we can choose odd primes $p_1<p_2<\dots<p_{2026}$ with $p_{i+1}>2p_i$ for every $i$ (for example $p_1=3$ and $p_{i+1}$ the smallest prime greater than $2p_i$). Put $a_i=p_i^2$.

Each $a_i$ is good, because its only prime factor is $p_i=\sqrt{a_i}$. The $a_i$ are pairwise coprime, being squares of distinct primes.

The differences are good. Let $i>j$ and write $q=p_i$, $p=p_j$, so $q>2p$ (as $q\ge p_{j+1}>2p$). Then \[a_i-a_j=q^2-p^2=(q-p)(q+p),\] and every prime factor of $a_i-a_j$ divides $q-p$ or $q+p$.

  • A prime factor of $q-p$ is at most $q-p$, and $q-p\le\sqrt{(q-p)(q+p)}$ because $q-p\le q+p$.

  • The number $q+p$ is even (both primes are odd) and greater than $2$, so each of its prime factors is $2$ or an odd prime dividing $\frac{q+p}2$; in both cases it is at most $\frac{q+p}2$. Moreover \[\Bigl(\frac{q+p}2\Bigr)^2\le(q-p)(q+p)\iff q+p\le4(q-p)\iff5p\le3q,\] which holds because $3q>6p$. So $\frac{q+p}2\le\sqrt{(q-p)(q+p)}$.

Hence every prime factor of $a_i-a_j$ is at most $\sqrt{a_i-a_j}$, and $a_i-a_j$ is good.

So $p_1^2,\dots,p_{2026}^2$ are pairwise coprime good numbers whose pairwise differences are good numbers.

Problem 2

MAlgebra · LPS VII, Chapter 22: Summation, Telescoping, and Discrete Transformation · Finite Difference and Summation Transformation

Real numbers $a_1,\dots,a_n$ satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n}ia_i=\frac{n(n^2-1)}{6}$. Let \[D_k=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i-\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}a_i\qquad(1\le k\le n-1).\] Determine the maximum value of $\min_{1\le k\le n-1}D_k$.

Show the answer

$\dfrac n2$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (A weighted average of the $D_k$)

Assume $n\ge2$. Write $D_k=\sum_{i=1}^n c_{k,i}a_i$ with $c_{k,i}=\frac1k$ for $i\le k$ and $c_{k,i}=-\frac1{n-k}$ for $i>k$, and use the weights $w_k=k(n-k)>0$. The coefficient of $a_i$ in $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}w_kD_k$ is \[\sum_{k\ge i}(n-k)-\sum_{k<i}k=\frac{(n-i)(n-i+1)}2-\frac{(i-1)i}2=\frac{n(n+1)}2-ni .\] Hence, using the two given sums, \[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)D_k=\frac{n(n+1)}2\sum_i a_i-n\sum_i ia_i=\frac{n(n+1)}2\cdot\frac{n(n-1)}2-n\cdot\frac{n(n^2-1)}6=\frac{n^2(n^2-1)}{12}.\] Also $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)=\frac{n(n^2-1)}6$. So the weighted average of $D_1,\dots,D_{n-1}$ with the positive weights $k(n-k)$ equals \[\frac{n^2(n^2-1)/12}{n(n^2-1)/6}=\frac n2 ,\] and therefore $\min_kD_k\le\frac n2$.

Equality is attained by $a_i=n-i$: indeed $\sum(n-i)=\frac{n(n-1)}2$ and $\sum i(n-i)=\frac{n^2(n+1)}2-\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6=\frac{n(n^2-1)}6$, and for every $k$ \[D_k=\Bigl(n-\frac{k+1}2\Bigr)-\Bigl(n-\frac{n+k+1}2\Bigr)=\frac n2 .\] The maximum of $\min_kD_k$ is $\boxed{\dfrac n2}$.

Solution 2 (Partial sums)

Assume $n\ge2$, let $T=\frac{n(n-1)}2$ and $S_k=a_1+\dots+a_k$ (so $S_n=T$). Since $\sum_{i}ia_i=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k+a_{k+1}+\dots+a_n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(T-S_{k-1})=nT-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}S_k$, the second condition says \[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}S_k=nT-\frac{n(n^2-1)}6=\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}6 .\tag{1}\] Now \[D_k=\frac{S_k}k-\frac{T-S_k}{n-k}=\frac{nS_k}{k(n-k)}-\frac{T}{n-k},\] so $D_k>\frac n2$ is equivalent to $S_k>\frac{k(n-k)}2+\frac{kT}n=\frac{k(2n-k-1)}2$. Suppose $D_k>\frac n2$ for every $k$. Adding these inequalities for $k=1,\dots,n-1$ gives \[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}S_k>\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{k(2n-1)-k^2}{2}=\frac{(2n-1)\,n(n-1)}4-\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{12}=\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}6,\] contradicting (1). Hence $\min_kD_k\le\frac n2$.

For $a_i=n-i$ both conditions hold and $S_k=\frac{k(2n-k-1)}2$ exactly, so every $D_k$ equals $\frac n2$. The answer is $\boxed{\dfrac n2}$.

Problem 3

HGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 48: Inversion and Circle-Preserving Transformation · Inversion with Power and Radical Structure

In triangle $ABC$, $\angle BAC>90^\circ$. Let $D$ be the reflection of $A$ across line $BC$, and let $P$ be a point on the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$. Let $X$ be the point where the internal angle bisector of $\angle ACP$ meets $AP$. A point $Q$ on side $BC$ satisfies \[\angle XQB+\angle AXC=90^\circ.\] Prove that $\angle PDQ+\angle BAC=180^\circ$.

Show the solution

Solution (An Apollonius circle and an inversion)

Throughout, $X$ lies on segment $AB$, as in the figure of the problem (see the remark below). Let $\alpha=\angle BAC>90^\circ$.

Step 1: $Q$ lies on the circle with diameter $XX'$. Let the external bisector of $\angle ACP$ meet line $AP$ at $X'$. In triangle $ACP$ the angle at $A$ is obtuse, so $CP>CA$; since $X'A/X'P=CA/CP<1$, the point $X'$ lies on line $AP$ beyond $A$. The internal and external bisectors are perpendicular, so $\angle XCX'=90^\circ$ and $C$ lies on the circle $\Gamma$ with diameter $XX'$. In the right triangle $XCX'$, $\angle XX'C=90^\circ-\angle CXX'=90^\circ-\angle AXC$.

Since $X$ is an interior point of segment $AB$, the line $CX$ separates $A$ from $B$; the point $X'$ (on ray $XA$) is on the side of $A$, and $Q$ (on segment $BC$) is on the side of $B$. The hypothesis gives $\angle XQC=180^\circ-\angle XQB=90^\circ+\angle AXC$, hence \[\angle XQC+\angle XX'C=180^\circ ,\] and $Q$, $X'$ lie on opposite sides of $XC$. Therefore $X,Q,C,X'$ are concyclic: $Q\in\Gamma$. So $C$ and $Q$ are the two points where $\Gamma$ meets line $BC$ ($Q\ne C$: with $\varphi=\frac12\angle ACP$ and $\gamma=\angle ACB$ we have $\angle XCB=\gamma-\varphi$ and $\angle AXC=180^\circ-\alpha-\varphi$, so $Q=C$ would force $2\varphi=90^\circ+\gamma-\alpha<\gamma$, whereas $2\varphi=\angle ACP>\angle ACB=\gamma$ because $P$ lies beyond $B$).

Step 2: $\Gamma$ is orthogonal to the circle $\omega=(APD)$. The points $X,X'$ divide $AP$ internally and externally in the same ratio $CA/CP$, so $(A,P;X,X')=-1$. If $K$ is the midpoint of $XX'$ (the centre of $\Gamma$), the harmonic property gives $KA\cdot KP=KX^2$. Hence the power of $K$ with respect to $\omega$ (a circle through $A$ and $P$) equals $KX^2$, the square of the radius of $\Gamma$: the circles $\Gamma$ and $\omega$ are orthogonal.

Step 3: $C$ and $Q$ are inverse in $\omega$. Since $D$ is the reflection of $A$ in $BC$, the centre $O$ of $\omega$ lies on $BC$. Invert in $\omega$. Line $BC$ passes through $O$, so it is mapped to itself; $\Gamma$ is orthogonal to $\omega$, so it is mapped to itself. Therefore the pair $\{C,Q\}=\Gamma\cap BC$ is mapped to itself. (Here $C\ne O$ because $CP>CA$, and $Q\ne O$ because, by orthogonality, the power of $O$ with respect to $\Gamma$ is the square of the radius of $\omega$, not $0$.) If $C$ and $Q$ were both fixed, they would both lie on $\omega$; at a common point of two orthogonal circles the radii are perpendicular, so $KC\perp OC$ and $KQ\perp OQ$, i.e. both $KC$ and $KQ$ would be perpendicular to $BC$, giving two different feet of the perpendicular from $K$ to $BC$, impossible. So the inversion swaps $C$ and $Q$, and $C\notin\omega$.

Step 4: the line $DQ$. Let $Z\ne A$ be the second intersection of line $AC$ with $\omega$. With directed angles modulo $180^\circ$, let $t$ be the tangent to $\omega$ at $D$; then $\measuredangle(DO,DZ)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(t,DZ)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(AD,AZ)$, and since $AD\perp BC$, $\measuredangle(CO,CZ)=\measuredangle(BC,AC)=90^\circ+\measuredangle(AD,AC)$. These are equal, so $O,C,D,Z$ are concyclic. Inverting this circle (it passes through $O$) gives a line through $D$ and $Z$ (both on $\omega$, hence fixed) and through the image $Q$ of $C$. Thus $D,Q,Z$ are collinear.

Step 5: the angle. As $P,A,Z,D$ lie on $\omega$ and $D,Q,Z$ are collinear, \[\measuredangle(DQ,DP)=\measuredangle(DZ,DP)=\measuredangle(AZ,AP)=\measuredangle(AC,AB).\tag{$*$}\] It remains to pass from lines to rays. Let $\beta=\angle ABC$; as $\alpha>90^\circ$, $\beta<90^\circ$. By the reflection, $\angle CBD=\beta$, while $\angle CBP=180^\circ-\beta$ (ray $BP$ is opposite to ray $BA$). The points $D$ and $P$ lie on the same side of $BC$ (the side opposite to $A$) and $\beta<180^\circ-\beta$, so ray $BD$ lies inside $\angle CBP$: the points $C$ and $P$ are on opposite sides of line $BD$. In triangle $DBP$, $\angle DBP=180^\circ-2\beta$, so $\delta=\angle BDP<2\beta$.

Measure rotations counterclockwise, with the figure oriented so that ray $AB$ is turned into ray $AC$ by the rotation $+\alpha$. The reflection in $BC$ reverses orientation, so ray $DB$ is turned into ray $DC$ by $-\alpha$. Since $Q$ is an interior point of segment $BC$, ray $DQ$ is ray $DB$ turned by $-\tau$, where $\tau=\angle BDQ\in(0^\circ,\alpha)$; since $P$ is on the other side of line $DB$ from $C$, ray $DP$ is ray $DB$ turned by $+\delta$. So ray $DP$ is ray $DQ$ turned by $\tau+\delta$, with $0^\circ<\tau+\delta<\alpha+2\beta$. On the other hand, by $(*)$ the line $DP$ is the line $DQ$ turned by the same angle as line $AB$ is from line $AC$, i.e. by $-\alpha$ modulo $180^\circ$. Hence $\tau+\delta\equiv180^\circ-\alpha\pmod{180^\circ}$. In the interval $(0^\circ,\alpha+2\beta)$ the only candidates are $180^\circ-\alpha$ and $360^\circ-\alpha$, and the second is excluded because $360^\circ-\alpha>\alpha+2\beta$ (equivalently $\alpha+\beta<180^\circ$). So $\tau+\delta=180^\circ-\alpha<180^\circ$, and this is the angle between rays $DQ$ and $DP$: \[\angle PDQ=180^\circ-\angle BAC .\]

Remark. Step 1 uses that $X$ lies between $A$ and $B$ (as in the official figure), so that $Q$ and $X'$ are on opposite sides of $CX$. If the bisector meets $AP$ beyond $B$, then $Q$ and $X'$ are on the same side of $CX$, the angle condition no longer puts $Q$ on $\Gamma$, and the conclusion is false.

Problem 4

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 31: Extremal Principle and Bounding Arguments · Bounding and Limiting Arguments

Let $n$ be a positive integer. There are $n$ knights sitting at $n$ seats forming the vertices of a regular $n$-gon around a round table. Every minute, one pair of adjacent knights swaps places. Find the minimum number of exchanges of adjacent knights such that every knight has visited all the seats in the end, and the initial arrangement coincides with the terminal one.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{n^2}2$ for even $n$; $\dfrac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$ for odd $n$

Show the solution

Solution (Winding numbers and crossing counts)

For $n=1$ nothing has to happen and the answer is $0$, which agrees with the formula for odd $n$; let $n\ge2$. Number the seats $0,1,\dots,n-1$ clockwise. Every exchange moves one knight one seat clockwise ($+1$) and the other one seat counterclockwise ($-1$). Follow a knight $A$ and record its position as an integer $x_A$ that starts at its seat number and changes by $\pm1$ at each of its moves; $x_A$ modulo $n$ is its seat. At the end $A$ is back at its seat, so $x_A$ has changed by $w_An$ for an integer $w_A$, the winding number of $A$. Let $m_A$ be the number of moves of $A$ and $E$ the number of exchanges; since each exchange moves two knights, $2E=\sum_A m_A$.

Lemma 1. $m_A\ge n|w_A|$, and $m_A\ge2(n-1)$ if $w_A=0$.

Proof. The first bound holds because $x_A$ changes by $\pm1$ per move. If $w_A=0$, the walk of $x_A$ is closed and meets all $n$ residues modulo $n$, so its largest and smallest values differ by at least $n-1$; a closed walk reaching both needs at least $2(n-1)$ steps. $\square$

Lemma 2. Let $c(A,B)$ be the number of exchanges between knights $A$ and $B$. Then $c(A,B)\ge|w_A-w_B|$ (for $n\ge3$).

Proof. Put $D=x_B-x_A$; it is never divisible by $n$, because $A$ and $B$ sit on different seats. When $A$ or $B$ is exchanged with a third knight, $D$ changes by $1$ and stays between the same two consecutive multiples of $n$, so $\lfloor D/n\rfloor$ does not change. When $A$ and $B$ are exchanged with each other, they sit on adjacent seats, so $D\equiv\pm1\pmod n$, and $D$ changes by $2$ towards the nearest multiple of $n$ and past it; then $\lfloor D/n\rfloor$ changes by exactly $1$. Over the whole process $D$ changes by $(w_B-w_A)n$, hence $\lfloor D/n\rfloor$ changes by $w_B-w_A$, and this needs at least $|w_A-w_B|$ exchanges between $A$ and $B$. $\square$

Finally, every exchange moves one knight by $+1$ and one by $-1$, so $\sum_A x_A$ never changes and \[\sum_A w_A=0 .\]

Even $n$: the answer is $\frac{n^2}2$. By Lemma 1 every knight moves at least $n$ times (if $w_A\ne0$ then $m_A\ge n$, and if $w_A=0$ then $m_A\ge2(n-1)\ge n$), so $E=\frac12\sum m_A\ge\frac{n^2}2$.

Conversely, for even $n$ perform $n$ rounds. In the odd-numbered rounds exchange the pairs of seats $\{0,1\},\{2,3\},\dots,\{n-2,n-1\}$; in the even-numbered rounds exchange $\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{n-1,0\}$. By induction, after an even number of rounds the knights who started on even seats stand on even seats and have all moved clockwise one step per round, and the others stand on odd seats and have moved counterclockwise one step per round; after an odd number of rounds the parities are swapped, which is exactly what the next round needs. After $n$ rounds every knight has walked once around the table in one direction, visiting every seat and returning to its own seat. This uses $n\cdot\frac n2=\frac{n^2}2$ exchanges. (For $n=2$ both rounds exchange the same pair.)

Odd $n\ge3$: lower bound $\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$. Let $P$, $N$, $Z$ be the sets of knights with $w>0$, $w<0$, $w=0$, of sizes $p,q,z$ (so $p+q+z=n$), and let \[s=\sum_{A\in P}w_A=\sum_{B\in N}|w_B| ,\] the two sums being equal because $\sum w_A=0$. Clearly $s\ge\max(p,q)$. Split $E=\sum_{\text{pairs}}c(A,B)$ into the pairs in $P\times N$, the pairs that meet $Z$, and the rest ($\ge0$). By Lemma 2, \[\sum_{A\in P,\,B\in N}c(A,B)\ge\sum_{A\in P,\,B\in N}\bigl(w_A+|w_B|\bigr)=qs+ps=(n-z)s .\] Let $X$ be the sum of $c(Z_0,B)$ over $Z_0\in Z$, $B\notin Z$, and $Y$ the sum of $c$ over pairs inside $Z$. Summing Lemma 1 over the knights of $Z$ gives $X+2Y=\sum_{Z_0\in Z}m_{Z_0}\ge2(n-1)z$, and Lemma 2 gives $X\ge\sum_{Z_0\in Z}\sum_{B\notin Z}|w_B|=2sz$.

  • $z=1$. Then $Y=0$, so $X\ge2(n-1)$; also $p+q=n-1$, so $s\ge\frac{n-1}2$. Hence $E\ge(n-1)s+2(n-1)\ge\frac{(n-1)^2}2+2(n-1)=\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$.

  • $z\ge2$. Here $X+Y=\frac{X+2Y}2+\frac X2\ge(n-1)z+sz$, so $E\ge(n-z)s+(n-1)z+sz=ns+(n-1)z$. If $z=2$, then $p+q=n-2$ is odd and $s\ge\max(p,q)\ge\frac{n-1}2$, so $E\ge\frac{n(n-1)}2+2(n-1)>\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$. If $z\ge3$, then $s\ge\frac{n-z}2$ and $E\ge\frac{n^2}2+\frac{z(n-2)}2\ge\frac{n^2+3n-6}2\ge\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$ since $n\ge3$.

  • $z=0$. By Lemma 1, $E=\frac12\sum m_A\ge\frac n2\sum|w_A|=ns$. As $p+q=n$ is odd, $s\ge\max(p,q)\ge\frac{n+1}2$. If $s\ge\frac{n+3}2$, then $E\ge\frac{n(n+3)}2$, which is more than enough. Otherwise $s=\max(p,q)=\frac{n+1}2$; reversing the direction of the table if necessary, $p=\frac{n+1}2$ and $q=\frac{n-1}2$. Then $s=p$ forces $w_A=1$ for all $A\in P$, and the $q$ knights of $N$ have $\sum|w_B|=q+1$: one has $w=-2$ and the others $w=-1$. Lemma 2 over all pairs now gives \[E\ge2p(q-1)+3p+(q-1)=(n+1)\cdot\frac{n-3}2+\frac{3(n+1)}2+\frac{n-3}2=\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2,\] counting the pairs $(+1,-1)$, $(+1,-2)$ and $(-2,-1)$.

In every case $E\ge\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$.

Odd $n\ge3$: a schedule with $\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2$ exchanges. Call the knights on the even seats $0,2,\dots,n-1$ right-movers (there are $\frac{n+1}2$) and those on the odd seats left-movers ($\frac{n-1}2$). Cyclically the seats then read $R\,L\,R\,L\cdots R\,L\,R$, with exactly one pair of adjacent right-movers (seats $n-1$ and $0$) and no two adjacent left-movers.

Phase 1: $n+1$ rounds. In each round, every left-mover is exchanged with the knight on its counterclockwise side. If the pattern of types is as described, that neighbour is a right-mover (no two left-movers are adjacent), and the exchanges of one round use disjoint pairs of seats. After the round every left-mover has moved $-1$, every right-mover except the one whose clockwise neighbour is also a right-mover has moved $+1$, and the pattern of types is the old one turned one seat counterclockwise; so the description stays true for the next round. Each round has $\frac{n-1}2$ exchanges.

Now look at the right-movers relative to the pattern (that is, add $1$ to every position after each round). The left-movers stand still; a right-mover advances $2$ seats if it moved and $1$ seat if it waited, and the one that waits is always the one at the left seat of the adjacent pair. So the right-movers cycle through the $\frac{n+1}2$ right-mover places of the pattern, each passing the waiting place once per cycle; in $n+1$ rounds each makes exactly two cycles and waits exactly twice. Hence after phase 1 every right-mover has moved $+(n-1)$ and every left-mover $-(n+1)$: every knight sits one seat counterclockwise of where it started.

Phase 2: a solo lap. Choose one left-mover $K$ and exchange it $n-1$ times in a row with its counterclockwise neighbour. Then $K$ moves $-(n-1)$ and every other knight moves $+1$, so every knight is back at its own seat.

Every knight has now visited all seats: a right-mover moved $n$ times clockwise in total, $K$ moved $2n$ times counterclockwise, and every other left-mover moved $n+1$ times counterclockwise in phase 1. The number of exchanges is \[(n+1)\cdot\frac{n-1}2+(n-1)=\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}2 .\]

Answer. The minimum is $\boxed{\dfrac{n^2}2}$ for even $n$ and $\boxed{\dfrac{(n-1)(n+3)}2}$ for odd $n$.

Day 2

Problem 5

MGeometry · LPS VII, Chapter 36: Coordinate Geometry and Analytic Representation · Cartesian Coordinate Setup

In the Cartesian plane, let \[A_1\left(\frac{q_1}{p_1},\frac{r_1}{p_1}\right),\qquad A_2\left(\frac{q_2}{p_2},\frac{r_2}{p_2}\right),\qquad A_3\left(\frac{q_3}{p_3},\frac{r_3}{p_3}\right),\] where $p_i,q_i,r_i\in\mathbb{N}^+$ $(i=1,2,3)$. Suppose that the three points lie inside the same circle of radius $R$, and \[p_1p_2p_3<\frac{1}{4R^2}.\] Prove that $A_1,A_2,A_3$ are collinear.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Area by the determinant)

Suppose the points are not collinear. The area of triangle $A_1A_2A_3$ is \[[A_1A_2A_3]=\frac12\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}q_1/p_1&r_1/p_1&1\\ q_2/p_2&r_2/p_2&1\\ q_3/p_3&r_3/p_3&1\end{pmatrix}\right|=\frac{|N|}{2p_1p_2p_3},\qquad N=\det\begin{pmatrix}q_1&r_1&p_1\\ q_2&r_2&p_2\\ q_3&r_3&p_3\end{pmatrix}.\] $N$ is an integer, and $N\ne0$ because the points are not collinear, so $|N|\ge1$ and \[[A_1A_2A_3]\ge\frac1{2p_1p_2p_3}>2R^2 .\] On the other hand, all three points lie in a disc of radius $R$, so every side is at most $2R$ and every height is at most $2R$; hence the area is at most $\frac12\cdot2R\cdot2R=2R^2$. This contradiction shows that $A_1,A_2,A_3$ are collinear.

Solution 2 (A cross product)

Put $u=\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}$ and $v=\overrightarrow{A_1A_3}$. Their cross product is \[u\times v=\Bigl(\frac{q_2}{p_2}-\frac{q_1}{p_1}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{r_3}{p_3}-\frac{r_1}{p_1}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{r_2}{p_2}-\frac{r_1}{p_1}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{q_3}{p_3}-\frac{q_1}{p_1}\Bigr).\] Multiplying out, every term has denominator $p_1p_2$, $p_1p_3$, $p_2p_3$ or $p_1^2$, and the terms with $p_1^2$ cancel ($\frac{q_1r_1}{p_1^2}-\frac{r_1q_1}{p_1^2}=0$). Hence $p_1p_2p_3\,(u\times v)$ is an integer $N$.

Since the three points are inside a circle of radius $R$, $|u|<2R$ and $|v|<2R$, so \[|N|=p_1p_2p_3\,|u\times v|\le p_1p_2p_3\,|u|\,|v|<4R^2p_1p_2p_3<1 .\] Thus $N=0$, i.e. $u\times v=0$, and $A_1,A_2,A_3$ are collinear.

Problem 6

HCombinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination · Parity and Coloring Arguments

Let a convex polygon be divided into finitely many small triangles, no two of which overlap. Let the set of vertices of the small triangles be $V$. A map $g:V\to V$ satisfies:

  • for any $P\in V$, $g^{(25)}(P)=P$;

  • if $A,B,C$ are the three vertices of the same small triangle, then $g(A),g(B),g(C)$ are also the three vertices of a small triangle.

Prove that there exists $X\in V$ such that $g(X)=X$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Euler's formula modulo 5)

We read the division as a triangulation: any two small triangles are disjoint, share exactly one vertex, or share exactly one full side (see the note). Let $V,E,T$ be the numbers of vertices, sides (edges) and triangles. The vertices and edges form a connected plane graph whose bounded faces are the $T$ triangles, so Euler's formula gives \[V-E+T=1 .\tag{1}\]

$g$ permutes vertices, edges and triangles. From $g^{(25)}=\mathrm{id}$, $g$ is a bijection of $V$. By hypothesis $g$ maps the vertex set of a triangle to the vertex set of a triangle; distinct triangles have distinct vertex sets and $g$ is injective, so $g$ induces a permutation of the triangles. Two vertices are joined by an edge exactly when they are two vertices of a common triangle; hence $g$ also maps edges to edges, injectively, so it permutes the edges.

Orbits. The group generated by $g$ has order dividing $25$, so every orbit (of vertices, edges or triangles) has size $1$, $5$ or $25$.

Assume that $g$ has no fixed vertex. A fixed edge $\{u,v\}$ would have $g(u)=v$, $g(v)=u$; then $g^2(u)=u$, so $g^{24}(u)=u$ and $u=g^{25}(u)=g(u)=v$, impossible. A fixed triangle would have its three vertices permuted by $g$; a permutation of three objects has order $1$, $2$ or $3$, and its order divides $25$, so it is the identity and the vertices are fixed, impossible. Therefore all orbits have size $5$ or $25$, and \[5\mid V,\qquad 5\mid E,\qquad 5\mid T .\] Then $5\mid V-E+T=1$, a contradiction. Hence $g$ has a fixed vertex.

Solution 2 (Counting angles)

We read the division as a triangulation: any two small triangles are disjoint, share exactly one vertex, or share exactly one full side. Let the polygon have $k$ corners; let $I$ be the number of vertices inside the polygon and $B$ the number on its boundary, and $T$ the number of triangles.

An angle count. The angles of all triangles add up to $T\cdot180^\circ$. Around an interior vertex they fill $360^\circ$; at a boundary vertex which is not a corner they fill $180^\circ$; at the corners they fill the interior angles of the polygon, which add up to $(k-2)180^\circ$. Hence \[T=2I+(B-k)+(k-2)=2I+B-2 .\tag{1}\]

$g$ respects this structure. As $g^{(25)}=\mathrm{id}$, $g$ is a bijection; it maps triangles to triangles injectively, hence permutes the triangles, and it maps every pair of vertices of a common triangle (an edge) to an edge. An edge lies on the boundary exactly when it belongs to only one triangle; since $g$ permutes the triangles, the triangles containing $g(e)$ are the images of those containing $e$, so $g$ maps boundary edges to boundary edges, and therefore boundary vertices (endpoints of boundary edges) to boundary vertices. So $g$ permutes the $B$ boundary vertices, the $I$ interior vertices, and the $T$ triangles.

Orbits. All orbits have size $1$, $5$ or $25$. If $g$ fixes no vertex, it fixes no triangle (a fixed triangle has its vertices permuted by a permutation of $3$ objects whose order divides $25$, i.e. the identity). Then $5\mid I$, $5\mid B$, $5\mid T$, and (1) gives $5\mid 2$, a contradiction. So some vertex is fixed.

Problem 7

SNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 17: Sequences, Recurrences, and Discrete Dynamics · Invariant and Monotone Sequence Behavior

Determine the minimum positive real number $c$ such that for any real number $a$, there exists a positive integer $n$ such that \[\|2^na\|\in[0,c]\cup\left\{\frac13,\frac15\right\}.\]

Show the answer

$c=\dfrac3{17}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Binary expansions)

Write $x_n$ for the fractional part of $2^na$; then $x_{n+1}$ is the fractional part of $2x_n$ and $\|2^na\|=\min(x_n,1-x_n)$.

$c\ge\frac3{17}$. Take $a=\frac3{17}$. The fractional parts $x_n$ ($n\ge1$) run through $\frac6{17},\frac{12}{17},\frac7{17},\frac{14}{17},\frac{11}{17},\frac5{17},\frac{10}{17},\frac3{17}$ periodically, so $\|2^na\|$ takes only the values $\frac3{17},\frac5{17},\frac6{17},\frac7{17}$; none of them is $\frac13$ or $\frac15$, and the smallest is $\frac3{17}$. Hence $c\ge\frac3{17}$.

$c=\frac3{17}$ works. Let $a$ be real and suppose that $\|2^na\|>\frac3{17}$ for all $n\ge1$; we show that some $\|2^na\|$ equals $\frac13$ or $\frac15$. If $a$ is a dyadic rational, some $x_n=0$; so $a$ is not dyadic and its binary expansion $a=\lfloor a\rfloor+0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots$ is unique, with $x_n=0.b_{n+1}b_{n+2}\ldots$. Note that \[\tfrac3{17}=0.\overline{00101101}\ (\text{binary}),\qquad 1-\tfrac3{17}=0.\overline{11010010}.\] The condition says: for every $n\ge1$, the tail $t_n=b_{n+1}b_{n+2}\ldots$ satisfies $0.t_n>\frac3{17}$ if it starts with $0$, and $0.\bar t_n>\frac3{17}$ if it starts with $1$, where $\bar t$ is the complementary word (exchange $0$ and $1$). The condition is symmetric under complementing, and for non-dyadic numbers the numerical order of $0.t$ agrees with the lexicographic order of the words.

(a) No tail starts with $000$ or $111$: such a tail would give $\|x_n\|<\frac18<\frac3{17}$.

(b) Let a tail start with $00$. It cannot be $001\,0\,0\ldots$ (then $0.t<0.00101<\frac3{17}$), nor $00101\,0\ldots$ (then $0.t<0.001011\le\frac3{17}$); after $001011$ the next digit is $0$ by (a), and then the next digit must be $1$ (otherwise $0.t<0.00101101\le\frac3{17}$). So a tail starting with $0010$ starts with $00101101$. Look at the tail beginning at the $11$ inside this block: it starts with $1101$, and by the complementary statement it must start with $11010010$. Hence the block $00101101$ is followed by $0010$, which again forces $00101101$, and so on forever. Then the tail equals $0.\overline{00101101}=\frac3{17}$ exactly, contradicting $0.t_n>\frac3{17}$. Consequently every tail starting with $00$ starts with $0011$, and (by complementing) every tail starting with $11$ starts with $1100$.

(c) If some tail $t_n$ starts with $00$, then by (b) the digits continue $0011\,00\,11\,00\ldots$ forever, so $x_n=0.\overline{0011}=\frac15$ and $\|2^na\|=\frac15$. The same happens if some tail starts with $11$: by (b) it starts with $1100$, and the tail two digits later starts with $00$. If no tail contains $00$ or $11$, the digits from $b_2$ on alternate, so $x_1\in\{\frac13,\frac23\}$ and $\|2a\|=\frac13$.

In every case some $\|2^na\|$ lies in $[0,\frac3{17}]\cup\{\frac13,\frac15\}$. Hence the minimum is $\boxed{c=\dfrac3{17}}$.

Solution 2 (The tent map and its two expanding branches)

For a real $x$ let $t=\|x\|\in[0,\frac12]$. Then $\|2x\|=T(t)$, where \[T(t)=\begin{cases}2t,& 0\le t\le\frac14,\\ 1-2t,&\frac14\le t\le\frac12\end{cases}\] (if $x=k\pm t$ with $k$ an integer, then $2x=2k\pm2t$, and the distance from $2t$ to the nearest integer is $2t$ or $1-2t$). Write $t_n=\|2^na\|$, so $t_{n+1}=T(t_n)$.

$c\ge\frac3{17}$. For $a=\frac3{17}$ the values $t_1,t_2,\dots$ are $\frac6{17},\frac5{17},\frac7{17},\frac3{17}$, repeating with period $4$ (indeed $T(\frac3{17})=\frac6{17}$, $T(\frac6{17})=\frac5{17}$, $T(\frac5{17})=\frac7{17}$, $T(\frac7{17})=\frac3{17}$). None of them is $\frac13$ or $\frac15$, and the smallest is $\frac3{17}$, so for $c<\frac3{17}$ this $a$ has no admissible $n$. Hence $c\ge\frac3{17}$.

$c=\frac3{17}$ works. Suppose, for a contradiction, that for some $a$ every $t_n$ ($n\ge1$) lies outside $[0,\frac3{17}]\cup\{\frac13,\frac15\}$. Then $t_n>\frac3{17}$ for all $n\ge1$. Moreover $t_n<\frac7{17}$ for all $n\ge1$: if $t_n\ge\frac7{17}$, then $t_n\ge\frac14$ and $t_{n+1}=1-2t_n\le\frac3{17}$. So \[\tfrac3{17}<t_n<\tfrac7{17}\qquad(n\ge1).\]

Case 1: $t_n\ge\frac14$ for all $n\ge1$. Then $t_{n+1}=1-2t_n$, i.e. $t_{n+1}-\frac13=-2\bigl(t_n-\frac13\bigr)$, so $\bigl|t_n-\frac13\bigr|=2^{n-1}\bigl|t_1-\frac13\bigr|$. The left side is less than $1$ for all $n$, so $t_1=\frac13$, which is excluded.

Case 2: $t_j<\frac14$ for some $j\ge1$. Call such an index a left index and put $t=t_j$, so $\frac3{17}<t<\frac14$. Then $t_{j+1}=2t$, and $2t<\frac7{17}$ gives $t<\frac7{34}$. As $2t>\frac6{17}>\frac14$, we get $t_{j+2}=1-4t$. If $1-4t\ge\frac14$, then $t_{j+3}=1-2(1-4t)=8t-1>\frac{24}{17}-1=\frac7{17}$, which is impossible. Hence $t_{j+2}=1-4t<\frac14$, and $j+2$ is again a left index. By induction, $j,j+2,j+4,\dots$ are all left indices and \[t_{j+2k+2}=1-4t_{j+2k},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad t_{j+2k+2}-\tfrac15=-4\bigl(t_{j+2k}-\tfrac15\bigr).\] So $\bigl|t_{j+2k}-\frac15\bigr|=4^k\bigl|t_j-\frac15\bigr|$, and since the left side stays below $1$, $t_j=\frac15$, which is excluded.

Both cases are impossible, so for every $a$ some $\|2^na\|$ lies in $[0,\frac3{17}]\cup\{\frac13,\frac15\}$. The minimum is $\boxed{c=\dfrac3{17}}$.

Problem 8

HNumber Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 37: Diophantine Equations: Descent and Infinite Processes · Infinite Descent

Prove that there does not exist a right-angled triangle with rational side lengths and an area of $2026$.

Show the solution

Solution (Three squares in progression and descent)

Let $p=1013$; it is prime and $p\equiv5\pmod 8$, and $2026=2p$.

Reduction. Suppose a right triangle has rational legs $a,b$, hypotenuse $c$ and area $\frac{ab}2=2p$. With $r=\frac c2$ we get $r^2\pm2p=\frac{a^2+b^2\pm2ab}4=\bigl(\frac{a\pm b}2\bigr)^2$. Write $r=\frac he$ in lowest terms ($h,e\ge1$). Then $h^2+2pe^2$ and $h^2-2pe^2$ are squares of rationals, hence of integers: \[h^2+2pe^2=s^2,\qquad h^2-2pe^2=t^2\qquad(s,t\ge0).\tag{S}\] We show that (S) has no solution in positive integers $h,e$; take one with $h$ minimal. Then $\gcd(h,e)=1$ (a common factor $g$ would give $g\mid s,t$ and a smaller solution $h/g$), and $t>0$ since $2p$ is not a square.

Parities. A common prime factor $q$ of $s,t$ divides $2h^2=s^2+t^2$ and $4pe^2=s^2-t^2$. If $q$ is odd and $q\ne p$, then $q\mid h$ and $q\mid e$; if $q=p$, then $p\mid h$, so $p^2\mid s^2-h^2=2pe^2$ and $p\mid e$; if $q=2$, then $4\mid 2h^2$, $h$ is even, $4\mid s^2-h^2=2pe^2$ and $e$ is even. All contradict $\gcd(h,e)=1$, so $\gcd(s,t)=1$, $s,t$ are odd, $2h^2=s^2+t^2\equiv2\pmod 8$ makes $h$ odd, and $8\mid s^2-t^2=4pe^2$ makes $e$ even, $e=2e'$.

A primitive triple. Put $\alpha=\frac{s+t}2$, $\beta=\frac{s-t}2$. They are coprime positive integers with \[\alpha^2+\beta^2=\frac{s^2+t^2}2=h^2,\qquad \alpha\beta=\frac{s^2-t^2}4=pe^2=4pe'^2 .\] So $(\alpha,\beta,h)$ is a primitive Pythagorean triple: there are coprime $m>k>0$ of opposite parity with $h=m^2+k^2$ and $\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{2mk,\,m^2-k^2\}$. Then $2mk(m-k)(m+k)=4pe'^2$, i.e. \[m\cdot k\cdot(m+k)\cdot(m-k)=2p\,e'^2 .\] The four factors are positive and pairwise coprime, so each is a square times a squarefree number, and these squarefree numbers are pairwise coprime with product $2p$. The factor $2$ belongs to the even one of $m,k$; the factor $p$ to any of the four. All numbers below are positive integers.

  • $m=2pa^2$, $k=b^2$, $m\pm k$ squares $c^2,d^2$ ($c,d$ odd): $c^2+d^2=2m\equiv0\pmod4$, impossible.

  • $m=2a^2$, $k=pb^2$, $m\pm k=c^2,d^2$: $c^2+d^2=4a^2\equiv0\pmod 4$ with $c,d$ odd, impossible.

  • $m=2a^2$, $k=b^2$, $m+k=pc^2$, $m-k=d^2$: from $d^2=2a^2-b^2$ with $b,d$ odd, $a$ is odd; then $pc^2=2a^2+b^2\equiv3\pmod 8$, but $pc^2\equiv5\pmod8$.

  • $m=2a^2$, $k=b^2$, $m+k=c^2$, $m-k=pd^2$: from $c^2=2a^2+b^2$, $a$ is even; then $pd^2=2a^2-b^2\equiv7\pmod8$, impossible.

  • $m=pa^2$, $k=2b^2$, $m\pm k=c^2,d^2$: $c^2=pa^2+2b^2\equiv5+2b^2\in\{5,7\}\pmod8$, not a square.

  • $m=a^2$, $k=2b^2$, $m+k=pc^2$, $m-k=d^2$: from $d^2=a^2-2b^2$, $b$ is even; then $pc^2=a^2+2b^2\equiv1\pmod8$, impossible.

  • $m=a^2$, $k=2b^2$, $m+k=c^2$, $m-k=pd^2$: from $c^2=a^2+2b^2$, $b$ is even; then $pd^2\equiv1\pmod 8$, impossible.

  • $m=a^2$, $k=2pb^2$, $m+k=c^2$, $m-k=d^2$: then $a^2+2pb^2=c^2$ and $a^2-2pb^2=d^2$, a solution of (S) with $h'=a$.

(Here $c^2\equiv d^2\equiv a^2\equiv1\pmod8$ for odd numbers was used throughout.) Only the last case survives, and it gives a solution of (S) with $h'=a\le a^2=m<m^2+k^2=h$, contradicting the minimality of $h$. Hence (S) has no solution, and no rational right triangle has area $2026$.
