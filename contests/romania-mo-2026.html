Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Determine the strictly monotone functions $f:\mathbb{N}^+\to\mathbb{N}^+$ such that \[f(f(n))=4n+3\quad\text{and}\quad f(f(n)-n)=2n+3\] for every positive integer $n$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=2n+1$

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Controlling the increments)

$f$ is increasing. A strictly decreasing function would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence $f(1)>f(2)>f(3)>\cdots$ of positive integers, which is impossible. Hence $f$ is strictly increasing, so $f(k+1)\ge f(k)+1$ for every $k$, and therefore \[f(y)-f(x)\ge y-x\qquad\text{whenever } y\ge x. \tag{1}\] Since $f(f(n)-n)$ must be defined, $h(n)=f(n)-n$ is a positive integer for every $n$.

The increments of $h$. The second equation for $n$ and $n+1$ gives \[f(h(n+1))-f(h(n))=(2n+5)-(2n+3)=2 .\] The difference is positive and $f$ is increasing, so $h(n+1)>h(n)$; by (1), $h(n+1)-h(n)\le 2$. Hence $h(n+1)-h(n)\in\{1,2\}$, and the increment $d_n=f(n+1)-f(n)=h(n+1)-h(n)+1$ satisfies $d_n\in\{2,3\}$ for every $n$.

The increments of $f$ are all $2$. The first equation for $n$ and $n+1$ gives \[4=f(f(n+1))-f(f(n))=\sum_{k=f(n)}^{f(n+1)-1}\bigl(f(k+1)-f(k)\bigr)=\sum_{k=f(n)}^{f(n+1)-1}d_k .\] The last sum has $f(n+1)-f(n)=d_n$ terms, each at least $2$, so $4\ge 2d_n$, that is $d_n\le 2$. Thus $d_n=2$ for every $n$, and $f(n)=2n+c$ with the constant $c=f(1)-2$.

The constant. Now $f(f(n))=2(2n+c)+c=4n+3c$, and this equals $4n+3$, so $c=1$.

Check. For $f(n)=2n+1$: $f(f(n))=2(2n+1)+1=4n+3$ and $f(f(n)-n)=f(n+1)=2n+3$. Hence the only such function is $\boxed{f(n)=2n+1}$.

Solution 2 (Find $f(1)$, then induct)

First, $f$ is strictly increasing: a strictly decreasing $f$ would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence $f(1)>f(2)>\cdots$ of positive integers. Also $f(n)-n\ge 1$ for every $n$, because $f(n)-n$ must lie in the domain $\mathbb{N}^+$.

The value $f(1)$. Let $a=f(1)$; then $a\ge 2$. Putting $n=1$ in the second equation gives $f(a-1)=5$. If $a=2$, this says $f(1)=5$, contradicting $f(1)=2$. So $a\ge 3$, hence $a-1\ge 2$, and since $f$ increases by at least $1$ at each step, \[5=f(a-1)\ge f(1)+(a-2)=2a-2 ,\] so $a\le 3$. Therefore $f(1)=3$.

Induction. Suppose $f(n)=2n+1$ for some $n$. Then $f(n)-n=n+1$, and the second equation reads $f(n+1)=2n+3=2(n+1)+1$. Starting from $f(1)=3$ we obtain $f(n)=2n+1$ for all $n\ge1$.

Check. $f(f(n))=2(2n+1)+1=4n+3$ and $f(f(n)-n)=f(n+1)=2n+3$, and $f$ is strictly increasing. So the answer is $\boxed{f(n)=2n+1}$.

Remark. This argument never uses the first equation $f(f(n))=4n+3$: the second equation and the monotonicity of $f$ already force $f(n)=2n+1$, which then satisfies the first equation automatically.

Solution 3 (Doubling the index)

Monotonicity. A strictly decreasing $f$ would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence $f(1)>f(2)>\cdots$ of positive integers, which is impossible; so $f$ is strictly increasing. Since $f(f(n)-n)$ must be defined, $h(n)=f(n)-n$ is a positive integer for every $n$.

The second equation at $n$ and $n+1$ gives $f(h(n+1))=2n+5>2n+3=f(h(n))$. As $f$ is increasing, $h(n+1)>h(n)$, so $h(n+1)\ge h(n)+1$, that is, $f(n+1)-f(n)\ge2$. Put \[d(n)=f(n)-2n-1 .\] Then $d(n+1)-d(n)=f(n+1)-f(n)-2\ge0$, so $d$ is non-decreasing.

Two identities. The first equation at the argument $f(n)$ gives $f(f(f(n)))=4f(n)+3$; since $f(f(n))=4n+3$, \[f(4n+3)=4f(n)+3. \tag{1}\] The first equation at the argument $h(n)=f(n)-n$ gives $f(f(h(n)))=4h(n)+3$; since $f(h(n))=2n+3$, \[f(2n+3)=4\bigl(f(n)-n\bigr)+3. \tag{2}\]

Comparing at doubled indices. Identity (2) at $2n$ reads $f(4n+3)=4\bigl(f(2n)-2n\bigr)+3$. Comparing with (1) gives $f(2n)=f(n)+2n$, that is, \[d(2n)=f(2n)-4n-1=f(n)-2n-1=d(n).\] Since $n+1\le 2n$ and $d$ is non-decreasing, $d(n)\le d(n+1)\le d(2n)=d(n)$, so $d(n+1)=d(n)$ for every $n$. Hence $d$ is a constant $c$.

The constant. By (1), $d(4n+3)=f(4n+3)-8n-7=4f(n)-8n-4=4d(n)$, so $c=4c$ and $c=0$. Thus $f(n)=2n+1$.

Check. $f(f(n))=2(2n+1)+1=4n+3$ and $f(f(n)-n)=f(n+1)=2n+3$. So the answer is $\boxed{f(n)=2n+1}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 38: Inequalities: Convexity and Functional Methods

Let $n\geq 2$ be a positive integer and $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\in(0,\infty)$ such that there exists a positive integer $k$ with \[a_1^{k-1}+a_2^{k}+\cdots+a_n^{k+n-2}\geq a_1^{k}+a_2^{k+1}+\cdots+a_n^{k+n-1}.\] Show that $a_1^k+a_2^k+\cdots+a_n^k\leq n$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (A term-by-term bound via geometric sums)

Write the hypothesis as \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{\,k+i-2}(a_i-1)\le 0. \tag{1}\]

Lemma. For every real $a>0$ and all integers $k\ge1$ and $j\ge k-1$, \[a^k-1\le k\,a^{j}(a-1).\]

Proof. For $a=1$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise $a^k-1=(a-1)(1+a+\cdots+a^{k-1})$. If $a>1$, each of the $k$ terms $a^t$ ($0\le t\le k-1$) is at most $a^{k-1}\le a^{j}$, so the bracket is at most $k a^{j}$; multiplying by $a-1>0$ gives the claim. If $0<a<1$, each term $a^t$ is at least $a^{k-1}\ge a^{j}$ (the powers of $a$ decrease), so the bracket is at least $k a^{j}$; multiplying by $a-1<0$ reverses this and again gives $a^k-1\le k a^j(a-1)$. $\square$

Conclusion. Apply the lemma to $a=a_i$ and $j=k+i-2\ge k-1$ and add over $i$. Using (1), \[\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(a_i^k-1\bigr)\le k\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^{\,k+i-2}(a_i-1)\le 0 ,\] that is, $a_1^k+a_2^k+\cdots+a_n^k\le n$.

Solution 2 (Monotone gaps between consecutive powers)

Gaps between powers. For $a>0$ and an integer $t\ge0$ put $D_t(a)=a^{t+1}-a^t$. Then \[D_{t+1}(a)-D_t(a)=a^{t+2}-2a^{t+1}+a^t=a^t(a-1)^2\ge0,\] so $D_0(a)\le D_1(a)\le D_2(a)\le\cdots$.

Reading the hypothesis. Moving everything to one side, the hypothesis says \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{k+i-2}(a_i)\le0 .\] Fix an integer $t$ with $0\le t\le k-1$. For every $i\ge1$ we have $t\le k-1\le k+i-2$, so $D_t(a_i)\le D_{k+i-2}(a_i)$; adding over $i$, \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{t+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_t(a_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{k+i-2}(a_i)\le0 .\]

A falling chain. Write $S_t=a_1^t+a_2^t+\cdots+a_n^t$. We have shown $S_{t+1}\le S_t$ for $t=0,1,\dots,k-1$, hence \[S_k\le S_{k-1}\le\cdots\le S_1\le S_0=n,\] and in particular $a_1^k+a_2^k+\cdots+a_n^k\le n$.

Remark. The proof gives more: the power sums $S_1,S_2,\dots,S_k$ decrease, and all are at most $n$.

Solution 3 (Log-convexity of the power sums)

Write $S_j=a_1^j+a_2^j+\cdots+a_n^j$ for integers $j\ge0$; all $S_j$ are positive and $S_0=n$.

Step 1: $S_k\le S_{k-1}$. For each $i$, \[(a_i-1)a_i^{\,k+i-2}-(a_i-1)a_i^{\,k-1}=(a_i-1)\,a_i^{\,k-1}\bigl(a_i^{\,i-1}-1\bigr)\ge0,\] because $a_i-1$ and $a_i^{\,i-1}-1$ are both positive, both negative, or one of them is zero (for $i=1$ the last factor is $0$). Adding over $i$ and using the hypothesis in the form $\sum_i(a_i-1)a_i^{\,k+i-2}\le0$, \[S_k-S_{k-1}=\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i-1)a_i^{\,k-1}\le\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i-1)a_i^{\,k+i-2}\le0 .\]

Step 2: the ratios increase. For $j\ge1$, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the lists $\bigl(a_i^{(j-1)/2}\bigr)_i$ and $\bigl(a_i^{(j+1)/2}\bigr)_i$ gives \[S_j^2=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^{(j-1)/2}a_i^{(j+1)/2}\Bigr)^2\le S_{j-1}S_{j+1}.\] Dividing by $S_jS_{j-1}>0$, the ratios $r_j=S_j/S_{j-1}$ satisfy $r_j\le r_{j+1}$ for all $j\ge1$.

Step 3: the chain. By Step 1, $r_k\le1$, hence $r_1\le r_2\le\cdots\le r_k\le1$. Multiplying, \[\frac{S_k}{S_0}=r_1r_2\cdots r_k\le1,\] that is, $a_1^k+a_2^k+\cdots+a_n^k=S_k\le S_0=n$.

Problem 3

Number Theory · LPS VII, Chapter 25: Diophantine Equations: Factorization and Algebraic Methods

Let $a$ and $b$ be real numbers with $\lfloor a\rfloor=\lfloor b\rfloor>0$. If $\lfloor a^n\rfloor\lfloor b^n\rfloor$ is a perfect square for every positive integer $n$, show that $a=b$.

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Equal floors survive squaring)

Let $m=\lfloor a\rfloor=\lfloor b\rfloor\ge1$; then $a,b\ge1$. Write $x_n=\lfloor a^n\rfloor$ and $y_n=\lfloor b^n\rfloor$.

Lemma 1. If $x\ne y$ are positive integers and $xy$ is a perfect square, then $|\sqrt y-\sqrt x|\ge1$.

Proof. Let $g=\gcd(x,y)$, $x=gx'$, $y=gy'$ with $\gcd(x',y')=1$. Then $x'y'=xy/g^2$ is an integer and the square of the rational number $\sqrt{xy}/g$, hence a perfect square (if $(r/s)^2$ is an integer with $\gcd(r,s)=1$, then $s^2\mid r^2$ forces $s=1$). Two coprime positive integers whose product is a square are themselves squares (compare the exponent of each prime), so $x'=u^2$, $y'=v^2$ with positive integers $u\ne v$. Then $|\sqrt y-\sqrt x|=\sqrt g\,|v-u|\ge1$. $\square$

Lemma 2. If $x_N=y_N$ for some $N\ge1$, then $x_{2N}=y_{2N}$.

Proof. Let $c=x_N=y_N\ge1$. From $c\le a^N<c+1$ we get $c^2\le a^{2N}<(c+1)^2$, so the integer $x_{2N}$ lies in $[c^2,\,c^2+2c]$; the same holds for $y_{2N}$. Suppose $x_{2N}\ne y_{2N}$, say $x_{2N}<y_{2N}$. Their product is a perfect square by hypothesis, so Lemma 1 gives $\sqrt{y_{2N}}-\sqrt{x_{2N}}\ge1$. But \[\sqrt{y_{2N}}-\sqrt{x_{2N}}\le\sqrt{c^2+2c}-c<(c+1)-c=1,\] a contradiction. $\square$

Conclusion. We have $x_1=y_1=m$, so Lemma 2 and induction give $\lfloor a^{N}\rfloor=\lfloor b^{N}\rfloor$ for every $N=2^t$, $t\ge0$. Two numbers with the same integer part differ by less than $1$, so $|b^N-a^N|<1$ for all these $N$. Suppose $a\ne b$, say $a<b$. Since $a,b\ge1$, \[b^N-a^N=(b-a)\bigl(b^{N-1}+b^{N-2}a+\cdots+a^{N-1}\bigr)\ge N(b-a).\] Hence $N(b-a)<1$ for every power of two $N$, which is impossible because $b-a>0$ is fixed and $N$ can be arbitrarily large. Therefore $a=b$.

Solution 2 (The first exponent with unequal floors)

Let $m=\lfloor a\rfloor=\lfloor b\rfloor\ge1$; then $a,b\ge1$. Suppose $a\ne b$, say $a<b$ (the other case is symmetric).

The first unequal floors. For every $j\ge1$ we have $a^j<b^j$, so $\lfloor a^j\rfloor\le\lfloor b^j\rfloor$. Also \[b^j-a^j=(b-a)\bigl(b^{j-1}+b^{j-2}a+\cdots+a^{j-1}\bigr)\ge j(b-a),\] which exceeds $1$ for large $j$; then $\lfloor b^j\rfloor\ge\lfloor a^j+1\rfloor>\lfloor a^j\rfloor$. So there is a smallest $j$ with $u=\lfloor a^j\rfloor<v=\lfloor b^j\rfloor$, and $j\ge2$ because $\lfloor a\rfloor=\lfloor b\rfloor$. By hypothesis $uv$ is a perfect square.

Gap lemma. If $u<v$ are positive integers and $uv$ is a perfect square, then $v\ge u+2\sqrt u+1$.

Proof. Let $d$ be the product of the primes that occur to an odd power in $u$. As $uv$ is a square, every prime occurs in $u$ and in $v$ to powers of the same parity, so $d$ is also the product of the primes occurring to an odd power in $v$. Then $u/d$ and $v/d$ are integers with all prime exponents even, so $u=dr^2$ and $v=ds^2$ with positive integers $r<s$. Hence \[v\ge d(r+1)^2=u+2dr+d=u+2\sqrt d\,\sqrt u+d\ge u+2\sqrt u+1. \qquad\square\]

Case $j=2$. From $a\ge m$ we get $u=\lfloor a^2\rfloor\ge m^2$, so $\sqrt u\ge m$; from $b<m+1$ we get $v\le b^2<(m+1)^2$, so $\sqrt v<m+1\le\sqrt u+1$. Thus $v<u+2\sqrt u+1$, contradicting the gap lemma.

Case $j\ge3$. By the choice of $j$, $C=\lfloor a^{j-1}\rfloor=\lfloor b^{j-1}\rfloor\ge1$. Put $e=\frac{j}{j-1}$, so $1<e\le\frac32$. From $C\le a^{j-1}$ we get $a^j=(a^{j-1})^e\ge C^e$, so $u>a^j-1\ge C^e-1$; also $a^j\ge a^{j-1}\ge C$, so $u\ge C$. From $b^{j-1}<C+1$ we get $v\le b^j<(C+1)^e$. Therefore \[v-u<(C+1)^e-C^e+1. \tag{$*$}\]

  • If $C=1$: $v<2^e\le2^{3/2}<3$, so $v\le2$, and $1\le u<v$ forces $u=1$, $v=2$; but $uv=2$ is not a square.

  • If $C\ge2$: we show $(C+1)^e-C^e\le2\sqrt C$. Let $\alpha=\frac32-e\ge0$. Since $(C+1)^\alpha\ge C^\alpha\ge1$, \[\begin{aligned}(C+1)^{3/2}-C^{3/2}&=(C+1)^\alpha(C+1)^e-C^\alpha C^e\\&\ge C^\alpha\bigl((C+1)^e-C^e\bigr)\ge(C+1)^e-C^e .\end{aligned}\] Moreover \[\begin{aligned}(C+1)^{3/2}-C^{3/2}&=\sqrt{C+1}+C\bigl(\sqrt{C+1}-\sqrt C\bigr)\\&=\sqrt{C+1}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{C+1}+\sqrt C}\\&\le\sqrt{C+1}+\frac{\sqrt C}2\le\frac32\sqrt{C+1},\end{aligned}\] and $\frac32\sqrt{C+1}\le2\sqrt C$ because $9(C+1)\le16C$ for $C\ge2$. Now $(*)$ gives $v-u<2\sqrt C+1\le2\sqrt u+1$, contradicting the gap lemma.

Every case leads to a contradiction, so $a=b$.

Problem 4

Geometry · LPS VII, Chapter 20: Parity, Coloring, and Process Termination

  • (a) Consider the four sides of a square and one of its diagonals. For each such segment we arbitrarily choose a direction, thus obtaining five vectors. Show that the modulus of the sum of these five vectors is at least equal to the length of the diagonal of the square.

  • (b) Let $n$ be a positive integer and consider $n$ lines that partition a square into convex polygons. Let $M$ denote the set of all segments that represent the sides of these polygons. Show that, for each segment in $M$, we can choose a direction such that the sum of the corresponding vectors has a modulus at most equal to the length of the diagonal of the square.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (Checkerboard colouring and greedy signs)

Let the square be $ABCD$ with side $s$, so its diagonal has length $s\sqrt2$.

(a) Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(s,0)$, $C=(s,s)$, $D=(0,s)$; by symmetry we may assume the diagonal is $AC$. Whatever directions are chosen, the sides $AB$ and $DC$ give $\varepsilon_1(s,0)$ and $\varepsilon_2(s,0)$, the sides $AD$ and $BC$ give $\varepsilon_3(0,s)$ and $\varepsilon_4(0,s)$, and the diagonal gives $\delta(s,s)$, with all signs in $\{1,-1\}$. The sum is \[\bigl(s(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2+\delta),\ s(\varepsilon_3+\varepsilon_4+\delta)\bigr).\] A sum of three numbers $\pm1$ is odd, hence has absolute value at least $1$. So both coordinates have absolute value at least $s$, and the modulus is at least $\sqrt{s^2+s^2}=s\sqrt2$.

(b) Setup. Lines that do not meet the interior of the square do not affect the partition, so we discard them, and if some of the lines coincide we keep only one copy; the remaining lines are distinct. The sides of the polygons are the pieces into which the lines and the sides of the square are cut by all the intersection points. A segment of $M$ either lies on the boundary of the square (a boundary segment) and is a side of exactly one polygon, or lies inside the square on exactly one of the lines and is a side of exactly two polygons, one on each side of that line. Since $M$ is a set, a side shared by two polygons is a single element of $M$ and receives a single direction.

Colouring. For each line choose one of its two open half-planes and call it positive. Colour a polygon black if it lies in an even number of positive half-planes and white otherwise. Two polygons with a common side on a line $\ell$ lie on the same side of every line except $\ell$, so they get different colours. Hence every interior segment is a side of exactly one black polygon.

Orientation. Orient the sides of every black polygon counterclockwise around that polygon. By the previous paragraph this orients every interior segment and every boundary segment of a black polygon exactly once. Going once around a polygon brings us back to the start, so the sides of each black polygon add up to the zero vector; hence all segments oriented so far add up to $\vec 0$.

The segments still to be oriented are the boundary segments of white polygons (thick in the figure). Each is horizontal or vertical, of length at most $s$. Let $t_1,\dots,t_p$ be the lengths of the horizontal ones and $w_1,\dots,w_q$ those of the vertical ones.

Greedy signs. If $0\le t_1,\dots,t_p\le s$, the signs can be chosen so that $|\pm t_1\pm\cdots\pm t_p|\le s$. Indeed, choose them one at a time: if the current partial sum $P$ is $\ge0$ subtract the next number, otherwise add it. If $P\in[0,s]$ then $P-t\in[-s,s]$, and if $P\in[-s,0)$ then $P+t\in[-s,s)$; so the partial sums stay in $[-s,s]$.

Choose the directions of the horizontal white boundary segments this way, and likewise for the vertical ones. The total sum of all vectors is then $(H,V)$ with $|H|\le s$ and $|V|\le s$, so its modulus is at most $\sqrt{s^2+s^2}=s\sqrt2$, the diagonal of the square.

Remark. Part (a) is the case of one line through a diagonal, so the bound in (b) is sharp.

Solution 2 (Opposite orientations on the two colours)

Let the square be $ABCD$ with side $s$ and diagonal $s\sqrt2$; let $\vec u=\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\vec v=\overrightarrow{AD}$, so $\vec u\perp\vec v$ and $|\vec u|=|\vec v|=s$.

(a) We may assume the diagonal is $AC$, with vector $\pm(\vec u+\vec v)$. The two sides parallel to $AB$ contribute $\varepsilon_1\vec u+\varepsilon_2\vec u$ and the other two $\varepsilon_3\vec v+\varepsilon_4\vec v$, with signs $\pm1$. With $\delta=\pm1$ for the diagonal, the sum is \[\vec S=(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2+\delta)\vec u+(\varepsilon_3+\varepsilon_4+\delta)\vec v .\] Both coefficients are odd integers, hence at least $1$ in absolute value, and since $\vec u\perp\vec v$, $|\vec S|^2\ge|\vec u|^2+|\vec v|^2=2s^2$. So $|\vec S|\ge s\sqrt2$.

(b) Discard the lines that miss the interior of the square, and keep one copy of any repeated line. Every segment of $M$ is either a boundary segment (on a side of the square, a side of one polygon) or an interior segment on exactly one line, a side of exactly two polygons lying on opposite sides of that line. Since $M$ is a set, a side shared by two polygons is a single element of $M$ and receives a single direction.

Two colours. Give every line a positive side, and colour a polygon black or white according as the number of positive half-planes containing it is even or odd. Neighbours across an interior segment on line $\ell$ differ only in their position relative to $\ell$, so they have different colours.

Orientation. Traverse every black polygon counterclockwise and every white polygon clockwise. When we go counterclockwise around a polygon, the polygon is on our left; two neighbours lie on opposite sides of their common segment, so their counterclockwise traversals run along it in opposite directions. Reversing the white one, we see that the two traversals of every interior segment run in the same direction. Give each segment of $M$ this direction (a boundary segment gets the direction of its only polygon). The figure shows the result.

Summing. Each polygon's sides, traversed this way, add up to $\vec 0$. Adding over all polygons, every interior segment is counted twice and every boundary segment once: \[2\vec I+\vec B=\vec 0,\] where $\vec I$ is the sum of the interior segments and $\vec B$ that of the boundary segments, all with the chosen directions. The required sum is therefore \[\vec I+\vec B=\tfrac12\vec B .\] The boundary segments on $AB$ and $DC$ are horizontal pieces of total length $2s$, so the horizontal component of $\vec B$ is at most $2s$ in absolute value; likewise the vertical component. Hence $|\tfrac12\vec B|\le\tfrac12\sqrt{(2s)^2+(2s)^2}=s\sqrt2$, the length of the diagonal.

Remark. Here the directions are given by one explicit rule; no step-by-step choice is needed.

Solution 3 (Closed walks in the cutting graph)

Let the square be $ABCD$ with side $s$; let $\vec u=\overrightarrow{AB}$, $\vec v=\overrightarrow{AD}$, so $\vec u\perp\vec v$, $|\vec u|=|\vec v|=s$, and the diagonal has length $|\vec u+\vec v|=s\sqrt2$.

(a) We may assume the diagonal is $AC$, with vector $\vec D=\pm(\vec u+\vec v)$. The sides $AB$ and $DC$ are parallel to $\vec u$, so their two vectors add up to $\vec0$ or $\pm2\vec u$; likewise $AD$ and $BC$ give $\vec 0$ or $\pm2\vec v$. Let $\vec U$ be the sum of the four side vectors.

  • If $\vec U=\vec 0$, the sum is $\vec D$, of length $s\sqrt2$.

  • If $\vec U=\pm2\vec u\pm2\vec v$, then $|\vec U|=2s\sqrt2$ and $|\vec U+\vec D|\ge|\vec U|-|\vec D|=s\sqrt2$.

  • If $\vec U=\pm2\vec u$ (or $\pm2\vec v$), then $\vec U\cdot\vec D=\pm2s^2$, so $|\vec U+\vec D|^2=4s^2+2s^2+2\,\vec U\cdot\vec D\ge6s^2-4s^2=2s^2$.

In every case the modulus is at least $s\sqrt2$.

(b) Setup. Discard the lines that miss the interior of the square, and keep one copy of any repeated line. A segment of $M$ is either an interior segment (a piece of one line between consecutive intersection points) or a boundary segment (a piece of a side of the square between consecutive points where lines meet the boundary or corners). Since $M$ is a set, a side shared by two polygons is a single element of $M$ and receives a single direction.

The cutting graph. Let $\Gamma$ be the graph whose vertices are all endpoints of segments of $M$ (including the four corners) and whose edges are the interior segments. If a vertex $V$ is strictly inside the square, every line through $V$ continues into the interior on both sides of $V$, so it contributes exactly two edges at $V$; hence $V$ has even degree. The sum of all degrees is twice the number of edges, so the number of boundary vertices of odd degree is even, say $2r$.

Pairing the odd vertices. List the odd boundary vertices counterclockwise around the square as $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_{2r}$. For $i=1,\dots,r$ add to $\Gamma$ the boundary segments that form the counterclockwise arc from $B_{2i-1}$ to $B_{2i}$ (thick in the figure). No other $B$ lies inside such an arc, so the arcs do not overlap. Each $B_k$ gains one edge, each vertex strictly inside an added arc gains two, and the other vertices gain none. In the new graph $\Gamma'$ every degree is even.

Closed walks. In a finite graph in which all degrees are even, the edges split into closed walks. Start at a vertex $V_0$ that has an unused edge and keep walking along unused edges until no unused edge is available. Each time the walk enters a vertex $W\ne V_0$ it has used an odd number of the edges at $W$, and $W$ has even degree, so the walk can leave again; hence it can only stop at $V_0$. Remove the edges of this closed walk: all degrees remain even, and we repeat until no edges are left. Direct every edge of $\Gamma'$ along its closed walk. The vectors along a closed walk add up to $\vec0$, so all segments of $\Gamma'$ together give $\vec 0$.

The rest. The segments of $M$ not yet directed are boundary segments outside the added arcs; direct them counterclockwise around the square. Those on $AB$ point along $\vec u$ and have total length $L_1\in[0,s]$; those on $CD$ point along $-\vec u$ and have total length $L_2\in[0,s]$. Similarly those on $BC$ and $DA$ have total lengths $L_3,L_4\in[0,s]$ and point along $\vec v$ and $-\vec v$. The total sum is \[\frac{L_1-L_2}{s}\,\vec u+\frac{L_3-L_4}{s}\,\vec v,\qquad |L_1-L_2|\le s,\ \ |L_3-L_4|\le s,\] so its modulus is at most $\sqrt{s^2+s^2}=s\sqrt2$, the length of the diagonal.

Remark. No colouring of the polygons is needed here. For a single line along a diagonal the configuration is that of (a), so the bound in (b) is sharp.

Problem 1

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 46: Functional Equations: Iteration, Rigidity, and Deep Structure

Determine the functions $f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ where $\mathbb{N}$ is the set of non-negative integers, with $f$ strictly increasing and $g$ surjective, such that \[f(nf(n^2)+n)\leq f(n^3)+f(g(n))\] for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

Show the answer

$f(n)=n$ and $g(n)=n$ for all $n$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Induction on the values of $g$)

Preparation. Since $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb N$, $f(m+1)\ge f(m)+1$, so \[f(y)-f(x)\ge y-x\qquad (y\ge x). \tag{1}\] Put $e(m)=f(m)-m$. By (1), $e$ is non-decreasing, and $e(0)=f(0)\ge0$, so $e(m)\ge0$ for all $m$.

Key inequality. Let $X=nf(n^2)+n=n^3+n\,e(n^2)+n\ge n^3$. By (1) and the hypothesis, \[n\bigl(e(n^2)+1\bigr)=X-n^3\le f(X)-f(n^3)\le f(g(n)),\] that is, \[f(g(n))\ge n\bigl(e(n^2)+1\bigr)\qquad\text{for all } n\in\mathbb N. \tag{2}\]

Step 1: $g$ is the identity. We prove $g(k)=k$ by strong induction on $k\ge0$. Assume $g(j)=j$ for all $j<k$ (nothing to assume for $k=0$). As $g$ is surjective, $g(n)=k$ for some $n$. We cannot have $n<k$, since then $g(n)=n\ne k$. If $n\ge k+1$, then $n^2\ge k$, so $e(n^2)\ge e(k)$, and (2) gives \[k+e(k)=f(k)\ge n\bigl(e(n^2)+1\bigr)\ge(k+1)\bigl(e(k)+1\bigr)=k+1+(k+1)e(k)>k+e(k),\] a contradiction. Hence $n=k$, i.e. $g(k)=k$.

Step 2: $f$ is the identity. With $g(n)=n$, (2) becomes $n+e(n)\ge n\,e(n^2)+n$, so $e(n)\ge n\,e(n^2)\ge n\,e(n)$ (because $n^2\ge n$). For $n\ge2$ this gives $(n-1)e(n)\le0$, so $e(n)=0$; and then $0\le e(0)\le e(1)\le e(2)=0$. Thus $e\equiv0$, i.e. $f(n)=n$.

Check. For $f=g=\mathrm{id}$ both sides equal $n^3+n$. The only pair is $\boxed{f(n)=n,\ g(n)=n}$.

Solution 2 (Counting preimages)

Since $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb N$, $f(y)-f(x)\ge y-x$ for $y\ge x$. Hence $e(m)=f(m)-m$ is non-decreasing and $e(m)\ge e(0)=f(0)\ge0$.

The basic bound. The argument $X=nf(n^2)+n$ equals $n^3+n\,e(n^2)+n\ge n^3$, so $f(X)-f(n^3)\ge X-n^3=n(e(n^2)+1)$. Together with the hypothesis $f(X)-f(n^3)\le f(g(n))$: \[\text{if } g(n)=k,\ \text{then } k+e(k)=f(k)\ge n\bigl(e(n^2)+1\bigr). \tag{1}\]

Step 1: $e\equiv0$. Suppose not: $e(N)\ge1$ for some $N$, which we may take $\ge1$. Then $e(k)\ge1$ for all $k\ge N$. Let $K=2N+2$. Since $g$ is surjective, choose for each $k\in\{N,N+1,\dots,K\}$ some $n_k$ with $g(n_k)=k$; these $K-N+1$ numbers are distinct. We bound each $n_k$:

  • if $n_k^2<k$, then $n_k<\sqrt K\le\frac{K+1}{2}$;

  • if $n_k^2\ge k$, then $e(n_k^2)\ge e(k)$, and (1) gives $n_k\le\dfrac{k+e(k)}{e(k)+1}=1+\dfrac{k-1}{e(k)+1}\le1+\dfrac{k-1}{2}=\dfrac{k+1}{2}\le\dfrac{K+1}{2}$.

So $K-N+1$ distinct non-negative integers are at most $\frac{K+1}{2}$, which allows at most $\frac{K+1}{2}+1$ of them. Hence $K-N+1\le\frac{K+3}{2}$, i.e. $K\le 2N+1$, contradicting $K=2N+2$. Therefore $e\equiv0$ and $f(n)=n$ for all $n$.

Step 2: $g\equiv\mathrm{id}$. Now (1) says $g(n)\ge n$ for every $n$. We show $g(k)=k$ by strong induction. Let $n$ satisfy $g(n)=k$; then $n\le g(n)=k$. If $n<k$, the induction hypothesis gives $g(n)=n\ne k$; so $n=k$ and $g(k)=k$.

Check. For $f=g=\mathrm{id}$, $f(nf(n^2)+n)=n^3+n=f(n^3)+f(g(n))$. The unique pair is $\boxed{f(n)=n,\ g(n)=n}$.

Problem 2

Algebra · LPS VII, Chapter 9: Polynomials and Root-Coefficient Strategy

Let $z_1,z_2,z_3\in\mathbb{C}$ with $|z_1|=|z_2|=|z_3|=1$. For every $k\in\mathbb{N}^+$, denote $s_k=z_1^k+z_2^k+z_3^k$. If $s_1,s_2,s_3\in\mathbb{Z}$, prove that $z_1^{12}=z_2^{12}=z_3^{12}=1$.

Show the 3 solutions

Solution 1 (The cubic with roots $z_1,z_2,z_3$)

Let $e_1=z_1+z_2+z_3$, $e_2=z_1z_2+z_2z_3+z_3z_1$, $e_3=z_1z_2z_3$. Newton's identities give \[e_1=s_1,\qquad 2e_2=s_1^2-s_2,\qquad 6e_3=s_1^3-3s_1s_2+2s_3,\] so $e_3$ is rational. Also $|e_3|=|z_1||z_2||z_3|=1$, and the only rationals of modulus $1$ are $\pm1$; hence $e_3=\pm1$.

Since $|z_i|=1$, $\overline{z_i}=1/z_i$. Therefore \[s_1=\overline{s_1}=\frac1{z_1}+\frac1{z_2}+\frac1{z_3}=\frac{e_2}{e_3},\qquad\text{so}\qquad e_2=e_3s_1 .\] Thus $z_1,z_2,z_3$ are the roots of $p(x)=x^3-s_1x^2+e_3s_1x-e_3$, and $p$ factors: \[\begin{aligned}e_3=1&:\quad p(x)=(x-1)\bigl(x^2-(s_1-1)x+1\bigr),\\ e_3=-1&:\quad p(x)=(x+1)\bigl(x^2-(s_1+1)x+1\bigr).\end{aligned}\] So one of the $z_i$ equals $\pm1$, and the other two are the roots of $x^2-tx+1$ with an integer $t$. Let $z$ be such a root. Then $z+\frac1z=t$ and $\frac1z=\overline z$, so $t=2\operatorname{Re}z\in[-2,2]$, i.e. $t\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$:

  • $t=\pm2$: $(z\mp1)^2=0$, so $z=\pm1$ and $z^2=1$;

  • $t=0$: $z^2=-1$, so $z^4=1$;

  • $t=1$: $z^2-z+1=0$; multiplying by $z+1$ gives $z^3=-1$, so $z^6=1$;

  • $t=-1$: $z^2+z+1=0$; multiplying by $z-1$ gives $z^3=1$.

In every case the order of $z$ divides $1,2,3,4$ or $6$, all divisors of $12$. Hence $z_1^{12}=z_2^{12}=z_3^{12}=1$.

Solution 2 (A trigonometric product)

The product. Expanding $(z_1+z_2+z_3)^3$ and $(z_1+z_2+z_3)(z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2)$ gives the identity \[6z_1z_2z_3=s_1^3-3s_1s_2+2s_3,\] so $P=z_1z_2z_3$ is rational with $|P|=1$; hence $P=\pm1$. If $P=-1$, replace each $z_i$ by $-z_i$: the new power sums are $(-1)^ks_k$, still integers, the new product is $1$, and the twelfth powers do not change. So we may assume $z_1z_2z_3=1$.

One of the numbers is $1$. Write $z_1=e^{i\alpha}$, $z_2=e^{i\beta}$; then $z_3=e^{-i(\alpha+\beta)}$. Since $s_1$ is real, \[0=\operatorname{Im}s_1=\sin\alpha+\sin\beta-\sin(\alpha+\beta).\] Using sum-to-product formulas, \[\sin\alpha+\sin\beta-\sin(\alpha+\beta)=2\sin\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2\Bigl(\cos\tfrac{\alpha-\beta}2-\cos\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2\Bigr)=4\sin\tfrac{\alpha}2\sin\tfrac{\beta}2\sin\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}2 .\] So one of $\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta$ is a multiple of $2\pi$, i.e. one of $z_1$, $z_2$, $z_1z_2=1/z_3$ equals $1$. Renumbering, $z_3=1$.

The other two. Then $z_1z_2=1$, so $z_2=1/z_1=\overline{z_1}$ and $s_1=1+2\cos\alpha$. Since $s_1$ is an integer, $2\cos\alpha\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$, so $\alpha$ is a multiple of $\frac{\pi}{2}$ or of $\frac{\pi}{3}$. In both cases $12\alpha$ is a multiple of $2\pi$, hence $z_1^{12}=e^{12i\alpha}=1$ and $z_2^{12}=\overline{z_1^{12}}=1$. Together with $z_3^{12}=1$ this proves the claim.

Solution 3 (Imaginary parts of the power sums)

Write $z_j=x_j+iy_j$ with $x_j,y_j$ real and $x_j^2+y_j^2=1$. For such a number, \[\operatorname{Im}z_j=y_j,\qquad\operatorname{Im}z_j^2=2x_jy_j,\] \[\operatorname{Im}z_j^3=3x_j^2y_j-y_j^3=3x_j^2y_j-y_j(1-x_j^2)=(4x_j^2-1)y_j .\] Since $s_1,s_2,s_3$ are real, $\sum_jy_j=0$, $\sum_jx_jy_j=0$ and $\sum_j(4x_j^2-1)y_j=0$, hence also $\sum_jx_j^2y_j=0$. Taking linear combinations, \[\sum_{j=1}^3P(x_j)\,y_j=0\qquad\text{for every polynomial $P$ of degree at most $2$.} \tag{$*$}\] A real number of modulus $1$ is $\pm1$, and $(\pm1)^{12}=1$. We consider how many of the $x_j$ coincide.

All $x_j$ distinct. For a fixed $j$ let $P(X)=\prod_{k\ne j}(X-x_k)$. Then $P(x_k)=0$ for $k\ne j$ and $P(x_j)\ne0$, so $(*)$ gives $y_j=0$. All three numbers are real, hence $\pm1$.

All $x_j$ equal to some $x$. Then $y_j^2=1-x^2$, so $y_j=\varepsilon_jc$ with $c=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ and $\varepsilon_j=\pm1$. From $\sum y_j=0$ we get $(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_3)c=0$; a sum of three numbers $\pm1$ is odd, so $c=0$, and all three numbers are $\pm1$.

Exactly two equal. Relabel so that $x_1=x_2=x\ne x_3$. With $P(X)=X-x$, $(*)$ gives $(x_3-x)y_3=0$, so $y_3=0$ and $z_3=\pm1$. Then $\sum y_j=0$ gives $y_2=-y_1$. If $y_1=0$, all three numbers are $\pm1$. Otherwise $z_1,z_2=x\pm iy_1$ are not real, so $|x|<1$, and $s_1=2x+z_3$ is an integer; hence $2x$ is an integer in $(-2,2)$, i.e. $2x\in\{-1,0,1\}$.

  • $x=0$: $z_1,z_2=\pm i$, and $z_1^4=z_2^4=1$.

  • $x=\pm\frac12$: then $4x^2-1=0$, so $\operatorname{Im}z_1^3=\operatorname{Im}z_2^3=0$. Thus $z_1^3,z_2^3$ are real of modulus $1$, i.e. $\pm1$, and $z_1^6=z_2^6=1$.

In every case each $z_j$ satisfies $z_j^d=1$ with $d\in\{2,4,6\}$, a divisor of $12$. Hence $z_1^{12}=z_2^{12}=z_3^{12}=1$.

Remark. The proof uses only that $s_2,s_3$ are real and $s_1$ is an integer.

Problem 3

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 6: Pigeonhole Principle and Density Arguments

Let $z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{2026}$ be complex numbers with $|z_i|\leq 1$ for $i=\overline{1,2026}$. Show that there exist $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\ldots,\varepsilon_{2026}\in\{-1,0,1\}$, not all zero, such that \[|\varepsilon_1z_1+\varepsilon_2z_2+\cdots+\varepsilon_{2026}z_{2026}|\leq 2^{-1000}.\]

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Pigeonhole on a grid of tiny squares)

For a subset $A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,2026\}$ let $\sigma(A)=\sum_{i\in A}z_i$ (with $\sigma(\varnothing)=0$). There are $2^{2026}$ subsets. Since $|z_i|\le1$, the real and imaginary parts of each $z_i$ lie in $[-1,1]$, so both coordinates of every $\sigma(A)$ lie in $[-2026,2026]$.

Let $h=2^{-1001}$. Cut the interval $[-2026,2026]$, of length $4052$, into $M=4052\cdot2^{1001}$ consecutive intervals of length $h$; this cuts the square $[-2026,2026]^2$ into $M^2$ small squares of side $h$. Since $4052<4096=2^{12}$, \[M^2<\bigl(2^{12}\cdot2^{1001}\bigr)^2=2^{2026}.\] By the pigeonhole principle two different subsets $A\ne B$ have $\sigma(A)$ and $\sigma(B)$ in the same small square, so \[|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)|\le h\sqrt2<2h=2^{-1000}.\] Let $\varepsilon_i=1$ if $i\in A\setminus B$, $\varepsilon_i=-1$ if $i\in B\setminus A$, and $\varepsilon_i=0$ otherwise. Then $\varepsilon_i\in\{-1,0,1\}$, not all zero (because $A\ne B$), and \[\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{2026}\varepsilon_iz_i\Bigr|=|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)|<2^{-1000}.\]

Remark. The same count can be done with areas (this is the official solution). If every two sums were at least $2\rho$ apart, with $\rho=2^{-1001}$, the $2^{2026}$ open disks of radius $\rho$ around them would be disjoint, with total area $\pi\cdot2^{24}$, yet all lie in the disk of radius $2027$ about $0$ (by the triangle inequality $|\sigma(A)|\le2026$), of area $\pi\cdot2027^2<\pi\cdot2^{24}$. This version has a little room to spare: with disks of radius $2^{-1002}$ it even gives the bound $2^{-1001}$.

Solution 2 (Sorting the subset sums twice)

For each subset $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,2026\}$ put $\sigma(A)=\sum_{i\in A}z_i$. Since $|z_i|\le1$, the real and imaginary parts of every $\sigma(A)$ lie in $[-2026,2026]$, an interval of length $4052<2^{12}$. Let $T=2^{1013}$; there are $2^{2026}=T^2$ subsets.

Sorting by real part. List all $T^2$ subsets so that the real parts of their sums are non-decreasing, and cut the list into $T$ blocks of $T$ consecutive subsets. The width of a block is the largest minus the smallest real part in it. Because the list is sorted, the last real part in each block is at most the first real part in the next block, so the widths of the blocks add up to at most the largest minus the smallest real part overall, which is at most $4052$. So some block $\mathcal B$ has width at most $\frac{4052}{T}<\frac{2^{12}}{2^{1013}}=2^{-1001}$.

Sorting by imaginary part. List the $T$ subsets of $\mathcal B$ so that the imaginary parts of their sums are non-decreasing. The $T-1$ gaps between neighbours add up to the largest minus the smallest imaginary part in $\mathcal B$, which is at most $4052$, so some gap is at most $\frac{4052}{T-1}$; and $\frac{4052}{T-1}<2^{-1001}$ because $4052\cdot2^{1001}<2^{12}\cdot2^{1001}-1=T-1$. This gives two different subsets $A\ne B$ in $\mathcal B$ whose sums differ by less than $2^{-1001}$ in imaginary part and, as both lie in $\mathcal B$, by less than $2^{-1001}$ in real part. Hence \[|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)|<\sqrt2\cdot2^{-1001}<2^{-1000}.\]

The signs. Put $\varepsilon_i=1$ if $i\in A\setminus B$, $\varepsilon_i=-1$ if $i\in B\setminus A$, and $\varepsilon_i=0$ otherwise. They are not all zero because $A\ne B$, and \[\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{2026}\varepsilon_iz_i\Bigr|=|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)|<2^{-1000}.\]

Problem 4

Combinatorics · LPS VII, Chapter 52: Advanced Combinatorial Structure and Hybrid Methods

Let $n\geq 5$ be an odd positive integer. For a convex polygon $P=A_1A_2\ldots A_n$ and a point $S$ in its interior, not on any diagonal of the polygon, we denote by $q_P(S)$ the number of distinct convex quadrilaterals with vertices in $\{A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n\}$ which contain $S$ in their interior. Determine the largest possible value of $q_P(S)$ over all convex polygons $P$ and points $S$ inside them not on diagonals.

Show the answer

$\dfrac{n(n^2-1)(n-3)}{48}$

Show the 2 solutions

Solution 1 (Counting quadruples in a half-plane)

Write $n=2m+1$ ($m\ge2$).

No two vertices are collinear with $S$. A line through two vertices $A_i,A_j$ meets the convex polygon exactly in the segment $A_iA_j$ (a vertex cannot lie strictly inside a segment contained in the polygon). So if $S$ were on such a line it would lie on the side or diagonal $A_iA_j$; a side is impossible since $S$ is interior, and diagonals are excluded.

When does a quadrilateral contain $S$? Any four vertices form a convex quadrilateral $Q$, and $S$ is not on its boundary (its sides are sides or diagonals of $P$). If the four vertices lie in an open half-plane bounded by a line through $S$, then so does $Q$, and $S\notin Q$. Conversely, if $S$ is outside the convex polygon $Q$, some line through $S$ leaves $Q$ in one open half-plane. Hence $S$ is inside $Q$ if and only if the four vertices of $Q$ are not contained in an open half-plane bounded by a line through $S$.

Counting the bad quadruples. For each vertex $A_i$ let $d_i$ be the number of vertices $A_j$ such that the angle from ray $SA_i$ to ray $SA_j$, measured counterclockwise, lies strictly between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ (it is never exactly $0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$). In the figure, $d_1=3$. If four vertices lie in an open half-plane through $S$, then exactly one of them, the "first" one in the counterclockwise order, sees the other three within its counterclockwise half-turn (no other one does: if $A_i$ and $A_j$ both did, the counterclockwise angles from $SA_i$ to $SA_j$ and from $SA_j$ to $SA_i$ would both be below $180^\circ$, yet they add up to $360^\circ$); conversely, if $A_i$ sees three vertices within its counterclockwise half-turn, these four vertices lie in an open half-plane through $S$. So the number of bad quadruples is $\sum_i\binom{d_i}3$ and \[q_P(S)=\binom n4-\sum_{i=1}^n\binom{d_i}{3}.\]

The sum of the $d_i$. For two vertices $A_i,A_j$ the counterclockwise angles from $SA_i$ to $SA_j$ and from $SA_j$ to $SA_i$ add up to $360^\circ$ and neither is $180^\circ$, so exactly one is below $180^\circ$. Hence $\sum_i d_i=\binom n2=nm$.

A convexity bound. For every integer $d\ge0$, \[\binom d3-\binom m3\ge\binom m2(d-m).\] Indeed $\binom{t+1}3-\binom t3=\binom t2$. For $d\ge m$ the left side is $\sum_{t=m}^{d-1}\binom t2\ge(d-m)\binom m2$; for $d<m$ it equals $-\sum_{t=d}^{m-1}\binom t2\ge-(m-d)\binom m2$. Summing over $i$ and using $\sum d_i=nm$: \[\sum_i\binom{d_i}3\ge n\binom m3,\qquad\text{so}\qquad q_P(S)\le\binom n4-n\binom m3 .\]

Simplifying. Since $n-1=2m$ and $n-3=2(m-1)$, $\binom n4=\frac{n\,m(2m-1)(m-1)}6$, and \[\binom n4-n\binom m3=\frac{n\,m(m-1)\bigl[(2m-1)-(m-2)\bigr]}{6}=\frac{n(m-1)m(m+1)}6,\] and since $(m-1)m(m+1)=\frac{(n-3)(n-1)(n+1)}{8}$ this equals $\dfrac{n(n^2-1)(n-3)}{48}$.

Equality. Take the regular $n$-gon and its centre $O$. A chord through $O$ is a diameter, and since $n$ is odd no two vertices are opposite, so $O$ lies on no diagonal. Seen from $O$, the other vertices are at angles $\frac{360^\circ k}{n}$, $k=1,\dots,n-1$, from any given vertex, and exactly $k=1,\dots,m$ give angles below $180^\circ$. So every $d_i=m$ and equality holds.

The largest possible value is $\boxed{\dfrac{n(n^2-1)(n-3)}{48}}$.

Solution 2 (Reducing to triangles)

Write $n=2m+1$. As in any convex polygon, a line through two vertices meets $P$ only in the segment joining them, so $S$ (interior, on no diagonal) lies on no line through two vertices. Consequently $S$ is never on a side of a triangle or quadrilateral with vertices among the $A_i$.

Quadrilaterals versus triangles. Let $t$ be the number of triangles with vertices among the $A_i$ that contain $S$. Count the pairs $(T,Q)$ where $Q$ is one of our quadrilaterals, $T$ is a triangle formed by three of its four vertices, and $S\in T$.

  • If $Q=ABCD$ contains $S$: the diagonal $AC$ cuts $Q$ into triangles $ABC$, $ACD$, and $S$ (not on $AC$) is in exactly one of them; likewise exactly one of $ABD$, $BCD$ contains $S$. So $Q$ is in exactly $2$ pairs (see the figure).

  • If $Q$ does not contain $S$, none of its four triangles does: $0$ pairs.

On the other hand, each triangle containing $S$ extends to a quadrilateral by any of the $n-3$ other vertices. Hence \[2\,q_P(S)=(n-3)\,t. \tag{1}\] So we must maximise $t$.

Counting triangles. A triangle with vertices among the $A_i$ fails to contain $S$ exactly when its three vertices lie in an open half-plane bounded by a line through $S$ (if they do, the triangle lies in that half-plane; if $S$ is outside the triangle, a line through $S$ separates them). For each vertex $A_i$ let $d_i$ be the number of vertices whose ray from $S$ is reached from ray $SA_i$ by a counterclockwise turn strictly between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. Each bad triple has exactly one vertex that sees the other two inside its counterclockwise half-turn: the first one counterclockwise inside the half-plane does, and no two vertices $A_i,A_j$ both do, since the counterclockwise angles from $SA_i$ to $SA_j$ and back add up to $360^\circ$ and cannot both be below $180^\circ$. Conversely, a vertex together with two vertices inside its counterclockwise half-turn forms a bad triple. So \[t=\binom n3-\sum_i\binom{d_i}2 .\] For each pair of vertices exactly one of the two counterclockwise angles between their rays is below $180^\circ$, so $\sum_i d_i=\binom n2=nm$.

Cauchy-Schwarz. $\sum_i d_i^2\ge\frac1n\bigl(\sum_i d_i\bigr)^2=nm^2$, hence \[\sum_i\binom{d_i}2=\frac12\Bigl(\sum_i d_i^2-\sum_i d_i\Bigr)\ge\frac{nm^2-nm}{2}=n\binom m2,\] and \[t\le\binom n3-n\binom m2=\frac{n\,m(2m-1)}3-\frac{n\,m(m-1)}2=\frac{n\,m(m+1)}6 .\]

Back to quadrilaterals. By (1), with $n-3=2(m-1)$, \[q_P(S)=\frac{(n-3)t}{2}\le\frac{n(m-1)m(m+1)}{6}=\frac{n(n^2-1)(n-3)}{48}.\]

Equality. For the regular $n$-gon and its centre $O$ (on no diagonal, since $n$ is odd and no two vertices are diametrically opposite), every $d_i=m$, so $t=\binom n3-n\binom m2$ and equality holds throughout. The maximum is $\boxed{\dfrac{n(n^2-1)(n-3)}{48}}$.
